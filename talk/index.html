the continuous mirror of computational social choice, and the pipeline that charts it

MARTIN KOUTECKÝ · COMPUTER SCIENCE INSTITUTE, CHARLES UNIVERSITY · choco-atlas.eu
One idea to leave with: take any paper about agents with preferences, replace the agents by a handful of types carrying continuous mass, and ask whether the problem got easier. Asking is mechanical. Answering used to be a paper. Now it is a pipeline run. Ten minutes: the idea in two pictures, why a machine can ask it, what came out, then the atlas live.
The original problem
Voters are indivisible.
Each has a ranking. An attacker pays to change some of them, and asks for the cheapest way to make a candidate win. Hard. Bribery under the Borda rule. NP-hard, like most of the field's problems, and studied for two decades.
No comsoc needed. Voters, rankings, an attacker with a budget. The question "cheapest way to change the outcome" is NP-hard for almost every voting rule. The field has hundreds of such hardness results. The claim of the project: a lot of that hardness comes from one thing, that voters are whole.
The mirror
Merge the voters into types, and let the types carry mass.
Same question, same rule, masses instead of heads. A linear program. Borda bribery, continuous version: polynomial. The hardness was the indivisibility.

Not always. Sometimes the continuous problem is as hard as the original, and that is the interesting case.

Same picture, voters merged into four bars. The bribery problem becomes a linear program and is polynomial. So for this one problem, the hardness was entirely the indivisibility. The project question: how much of the field's hardness is like that, and where hardness survives, what is it made of. Note the footer: sometimes nothing changes. That is a finding too.

A question that is the same for every paper is a question a machine can ask six hundred times, with a proof attempt attached.

The point for this audience. The mirror question has a fixed shape: does the problem survive replacing agents by masses, and if so, does its complexity change. A fixed-shape question is what a language model pipeline is good at asking at scale, and the proof attempts are where current models earn their keep. Not every paper has a mirror; for some, the continuous version is the same problem after scaling, and the pipeline says so.

Walk down the funnel. Thirteen thousand candidate papers from OpenAlex, fourteen hundred pulled so far, screened for social choice, then judged: does the society continuize, and which result is the anchor. Of the green papers, 175 have a mirror posed and a proof attempted, 142 of those proofs survived an adversarial review by a second model. Five stages, each a model run, all transcripts kept. My job is checking judgments, not writing proofs.
