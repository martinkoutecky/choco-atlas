The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational result in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Equations (1)–(9) define computations used by the system, and the evaluation reports observations and a proposed study; neither supplies the kind of named result required as an anchor. So there are no per-anchor mirror questions I can responsibly claim under your rules.

The strongest possible positive lead is the Vote scene’s tally rule, Equation (9), though it is not a valid anchor. For a large online museum curation event deciding whether to add one image, let the three types be participants who vote yes, no, or abstain. Let \(\mu_+\), \(\mu_-\), and \(\mu_0\) be their respective population shares, with rational masses summing to \(1\). The decision problem is: given these masses, determine whether to add the image, outputting \(1\) exactly when \(\mu_+ \ge \mu_-\), and \(0\) otherwise. This mirrors the paper’s rule that the image is added when yes votes are at least as numerous as no votes. The type is complete for this decision: other preferences and actions do not affect the Vote scene’s tally.

A large online community is a plausible regime: the paper discusses crowdsourcing and its future mass audience, while the number of ballot types stays at three as the number of participants grows. The authors would likely recognize the threshold rule as their voting decision applied to a population distribution. This isolated problem is tractable in time polynomial in the input length and support size. It generates no meaningful hardness-transfer question; it is a direct fractional version of vote counting.

That is a narrow mirror, and its weakness is also its limit: the paper’s described sessions involve small, synchronous groups, and the threshold calculation offers no computational challenge. It says nothing about continuous models of the argumentation, workflow control, or the paper’s other group decision procedures. Without a named computational anchor, I would present it only as a plausible population-level extension, not as the requested anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that this paper states no computational result to continuize. It presents an implemented workflow, aggregation formulas, preliminary user observations, and a proposed evaluation. It does not formulate or analyze a complexity problem, optimization problem, or algorithmic task as a research result. The proponent is right to say there is no eligible named result; that leaves no anchor for a continuous mirror of the paper’s computational contribution.

The Vote-scene example is the strongest counterproposal. A large online curation event could have yes, no, and abstain types, with the image accepted when \(\mu_+ \ge \mu_-\). The paper does anticipate an open-web audience, so it would be too strong to dismiss a large population as implausible. But the proposal continuizes a tally rule, not a computational result. The paper defines that rule as one operation inside a small-group workflow; it does not study the computation of the tally. Even if authors recognized the mass version as a natural extension, it would not supply the missing result to mirror.

A richer version could distribute types by image preference, zoom activity, or tags. That still does not produce a computational question from the paper: the authors specify aggregation procedures for those inputs, but make no algorithmic claim about them. And retaining the parts that make this curation system distinctive—bilateral argument, exchanged tags, and transitions driven by the session’s activity—requires representing who interacts with whom and in what order. Pooling those histories into population shares would no longer describe the same session. Adding costs or strategic interventions could create a new computational problem, but those would be new questions, not mirrors of results here.

So the negative case is strong under the programme’s stated gate: there is no computational result here whose continuous version can be assessed. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile future model could grow out of this system. The paper’s mass-audience ambition makes such a model conceivable; it simply does not turn the paper’s tally formula or workflow into a computational result.
