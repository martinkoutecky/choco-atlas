What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Jefferson aggregation over continuous rater types; it does not mirror a named computational result, or the paper’s empirical findings on representativeness and residual forest structure.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive angle is continuous-mass Jefferson aggregation of tree approvals. Its type is a complete approval set \(A\subseteq X\), where \(X\) is the finite set of inventoried trees; \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of raters with exactly that approval set. A plausible regime is a large forestry-service or conservation consultation in which many trained raters assess the same tagged inventory under shared instructions, so the number of raters is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct approval sets.

Here is a precise candidate problem, though it has no qualifying paper result to anchor it. The input is \(X\), each tree’s rational basal area \(b_x\), rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\), and a fixed tie-breaking order. Starting with \(S=\varnothing\), repeatedly select the unselected tree maximizing
\[ J_x(S)=\sum_{A\ni x}\frac{\mu_A}{1+|A\cap S|}. \]
Stop when the selected trees’ total basal area exceeds \(30\%\) of the stand’s initial basal area, and output the selected set \(S\). This keeps the paper’s discrete trees and its Jefferson aggregation rule; only the rater population becomes continuous. The expected result is tractability: each round’s scores can be computed from the \(\tau\) approval types, giving a direct polynomial-time algorithm in the number of trees, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Natural follow-up questions concern the other rules studied in the paper, especially continuous-mass Phragmén aggregation, and how computation changes with tie handling or the stopping threshold.

The limitation is decisive for the requested anchors: the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered material is sections and equations; its findings are empirical comparisons on experiments with 6–20 raters, not named complexity results. So this is a plausible continuous mirror of the paper’s aggregation problem, but I cannot honestly present it as a mirror of a qualifying named result. That is also the weakest point: the authors might recognize the model, but the paper neither studies a high-multiplicity regime nor establishes a computational result for the underlying problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Its formal equations define approval rules; its findings compare those rules empirically across 50 forest experiments. It proves no complexity classification or algorithmic theorem. The proponent’s proposed Jefferson question acknowledges this gap, so it cannot supply the missing anchor.

The proposed mass model is still sensible: represent raters by masses \(\mu_A\) over approval sets \(A\), and select each tree \(x\) using the score
\[ J_x(S)=\sum_{A\ni x}\frac{\mu_A}{1+|A\cap S|}. \]
A corresponding Phragmén model, or a comparison of all three rules, could also be formulated. But those would be new computational questions about the rules. The paper’s contribution is to measure how their outputs affect representativeness and forest structure; it does not establish a computational claim whose continuous-population counterpart those questions could preserve. The same objection applies to every proposed mirror of the paper’s empirical findings.

I would not argue that a continuous rater population is inherently implausible, that trees must become divisible, or that an answer matching the discrete one would make the question worthless. The proposed mirror avoids those objections. The negative case is narrower: on the criteria given, it defeats the paper as an anchor for the continuization programme, but it cannot prove that no related continuous voting question would be worth studying.
