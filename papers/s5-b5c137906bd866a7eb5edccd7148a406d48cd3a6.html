The continuous mirror question

Input a finite set of agent types \(T\), a compatibility graph \(H\) on \(T\), a strict preference order \(\prec_t\) over each type’s neighbors, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Choose deletion amounts \(d_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) to minimize \(\sum_{t\in T}d_t\), subject to the induced preference system on \(R_d=\{t:\mu_t-d_t>0\}\) admitting a master list. What is the optimum and an optimal deletion?

The model it lives in

A population distribution over interchangeable preference types on a compatibility graph; deletion variables remove mass by type, the objective is total mass removed, and feasibility requires the residual support to admit a master list.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only MLVD’s Theorem 8 and its type-deletion hardness; it leaves the swap and edge-deletion results and the matching-application results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For arbitrary rational masses, what approximation and parameterized complexity bounds hold in the support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Master-List Vertex Deletion. The anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper by a reduction from Hitting Set: MLVD is NP-hard and W[2]-hard parameterized by \(k\); moreover, unless \(\mathsf{FPT}=\mathsf{W}[1]\), no FPT algorithm parameterized by \(k\) can achieve an \(f(k)\)-approximation for any computable \(f\). These claims hold even on bipartite graphs with strict preferences. [Theorem 8](https://real.mtak.hu/204567/7/Schlotter_Recognizing.pdf)

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of agent types, an undirected compatibility graph \(H\) on \(T\), a strict ranking \(\prec_t\) of each type \(t\)’s neighboring types, and a rational population distribution \(\mu\) with \(\mu_t>0\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A type’s full description includes its role, its neighboring types, and its preferences; agents who differ in any modeled feature belong to different types. Choose deletion amounts \(d_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). The residual system is the graph and preferences induced by \(R_d=\{t:\mu_t-d_t>0\}\). Find \(d\) minimizing \(\sum_t d_t\), subject to that residual system admitting a master list. A solution gives both the deletion amounts and a master order. Equivalently, this minimizes \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\) over type sets \(S\) whose deletion leaves a master-list system.

This describes a large peer-allocation or placement network where a global priority or reputation order is meant to govern local rankings. Many agents share each complete role profile, and each profile ranks the same neighboring profiles in the same order. The population size \(N\) can be much larger than the support size \(\tau=|T|\). Discrete counts \(n_t\) correspond to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). The optimization concerns removing a share of the population to eliminate preference conflicts; it keeps the paper’s master-list condition as the feasibility test.

The connection to MLVD is direct: deleting a type removes its vertex and restricts the remaining preference lists, just as deleting a vertex does in the paper. Partial deletion of a type cannot help feasibility: if any positive mass remains, that type’s preferences and comparisons still constrain the master list. Thus the continuum version is a weighted support-deletion problem. With equal masses \(\mu_t=1/\tau\), a budget of \(k/\tau\) is exactly a limit of \(k\) type deletions. The paper’s Hitting Set construction therefore suggests that hardness transfers: its combinatorics live in the choice of types to delete, not in the number of copies of each type. Replicating each type arbitrarily many times leaves \(\tau\) and the normalized decision unchanged.

I expect this problem to be Class B: hardness transfers. In particular, the equal-mass restriction preserves NP-hardness, and it preserves the parameterized obstruction when the parameter is the number of type classes deleted. Whether nonuniform rational masses yield useful approximation or parameterized algorithms is a further question; the relevant input measures are \(\tau\), the preference-system description, and the mass bit length \(L\).

The weakest point is the type-level interpretation. The paper’s vertices are named agents whose preferences concern named neighbors. This mirror assumes that agents within a cohort can instead be treated as interchangeable, ranking neighboring cohorts; in an individual-level account, that amounts to treating same-type agents as tied within their cohort. That is a plausible high-multiplicity preference system, and the mirror retains the paper’s central deletion-to-master-list question, but an author who regards distinctions among individual identities as essential may reject that regime. I would limit the claim to MLVD; this does not establish mirrors for the paper’s swap or edge-deletion results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor is Theorem 8, the NP-hardness and W[2]-hardness result for MLVD. Its strongest mirror is also the hardest to reject: a symmetric market with large cohorts, class-based compatibility, and shared rankings of neighboring classes is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. The authors could reasonably recognize that model.

The strongest case against it is that the exact mirror is weighted support deletion. For masses \(\mu_t\), choosing deletion amounts \(d_t\) changes feasibility only when a type disappears: while \(0<\mu_t-d_t\), the same preference constraints remain. So an optimum can take \(d_t\) only as \(0\) or \(\mu_t\), and the problem is simply minimum-weight vertex deletion on the type graph. With equal masses, its value is \(\mathrm{distvert}/\tau\). The Hitting Set reduction carries over; the parameter “number of types deleted” does too, though hardness for a mass-budget parameter would need separate analysis.

That makes the population distribution an input of rational weights, but gives fractional deletion no role in feasibility and offers no evident opening for continuous-optimization methods. A formulation where removing only part of a cohort weakens or averages its preference constraints would have to replace the exact master-list condition with a different, approximate one.

This is a limited objection, not a universal defeat. The cohort model is coherent, and the weighted support-deletion question is well-posed. I can argue that it may be a modest weighted reformulation rather than a productive continuization, but I cannot honestly claim this anchor fails in every plausible scenario.
