The continuous mirror question

Given an atomless population with rational role masses \(\alpha_a\le\alpha_b\le\alpha_c\) summing to \(1\), fixed \(K\ge1\), types \(t=(d,j)\) for \(d\in\{a,b,c\}\) and \(j\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\), masses \(\mu_{d,j}=\alpha_d/(K+1)\), ranks \(r_{d,j}=1-\alpha_d/\alpha_c+j\alpha_d/(K\alpha_c)\), and balanced type-level weights, can one construct a measurable partition into coalitions with no positive-mass coalition whose members all strictly prefer it to their assigned coalitions? A type \((d,j)\) member’s utility in coalition \(X\) is \(r_{d,j}-\bar r_d(X)+\sum_{e\ne d}w_e^d\bar r_e(X)\), omitting terms for absent roles, where \(\bar r_e(X)\) is the mass-weighted average rank of role \(e\) in \(X\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population distributed over the finite types \((d,j)\), with type masses, ranks, and balanced weights as specified in the mirror question. A feasible outcome is a measurable partition, representable by coalition masses; stability means that no positive-mass coalition strictly benefits all its members. The task is to construct a stable partition, with the grand coalition as the proposed witness.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s balanced-weight core existence result through the grand coalition. It leaves Theorem 2, Example 1, and the nonexistence result for unbalanced weights in Example 2 unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly applying your anchor rule, this paper has no named computational result: it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic running time. The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here. Under the paper’s three-sided status preferences, balanced weights, and rank setup with \(n_a\le n_b\le n_c\), it says that the set of core-stable coalition structures is nonempty. Its proof establishes the stronger fact that the grand coalition is core stable. That is an existence theorem, not a theorem that a computational problem is in P or has any other complexity.

The best positive case, then, is an existence mirror of Theorem 1—not a claim that the paper supplies a computational anchor. Consider a large organization in which people from three roles form teams and care about their standing within their own role and the average standing of the other roles. Their complete type is their role, rank band, and balanced communication weights. There may be many people but only \(3(K+1)\) types, for fixed \(K\); each type’s mass is its fraction of the workforce. This makes high multiplicity plausible, while preserving the paper’s status-based preferences.

Here is a precise version of the mirror. Let the three role masses be rational numbers \(\alpha_a\le\alpha_b\le\alpha_c\), summing to \(1\). For a fixed integer \(K\ge1\), use types \(t=(d,j)\), where \(d\in\{a,b,c\}\) and \(j\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\). Give type \((d,j)\) mass \(\mu_{d,j}=\alpha_d/(K+1)\) and normalized rank \(r_{d,j}=1-\alpha_d/\alpha_c+j\alpha_d/(K\alpha_c)\). The type-level weights satisfy the paper’s balanced-weight conditions. An outcome is a countable measurable partition of this atomless population into coalitions. For a coalition \(X\), let \(x_{d,j}(X)\) be its mass of type \((d,j)\), and let \(\bar r_d(X)\) be the mass-weighted average rank of its members of role \(d\), when that role is present. A type-\((d,j)\) member’s utility in \(X\) is \(r_{d,j}-\bar r_d(X)+\sum_{e\ne d}w_e^d\bar r_e(X)\), omitting terms for absent roles. The problem, Balanced Status Core Construction\(_\infty\), is: given these masses, ranks, and weights, output a partition with no positive-mass coalition whose members all strictly prefer it to their assigned coalitions. The objective is stability feasibility, as in the paper; there is no welfare objective.

I expect this restricted mirror to be tractable: output the grand coalition. The paper’s proof extends to the evenly spaced rank masses above: each role’s full-population average is the midpoint of its rank interval, and the balanced-weight argument rules out a blocking coalition, including one formed from fractional amounts of types. This mirrors Theorem 1’s existence guarantee as a continuous-population statement. It does not show that discrete hardness dissolves, since the paper supplies no hardness anchor.

The scope is only Theorem 1’s balanced-weight existence result. The paper’s unique ranks are replaced by shared rank bands, which is a real co-modification of the model; the authors may accept it as a status-based mass analogue, but that is the weakest point. The answer is also essentially built into the source proof, so this is a weak case for a computational programme: it gives a clean continuous question and a likely Class A answer, but no new complexity landscape. Natural follow-ups would be whether the result holds for arbitrary rank distributions, and whether core existence can fail in the mass model when weights are unbalanced.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper offers no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves an existence claim and supplies its witness: the grand coalition is core stable. Theorem 2 is another structural stability guarantee. Neither asks for a complexity classification, algorithm, or optimization result. Calling the proposed task Balanced Status Core Construction\(_\infty\) does not change what the paper established.

The proponent’s improved model is plausible: repeated rank bands can make a large population share complete types, and the balanced-weight argument appears to extend to fractional masses. That could support a clean continuous existence theorem. But its natural construction is still the whole population. Turning it into a search problem adds an algorithmic question that the paper did not study; studying verification or changing the preferences to make construction difficult would move further from its results. The paper’s examples of nonexistence likewise do not supply a computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong on fit with ChoCo’s computational aim, but weak as a universal modeling claim: a sensible continuous existence mirror seems available. The case against investing in it is that it would continuize an analytic theorem, not chart the computational landscape the programme is meant to fill.
