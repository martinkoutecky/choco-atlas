The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see an honest computational anchor in this paper. Its named results do not classify a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally tractable. Under your anchor requirement, there are therefore no anchors to mirror.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It characterizes the strategy-proof rules on the mixed single-peaked/single-dipped domain: strategy-proofness is equivalent to group strategy-proofness, and every such rule has a two-step form. Single-peaked agents’ reported peaks determine either one alternative or two adjacent alternatives in the rule’s range; if there are two, single-dipped agents’ preferences help decide between them. Proposition 5, also proved here, gives a range-based characterization of when these rules are Pareto efficient. Both are axiomatic results, not computational ones.

A population version is easy to imagine: types would be complete single-peaked or single-dipped rankings, and mass would be the fraction of society holding each ranking. But asking which rules over those distributions are strategy-proof or Pareto efficient continuizes the paper’s axiomatic questions, which are outside ChoCo’s scope. Turning it into a computational question—such as detecting or minimizing a mass coalition’s profitable misreports—would add a new problem the paper does not solve. It would also need choices the paper leaves open: its coalition rules can depend on named agents, while a mass model needs a principled way to express those rules using type masses.

That is the positive case’s weakest point, and here it is decisive: the paper studies strategic mechanism design, but supplies no named computational result for a continuous-population complexity question to inherit. A continuous extension may be interesting future work, but I would not present one as a mirror anchored in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the decisive point: the paper states no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes strategy-proof rules, and Proposition 5 characterizes when those rules are Pareto efficient. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an optimization problem. That is a strong objection to calling either result a ChoCo computational anchor.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: many residents may share the same single-peaked or single-dipped ranking over facility locations. So I would not reject the mirror on the ground that mass is meaningless. The harder problem is that the paper’s questions concern incentives. For a deterministic rule based only on an atomless population distribution, one person’s report changes no mass and hence no outcome; individual strategy-proofness then imposes no constraint. One can repair this by studying profitable deviations by positive-mass coalitions, but that changes the incentive question and requires a model of measurable coalitions and rules over type masses.

That repair does not rescue Theorem 1 as a computational mirror. The paper permits coalition rules that depend on named agents, while the aggregate model needs a representation in which identities disappear. The authors themselves leave type-anonymity for future work. One could choose a particular encoding of anonymous mass-based rules and ask how to test or design them, but the encoding and computational task would be new choices, not problems supplied by Theorem 1. Within the paper’s characterized class, group strategy-proofness also rules out the natural search for a profitable coalition.

Proposition 5 fares no better as an anchor. It is an axiomatic statement about a rule’s range and its compatibility with efficiency. A computational question about choosing a range or designing a rule subject to efficiency would need an objective, an input representation, and a complexity measure that the paper does not provide. Making the electorate continuous does not supply those ingredients.

So the negative case is strong against presenting this paper as a ChoCo computational mirror: its two named results are axiomatic, and the most natural mass-based reformulation either makes individual strategy-proofness vacuous or changes the problem to coalition incentives. The broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile computational work is less secure. A positive-mass coalition model for facility location could be valuable, but it would be a new project rather than a computational question anchored in this paper.
