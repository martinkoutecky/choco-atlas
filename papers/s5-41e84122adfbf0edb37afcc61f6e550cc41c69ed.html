None gives an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper specifies no computational input, output, or running-time parameters. A continuous-population model could be coherent, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The strongest rescue is Theorem 4. Suppose peaks are types \(t\), with population mass \(\mu_t\), and per-capita endowment is \(\bar\omega\). A continuum version could choose mass \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) to receive their peaks, requiring
\[ \sum_t q_t(t-\bar\omega)=0. \]
But the theorem permits any agreeable selection; it gives no objective or canonical way to choose among them. If the task is merely to find an agreeable selection, \(q_t=0\) always works, and unanimity specifies the grand coalition when it is feasible. Maximizing the mass assigned peaks, or using some other criterion, would add a new design objective. That might support a separate computational paper, but it is not a mirror of Theorem 4. The other candidate results have the same problem: extending their axioms to a continuum would still be an axiomatic question, not a computational one.

This case does not show that population continuization is incoherent. A cohort of agents with shared peak types and a supply scaled per capita is plausible, and the article’s continuous commodity is no reason to reject a continuous population model.
