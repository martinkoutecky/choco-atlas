What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 2 and Theorem 1 from Section 4; it leaves the experiments and axiomatic characterization alone. Both covered results already concern weighted QRJA.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous-population version of \(\ell_{1/2}\) QRJA. Consider a large panel of evaluators assigned pairwise comparisons from a fixed catalogue of candidates. Each evaluator reports that candidate \(a\) is better than candidate \(b\) by \(y\) units, with responses rounded to a standard grid. The population can be much larger than the set of distinct reports: \(M\) evaluators may produce only \(\tau\) distinct judgment types.

A type is the complete report \(t=(a,b,y)\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of evaluators making that report. The decision variable is a rating vector \(x\in\mathbb R^n\), defined up to a common shift. The problem, which I would call Continuous \(\ell_{1/2}\)-QRJA, is: given the candidate set and a finite support of rational masses \((t,\mu_t)\), find \(x\) minimizing
\[ \Phi_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\,|x_a-x_b-y|^{1/2}. \]
This is the same aggregate discrepancy QRJA measures, now over a population of judgment-producing agents. If QRJA’s input has weights \(w_i\), normalize them by \(W=\sum_i w_i\) and combine identical reports: \(\mu_t=\sum_{i:J_i=t}w_i/W\).

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for every fixed \(p<1\), it is NP-hard to approximate \(\ell_p\) QRJA within a multiplicative factor \(1+c/n^2\) for some constant \(c>0\); consequently, unless \(P=NP\), there is no multiplicative FPTAS. The paper proves this by reducing from Max-Cut. For \(p=1/2\), the reduction’s weights are integers, so they can be represented as counts of evaluators. With \(\mu_t\) equal to each type’s count divided by the total count, the continuous objective is the discrete objective scaled by a common constant. The approximation hardness therefore carries over. I expect this mirror to be hardness-transferring (Class B): fractional population masses do not erase a hardness result that already holds on finite populations.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 1, also proved in this paper. For every absolute constant \(p\ge1\), under its polynomially bounded input-number assumption, \(\ell_p\) QRJA can be solved in \(O(m^{1+o(1)})\) time with additive error \(\exp(-\log^c m)\), for any constant \(c>0\). The corresponding mirror, Continuous \(\ell_p\)-QRJA, has the same judgment-type support and mass input, and asks for a rating vector minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_t|x_a-x_b-y|^p\). Under the same numerical assumptions, I expect it to be tractable: merge repeated reports into their total masses and apply the paper’s algorithm to the support. In a bit-complexity account, the input length \(L\) must also be counted. This anchor gives a companion problem for each fixed \(p\ge1\), with the paper’s almost-linear guarantee applying in support size \(\tau\).

The panel scenario is credible for the paper’s social-choice formulation: its inputs are relative quantitative judgments, and it explicitly allows judges to be people. A large, recurring evaluation panel on a fixed set of policy options or products can have many respondents and relatively few distinct pair-and-response types. It is also a computationally relevant high-multiplicity regime: the mass representation records the share of the panel making each report, rather than requiring one record per person. The mirror covers the theoretical QRJA results in Section 4, not the experiments on particular contest datasets or the paper’s axiomatic characterization.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already permits weights on individual judgments, so normalization and aggregation of repeated reports may look like a change in interpretation and input encoding rather than a new mathematical model. Theorem 2’s hardness is also carried by candidate and graph structure; it shows that continuity does not help on these instances, rather than identifying hardness caused by large population size. I still believe the panel interpretation is one the authors would recognize as QRJA, and that the hardness mirror is precise and honest. Whether this counts as a distinct continuous-population question depends on whether the referee sees the population semantics and support-compressed regime as meaningful beyond the paper’s existing weighted input.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a novelty collision: the proposed population model is already the paper’s weighted QRJA problem, written as a distribution. For report types \(t=(a,b,y)\), set \(\mu_t=w_t/\sum_u w_u\). Then the continuous objective is exactly the paper’s objective divided by \(\sum_u w_u\). That normalization changes neither the minimizers nor the multiplicative approximation question. The paper already accepts weighted judgments, so it already represents rational masses over report types; merging duplicate reports is ordinary input compression. The evaluator-panel scenario makes this interpretation plausible, but does not create a new computational object.

That defeats both anchors. For Theorem 2, the Max-Cut reduction’s integer weights can be read as evaluator counts, and normalization preserves its hardness gap. But that shows the existing theorem already applies to this population reading; it does not establish a new continuous result. For Theorem 1, a distribution supported on \(\tau\) reports is just a weighted QRJA instance with \(\tau\) judgments after merging duplicates. Its algorithm is already the result the proposed mirror asks for. Extending the numerical guarantee to masses with unrestricted bit lengths might require more analysis, but that would refine the weighted-input result, not supply a distinct mirror.

A richer panel model does not rescue the case while it remains QRJA. If evaluators submit whole vectors of judgments, QRJA’s additive loss depends only on the marginal frequencies of each report, so correlations between an evaluator’s reports disappear. Making those correlations, identities, or histories matter could yield a new problem, but it would change the objective and leave these theorems behind. The negative case is therefore strongest as a claim of redundancy: this paper already does the computation on weighted report types. Its weak point is that the authors present weights as judgment weights rather than explicitly as population shares; a reader who treats that semantic distinction as enough for ChoCo may still find the panel interpretation worthwhile.
