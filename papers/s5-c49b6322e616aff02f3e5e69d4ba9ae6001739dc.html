The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(A\), \(k\), \(\gamma=(1-p)/p\in(0,1)\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types \(T=L(A)\), and an evidence scale \(\lambda>0\), output a \(k\)-subset \(S\subseteq A\) maximizing \(\sum_{\sigma:\sigma^{-1}(1)\in S} \frac{\gamma^{\lambda\sum_{\rho\in T}\mu_\rho d_K(\rho,\sigma)}}{\sum_{\pi\in T}\gamma^{\lambda\sum_{\rho\in T}\mu_\rho d_K(\rho,\pi)}}\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity noisy-orders society with ranking types \(T=L(A)\), mass \(\mu_\rho\), and evidence scale \(\lambda\); the decision variable is a discrete \(k\)-subset \(S\), and the objective is the posterior probability that the hidden top alternative lies in \(S\).

The objection that survived

The mirror needs \(\lambda\) in addition to \(\mu\), and as \(\lambda\to\infty\) the posterior concentrates on minimizers of \(D_\mu\), raising a legitimate concern that it models weighted finite-data likelihood rather than an intrinsic continuum population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

It directly covers Objective 1, including Theorems 3.2 and 3.3 and the high-noise scoring boundary; it does not directly mirror Objectives 2 and 3 or the empirical comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-hardness persist when \(\mu\) has fixed or very small support while \(\lambda\) grows?
  • What exact or approximate complexity results hold when \(m\) or \(k\) is fixed, and how should \(\lambda\) be encoded?
  • Can the high-noise score characterization yield approximation schemes or identify the boundary between posterior selection and population-level ranking?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s Objective 1 under the noisy-orders model. I would call it Continuous Top-Set MLE\(_\infty\), and I would make it the lead case.

Let \(A\) be the \(m\) alternatives and let the type space be \(T=L(A)\), the set of complete rankings. A continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings: \(\mu_\rho\) is the fraction of respondents submitting ranking \(\rho\). The distribution is given sparsely by its nonzero types, which is the natural representation when a very large population has relatively few ranking templates.

Retain the paper’s hidden true ranking \(\sigma^\ast\), uniform over \(L(A)\), and its noisy-orders likelihood. Write \(\gamma=(1-p)/p\), where \(p>1/2\), and define the average Kendall distance

\[ D_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{\rho\in L(A)}\mu_\rho d_K(\rho,\sigma). \]

The likelihood of a population with mass profile \(\mu\) is

\[ w_\mu(\sigma)=\gamma^{\lambda D_\mu(\sigma)}, \]

where \(\lambda\) is the population or evidence scale. For a discrete profile with \(n_\rho\) voters of type \(\rho\), set \(\lambda=n\) and \(\mu_\rho=n_\rho/n\); then \(\lambda D_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_\rho n_\rho d_K(\rho,\sigma)\), exactly reproducing the paper’s likelihood up to the normalization constant, which is independent of \(\sigma\).

Thus define

\[ P_\mu(\sigma^\ast=\sigma) = \frac{\gamma^{\lambda D_\mu(\sigma)}} {\sum_{\pi\in L(A)}\gamma^{\lambda D_\mu(\pi)}}. \]

The problem is:

\[ \text{given }(A,k,\gamma,\lambda,\mu),\text{ output a }k\text{-subset }S\subseteq A \]

maximizing

\[ \Pr_\mu[\sigma^\ast{}^{-1}(1)\in S] = \sum_{\sigma:\,\sigma^{-1}(1)\in S}P_\mu(\sigma^\ast=\sigma). \]

Ties may be resolved arbitrarily. This is precisely the paper’s question about selecting a set most likely to contain the strongest alternative; only the population representation has changed from an explicit voter list to a distribution over voter types. The hidden ranking and the selected set remain discrete, so this is genuinely population continuization rather than continuization of the outcome space.

The natural regime is a large product-development or crowdsourcing survey. Millions of respondents rank the same fixed menu of designs, but respondents are drawn from a modest number of taste segments, institutional groups, or ranking templates. If the platform elicits complete rankings, two respondents with the same ranking are indistinguishable for this problem and therefore legitimately form one type. The interesting regime is \(\lambda\) very large while \(|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\) is much smaller. This is exactly the high-multiplicity interpretation: the meaningful quantity is the fraction of the population reporting each ranking, not the identity of every respondent.

The anchor is the paper’s Theorem 3.2, proved as a result of this paper, although the proof is deferred to its full version. It states:

For every \(k\in[m-1]\), computing an optimal solution to Objective 1 is NP-hard under both noisy orders and noisy comparisons.

Objective 1, restated fully, is: given noisy observations generated from a uniformly random hidden ranking, compute a \(k\)-subset \(S\) maximizing the posterior probability that the hidden ranking’s top alternative belongs to \(S\). The theorem is not merely a citation: the paper derives it using the NP-hardness of computing the relevant maximum-likelihood ranking, namely Kemeny ranking under noisy orders and minimum-feedback ranking under noisy comparisons.

My expectation for Continuous Top-Set MLE\(_\infty\) is therefore Class B: hardness transfers. Every finite discrete instance embeds exactly by setting \(\mu_\rho=n_\rho/n\) and \(\lambda=n\). A solver for the continuous problem would solve the paper’s discrete problem with the same posterior objective and the same optimal \(k\)-subset. More importantly, the hardness is driven by the number and arrangement of alternatives—the feedback-ranking/Kemeny combinatorics—not by the individuality of voters. That is precisely the kind of hardness the continuum should not dissolve.

The paper’s positive high-noise analysis supports, rather than undermines, this mirror. For example, Theorem 3.3 shows that in sufficiently noisy instances optimal Objective 1 solutions lie among the alternatives with largest extended scores. In the continuous model that score is simply

\[ \operatorname{sc}_\mu(a) = \sum_{\rho}\mu_\rho \sum_{b\neq a} \mathbf 1[\rho(a)<\rho(b)], \]

which is the population-average Borda score under noisy orders. So the same mirror naturally exposes a tractable high-noise boundary while preserving the exact hard problem outside that regime.

The main further questions are whether the hardness persists under the stronger promise \(|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\ll\lambda\), whether fixed \(k\) or fixed \(m\) yields fixed-parameter or polynomial algorithms, and whether posterior probabilities admit useful additive approximations even when exact top-set selection is hard. One should also compare sparse-support and dense-coordinate encodings of \(\mu\), since \(L(A)\) has size \(m!\).

The weakest point is the evidence-scale parameter \(\lambda\). It is needed because the paper’s posterior depends on the number of observations, not only on normalized frequencies; simply replacing the voter list by \(\mu\) and discarding \(n\) would change the problem. I would defend \(\lambda\) as the continuous analogue of sample intensity, not as an agent-specific attribute, but a referee could reasonably ask whether the programme wants a mirror consisting only of \(\mu\). Even with that qualification, the mirror is exact on rational high-multiplicity instances, preserves the authors’ likelihood and objective, and gives a precise computational question whose expected answer is that the paper’s hardness survives continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror does not really continuize the paper’s population; it continuizes a finite statistical dataset.

In the paper, \(n\) is the number of independent observations of a hidden ranking. If \(n_\rho\) voters submit ranking \(\rho\), then the posterior is determined by

\[ \gamma^{\sum_\rho n_\rho d_K(\rho,\sigma)}. \]

Replacing \(n_\rho\) by \(\lambda\mu_\rho\) gives exactly the proponent’s formula. But \(\lambda\) is indispensable: \(\mu\) alone contains only frequencies, whereas the posterior depends on the amount of evidence. This creates a basic dilemma.

If \(\lambda\) is discarded, the result is no longer the paper’s maximum-likelihood problem; it is an arbitrary posterior over a fractional dataset of unit mass. If \(\lambda\) is interpreted as the size of the continuous population, then the genuine continuum limit has \(\lambda\to\infty\), and the posterior concentrates on the minimizers of

\[ D_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_\rho \mu_\rho d_K(\rho,\sigma). \]

The finite-sample uncertainty that makes Objective 1 interesting disappears. What remains is essentially identification of a population-level maximum-likelihood ranking, not selection under posterior uncertainty. A finite \(\lambda\) therefore describes a weighted observation measure, not a continuum of voters.

There is a second concern. The distribution \(\mu\) is computationally passive. The objective depends on it only through the weighted pairwise counts

\[ N_{ab}=\lambda\sum_\rho \mu_\rho \mathbf 1[\rho(a)<\rho(b)]. \]

Thus the proposed mirror is equivalent to allowing the paper’s weighted tournament to have real rather than integer edge weights. No mass is transferred, allocated, constrained, or optimized; the population does not become the object of computation. The continuum is merely a reparameterization of the input data, and all of the combinatorics remain in the hidden permutation space. That makes the mirror less informative for ChoCo than a problem in which population mass is itself manipulated or enters a genuine continuous optimization model.

This is the best objection to Theorem 3.2, but it does not defeat the strengthened mirror. The programme explicitly accepts high-multiplicity societies represented by rational proportions, and it permits an accompanying scale parameter when the model genuinely depends on population size. A survey of millions of respondents drawn from a small number of stable ranking templates is a perfectly plausible high-multiplicity regime. The ranking is a complete type for this problem, and repeated rankings are indistinguishable in every relevant respect. Replicating every observation arbitrarily many times makes \(\lambda\) as large as desired without changing the normalized society.

More importantly, Theorem 3.2 is a named computational result of the paper. Setting \(\mu_\rho=n_\rho/n\) and \(\lambda=n\) reproduces every discrete instance exactly, including its posterior and optimal \(k\)-subset. The hardness is driven by the alternative set and the Kemeny/minimum-feedback-ranking structure, not by voter identity. That is precisely a legitimate Class B outcome under the programme. Calling the resulting question “only a reparameterization” would incorrectly treat hardness transfer as a defect, when the programme explicitly regards it as a worthwhile conclusion.

The paper’s high-noise theorem also survives naturally: the extended score becomes the population-average Borda score,

\[ \operatorname{sc}_\mu(a) = \sum_\rho \mu_\rho \sum_{b\ne a} \mathbf 1[\rho(a)<\rho(b)]. \]

So the continuous formulation has both a meaningful high-multiplicity interpretation and an exact computational anchor.

Accordingly, the honest negative case is weak. One can argue that this is a statistically flavoured, coefficient-level lift rather than a rich new continuous optimization problem, and that its continuum limit degenerates as evidence grows. But neither point defeats the best mirror, and neither is enough to deny it a place in the programme. The proponent’s anchor survives.
