What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation targets Theorem 1.2's multi-cake disjoint-selection guarantee; it leaves the other theorems and Corollary 1.5 without computational continuations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 1.2, proved in this paper: with \(k\) cakes cut into \(n\) pieces each and \(p\le k(n-1)+1\) hungry players, each having a nonempty closed set of acceptable one-piece-per-cake selections at every division, some division lets at least \(\lceil p/[2k(k-1)]\rceil\) players receive mutually disjoint preferred selections. If \(p\) divides \(k(n-1)+1\), the bound improves to \(\lceil p/[k(k-1)]\rceil\).

That is a plausible *population* to continuize. Types could be complete preference correspondences \(A_t(x)\) over divisions \(x\in(\Delta_{n-1})^k\), with society masses \(\mu_t\). For a population scale \(P\), a candidate problem—call it High-Multiplicity Multi-Cake Selection—would choose a division \(x\) and masses \(y_{t,I}\) assigned to acceptable selections \(I\in A_t(x)\), maximizing \(\sum_{t,I}y_{t,I}\), subject to \(\sum_I y_{t,I}\le P\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,I:I_j=i}y_{t,I}\le1\) for each piece \(i\) of each cake \(j\). A solution is an optimal division and feasible mass assignment. The regime would have \(\tau\ll P\), with the number of pieces scaling with the population, as the theorem’s condition already requires. This is recognizably a high-multiplicity version: agents of a type share their complete preferences, while mass can be split among selections.

But this is not an eligible computational anchor under your criteria. Theorem 1.2 proves an existence bound; it gives no algorithm, running time, or complexity classification. Nor does the paper specify a finite encoding for its arbitrary preference correspondences, which a computational version would need. The analogous question of whether the optimum reaches \(P/[2k(k-1)]\) is a reasonable conjectural existence mirror, but the paper supplies no basis for predicting the optimization problem’s complexity class. So the paper has a promising high-multiplicity *mathematical* analogue, but no named computational result to support a compliant computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.2 is an existence theorem, not a computational result: it gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or finite input model. The proposed “High-Multiplicity Multi-Cake Selection” problem adds an optimization objective the paper never studies. Its key input, \(A_t(x)\), is an arbitrary closed preference correspondence. The paper gives no finite representation or way to test which selections are acceptable at a division \(x\). The displayed packing LP only describes assignments once \(x\) and the acceptable selections are known; it does not make the joint search over divisions computable. Closedness supports the topological existence proof, but supplies no algorithmic structure.

A more careful mirror could encode preferences with rational piecewise-linear utility functions and use a finite list of complete preference types. That would be a coherent computational problem, but its complexity would depend on the added utility representation and on how preferences change with the cuts. The theorem’s guarantee applies to arbitrary closed preferences and says nothing about those features. Such a study might be worthwhile on its own; it would not be a computational continuation of a result in this paper.

The shift-cover theorem offers a similar temptation: with a fixed partition and explicitly listed acceptable shift selections, fractional coverage can be written as an LP. But the paper again proves existence through topological assumptions, without specifying how to compute acceptable selections as the partition varies. The computational substance would come from a new encoding of those preferences.

So this anchor does not support a green computational mirror as stated. The negative case is strongest on that point: the paper contains no named computational result to continue, and its broad preference model is not algorithmically specified. It is weaker as a universal claim. A finite, plausible preference model could yield a useful high-multiplicity optimization problem that the authors might recognize as theirs; the paper simply gives no reason to expect its complexity story to carry over.
