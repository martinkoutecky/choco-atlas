The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no anchor, so there is no continuous question to defeat one by one. That is the strongest point against a mirror of this paper: it reports an algorithm and empirical accuracy–cost tradeoffs, but proves no named computational result. Turning “maximize accuracy” or “minimize queries at a target accuracy” into a formal optimization problem would be useful new work, but it would supply a problem the paper never states or classifies.

The strongest plausible mirror would put a distribution over interchangeable experts, grouped by their reliability, availability, and query cost, then ask how to allocate a large pool’s effort across incoming cases. That is coherent in some crowdsourcing settings, and grouping identical experts is not itself a distortion. But it moves away from this paper’s medical setting, whose small, heterogeneous coalition is central to its motivation. Keeping each expert’s distinct trust and availability makes the types distinct; grouping them removes the individual learning that drives the paper’s bandit ranking and cold start. The paper itself notes that large homogeneous medical groups are rare.

Alternatively, put a distribution over cases and optimize average accuracy and query cost. Cases, though, are the objects being labeled, not the population of decision-making agents. And to preserve the paper’s online behavior, the model must retain how each arriving case updates expert trusts and changes later query choices. A type-level average would discard that evolving history; retaining it leads toward a new stochastic-control problem whose objective, assumptions, and policy class the paper does not specify.

So I would reject a continuous mirror *of the paper’s results*: there is no computational result to mirror, and the most plausible continuizations change the problem substantially. The universal claim that no scenario could make a worthwhile mirror is weaker. A carefully formulated high-multiplicity model of crowdsourced experts might be worthwhile, but it would be an extension of the research question, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s stated results.
