The closest named results are Proposition 4.1, proved here, which characterizes measures satisfying four axioms as \(\mu(N,W)=a\sum_{i\in N}DP_i(N,W)^2\), and Proposition 5.1, also proved here, which expresses the sum of squared Shapley–Shubik indices as an alternating overlap formula over subfamilies of minimal winning coalitions. Both are axiomatic or algebraic results, not complexity results. Section 6 computes values for UNSC variants, but does not state a named computational result.

The most plausible regime would be a large organization with many interchangeable voting members in a small number of institutional roles. One could let a type be a role, its mass be that role’s population share, and ask for the limiting concentration of power. The replication axiom suggests normalizing by population size: replicating each member \(t\) times divides the paper’s measure by \(t\). But this would require defining continuous minimal winning coalitions and a corresponding power measure; the paper supplies neither. In a nonatomic population, even the notion of an inclusion-minimal winning coalition needs additional choices.

That could motivate a follow-up model, but it would be a new continuous measure and computational question, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a qualifying mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. The other analyst correctly identifies Proposition 4.1 and Proposition 5.1, but both are algebraic or axiomatic results. Proposition 4.1 characterizes a measure on finite monotonic voting games; Proposition 5.1 rewrites another measure using unions of minimal winning coalitions. Neither asks for an algorithm or classifies computational complexity. Continuizing either result would remain an axiomatic or analytic project, outside ChoCo’s stated scope.

The most promising population setting is a council with many interchangeable members in a few roles. The paper’s replication axiom makes that setting plausible. Its two-role formula even gives a natural limit: if \(n_1/n\to p\) and \(k_\ell/n_\ell\to q_\ell\), then
\[ n\,\mu(n_1,k_1;n_2,k_2)\longrightarrow \frac{p q_1^2+(1-p)q_2^2}{\bigl(pq_1+(1-p)q_2\bigr)^2}. \]
That could support an asymptotic study of concentration across quota rules. It still supplies no computational question from this paper. The raw measure tends to zero under replication; retaining a nonzero limit requires the additional normalization by \(n\). Extending beyond the two-role quota family also requires defining how to measure overlap among a continuum of minimal winning coalitions. The paper gives no such definition, and its finite average over those coalitions has no canonical continuous counterpart.

A new computational problem could certainly be built around mass-based voting rules. But it would need new primitives and a new computational target; it would not be a continuous mirror of either proposition. That is a strong reason to reject this paper as a qualifying ChoCo mirror. It does not prove that no worthwhile project could grow from the paper’s ideas.
