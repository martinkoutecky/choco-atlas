The continuous mirror question

Given \(r\) regions, a finite support \(T\) of incumbent types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s home region \(k(t)\) and nonnegative benefits \(b_t^k\) (zero outside its home region), and entrant benefits \(\beta^k\) normalized per incumbent, compute the limiting EOL allocation. For each region set \(G_k=\beta^k+\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t b_t^k\); let \(k^\ast\in\operatorname{argmax}_k G_k\), \(G=\max_k G_k\), \(S=\max_{k\ne k^\ast}G_k\), \(I=\max(\beta^{k^\ast},S)\), and \(q=\sum_{t:k(t)=k^\ast}\mu_t\). If the optimum is unique and \(q>0\), return entrant allocation \(I\), per-incumbent allocation \(y_t=(G-I)/q\) for types in \(k^\ast\), and \(y_t=0\) otherwise. If the optimum is tied or \(q=0\), return entrant allocation \(G\) and \(y_t=0\) for all types. The output satisfies \(I+\sum_t\mu_t y_t=G\) in the unique, positive-mass case.

The model it lives in

Types are incumbent firms with the same home region and benefit vector, carrying mass \(\mu_t\); the entrant remains distinguished, with benefit \(\beta^k\) on a per-incumbent scale. Select a region maximizing \(G_k\) and compute the limiting EOL allocation by the stated formula.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s EOL formula and its polynomial-time computation consequence in a high-multiplicity regime. It leaves a formal continuum-player nucleolus, WOL, and the paper’s axiomatic characterizations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what coalition model, if any, does a continuum-player nucleolus coincide with this limiting EOL allocation?
  • How does the limit behave when the entrant’s benefit remains \(O(1)\) as the incumbent population grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is the large industrial cluster version of the paper’s EOL allocation. Imagine a country with many incumbent firms but only a small number of distinct profiles: a type records a firm’s region and its benefit vector across possible plant locations. Firms of one type are identical in every respect used by the model. The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a distinguished entrant. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of incumbent firms of type \(t\). This fits a setting where many firms share a limited set of sector, size, and regional-spillover profiles, so \(N\), the number of firms, is large relative to \(\tau\).

My single anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that for every agglomeration problem \(A\), the nucleolus of its associated game is the egalitarian optimal location rule, EOL: if \(k^\ast\) is an optimal region, \(g(A)\) is the best region’s total benefit, and \(I_0(A)\) is the entrant’s outside-option benchmark, then the entrant receives
\[ I_0(A)+\frac{g(A)-I_0(A)}{|P_{k^\ast}|+1}, \]
each firm in \(P_{k^\ast}\) receives
\[ \frac{g(A)-I_0(A)}{|P_{k^\ast}|+1}, \]
and firms outside that region receive zero. The paper explicitly notes after the theorem that this formula makes the nucleolus polynomial-time computable in these games. Strictly, the printed theorem states the identity with EOL rather than a complexity class; its explicit formula and the paper’s stated consequence make it the right algorithmic anchor. The paper offers no comparable numbered hardness result.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous EOL Computation for Agglomeration. An instance has \(r\ge 2\) regions; a finite support of rational types \(t\), each with a home region \(k(t)\) and nonnegative benefits \(b_t^k\), where \(b_t^k=0\) outside its home region; the rational masses \(\mu_t\); and the entrant’s benefits \(\beta^k\). Benefits are measured on a population-normalized scale: for a finite population of \(N\) incumbents, \(\beta^k\) represents the entrant’s benefit divided by \(N\). For each region set
\[ G_k=\beta^k+\sum_t\mu_t b_t^k. \]
The task is to return an optimal region, the normalized best and second-best aggregate benefits, and the limiting EOL allocation. Write \(G=\max_kG_k\), choose an optimal \(k^\ast\), let \(S=\max_{k\ne k^\ast}G_k\), \(I=\max(\beta^{k^\ast},S)\), and \(q=\sum_{t:k(t)=k^\ast}\mu_t\). When the optimum is unique and \(q>0\), the entrant receives \(I\), each incumbent of a type in \(k^\ast\) receives per-firm allocation \((G-I)/q\), and every other type receives zero. If the optimum is tied, or \(q=0\), the limiting allocation gives the entrant \(G\) and everyone else zero. The allocation balances: \(I+\sum_t\mu_t y_t=G\).

This is the high-multiplicity limit of Theorem 2’s allocation: with \(Nq\) firms in the winning region, the local firms’ per-firm EOL share approaches \((G-I)/q\), while the entrant’s share divided by \(N\) approaches \(I\). The model still asks the paper’s question—where should the entrant locate, and how should the resulting benefit be divided?—with the entrant distinguished and the incumbent population continuous. It does not make outcomes, time, or uncertainty the continuous object.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: aggregate the masses by region, compare the \(r\) values \(G_k\), and calculate the allocation. It takes \(O(r\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations, polynomial in \(r,\tau\), and the input bit length. Further questions include whether a formally defined continuum-player nucleolus agrees with this limiting allocation when coalitions can contain fractional type mass, and how the allocation behaves when the entrant’s benefit does not scale with population.

The soft spot is that this is a limit of the paper’s explicit solution, not a new continuous nucleolus theorem. The entrant’s single-person equal share vanishes on the population-normalized scale, leaving it its outside-option benchmark while incumbent mass divides the remaining surplus. Authors might view that as the right large-population limit; they might instead regard it as changing the bargaining weight of the entrant. Also, this anchor is algorithmic rather than hardness-based, and the paper’s discrete problem is already easy by its own account. So the mirror is plausible and precise, but it offers less of the programme’s central prize—showing whether discrete hardness dissolves in a continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only plausible anchor, and it is a thinner computational result than the proponent’s framing suggests. It proves that the finite game’s nucleolus equals EOL; the paper then observes that the formula makes the nucleolus polynomial-time computable. There is no separate complexity theorem, input-size analysis, or algorithmic bottleneck. That leaves a real result to mirror, but not the kind of complexity landscape the programme is set up to chart.

The proponent’s large-cluster scenario is plausible: firms can share a region and benefit profile, and mass is a sensible way to describe them. The stronger version of the mirror would keep the entrant as a distinguished player and assign incumbent types a mass. But that gives a population limit of the EOL allocation, not automatically a nucleolus for a continuum game. The paper’s nucleolus is defined by lexicographically comparing excesses over the coalitions of a finite player set. With fractional coalitions, that definition needs new choices. In particular, the game grants regional benefits only when a coalition contains the whole region; for nonatomic incumbents, individual agents cannot block, while allowing fractional regional coalitions changes the coalition values. The paper’s theorem does not settle which continuum game or solution concept should replace it.

One can avoid that problem by asking only for the limiting EOL allocation, as the proponent does. That is a coherent extension of the rule, and the entrant can remain distinct. Yet it reduces to summing benefits by region, comparing the resulting totals, and distributing the surplus by mass. This is a mass-allocation question, not a new computational question about the nucleolus. Asking for the continuum solution concept instead would raise a potentially interesting cooperative-game question, but it would turn the task into defining and analyzing continuum coalitions, outside this programme’s computational focus.

So the anchor is not defeated by saying that firms lack multiplicity, or that the entrant must disappear: the cluster regime and a distinguished entrant are both defensible. The negative case rests on the narrower point that the best population mirror either extends a closed-form allocation rule or asks for a new continuum-game definition. The former offers little complexity work; the latter changes the mathematical problem. That is a credible reason to deprioritize this paper, though not an airtight case that no useful mirror could be formulated.
