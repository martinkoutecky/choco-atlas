The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper has a natural continuous-population analogue, but it has no eligible computational anchor. The full text contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting an algorithmic or complexity result. Its results are empirical: \(23.3\%\) of participants submitted a suboptimal rank-order list, and those participants earned \(\$18.20\) on average versus \(\$22.80\) for truthful reporters. Those findings motivate a mirror, but they cannot serve as the required named anchors.

The best mirror is a continuous deferred-acceptance model of misrepresentation and welfare loss.

Consider a large residency or school-matching market with a finite set \(P\) of programs. A student type is a complete tuple

\[ t=(\succ_t,h_t,z_t), \]

where \(\succ_t\) is the student’s true ranking of programs, \(h_t\) is the priority or HST-score class used by programs, and \(z_t\) records any behavioural characteristics included in the model, such as advice, trust, or overconfidence. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types. A program \(j\) has per-capita capacity \(q_j\); this is the necessary scaling of the paper’s finite capacities. A rank-order list \(r\) is the student’s action.

Let \(x_{t,r}\) be the mass of type \(t\) submitting list \(r\), with

\[ \sum_r x_{t,r}=\mu_t. \]

Run student-proposing deferred acceptance on these report masses: each program accepts the highest-priority mass up to \(q_j\), and rejected mass proceeds to its next reported program. The continuous outcome is the mass \(y_{t,j}\) assigned to each program.

The natural objective is the aggregate analogue of the paper’s dependent variables:

\[ M(x)=\sum_t\sum_{r\neq \succ_t}x_{t,r}, \]

the mass that misrepresents preferences, and

\[ W(x)=\sum_{t,j}y_{t,j}u_t(j), \]

the aggregate student welfare. The welfare cost of misrepresentation is

\[ L(x)=W(x^{\mathrm{truth}})-W(x). \]

A precise problem is therefore:

Continuum Deferred-Acceptance Misreporting-Loss. Given \(P\), capacities \(q\), finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu\), true rankings \(\succ_t\), priorities \(h_t\), utilities \(u_t\), and a feasible mass report plan \(x\), compute the deferred-acceptance allocation, the misrepresentation mass \(M(x)\), and the welfare loss \(L(x)\).

A more intervention-oriented version would let an organizer choose how much mass of each type receives credible advice, subject to a budget, with the advice changing the type’s report distribution. The objective would be to minimize \(L(x)\) or \(M(x)\). That directly reflects the paper’s discussion of training and advice, while keeping the population—not the allocation itself—as the continuous object.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a recurring national or specialty-specific matching market with thousands of applicants but relatively few profiles: applicants sharing a specialty, geography, preference ordering, priority-score band, and advice or trust profile. For example, \(N\) might be in the tens of thousands while the number of complete types \(\tau\) is in the hundreds or low thousands. The original experiment’s five programs and integer HST scores already provide a finite type vocabulary; the continuous model replaces repeated named applicants by their masses.

The authors would likely recognize this as their problem’s analogue because it retains the applicant-proposing deferred-acceptance mechanism, truthful versus strategic rank lists, heterogeneous student priorities, program capacities, and the measured welfare consequences of misreporting. It is not merely fractional matching: the population and the matching process are both represented at mass level.

With fixed \(m\), finite rankings, and an exogenous report plan, I would expect the problem to be Class A: continuum deferred acceptance can likely be computed by mass-threshold rounds, and the welfare statistics then follow by linear aggregation. If reporting behaviour or advice allocation is optimized, the interesting question becomes whether the resulting LP or flow formulation remains tractable when the report space grows with \(m\).

The weakest point is decisive: the paper studies observed human behaviour, not a computational problem. The report kernel \(x\) is not supplied by the paper and cannot simply be inferred from the \(23.3\%\) statistic. Making it endogenous would require a behavioural or equilibrium model that the authors did not formulate. Thus this is a credible continuous mirror of the paper’s subject, but not a mirror anchored in one of its named computational results; under the programme’s strict grading rule, that limits the strength of the positive case substantially.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong at the eligibility stage: this paper contains no named computational result. It proves no complexity classification, algorithmic theorem, approximation guarantee, or computational impossibility result. Its findings are empirical estimates—most notably that \(23.3\%\) of participants submitted a suboptimal list—not results about solving a computational problem. That is decisive under the programme’s anchor rule.

The proposed continuous deferred-acceptance model is mathematically coherent, but it reverses the paper’s input and output. Given type masses \(\mu\) and a report plan \(x_{t,r}\), one can run mass deferred acceptance and calculate

\[ M(x)=\sum_{t,r\ne\succ_t}x_{t,r} \]

and aggregate welfare \(W(x)\). But \(x\) is precisely the behaviour the paper tries to explain. The paper supplies no behavioural law from \(\mu\), advice, trust, ability, or expectations to \(x\). Thus the proposed computation takes the phenomenon as an input and aggregates it; it does not mirror the paper’s research question.

The best rescue is to make reporting endogenous. Yet each version imports a new model. Under ordinary deferred-acceptance preferences, truthful reporting is optimal, so rational strategic behaviour eliminates the phenomenon. To preserve misrepresentation one must add a bounded-rationality, stochastic-choice, or cognitive-error technology. To study advice, one must additionally specify advice costs and causal effects on reporting. To optimize interventions, one must choose an objective and budget. None of these objects appears in the paper; each would be a new behavioural mechanism-design project.

The high-multiplicity setting itself is not the problem. Residency markets plausibly contain many applicants sharing rankings, priority classes, and observable behavioural categories, and per-capita capacities are a legitimate scaling. What fails is the connection to the paper’s contribution: a continuous population can preserve frequencies of mistakes, but only after the missing report distribution has been supplied externally.

So the honest verdict is not that a continuum model cannot be written. It can. The problem is that the proposed model is either a routine aggregation of observed behaviour or a new endogenous behavioural model with assumptions absent from the paper. Since there is no named computational result to continuize, no worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper survives the programme’s gate.
