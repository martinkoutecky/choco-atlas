The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of contact classes \(J\), rational class masses \(\nu_j\), a row-stochastic contact matrix \(K\), initial red, blue, and agnostic masses \(R_j,B_j,A_j\) summing to \(\nu_j\), a horizon \(\tau\), and a seed budget \(\beta\), choose \(0\le x_j^B\le B_j\) and \(0\le x_j^A\le A_j\) with \(\sum_j(x_j^B+x_j^A)\le\beta\). Set \(R_j^0=R_j+x_j^B+x_j^A\) and \(A_j^0=A_j-x_j^A\). At each round define \(r_j^t=R_j^t/\nu_j\), \(a_j^t=A_j^t/\nu_j\), \(q_j^{R,t}=\sum_\ell K_{j\ell}r_\ell^t\), and \(q_j^{A,t}=\sum_\ell K_{j\ell}a_\ell^t\), then update \(R_j^{t+1}=\nu_j(q_j^{R,t}+r_j^tq_j^{A,t})\) and \(A_j^{t+1}=A_j^tq_j^{A,t}\). What is \(\max_x\sum_jR_j^\tau\), and what approximation guarantees can be achieved in time polynomial in \(|J|\), \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\)?

The model it lives in

A finite-horizon campaign problem over a distribution of contact classes: each class has a mass and contact probabilities, and the decision is how much blue and agnostic mass to seed within a fractional budget. The objective is the total red mass after \(\tau\) rounds under the stated limiting dynamics.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8's finite-horizon adoption-maximization result for exchangeable block networks. It leaves the paper's consensus-probability results, convergence-time bounds, and arbitrary graph instances outside the proposed mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the fractional-seeding objective retain the diminishing-returns structure needed for a \(1-1/e-\epsilon\) approximation?
  • What approximation guarantees and running times are possible in terms of \(|J|\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 8, the paper’s result for adoption maximization. In the paper’s Definition 2, a seed set \(A\) of at most \(k\) vertices is turned red, and the objective is to maximize the expected number of red vertices after \(\tau\) rounds. Theorem 8, proved in this paper, says its Monte Carlo greedy algorithm achieves a \(1-1/e-\epsilon\) approximation with high probability in time \(O(9k^2\omega(n)n\tau/(\epsilon^2p))\), polynomial when \(1/p\) is polynomial; here \(p\) is the minimum positive transition probability, as the proof uses it. The printed statement appears to invert \(p\). The paper reports a Maximum Coverage hardness result from [22] in prose, but has no numbered in-paper hardness theorem to use as an anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large online community in which users fall into a few contact-profile classes: members of a class have the same probabilities of contacting each other class, the same campaign cost, and the same initial state. Millions of users might then be represented by dozens of classes. The mass of a class is its fraction of the population, and a campaign chooses how much mass to seed. This preserves the paper’s finite-horizon adoption objective; it does not try to preserve the random fluctuations that govern eventual consensus.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Let \(J\) be the finite set of contact classes, with rational masses \(\nu_j>0\) summing to \(1\). Let \(K_{j\ell}\) be the probability a user in class \(j\) samples a user uniformly from class \(\ell\). The input also gives rational initial red, blue, and agnostic masses \(R_j,B_j,A_j\), summing to \(\nu_j\), a horizon \(\tau\), and a seeding budget \(\beta\), measured as a fraction of the whole society.

Choose nonnegative masses \(x_j^B\le B_j\) and \(x_j^A\le A_j\) to seed from each class’s blue and agnostic populations, with \(\sum_j(x_j^B+x_j^A)\le\beta\). Initially set \(R_j^0=R_j+x_j^B+x_j^A\) and \(A_j^0=A_j-x_j^A\). At each round let \(r_j^t=R_j^t/\nu_j\), \(a_j^t=A_j^t/\nu_j\), \(q_j^R=\sum_\ell K_{j\ell}r_\ell^t\), and \(q_j^A=\sum_\ell K_{j\ell}a_\ell^t\). The limiting mass dynamics are

\(\displaystyle R_j^{t+1}=\nu_j(q_j^R+r_j^tq_j^A),\qquad A_j^{t+1}=A_j^tq_j^A.\)

The first equation counts users who sample a red user, plus red users who sample an agnostic one and therefore keep their color. The objective is to maximize \(F_\tau(x)=\sum_jR_j^\tau\). A solution is a feasible seed-mass allocation; for the approximation version, require \(F_\tau(x)\ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\operatorname{OPT}\).

This is a population limit of the paper’s model, not just a change of units: make \(N\nu_j\) exchangeable users in each class, with each user sampling according to \(K\), and the normalized expected red mass after fixed \(\tau\) converges to the stated recurrence as \(N\) grows. The problem asks the authors’ recognizable campaign question—where to spend a seeding budget to maximize adoption after a given number of rounds—while replacing individual vertices with mass. I expect a Class A approximation result in the support size \(|J|\), \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\), if the limiting objective retains the diminishing-returns structure behind the paper’s greedy guarantee. Evaluating the recurrence for a proposed allocation is already efficient; proving that optimization guarantee is the central open step.

The scope is just Theorem 8’s finite-horizon adoption-maximization result. I would not use Theorem 7’s consensus-probability FPRAS as the lead: eventual fixation is much more sensitive to fluctuations that disappear in a deterministic population limit. Nor does Theorem 8’s finite-\(n\) running time already give the proposed compressed algorithm: in a block population, individual transition probabilities can shrink with \(N\). The continuous problem would need its own support-sensitive analysis.

The weakest point is precisely that passage to block masses discards arbitrary neighborhood structure. The mirror is convincing for exchangeable, block-structured communities, but does not cover general graphs. It is also open whether fractional seeding preserves the same \(1-1/e\) boundary or makes the continuous optimization easier. Those are real questions for the mirror, not reasons to dismiss this regime: large campaigns over users with shared contact profiles are a sensible high-multiplicity instance of the paper’s problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 8’s input is a graph, and its campaign choices can depend on where vertices sit in that graph. For an arbitrary graph, a type-level mixing matrix \(K\) generally does not preserve that information: two vertices with the same probabilities of contacting each class can still have different neighborhoods and different effects when seeded. An exact aggregation may therefore need nearly one type per vertex, leaving no useful compression. The proposed recurrence gives a valid population limit for exchangeable block interactions, but it does not continuize Theorem 8 across the paper’s general graph inputs; it selects a special network model.

That objection does not dispose of the best mirror. A dense block network with random contacts is a sensible regime for large communities, and finite-horizon seeding still asks the paper’s recognizable campaign question. In that regime, taking \(\beta N\) seeds preserves a nonzero population effect, and the mass recurrence is a plausible limit. The continuous optimization problem remains to be analyzed even if the dynamics themselves are easy to evaluate.

So the case against is weak at its most important point: it narrows the mirror to exchangeable block networks, but cannot show that this regime is implausible or that its support-sensitive campaign problem is unworthy of the programme. I can challenge the claim that it mirrors the paper’s general graph result; I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 8.
