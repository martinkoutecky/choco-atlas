What the mirror covers

No named computational result is covered by a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest possible case is that the paper takes the population seriously as an infinite object and studies how anonymous social comparisons behave there. A possible scenario would have many people whose welfare levels fall into a small finite set. But that gives only a conceptual resemblance; I cannot make a positive case that meets your anchor requirement.

There is no named computational result in the paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for finite-valued worlds \(W_F\), the Sum Preorder is the largest preorder satisfying Strong Pareto, Permutation Invariance, and Quasi-Independence. Proposition 2, also proved here, gives the corresponding largest-preorder result for \(W=\{0,1\}^X\) using Strong Pareto and Permutation Invariance. Proposition 1, cited from Askel (2018), says those two axioms are incompatible with completeness on that binary domain. These are axiomatic maximality and impossibility results; none asserts an algorithmic or complexity result of the kind the brief requires. They therefore cannot honestly serve as anchors, and there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The tempting model would give each welfare level \(r\) a population mass \(\mu_r\), then compare allocations using those masses. The regime might be a large society with few welfare types. But the paper’s worlds are functions \(w:X\to\mathbb R\) on a countably infinite population. A finite-valued world can assign infinitely many people to each of its welfare levels, so it does not generally induce normalized type masses. The Sum Preorder compares worlds through the unconditional sum \(\sum_{x\in X}(w(x)-v(x))\), including divergence to positive infinity. A normalized distribution over welfare types instead gives a finite weighted sum and can rank pairs that the paper’s relation leaves incomparable. The proposed model changes the central comparison the theorem characterizes.

My weakest point is also the reason I stop short of a mirror: the paper is already about infinite populations, but its results answer axiomatic questions rather than computational ones. Calling a mass-based welfare comparison its computational mirror would require inventing a task and an anchor the paper does not supply.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that the paper has no named computational result to anchor a mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes the largest preorder satisfying three axioms; Proposition 2 gives a related axiomatic result on binary worlds; Proposition 1, cited from earlier work, is an impossibility result about completeness. None studies an algorithmic problem, its input, or its complexity. Under ChoCo’s standard, that is decisive: there is no paper result here for a continuous computational question to mirror.

The strongest rescue would be to treat welfare levels as voter types and replace the population with their normalized masses. But the paper’s finite-valued worlds can assign countably infinitely many people to each welfare level. They do not supply fractions for those groups. Nor is there a permutation-invariant probability measure on the countable set \(X\): invariance gives every singleton the same mass, which must be either zero (making the total mass zero) or positive (making it infinite). Frequencies based on an enumeration would depend on that enumeration, while the paper requires invariance under arbitrary permutations.

A distribution over welfare levels would also lose distinctions the paper’s axioms preserve. In particular, Strong Pareto treats a strict improvement to even one person as socially strict. A mass representation that records only type proportions cannot distinguish worlds differing at one zero-mass point. Keeping that pointwise information requires retaining the individual assignment; changing Pareto to require improvements on positive mass instead changes the normative question. Either way, the proposed mass model no longer represents the comparisons characterized by the paper.

One could invent a policy or intervention problem over a finite set of welfare types, with costs and feasible changes, then ask for an efficient algorithm. That might be worthwhile research, but the action space and computational task would come from the invention, not from any result in this paper. Even a decision procedure for the Sum Preorder would need a finite encoding of countably infinite worlds that the paper does not provide. So the negative case is strong on whether this paper has a ChoCo mirror, though it cannot rule out related computational work motivated by the same topic.
