The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified, and it presents no formulation that the authors then use to compute solutions. Proposition 4 proves existence of an ICPS-stable allocation; Propositions 6 and 7 prove uniqueness and assortativity under stronger test assumptions. Those are economic structure results, not computational results. They are proved here, but none can honestly serve as the required anchor.

There is a plausible continuous-population problem latent in the model. Consider a large labor market with many interchangeable firms and workers: a type records all problem-relevant attributes, including productivity, beliefs, and access to tests. The instance gives rational masses \(\mu_a\) of firm types and \(\nu_b\) of worker types, plus surplus and test data. A solution assigns matching mass \(x_{ab}\) to each type pair, with row and column totals bounded by the available masses, and specifies transfers so that no type pair can profitably block after choosing a feasible test. A repeated hiring market with many firms and candidates in a few role, qualification, and test categories is a plausible regime: the population can be large while the number of distinct types stays small.

That would be a worthwhile new computational question, but it is not a computational result of this paper. Proposition 4 motivates asking how to find a stable allocation; Propositions 6 and 7 suggest that strong tests may make the answer easy to characterize. Neither gives the authors’ complexity result for such a problem. A finite-type formulation might lead to a matching LP if its test-adjusted pair values can be computed, but that would need to be established for the proposed model.

The weakest point is that the paper’s baseline has only two productivity types and its strongest test results already force an assortative matching. The continuous setting is plausible, but the computational landscape—and even the richer type and test structure needed to make it interesting—would have to be supplied by a follow-on study. So the honest positive case is a candidate research direction, not a per-anchor mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Propositions 4, 6, and 7 establish existence, uniqueness, and assortativity; the other propositions concern refinement, welfare, and comparative statics. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm. Their continuous counterparts would be analytical results, outside ChoCo’s stated computational scope.

The proponent’s best candidate is still sensible: a labor market with many firms and workers in finitely many complete types, rational masses, and a specified menu of tests. One could choose matching masses \(x_{ab}\), represent pairwise surpluses and transfers, and ask for an ICPS-stable allocation. The high-multiplicity setting is plausible, so objections based on agents being inherently individuated or mass being meaningless do not defeat it.

But that candidate adds a computational task the paper never studies. Proposition 4’s existence proof can motivate asking how to find a stable allocation; it does not supply a complexity result for that task. The same applies to the uniqueness and assortativity results in Propositions 6 and 7. To make the proposed task precise, one would also need to specify how tests are represented, what information their accuracy parameter actually describes, and what constitutes a computed stable output. A finite, specified version could be a matching optimization problem; making it richer could create new algorithmic questions, but their complexity would come from added primitives rather than a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong under the requested standard for a *mirror*: the paper offers no computational anchor, and the proposed mass-matching problem is a follow-on question, not a continuization of one. It is weak as a claim that no worthwhile ChoCo project could grow from the paper. The labor-market model makes a plausible starting point for new work, and this paper alone does not justify ruling that out.
