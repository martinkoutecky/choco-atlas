The continuous mirror question

Given worker types \(A\) and position types \(B\) with rational masses \(\alpha_a,\beta_b\), sincere rankings over counterpart types and outside options, and a sincerely stable target flow \(x^\star\), find a report profile or certify that none exists. A flow \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) must satisfy \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\beta_b\); it records type-level assignment mass while each individual receives one partner or remains unmatched. The instance specifies a computable stable-flow mechanism \(R\), type utilities consistent with the rankings, and cohorts as the strategic actors, each submitting one ranking for its type. Ask whether there is a cohort Nash equilibrium in which no cohort can submit a closer ranking under Kendall distance without lowering its per-capita expected utility, and \(R\)’s expected flow is \(x^\star\).

The model it lives in

A population represented by rational masses over complete matching-relevant types, with type-level assignment flows and one strategic cohort per type. Each cohort controls its type’s report; stability is defined by the absence of a positive-mass blocking pair, and honesty is measured by Kendall distance from the sincere ranking. This is a cohort or block-deviation extension, not an atomless model of unilateral individual incentives.

The objection that survived

A cohort controls a joint report, whereas the paper’s main game gives each individual a unilateral report; the proponent acknowledges this difference but does not establish that the authors would accept cohort Nash equilibrium as their problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the equilibrium-refinement procedure for fully randomized mechanisms in Lemma 4.1 and its supporting construction. It leaves the paper’s stability guarantees, egalitarian impossibility result, Gale–Shapley woman-optimality result, and other extensions unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can closer-report deviations and their effects on a specified stable-flow mechanism be found efficiently?
  • Does equilibrium realization remain tractable when cohorts may split their mass across different reports?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow. This paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result. My lead—and only computational anchor—is Lemma 4.1, proved here: for a fully randomized stable matching mechanism, the authors’ Algorithm 1 starts from preference lists truncated after a chosen sincerely stable matching and repeatedly corrects a more-honest deviation. It terminates because the potential \(\sum_y K(\Pi_y,\PP_y)\) is a nonnegative integer that drops by at least one per update; the paper bounds the number of updates by \(O(|M||W|^2+|M|^2|W|)\). Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3 establish the purpose of that procedure: it preserves an equilibrium with the chosen matching as the unique reported-stable matching, and checking the restricted deviations suffices to establish minimal dishonesty. This is an algorithmic result, but not a polynomial-time classification: the cost of finding a violating deviation and evaluating an arbitrary mechanism is not bounded.

I would mirror it as Continuous Cohort Equilibrium Realization. The regime is a large two-sided labor market: many workers and positions, but relatively few complete matching-relevant types, such as graduate specializations and employer sectors. A type includes its side, its ranking over counterpart types and the outside option, its capacity or eligibility attributes, and its honesty-relevant attributes. Each type has a rational mass; the number of participants is large while the number of types \(\tau\) stays moderate. Cohort-level preferences are plausible when, for example, a large group of near-identical applicants ranks the same job categories.

For a precise model, let \(A\) and \(B\) be the worker and employer types, with masses \(\alpha_a,\beta_b\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\). A matching is a flow \(x_{ab}\ge0\), with row and column sums bounded by the corresponding masses; unmatched mass is allowed. A flow is stable when no pair of types has positive mass on both sides who would each prefer to match one another to their current assignments. Reports are rankings of counterpart types. A mechanism \(R\) maps reported rankings and masses to a lottery over flows stable under those reports; its output is the induced per-person partner lottery for each type. The mechanism and its evaluation procedure are part of the input, with their computational cost counted.

The strategic actors are the cohorts: all members of a type submit one report together. Their utility is per-capita expected utility from assigned partner types, with the utility representation included in the type. They seek a pure Nash equilibrium, refined by the paper’s Kendall-distance notion of minimal dishonesty: a cohort cannot move its report closer to the sincere ranking without making its outcome strictly worse. The instance also gives a sincerely stable target flow \(x^\star\). The question is whether one can find a report profile that is an equilibrium, satisfies this honesty refinement, and makes \(R\)’s expected flow equal to \(x^\star\). That asks for the underlying equilibrium and matching, not an encoding of them.

This is the lead mirror because it follows the paper’s equilibrium-finding construction closely: the action is a ranking, the outcome is a matching, and the refinement measures how far reports depart from sincere preferences. Its continuous object is the population of workers and positions; the flow is the mass-level description induced by that choice. Under cohort-level actions, the finite-potential idea suggests a tractable direction: if the two sides have \(p\) and \(q\) types, whole-cohort updates would give at most \(O(pq^2+p^2q)\) strict improvements. I would expect a Class A result for a specified mechanism with efficient evaluation and deviation checks. That is a projection, not a theorem from this paper: searching all closer rankings can still be expensive, and Lemma 4.1 does not bound that search. The immediate questions are whether every sincerely stable flow can be realized this way, whether improving deviations can be found efficiently, and whether allowing each cohort to split its mass across reports changes existence or tractability.

The mirror covers the equilibrium-construction result for fully randomized mechanisms. It does not claim a continuous-population version of the paper’s complexity landscape; there is no hardness anchor here from which to ask whether hardness dissolves.

The weakest point is the cohort-as-player assumption. A cohort controlling its entire mass is a coordinated group, while the paper’s central model gives each individual a unilateral deviation. If individuals instead act independently in an atomless population, a single report may have no effect on the aggregate flow, and the strategic problem can collapse. The type-level market is a credible high-multiplicity scenario, but showing that its cohort game is the continuous analogue the authors would accept is the real burden of this case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against starts with the sole anchor: Lemma 4.1. Its bound counts updates, not computation. Finding a violated minimal-dishonesty condition may require searching all closer preference lists and evaluating the mechanism after each deviation. The paper gives no cost for either operation, and \(r\) is an arbitrary stable-matching mechanism. Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3 establish that the procedure preserves the desired equilibrium properties; they do not fill in that missing cost. So the paper’s named algorithmic result is a finite descent argument, not a tractability result.

The proposed cohort version does not preserve the game that descent argument analyzes. When a whole type controls its mass and submits one report, that type is acting as a coalition. In the paper, each named individual chooses a report unilaterally. If instead each person in a type acts independently in an atomless population, one person’s report changes no aggregate mass. A mechanism driven only by the distribution of reports therefore cannot capture the effect of that deviation on the outcome. Retaining those individual incentives requires additional, person-level allocation information beyond the proposed flow.

A type-level flow can still be a sensible model: rankings over counterpart types, capacities, and blocking pairs can all be defined. But those choices change the strategic object. The authors would need to specify how a cohort’s collective report relates to individual incentives, how the matching mechanism extends to flows, and how deviation checks are computed. Making those checks efficient is part of the open computational question; assuming it in the setup would assume away the main obstacle. The proposed iteration bound in \(p\) and \(q\) does not establish that those checks are tractable.

A stronger rescue might mirror the paper’s sincere-stability theorem or its result about strong equilibria. Those are meaningful structural claims, but neither supplies a computational problem by itself. Turning them into equilibrium-search or verification problems for a continuum flow market would again require choosing a new strategic unit and a new mechanism model. That may be worthwhile research, but it is not a computational consequence of the paper’s results.

I would therefore reject the proposed mirror as a direct ChoCo anchor: its only algorithmic foothold does not yet identify a complexity question whose continuous version follows from the paper. The negative case is weaker than the universal claim, though. Large markets with repeated preference cohorts are plausible, and a carefully specified cohort game could be valuable in its own right. I can argue that this paper does not establish that such a project is worthwhile; I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous matching mirror.
