The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result, so it has no qualifying anchor under your rules. Algorithms 1 and 2 are named procedures, and Section III-D claims an efficient computation, but neither is a numbered complexity result. I can’t honestly supply the requested anchor-based case.

There is still a plausible continuous mirror. Let \(Q\) be the objects and let each type \(t\) be a strict partial order on \(Q\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of evaluators with that partial order. For each ordered pair, set \(r_\mu(x,y)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[x<_t y]\). The question would be: find the critical cutoff
\[ \kappa=\inf\{\lambda\in[0,1]:\operatorname{TC}(\{(x,y):r_\mu(x,y)\ge\lambda\})\text{ is a strict partial order}\}, \]
and return the transitive closure of the pairs with \(r_\mu(x,y)>\kappa\). This is a right-limit version of the paper’s threshold-and-cycle-avoidance problem.

A plausible regime is a large population of evaluators comparing the same options using a limited collection of criteria or evaluation templates. Many evaluators can share a partial-order type, so the number of people can be large while the support size \(\tau\) stays small. The paper’s multi-criteria pollution example gives some grounding for that scenario, though its actual five voters do not themselves establish a high-multiplicity regime. The authors would likely recognize the mass-based aggregation question: they define pairwise preference frequencies and explicitly treat partial orders as voters.

For this candidate mirror, I expect tractability: compute the pairwise masses and a max–min transitive closure, in time polynomial in \(|Q|\), \(\tau\), and the rational input’s bit length. But that is a conclusion about the proposed mirror, not a result anchored in a numbered theorem of this paper.

The weakest point is the threshold boundary. With continuous masses, the paper’s minimum cutoff using \(\ge\) may fail to be attained: a tied cycle can require a cutoff strictly above its weakest edge. The right-limit formulation handles that, but it changes the precise question. So the paper supports a credible continuous aggregation model, yet does not provide the named computational anchor needed for the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper offers no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing the complexity of a computational problem. Its main contribution is a threshold aggregation rule; Algorithms 1 and 2 describe how to compute it, and the paper gives running-time observations, but it does not chart a complexity landscape for bribery, control, campaigning, or robustness. The proponent therefore has no qualifying result to anchor a mirror to.

The proposed population model itself is credible. Evaluators who share a complete partial-order type can form a sensible high-multiplicity society, for example in a large review panel using standardized criteria. The threshold boundary problem also has a natural repair: choose a cutoff from the finitely many pairwise masses, or use the right-limit convention in the proposal. Neither point defeats that model.

The best scope objection is that this mirror asks how to evaluate a weighted aggregation rule. To turn it into a ChoCo-style intervention problem would require adding a cost or action model over the society, which the paper does not supply. That would be a new problem rather than a mirror anchored in one of its results. Still, I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: the weighted rule-evaluation question is coherent, and the paper’s lack of a qualifying computational result limits the case for a mirror without refuting that question.
