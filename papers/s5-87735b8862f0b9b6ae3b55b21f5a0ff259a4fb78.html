The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric nonnegative rational interaction matrix \(a_{tu}\), and fixed rational \(0<\alpha<1\), let \(d_t=\sum_u a_{tu}\mu_u>0\). Find an initial seed vector \(x\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\) of minimum total mass \(\sum_t x_t\) such that the irreversible cascade \(q_t^{(0)}=x_t\), \(q_t^{(k+1)}=q_t^{(k)}+(\mu_t-q_t^{(k)})\mathbf{1}[\sum_u a_{tu}q_u^{(k)}\ge\alpha d_t]\) reaches \(q_t^{(K)}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\) at some finite round \(K\).

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchored positive case under your stated rule: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another computational complexity class. Its theorems give bounds on minimum set sizes and counts of such sets. The closest result for a mirror is Theorem 1, proved here: for \(\alpha\)-BP on \(G=(V,E)\), \(1\le MD_\alpha(G)\le \frac{\delta+1/\alpha}{\delta+1}\alpha n\). Theorem 2, also proved here, gives \(2\alpha\sqrt n-1\le\overleftarrow{MD}_\alpha(G)\le n\) for \(\alpha>3/4\), and \(1\le\overleftarrow{MD}_\alpha(G)\le n\) for \(\alpha\le3/4\). Theorem 3 is cited from [21] and bounds \(MD_r(G)\) by \(r\le MD_r(G)\le\frac{r}{1+\delta}n\). These are substantive combinatorial results, but none establishes a computational complexity result. The paper’s mention of a greedy algorithm does not supply a numbered complexity anchor.

The strongest plausible mirror I see is the Minimum-Mass \(\alpha\)-Dynamo problem. Take a finite set \(T\) of voter-like interaction types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric nonnegative rational interaction matrix \(a_{tu}\), and a rational threshold \(0<\alpha<1\). Type \(t\) has total interaction weight \(d_t=\sum_u a_{tu}\mu_u>0\). Choose initial black masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) to minimize \(\sum_t x_t\). Starting from \(q_t^{(0)}=x_t\), evolve by
\[ q_t^{(k+1)} =q_t^{(k)}+(\mu_t-q_t^{(k)}) \mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_u a_{tu}q_u^{(k)}\ge\alpha d_t\right]. \]
A solution is a vector \(x\) and a finite round \(K\) for which \(q_t^{(K)}=\mu_t\) for every type. Here a type is a complete interaction role, and mass is the fraction of a large population in that role. A plausible regime is a large community organized into a small number of cohorts with shared interaction profiles. This keeps the paper’s monotone fractional-threshold cascade and its minimum-seed objective; it changes arbitrary graphs to a finite-type interaction model.

That gives a concrete, plausible continuous population question, and Theorem 1 is relevant motivation: when minimum degree grows proportionally with population size, its upper bound becomes a seed fraction of at most \(\alpha+O(1/n)\). But that observation does not prove a result for the proposed model, and Theorem 1 does not say the optimization problem is in P. I would leave the proposed problem’s complexity open rather than assign it a class from this paper.

The weakest point is therefore decisive for the requested format: the mirror has a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but no qualifying named computational anchor. Theorem 1 supports why someone might study the mass version; it cannot honestly be presented as the paper’s complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirrors have no computational result in this paper to mirror. Theorem 1 gives bounds on the minimum size of an \(\alpha\)-BP dynamo; Theorem 2 gives such bounds for the two-way process. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem or analyzes the running time of an algorithm. Theorem 3 is cited from earlier work and also gives size bounds. The paper’s greedy construction helps prove a bound, but the paper does not establish a complexity result for finding a minimum dynamo. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, that is decisive: a continuous minimum-seed problem may be worth studying, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result established here.

The best repair is a finite-type model for a large community with repeated interaction roles, asking for the minimum mass of initial adopters that makes everyone adopt. That is a sensible question, and it does make the population continuous. But it replaces the paper’s arbitrary graph with a homogeneous interaction model: members of a type must have the same relevant interaction profile, so their mass can determine how they update. In a sparse network, two vertices with the same degree and threshold can still have different neighbors and play different roles in the cascade. Capturing those differences may require making many vertices distinct types, losing the high-multiplicity regime. Dense, uniformly connected cohorts avoid that problem, but define a restricted model whose complexity landscape would be a new research contribution, not a result this paper points toward.

For Theorem 1, that repair is most plausible: \(\alpha\)-BP already uses a fraction-of-neighbors threshold. A mass version could ask for the minimum seed fraction in a dense block or graphon model. But the theorem’s bound depends on \(n\) and the graph’s minimum degree, and its upper bound does not itself give a minimum-dynamo algorithm. Its scale can also disappear in the continuum: on a star, one seed suffices, so the seed fraction tends to zero. Dense graph sequences may instead have a positive limiting seed fraction, but that makes the mirror specific to a chosen dense regime. The possible mass optimization is a valid follow-up problem; it does not rescue Theorem 1 as a computational anchor.

Theorem 2 is a weaker anchor still. The proponent’s update rule only adds black mass, so it models irreversible \(\alpha\)-BP, not the two-way process in this theorem. A correct two-way cohort model could make each type switch according to its current interaction mass. Yet the theorem’s lower bound for \(\alpha>3/4\) is only \(2\alpha\sqrt n-1\), whose population fraction tends to zero; cycles give a contrasting case where the minimum dynamo is all \(n\) vertices. Ordinary finite-type mixing cannot preserve a cycle’s local propagation, while a spatial or local-limit model could preserve it only with a different, more elaborate representation. Those are possible choices for a new model, but the paper provides no computational result to select among them or motivate a complexity claim.

Theorem 3 is both cited prior work and a poor mass-scale fit. It assumes fixed \(r\); on complete graphs the minimum seed size is \(r\), so the seed fraction tends to zero as \(n\) grows. Scaling the threshold with degree could produce a nonzero mass problem, but that changes the fixed-\(r\) model into a fractional-threshold one. Keeping the fixed seed count would require tracking vanishing masses or finite-size corrections, rather than optimizing population mass.

So I would reject the proponent’s case as a mirror of this paper’s results, while not claiming that no interesting continuous threshold-cascade problem exists. Large, homogeneous communities provide a credible setting for one. The negative case is strongest at the paper-to-programme boundary: answering the proposed question would require choosing a new graph regime and studying its optimization problem, not continuizing a named computational result here.
