Why no mirror

A distribution over ballot types is a sensible way to supply inputs to the authors’ rating method. But the three proposed anchors establish existence, structure, or continuity; none asserts an algorithmic guarantee or hardness. Computing the rating from type masses is a plausible follow-up, but it is not a computational result established by these anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is a continuous-population evaluation of the CLC–Zermelo rating. Let \(A\) be the options and let \(T\) be the ballot types: complete rankings, or the truncated rankings and ties allowed by the paper. The input is a list of supported types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). A type contributes its mass to each expressed pairwise preference; definite indifference contributes half in each direction, while an omitted comparison contributes neither. This gives relative Llull scores \(v^\mu_{xy}\).

The task is to compute the fractions \(\phi\) assigned to the options: project \(v^\mu\) by the paper’s CLC projection and return the normalized Zermelo strengths. Equivalently, the strengths maximize the paper’s likelihood function \(F\) on the simplex, with zero strengths allowed in the reducible case. A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballot templates—for example, millions of respondents but only tens or hundreds of supported types. The authors would likely recognize this as computing their rating from a population distribution. The natural expectation is Class A, through aggregation followed by optimization or numerical solution; further questions include the bit complexity of the output, approximation guarantees near reducible inputs, and convergence rates for the iteration in equations (43)–(44).

But I cannot give this mirror a qualifying anchor under your criteria. The nearest results are Theorem 2.1 (partly credited to Zermelo [25], with the smooth-dependence result proved here): for an irreducible matrix, there is a unique positive normalized likelihood maximizer, it satisfies equations (22)–(23), and it depends smoothly on the scores. Theorem 2.2 (parts (a) and (b) contained in [25], with continuity part (c) proved here) describes the unique limiting strengths when there is a top dominant irreducible component: strengths vanish outside that component, and vary continuously under the stated conditions. Theorem 4.4, proved here using the cited CLC continuity result and Theorems 2.1–2.2, says that the final CLC–Zermelo fractions depend continuously on the original Llull matrix. These are results about existence, structure, and continuity—not a complexity classification or an efficiency guarantee. The paper gives an iteration, but establishes no runtime, convergence rate, or bit complexity for it.

The weak point is also central: the paper already works with voter-normalized pairwise scores and fractional outcomes. Replacing those scores with an explicit distribution over ballot types makes the population continuous in the programme’s sense, but may mostly re-encode an aggregate input that the paper already treats. Thus there is a plausible population-evaluation question here, but no named computational result in the paper that supports the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that all three anchors depend on the same object the paper already uses: the Llull score matrix. For a society distribution \(\mu\) over ballot types, let

\[ v^\mu_{xy}=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,p_t(x,y), \]

where \(p_t(x,y)\) is the contribution of type \(t\) to the comparison \(x\) versus \(y\). The proposed rating is then the paper’s existing map \(\Phi(v^\mu)=Z(P(v^\mu))\). The population distribution enters only through this linear aggregation.

Theorem 2.1 concerns the Zermelo solution as a function of the scores \(v_{xy}\); its equations do not use the voter identities or ballot-type support. Replacing finite-voter proportions with \(\mu\) therefore gives the same solution and the same smoothness claim. Asking for a runtime or bit-complexity bound would be a new algorithmic result, not a mirror of the theorem’s asserted result.

Theorem 2.2 likewise describes strengths from the score matrix’s dominant component. In a population model, that component is determined by the positive entries of \(v^\mu\). The theorem’s boundary behavior carries over unchanged, while the distribution over types supplies no additional structure for it to exploit.

Theorem 4.4 makes the point most explicitly: the final fractions depend continuously on the original Llull matrix. A continuous society maps into that same matrix space, so the theorem already applies after aggregation. Calling the input a population distribution does not create a new continuity question.

A large election with many repeated ballot types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; that is not the objection. The difficulty for a mirror is that the paper deliberately discards everything about the society beyond pairwise scores. Given \(\tau\) supported types, computing those scores takes a sum over the types; after that, the rating calculation has exactly the same input as before. In particular, any algorithmic question about the score-to-rating map is the same question whether the scores came from named voters or from masses. Adding type-specific prices, costs, or intervention choices to make the population matter would change the problem the paper solves.

There is a plausible implementation project here: establish exact or approximate complexity bounds for computing CLC–Zermelo fractions from rational score matrices, perhaps with attention to reducible inputs. But the paper states no such complexity result, and phrasing the input as a distribution over ballot types does not make that project population-specific. So none of the three named results supplies a qualifying computational anchor.

That is a strong negative case for a mirror of this paper’s results, but not an airtight case that no useful follow-up exists. It is weakest at the boundary between a continuous mirror and a new study of the computation of an existing rating method.
