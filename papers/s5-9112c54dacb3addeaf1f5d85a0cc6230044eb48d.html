The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) indivisible goods, a known bundle distribution \(\mathcal D\) over \(2^{[n]}\), and a society with support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and one gross-substitutes valuation \(f_t\) per type, how well can a learner using value queries to the \(f_t\) approximate them? Find the smallest achievable \(\alpha\) such that, with confidence at least \(1-\delta\), its polynomially evaluable hypotheses satisfy \(\Pr_{t\sim\mu,\,S\sim\mathcal D}[\hat f_t(S)\le f_t(S)\le\alpha\hat f_t(S)]\ge1-\epsilon\), with running time and query complexity measured in \(n\), \(|T|\), the input bit lengths, \(1/\epsilon\), and \(1/\delta\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over customer types, each type carrying mass \(\mu_t\) and a complete shared gross-substitutes valuation \(f_t\). The learner queries \(f_t(S)\) for type-bundle pairs and outputs one succinct hypothesis \(\hat f_t\) per type, minimizing the approximation factor under the population-weighted guarantee.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s gross-substitutes lower-bound setting and extends Theorem 1’s XOS and subadditive PMAC guarantees to type-weighted learning; it leaves the other valuation subclasses and the separate value-query and price-learning results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the best achievable approximation factor as a function of \(n\), \(|T|\), and the accuracy parameters, including when the masses are spread across many types rather than concentrated on one?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is valuation-profile learning for a high-multiplicity customer base. Take a retailer or wholesaler with many customers buying bundles from \(n\) goods. Customers fall into \(\tau\) observable segments, each defined completely for this task: everyone in segment \(t\) has the same valuation function \(f_t\), including the same bundle preferences and any relevant costs or budgets. The society is the rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those segments, with many customers but comparatively few types. A discrete panel with \(N_t\) customers in segment \(t\) gives \(\mu_t=N_t/N\); the continuous model lets those shares be arbitrary binary rationals.

The target is a succinct hypothesis \(\hat f_t\) for each segment, and the objective is to minimize the approximation factor \(\alpha\) while keeping the fraction of mispredicted customer–bundle pairs small:
\[ \Pr_{t\sim\mu,\;S\sim\mathcal D}\!\left[\hat f_t(S)\le f_t(S)\le\alpha\hat f_t(S)\right]\ge 1-\epsilon. \]
Here \(\mathcal D\) is the paper’s distribution over bundles; it is *not* the society distribution. The continuized object is the customer population, measured by \(\mu\). For the passive version, examples identify the segment and report its value for a sampled bundle. For the value-query version, a learner may request \(f_t(S)\) for a segment and bundle of its choice. These are still the paper’s underlying learning questions, now evaluated over a continuum of customers. The paper’s customer-understanding motivation makes segment-level valuation learning recognizable to its authors; the additional assumption is that segment membership is observable.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper using a lower bound from Balcan and Harvey [5] and the paper’s Lemma 2. Its full claim is that no algorithm can PMAC-learn gross-substitutes valuations to approximation factor \(o(n^{1/3}/\log n)\), even when the bundle distribution is known and value queries are allowed. The paper proves the bridge to that lower bound by showing that matroid rank functions are gross substitutes.

This anchors Continuous Gross-Substitutes Segment Learning: given \(n\) goods, observable segment labels with rational masses \(\mu\), gross-substitutes target functions \(f_t\), and known \(\mathcal D\), use value queries to the segment functions and output polynomially evaluable \(\hat f_t\) meeting the population-weighted guarantee above. Ask for the best achievable \(\alpha\) in time polynomial in the input and oracle-query lengths, \(n\), \(\tau\), \(1/\epsilon\), and \(1/\delta\). I expect the paper’s approximation barrier to transfer: the continuous problem includes \(\mu\) concentrated on one segment, so any algorithm for all societies would also learn the original single-valuation instance. This is an information-theoretic approximation lower bound, not an NP-hardness result. Theorem 5’s stronger query access makes the boundary meaningful: even abundant information about a large customer cohort does not remove this barrier when its shared valuation is hard.

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It establishes \(\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt n)\) PMAC learnability for XOS and subadditive valuations. More specifically, XOS valuations have an \(O(\sqrt n)\) upper bound and an \(\Omega(\sqrt n/\log n)\) information-theoretic lower bound; subadditive valuations have an \(O(\sqrt n\log n)\) upper bound, with the lower bound inherited from the XOS subclass.

This gives Continuous XOS/Subadditive Segment Learning: same society and weighted guarantee, but with passive labeled examples drawn from customers and bundles, and with each segment valuation in XOS or subadditive. Its expected direction is Class A for approximate learning: run the paper’s learner for each sufficiently large segment, allocate samples across segments, and ignore segments whose total mass is at most the allowed error. The factor remains \(\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt n)\), with polynomial overhead in \(\tau\) and the accuracy and confidence parameters. This does not claim exact reconstruction; it mirrors the paper’s approximate learning objective and its limit on improving the factor. It raises the natural question whether shared structure across segments can improve on separate per-segment learning.

The case’s weakest point is that the continuum chiefly changes the population-weighted objective and input representation; it does not soften the bundle-domain difficulty. In particular, the lead lower bound already holds for a society with one type, so the masses do not create a new algorithmic lever there. If a referee requires population continuity itself to change the core computational structure, this mirror may feel like a high-multiplicity extension of valuation learning rather than a new continuous optimization problem. Still, it is a genuine customer-population model, the paper’s own setting, and the singleton case gives a clear boundary rather than an inflated promise. This mirror covers the paper’s PMAC learnability results, not its separate value-query and price-learning results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has named computational results, so the case against a mirror cannot rest on the absence of complexity theorems. Its Theorems 1 and 5 give algorithmic and information-theoretic bounds for learning valuation functions. Nor is high multiplicity impossible: a retailer could have many customers in a small number of segments. The stronger objection is that the population does little computational work in any faithful version of these anchors.

The paper learns one consumer’s valuation \(f:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb R_+\). Its random input is a bundle drawn from \(\mathcal D\); the unknown function is fixed. The proposed segment-learning mirror adds a family \(f_t\) and weights its errors by \(\mu_t\). With observable segments, that becomes learning on the enlarged domain \(T\times 2^{[n]}\). At a chosen accuracy, segments of total mass at most \(\epsilon\) can be skipped, and the others can be learned with the paper’s algorithms, with sample allocation adjusted for their masses. The rational population shares affect weighting and sample needs; they do not create a new computational structure in the paper’s learning problem.

That undercuts the Theorem 5 anchor specifically. Its lower bound already applies to a single valuation, so concentrating \(\mu\) on one segment recovers the paper’s original problem. With several segments, the natural extension asks for one learner per shared valuation. The hard part remains learning each \(f_t\); the population mass only weights which types matter to the guarantee. Even a better formulation that asks for one representative population valuation, \(\bar f(S)=\sum_t\mu_t f_t(S)\), makes the population a way to define a target function. For finite support, an exact query to \(\bar f\) can be computed by querying each \(f_t\) and taking the weighted sum. The population changes the target’s coefficients, while the learning task remains valuation learning.

Theorem 1 has the same problem. Separate PMAC learners give the proposed type-wise result, with the familiar \(\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt n)\) XOS barrier and the corresponding subadditive bound. A weighted guarantee can make rare segments irrelevant at fixed \(\epsilon\), but that is a standard distributional-learning effect. If the goal is instead to learn \(\bar f\), finite weighted sums preserve subadditivity and XOS, so the paper’s function classes and learning questions remain the natural starting point. A richer task—such as discovering unobserved segments or exploiting shared structure across their valuations—might be worthwhile. It would require a new model of latent types or cross-type structure; the paper’s theorems do not supply those questions.

There is also a modeling cost behind the proposed segment interpretation. A ChoCo type must include every feature the problem uses. Here that means the same valuation on every bundle. Observable demographic labels do not by themselves guarantee that customers in a segment meet this condition. One can stipulate exact shared valuations, as the proponent does, and that is a coherent high-multiplicity regime. But the stipulation yields repeated copies of the paper’s unknown target, not a population object whose redistribution or intervention is computationally studied. In the separate-output version, labels let the learner retain type identity; in the aggregate version, averaging removes that identity.

So the strongest negative case is about fit: the plausible mirrors either repeat the paper’s single-target learning problem for each type or fold population shares into one valuation-learning target. Neither makes the society itself the computational object in the way ChoCo’s bribery, control, or robustness problems do. This is not a decisive proof that no useful mirror exists. The retailer scenario is credible, and a deliberately richer multi-type learning model could be useful. The case is weakest at the universal claim: the material does not rule out such a model; it shows that the proposed anchors, as stated, do not yet make population continuization do enough work to justify a ChoCo mirror.
