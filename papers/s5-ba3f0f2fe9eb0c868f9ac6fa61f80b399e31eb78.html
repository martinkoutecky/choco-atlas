The case FOR (proponent)

The anchor requirement is decisive here: this paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its introduction says winner determination was shown NP-hard in Rothkopf, Pekĕc, and Harstad; that is a result cited from elsewhere, not a numbered result of this paper. The running-time and pruning claims are experimental, and the square-root criterion’s theoretical guarantee is credited to the authors’ earlier paper. So there are zero eligible anchors, and I can’t honestly present an anchor-compliant positive case.

The strongest candidate mirror latent in the paper is *Continuous High-Multiplicity Winner Determination*. Consider a large auction with \(n\) commodities and \(\tau\) bidder types. A type \(t\) is a complete bid: an integer bundle \(q_t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^n\) and price \(p_t\ge 0\). Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the bidder population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(\kappa_j\) be the supply of commodity \(j\) per bidder. Choose \(x_t\), the mass of type \(t\) whose bids are accepted, to maximize normalized declared welfare:

\[ \max \sum_{t=1}^{\tau} p_t x_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau} q_{tj}x_t\le \kappa_j\ \ (j=1,\ldots,n), \qquad 0\le x_t\le \mu_t. \]

Each accepted bidder still receives their entire requested bundle; \(x_t\) selects a share of an interchangeable type’s population. With rational inputs this is an LP, solvable in time polynomial in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length. The expected outcome is tractable.

A plausible regime is a large recurring capacity auction with millions of small bidders but only dozens or hundreds of standardized bid types. The discrete bridge is direct: for a population of size \(N\), take \(N\mu_t\) bidders of each type and \(N\kappa_j\) units of each commodity. Requiring \(Nx_t\) to be integral recovers winner determination on that repeated-type instance; the continuum removes those count restrictions. This makes the candidate recognizable as the paper’s underlying welfare-maximization problem, and connects closely to the LP relaxation the authors already compute.

The weak point is also clear: the paper uses that LP as a bound while seeking an exact allocation of whole bids. The mirror makes the accepted *mass* continuous. That is defensible for an anonymous, high-multiplicity population in which each individual still gets either the full bundle or nothing, but the authors might regard it as a relaxation of their problem. It covers winner determination alone; it does not mirror the paper’s branch-and-bound performance findings or bidding-order experiments. Since the paper supplies no eligible numbered anchor, this remains a concrete positive candidate, not a case that satisfies the requested anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is paper specific: it has no eligible anchor. It proves no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about a computational problem. Its claims about branch and bound, pruning, and bid ordering are experimental; the cited NP-hardness result is from earlier work, and the square root guarantee is credited to the authors’ earlier paper.

The proponent’s best salvage is continuous high-multiplicity winner determination. It is a sensible model: a type can be a complete bid \((q_t,p_t)\), and an auction with many bidders repeating a modest set of bids is plausible. Its formulation is

\[ \max \sum_t p_t x_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t q_{tj}x_t\le \kappa_j,\qquad 0\le x_t\le \mu_t. \]

But this does not mirror a result the paper establishes. It is the paper’s own fractional LP bound, aggregated by bid type. Allowing accepted mass to be fractional makes that LP the whole problem; retaining exact winner determination requires integer acceptance counts and leaves the discrete high-multiplicity problem. The paper’s contribution—when that LP makes exact search practical, and which branching choices help—is experimental evidence about finite auctions. Replacing that search with the LP asks a different, already formulated optimization question.

That is the best negative case, but it has a real limit: the proposed mirror is coherent, computational, and grounded in a plausible high-multiplicity regime. If the programme accepts a paper’s LP relaxation as a worthwhile mirror of its underlying problem, this candidate survives.
