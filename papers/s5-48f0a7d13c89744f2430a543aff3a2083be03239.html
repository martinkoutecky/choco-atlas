The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of CDM types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), core options \(C\), candidate additions \(B\), a target \(x^\star\in C\), and each type’s utilities \(u_t(x)\) and context pulls \(p_t(z,x)\), choose \(Z\subseteq B\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[v_t(x^\star;Z)>v_t(x;Z)\ \forall x\in C\setminus\{x^\star\}]\), where \(v_t(x;Z)=u_t(x)+\sum_{z\in Z}p_t(z,x)\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete CDM response types; the decision is a shared set of added alternatives \(Z\subseteq B\), and the objective is the population mass whose target option is the strict favorite among \(C\).

The objection that survived

The intervention remains a finite choice of \(Z\subseteq B\), so this may be a weighted high-multiplicity extension of existing algorithms rather than a route to new continuous-optimization machinery.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors cover the paper’s AGREEMENT result in Theorem 1, DISAGREEMENT result in Theorem 2, and PROMOTION result in Theorem 7. They leave the approximation algorithms, restricted-model tractability results, and experiments uncontinuized.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is mass-weighted agreement under MNL, anchored by Theorem 1. It has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a transport agency changes the options shown to a large population of commuters. Each type is a complete choice-response profile—its utility for every option—and its mass is the share of commuters with that profile. There might be millions of commuters but only a modest number \(\tau\) of modelled profiles. The agency chooses which candidate options to introduce, and every commuter sees the same additions. The mass records who holds each type; the choice remains a set of options.

Here is a precise version. Let \(C\) be the core options, \(B\) the candidate additions, and let \(\mathcal T\) be a finite support of types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For MNL, give each type positive rational weights \(w_{t,x}\) for options \(x\in C\cup B\); these are the exponentiated utilities, so the instance has an exact binary encoding. For \(Z\subseteq B\), write
\[ q_t(x;Z)=\frac{w_{t,x}}{\sum_{y\in C}w_{t,y}+\sum_{z\in Z}w_{t,z}}. \]
Define the normalized pairwise disagreement density
\[ D_\mu(Z)=\sum_{\{t,s\}\subseteq\mathcal T}\mu_t\mu_s \sum_{x\in C}\left|q_t(x;Z)-q_s(x;Z)\right|. \]
The continuous MNL Population Agreement problem asks for a set \(Z\subseteq B\) minimizing \(D_\mu(Z)\). Its maximization version asks for a set maximizing the same quantity. These are computational questions about a population distribution, with input size measured in \(|C|,|B|,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\).

This weighting is the natural lift of the paper’s person-level objective. If a finite population has \(N_t\) people of each type, with \(\mu_t=N_t/N\), then pairs of people with the same type contribute zero disagreement, while cross-type pair counts are proportional to \(N_tN_s\). Dividing the original pair sum by \(N^2\) gives \(D_\mu\). So mass means population share, not a fractional choice or a probabilistic outcome.

Theorem 1, proved in the paper, is my lead anchor: AGREEMENT under MNL is NP-hard even with two core items and two individuals whose utilities agree on every candidate alternative. Its reduction is from PARTITION. In the construction, the two individuals’ exponentiated weights for each candidate \(z\) are both \(z\); their core weights are \((t,t)\) and \((3t,2t)\), where the candidate integers sum to \(2t\). Their disagreement is minimized when the selected candidates sum to \(t\). In the continuous problem, give the two corresponding types mass \(1/2\) each: \(D_\mu\) is a constant multiple of the paper’s disagreement, so the same subset is optimal exactly when the partition exists. There is an apparent one-word typo in the proof: it calls the constructed instance DISAGREEMENT, although the theorem statement and the proof’s minimization at \(s_Z=t\) establish hardness for AGREEMENT.

There is a second, distinct MNL mirror. Theorem 2, also proved in the paper, says DISAGREEMENT is NP-hard with one core item and two individuals having identical utilities on every candidate alternative. Its reduction is from SUBSET SUM: the two individuals’ core weights are \(2t\) and \(t/2\), while each candidate \(z\) has weight \(z\) for both. The disagreement is maximized when the selected candidates sum to \(t\). Assigning the two types mass \(1/2\) again makes the continuous objective a constant multiple of the original one. The corresponding MNL Population Disagreement problem is therefore precisely the maximization version of \(D_\mu\), on that restricted instance family.

For promotion, use a type-weighted version of the paper’s CDM objective. Each type has rational baseline utilities \(u_t(x)\) and context pulls \(p_t(z,x)\); after adding \(Z\), its context-dependent utility for a core option is
\[ v_t(x;Z)=u_t(x)+\sum_{z\in Z}p_t(z,x). \]
Since all core options share the same softmax denominator, a target is a strict favorite among \(C\) exactly when its \(v_t\) is strictly largest there. The continuous CDM Population Promotion problem takes a target \(x^\star\in C\) and asks for \(Z\subseteq B\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\, \mathbf 1\!\left[v_t(x^\star;Z)>v_t(x;Z) \text{ for every }x\in C\setminus\{x^\star\}\right]. \]
A solution is the chosen set \(Z\); the objective is the population share for whom the target is the strict favorite.

The anchor is Theorem 7 in Appendix A.1, proved in the paper: CDM PROMOTION is NP-hard even with two individuals and two core items. For a SUBSET SUM instance with target \(t\), the proof gives the two individuals baseline utilities \((t+\varepsilon,0)\) and \((\varepsilon,t)\) on \((x^\star,y)\), where \(0<\varepsilon<1\). Each candidate integer \(z\) adds pull \((0,z)\) for the first individual and \((z,0)\) for the second. If the selected candidates sum to \(s\), the first prefers \(x^\star\) iff \(s<t+\varepsilon\), and the second does so iff \(s>t-\varepsilon\). Because \(s\) is an integer, both prefer it exactly when \(s=t\). With equal masses, the optimum promotion mass is \(1\) if the subset exists and \(1/2\) otherwise. Thus the continuous mirror is hard even at \(\tau=2\); moreover, additive approximation error below \(1/4\) would distinguish the two cases.

The authors would have good reason to recognize these as their problems: they study interventions on a shared choice set, define group objectives from individual choice responses, and explicitly motivate large populations such as city residents and website visitors. The type parameters here are exactly the information their choice models use. The finite support is important: the claim is a regime with many people sharing a relatively small set of complete response profiles, not that every choice-model population must be high-multiplicity.

For all three exact optimization problems, I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). The reductions already use only two types; the combinatorics sit in selecting candidate alternatives, not in the number of people. Replicating each type many times changes the population size but not the set of feasible additions or which addition is optimal. The mirror still gives useful follow-up questions: how do additive approximation guarantees for MNL agreement or disagreement depend on \(\tau\) and \(\varepsilon\), and which CDM restrictions make population promotion tractable? The promotion reduction already rules out a small additive error guarantee for unrestricted CDM unless P=NP.

This case covers the paper’s AGREEMENT, DISAGREEMENT, and PROMOTION optimization problems, conditional on the choice-model parameters being given. It does not continuize model learning, time, or the outcome space.

My weakest point is that the paper’s formula sums over its set \(A\) of model components without explicit prevalence weights. A mass-weighted pair objective changes the question if those components are intended to count equally regardless of how many people they represent. I think the mirror is still defensible: the paper also frames its objective in terms of people and large groups, for which weighting by population share is the direct high-multiplicity interpretation. But that interpretation should be made explicit. The paper already models heterogeneous components; it does not already study these continuous population masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is about priority, not validity: these mirrors may add little that is specific to continuous optimization. In each one, the intervention is still a finite subset \(Z\) of candidate alternatives. Population masses change how the objective weights the finite set of response types; they do not create a new mass-allocation decision. That makes the mirrors look like weighted versions of the paper’s existing problems, with the same alternative-selection combinatorics.

That concern does not defeat Theorems 1 or 2. Their reductions use only two distinct response types, and assigning those types positive population masses gives a sensible high-multiplicity instance. The weighted disagreement objective is a natural interpretation for commuters or consumers grouped by complete choice behavior. The fact that the reduction’s difficulty lies in selecting alternatives is not a valid objection under this programme, and the mirror remains a well-posed computational question.

Theorem 7 is harder to dismiss. A population share whose members have the same utilities and context pulls is a coherent type, and the mass-weighted promotion objective directly measures what fraction of the population favors the target. Its two-type reduction carries over. The strict-favorite condition and discrete intervention set do not undermine the mirror; both have natural versions the authors could recognize.

There is a narrower reason to question the payoff. The paper’s algorithms already aggregate over model components, and the continuous versions appear to replace equal component contributions with prevalence weights. The examples make those weights plausible: the paper studies large groups and reports subpopulation splits. But that means the obstacle is not that the population regime is nonsensical or unavailable. It is that the resulting research programme may be a small weighted extension of existing algorithms, rather than a route to substantially different continuous-optimization machinery. The stated scope, however, includes computational questions over a distribution of types, and it treats hardness that persists under continuization as a useful boundary result. So this is a reason to rank the mirrors lower, not a reason to reject them.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case. The paper has named computational results, and the proposed large-population settings, complete behavioral types, and mass-weighted objectives all fit the programme’s definition. No sound objection here defeats all three anchors; the negative case is weak exactly where it would need to be strongest: the mirrors seem worthwhile to ask, even if their likely contribution is mainly a weighted high-multiplicity analysis.
