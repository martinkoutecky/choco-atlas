The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), resistances \(\alpha_t\in(0,1]\), baseline opinions \(s_t\in[0,1]\), a rational row-stochastic type-mixing matrix \(P\), and a target mass \(\beta\in[0,1]\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t=\beta\). Set \(\bar{s}_t(x)=s_t+(x_t/\mu_t)(1-s_t)\), \(R=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha)\), and \(M=(I-(I-R)P)^{-1}R\). Find an \(x\) maximizing the mean equilibrium opinion \(\mu^\top M\bar{s}(x)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type Friedkin–Johnsen mean-field model: agents in each type share resistance and baseline opinion, respond to type-average expressed opinions according to \(P\), and a mass \(x_t\) of each type receives the paper's all-or-nothing persuasion intervention. The objective is the population mean equilibrium opinion, a linear function of the intervention masses.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3's exact top-\(k\) influence selection objective under exchangeable type-average interactions; it does not directly carry over the graph-specific guarantees of Theorems 1–2, Theorem 3's stated runtime bound, or the empirical network-scale results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize when a finite graph with a cohort partition realizes the type-average dynamics exactly after within-type interventions.
  • Quantify how the optimal intervention changes when agents' opinions, resistances, or interaction patterns vary within a type.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a high multiplicity version of OPINIONMAX. The paper’s people become a large population partitioned into a small number \(\tau\) of exchangeable types. A type records an agent’s resistance \(\alpha_t\), baseline internal opinion \(s_t\), and peer mixing profile \(P_{t,*}\). Here \(P_{tu}\) is the share of type \(t\)’s attention that goes to type \(u\); when an agent listens to type \(u\), they receive the average expressed opinion of that type. Members of one type are interchangeable in every respect the model uses.

This fits, for example, a large platform or public campaign with millions of users grouped into a few dozen stable cohorts: members of each cohort have the same stance and susceptibility, and draw influence from the same proportions of other cohorts. The population size is large relative to the number of distinct types. The intervention remains the paper’s intervention: set selected agents’ internal opinions to \(1\). Only its scale changes from choosing \(k\) named people to choosing a mass \(\beta\) of the population. Each person still receives an all-or-nothing intervention; a type’s resulting average opinion can be fractional because only part of its mass is treated.

I would ask the authors to consider this precise problem:

Continuous Type OPINIONMAX. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), \(|T|=\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational resistances \(\alpha_t\in(0,1]\), rational baseline opinions \(s_t\in[0,1]\), a rational row-stochastic peer-mixing matrix \(P\), and a rational target mass \(\beta\in[0,1]\). Choose intervention masses \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\beta\). Type \(t\)’s post-intervention mean internal opinion is
\[ \bar{s}_t(x)=s_t+\frac{x_t}{\mu_t}(1-s_t). \]
Let \(R=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha)\) and \(M=(I-(I-R)P)^{-1}R\). The equilibrium type-average expressed opinions are \(z(x)=M\bar{s}(x)\). Find an \(x\) maximizing the society’s mean expressed opinion \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t(x)\).

This is the same equilibrium objective and the same choice of whom to persuade as in the paper; the mean just normalizes its total by population size. In fact, writing \(w_t=((\mu^\top M)_t/\mu_t)(1-s_t)\), the objective is a constant plus \(\sum_t w_t x_t\). So the problem is a bounded linear program, solvable by computing the type-level influence scores and allocating the target mass to the highest-scoring types, splitting mass at a tie. In the programme’s terms, I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable. The continuous question is not about fractional opinions as outcomes: it is about allocating intervention mass across a continuous population.

My anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It concerns the paper’s original OPINIONMAX problem: for a graph with \(n\) nodes and \(m\) edges, choose the size-\(k\) node set maximizing \(\sum_{i\in T}\Delta(i)\), where \(\Delta(i)=\rho_i(1-s(i))\). Algorithm 3 progressively refines estimates of these scores until it identifies an optimal set. The theorem states that, for sufficiently small error parameter \(\epsilon\), its running time is bounded by
\[ O\!\left(\frac{d^+_{\max}n}{\alpha_{\min}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon} +\frac{m}{k_{\mathrm{gap}}\alpha_{\min}}\right), \]
where \(d^+_{\max}\) is maximum out-degree, \(\alpha_{\min}\) is minimum resistance, and \(k_{\mathrm{gap}}\) is the difference between the \(k\)-th and \((k+1)\)-st largest true influence scores. This is my strongest anchor because it solves the exact underlying selection problem, rather than only estimating its answer. The paper notes that equal scores at the selection boundary can cause termination problems; the mass version can return any optimal split across tied types.

The theorem is an algorithmic anchor, not a claim that OPINIONMAX is in \(P\) under the usual bit-length measure: its stated bound depends on \(\alpha_{\min}^{-1}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{gap}}^{-1}\). The continuous type problem nevertheless has a direct polynomial-time solution in \(\tau\) and the rational input length. There is no named hardness result in the paper to anchor a hardness-transfer question. My scope here is just OPINIONMAX and its exact selection objective, not every algorithm or experiment in the paper.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem in a natural high multiplicity regime: it keeps the Friedkin–Johnsen dynamics, equilibrium aggregate, and persuasion action intact, while replacing a unit count with a population fraction. The weakest point is the exchangeability assumption. In the paper’s arbitrary graph, individuals can have distinct incoming influence and structural centrality; in this mirror, a type-level mixing model compresses that topology. The result is a credible mirror for cohort-structured societies, not a continuum limit of every social network. Useful follow-up questions would be how the type model approximates finite block networks, how robust the solution is to within-type variation, and whether tractability persists when the type space is represented succinctly rather than listed explicitly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that Theorem 3 solves a graph-specific problem: rank individual nodes by \(\Delta(i)=\rho_i(1-s(i))\), where each \(\rho_i\) reflects that node’s position in the full network. Its runtime bound also depends on \(1/k_{\mathrm{gap}}\) and \(1/\alpha_{\min}\), and the paper acknowledges termination trouble when boundary scores tie. So it is not a uniform polynomial-time complexity classification for OPINIONMAX.

The proposed type model does not follow exactly from merely grouping nodes with the same resistance, opinion, and proportions of neighbors in each type. After treating only part of a type, treated and untreated agents have different internal opinions; in an ordinary graph, their particular neighbors determine how those differences propagate. The type-level matrix \(P\) captures that process exactly only with stronger exchangeability assumptions, such as agents responding to type averages in a mean-field model. Preserve arbitrary graph interactions, and the exact types may need to distinguish nearly every node. Accept the type-average model, and the paper’s graph-specific selection problem has become a cohort-level variant.

That is a real fidelity objection to this version of the mirror, but it does not defeat the best version. A large campaign population with genuinely type-based interactions is a plausible setting, and the mass intervention preserves the paper’s objective and action. The authors could reasonably recognize it as their OPINIONMAX problem in an exchangeable regime. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case: the critique weakens the connection to Theorem 3’s network algorithm, but the continuous mirror itself survives.
