The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) schools, \(\tau\) complete student types specifying school rankings and school priority scores, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) that are multiples of \(h=1/n\), and per-capita capacities \(q_u\), does there exist a randomized threshold mechanism computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length, and the guarantee parameters? A replacement of one student changes mass \(h\) from one type to another; the threshold law should be \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-differentially private under such replacements, giving each student expected utility loss at most \(\varepsilon+\delta\) from truthful reporting. With probability at least \(1-\beta\), the induced mass allocation \(x_{t,u}\) should be feasible, \(\alpha\)-approximately stable, and school-dominant relative to a school-optimal stable mass allocation, using symmetric fractional treatment of tied cohorts at cutoffs; the per-capita capacities should satisfy \(q_u/h \ge \Omega\!\left(\frac{\sqrt m}{\varepsilon\alpha}\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(n,m,\frac1\delta,\frac1\beta\right)\right)\).

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case rests on one result, Theorem 6, proved in this paper in Section 5. It says that Private-DA-School(ε, δ) is \((ε,δ)\)-differentially private on students’ preference and score data, and therefore \((ε+δ)\)-approximately dominant-strategy truthful for students. With probability at least \(1-β\), it outputs admission thresholds inducing a feasible, \(α\)-approximately stable, school-dominant matching, provided every school’s capacity satisfies
\[ C_u=\Omega\!\left(\frac{\sqrt m}{εα}\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(n,m,\frac1δ,\frac1β\right)\right). \]
This is the paper’s main constructive result; it is not a polynomial-time classification, and I would not present it as one.

The convincing regime is a large centralized admissions round with many applicants but a relatively small number \(τ\) of recurring *complete* applicant types: each type specifies both the applicant’s school ranking and the scores by which schools rank them. For example, standardized application rankings and credential profiles could produce many applicants with the same type. The regime needs \(τ\ll n\); if each applicant has a unique school score profile, this high-multiplicity mirror loses its point. Schools remain finite in number, and their capacities grow with the applicant population.

I would ask the following as the continuous problem: High-Multiplicity Private Approximate School Matching. The input gives \(m\) schools, a finite set of \(τ\) complete student types, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, and per-capita capacities \(q_u\). Let \(h=1/n\) be the mass of one applicant, so each \(\mu_t\) is a multiple of \(h\), and \(q_u/h\) is school \(u\)’s discrete capacity. Types carry strict rankings over schools and school priority scores; members of one type are interchangeable to the schools, with tied cohort mass divisible at a cutoff. The type support can be small even when \(n\) is large.

Given this market and parameters \(ε,δ,α,β\), compute a randomized threshold mechanism whose output induces an allocation \(x_{t,u}\) of type mass to schools. Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_u x_{t,u}+x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,u}\le q_u. \]
The allocation must have no blocking pairs with filled seats, and any school below \((1-α)q_u\) occupancy must have no acceptable student mass that prefers it to its assignment—the mass version of the paper’s \(α\)-approximate stability. It must also be school-dominant relative to the school-optimal exactly stable allocation \(x^*\): if \(x_{t,u}>x^*_{t,u}\) and \(x^*_{t',u}>x_{t',u}\), then school \(u\) must prefer \(t\) to \(t'\).

The mechanism’s threshold law must be \((ε,δ)\)-differentially private when one report replaces mass \(h\) of type \(t\) by mass \(h\) of type \(t'\). Consequently, a marked applicant’s expected utility gain from misreporting is at most \(ε+δ\), for every cardinal utility consistent with their ranking. With probability at least \(1-β\), the induced allocation must meet the matching guarantees above. The translated capacity promise is
\[ \frac{q_u}{h}\ge \Omega\!\left(\frac{\sqrt m}{εα} \operatorname{polylog}\!\left(n,m,\frac1δ,\frac1β\right)\right). \]
A computational version would ask for a sampler running in time polynomial in \(m,τ\), the input bit length, and the guarantee parameters. That runtime target is part of the proposed mirror, not a bound established by Theorem 6.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). The type-level allocation has only \(τm\) mass variables, and the stability conditions have a direct aggregate interpretation. A type-aggregated deferred-acceptance procedure with noise calibrated to a one-applicant mass change looks like a plausible route. The result would preserve the paper’s central task—computing an approximately stable, school-favoring matching while protecting student incentives—while asking whether the computation can be done in the high-multiplicity representation. It does not continuize the schools or the outcome space.

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s algorithmic guarantee only. It does not claim a hardness result, and the paper offers no named computational hardness result here to prefer as an anchor.

The soft spot is truthfulness in the strict atomless limit: if \(h=0\), an individual changes no mass, so truthful reporting becomes automatic for any anonymous mechanism. Keeping \(h\) makes the finite-population incentive guarantee meaningful while the society and allocation are represented by continuous masses. There is a second, related cost: the low-\(τ\) regime requires repeated score profiles and divisible treatment of tied cohort mass. The paper allows arbitrary individual scores, so its theorem does not itself supply this high-multiplicity algorithm. Still, those are explicit features of a plausible admissions regime, and the problem being mirrored remains the paper’s matching problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is a legitimate algorithmic anchor: it gives a mechanism and proves privacy, approximate truthfulness, stability, and school dominance. So “there is no computational result to mirror” does not apply.

The strongest objection is that its central guarantee is about a *particular student’s* report. The paper gives each student a score at every school and assumes scores are unique. If a type is complete for the mechanism, those scores distinguish students: in the paper’s stated model, a low-support type distribution generally cannot represent the market. Erasing those distinctions to form cohorts changes the school priorities. In the atomless limit, an individual’s report changes no mass, so truthfulness becomes automatic and no longer captures the theorem’s privacy-based incentive result.

The proponent’s \(h=1/n\) repair restores a meaningful individual deviation, but it makes the atom size essential: the same distribution \(\mu\) can represent populations with different \(n\), and hence different sensitivity and capacity guarantees. The resulting question is a high-multiplicity encoding of a finite mechanism. That is within ChoCo’s scope, but it does not by itself establish a useful algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and input bit length.

This weakens the proposed mirror most when it claims to preserve the paper’s worst-case model. But it does not defeat the best version. Admissions with repeated score profiles and tied priorities are plausible; an exchangeable lottery or fractional treatment at a cohort cutoff could give a recognizable mass-based matching problem with nontrivial finite-\(h\) incentives. That would change the strict-priority model, but the paper’s authors could reasonably recognize it as their matching problem. A compressed private mechanism for that regime could be worthwhile.

So the negative case is weak on the universal claim. It identifies a real fault line—atomless mass loses the individual incentive content, while exact unique priorities destroy type compression—but it cannot rule out a worthwhile high-multiplicity mirror with grouped priorities.
