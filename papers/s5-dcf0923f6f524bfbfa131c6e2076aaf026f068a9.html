The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a qualified yes: this paper supports a continuous-population mirror extremely naturally, although the direct mirror is also close to a prior-art collision because the paper already formulates its electorate as a distribution.

Take approval sets as voter types. For candidates \(C\), a type is \(A\subseteq C\), and a society is \(\mu\in\Delta(2^C)\), where \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of voters approving exactly \(A\). In a finite high-multiplicity realization, \(\mu_A=n_A/n\): there may be millions of voters but only \(\tau\ll n\) distinct approval types. This is plausible for large participatory-budgeting electorates, polling populations, or citizens’ assemblies in which many residents share approval patterns. The type is complete for this problem: voters with the same approval set are interchangeable.

The decision remains an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\). Population continuity appears only in the society and in the quota conditions. For example, the JR violation mass of an unchosen candidate \(c\) is

\[ \mu\bigl(\{A:c\in A,\ A\cap W=\varnothing\}\bigr). \]

The paper’s sampling model is then an observation-limited version of this continuous society: the rule sees iid samples \(A\sim\mu\), rather than the whole distribution.

My lead question is:

Continuous EJR\({}^{+}\) Committee Identification. Given \(C\), \(k\), \(\varepsilon>0\), \(\delta>0\), and sample access to an unknown society \(\mu\in\Delta(2^C)\), find a rule \(f\) and the smallest sample budget \(t\) such that, after observing \(t\) iid approval types, \(W=f(A_1,\ldots,A_t)\) satisfies

\[ \mu\bigl(\{A:c\in A,\ |A\cap W|<\ell\}\bigr) < \ell\left(\frac{1}{k+1+\varepsilon}\right) \]

for every \(c\notin W\) and every \(\ell\in[k]\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\), uniformly over all \(\mu\).

The anchor is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper. It states that Sample-Convergent-Search-PAV outputs an \(EJR_\varepsilon^{+}\) committee with probability at least \(1-\delta\), using

\[ O\!\left( \varepsilon^{-2}\log^{9}k\log\log k\log(m/\delta) \right) \]

samples. At the standard EJR\({}^{+}\) value \(\varepsilon=1/(k(k+1))\), this gives \(\widetilde O(k^{4}\log(m/\delta))\) samples. This is a genuine continuous-population question: mass replaces voter counts, while the committee itself remains discrete. The expected outcome is Class A in sample complexity, though not necessarily polynomial running time in \(m\), since the algorithm’s empirical PAV maximization can itself be expensive.

The second, complementary question is:

Continuous JR Sample Lower-Bound Problem. Given \(C\), \(k\), \(\varepsilon\), \(\delta\), and iid sample access to an unknown \(\mu\), determine the minimum worst-case \(t\) for a rule that outputs a committee \(W\) satisfying

\[ \mu\bigl(\{A:c\in A,\ A\cap W=\varnothing\}\bigr) < \frac{1}{k+1+\varepsilon} \]

for every \(c\notin W\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\).

The anchor is Theorem 5.7, also proved here. It states that there are constants \(c_1,c_2,c_3\) such that, for \(\varepsilon\le c_1/k\) and \(\delta\le c_2k^2\varepsilon^2\), no sample-based rule can \((t,\delta)\)-satisfy \(JR_\varepsilon\) when

\[ t\le c_3\varepsilon^{-2}k^{-1}. \]

The hard instances have \(m\) and \(1/\delta\) polynomial in \(k\). At the Hare-JR value \(\varepsilon=1/(k(k+1))\), this yields an \(\Omega(k^3)\) lower bound. Crucially, the construction uses only \(k+1\) singleton approval types, so it remains hard in an especially strong high-multiplicity regime: many voters repeat a very small number of complete types. I would classify this as an information-theoretic hardness-transfer result, not continuum-specific hardness. Replicating each type many times gives finite electorates, and the paper’s sampling-with/without-replacement discussion supports the finite-to-continuous bridge.

The authors would plainly recognise both questions as theirs rather than as a weakened surrogate. Their central notation is already \(E=(C,P,k)\), with \(P\) a distribution over approval sets, and their rules are explicitly evaluated against the underlying population distribution. The continuous mirror therefore preserves the substantive problem—proportional representation of an electorate—rather than replacing it with fractional committees or a different outcome space.

The weakness is equally plain: the paper is already, in a substantial sense, a continuous-population paper. A direct mirror of its sample-complexity model would largely restate its own formulation, so I would not present this as a novel continuization discovered by ChoCo. The positive case is instead that the paper supplies unusually strong evidence that this is a sensible high-multiplicity computational regime, with both constructive upper bounds and sharp lower-bound phenomena. The remaining ChoCo questions would be to separate full-information continuous optimization from sample-access complexity, determine exact running-time complexity for explicit \(\mu\), close the gap around the \(k^3\) JR bound, and identify whether relaxed axioms such as \(\alpha\)-JR admit genuinely different continuous algorithms.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong here because the collision the proponent admits is not incidental: the paper already is the continuous-population model.

Its election is explicitly \(E=(C,P,k)\), where \(P\in\Delta(2^C)\) is the population distribution over approval types. JR and EJR\({}^{+}\) are defined directly by masses under \(P\), and the algorithm receives samples from \(P\). Replacing \(P\) by \(\mu\) changes notation, not the problem. This is stronger than prior high-multiplicity work supporting a mirror: the paper’s central model is already a distribution over voter types.

Theorem 4.3 therefore cannot anchor an independent continuous mirror. If the proposed mirror retains sample access to an unknown \(\mu\), it is exactly the theorem’s question: how many samples from \(P\) suffice to output a committee satisfying the population axiom? If \(\mu\) is instead supplied explicitly, the sample-complexity question disappears. One obtains the ordinary full-information approval-election problem, which the paper itself has already formulated. For rational \(\mu\), multiplying by a common denominator produces an ordinary finite profile with the same JR and EJR\({}^{+}\) inequalities. Allowing arbitrary real masses does not create a better mirror: without an encoding or oracle model, it is not a computational input.

The theorem also does not secretly provide a different full-information computational result. Sample-Convergent-Search-PAV estimates PAV scores from observations and proves correctness relative to the hidden distribution. Its empirical maximization over committees is not a separate continuous-optimization formulation over the population. A full-information algorithm for constructing an EJR committee could certainly be worth studying, but it would be a new approval-based committee problem, not a continuization of Theorem 4.3.

Theorem 5.7 is even less capable of supporting a distinct mirror. Its lower bound is caused by statistical indistinguishability: the algorithm does not know which of \(k+1\) singleton approval types has the slightly larger mass. If the society \(\mu\) is explicitly given, the distinguished type is immediately visible and the lower bound vanishes. If \(\mu\) is hidden and only samples are available, the proposed continuous question is precisely the theorem already proved. Replicating each type among many named voters does not create a new continuous problem; it merely realizes the same sampling distribution as a finite high-multiplicity electorate.

Nor can another type space rescue the mirror. For every result in this paper, the approval set \(A\subseteq C\) is already a complete type. Refining it with identities, demographics, locations, or latent attributes changes nothing unless those attributes affect the axiom or the algorithm; if they do, one has changed the problem. Coarsening the type space loses exactly the approval information on which JR, EJR\({}^{+}\), PAV, and the lower-bound instances depend. Participatory budgeting, polling, and citizens’ assemblies provide convincing applications, but they do not supply a new mathematical population model.

Thus the paper is valuable evidence that distributional, high-multiplicity approval elections are sensible and fertile. It may even be useful background for ChoCo’s sample-access agenda. But it offers no worthwhile continuous mirror in the programme’s sense: the sampling version is already continuous in the paper, while the explicit-distribution version is merely its full-information formulation or an ordinary finite profile written in normalized units. The only way to obtain a genuinely different mirror would be to change the computational question, the information model, or the outcome problem—at which point it would no longer mirror either named result.
