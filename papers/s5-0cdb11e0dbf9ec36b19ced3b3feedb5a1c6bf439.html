What the mirror covers

The proposed batch-sizing problem covers worker allocation and answer submission; it leaves the paper’s adaptive per-response stopping policy, worker tracking, EM learning, and experiments outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the response process define a well-specified posterior decision from limiting answer-label masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a continuous-cohort version of the controller. Picture a large annotation service whose workers fall into a small number of standardized profiles: each type has the same error parameter, task expertise and per-response cost. If the eligible workforce has \(N\) workers and \(\tau\) such profiles, a regime with \(N\gg\tau\) makes type mass a natural description. The type must include every worker attribute used by the model.

One possible problem is Cohort Batch Sizing for Open Questions. An instance gives a task, its current response history, a rational prior over its true answer, difficulty and bandwagon parameter, and a rational worker pool \(\mu\) over the finite type set \(T\). The answer space remains countably infinite, and responses follow the paper’s accuracy and Chinese Restaurant Process model. Choose masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each worker type to poll in one batch; observe the limiting response mass by answer label as the finite worker pool grows; then submit the posterior most likely answer. The objective is to maximize expected correct or incorrect answer utility, less the polling cost:
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ C_C\mathbf 1[\hat v=v]+C_W\mathbf 1[\hat v\ne v] -\sum_{t\in T}c_t x_t \right]. \]
This continuizes the workforce, not the answers. It captures a recognizable part of the paper’s question—how much crowd input is worth buying—but it is a restricted batch version of LazySusan, not the paper’s full adaptive controller.

There is a decisive limitation under your anchor rule: the paper has no qualifying named computational result. It presents LazySusan and an EM algorithm and reports experiments, including the 83.2% error reduction, but gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary or proposition establishing a complexity classification. The printed theorem is unnumbered and concerns when the response model can favor a common wrong answer; it says nothing about computational complexity. So I cannot honestly supply an anchor, restate one as a complexity result, or assign the proposed batch-sizing problem to Classes A, B or C from this paper.

The soft spot is substantial: cohort polling removes the paper’s individual ballot-by-ballot stopping decisions, and the continuum limit of its correlated-error process would itself need careful formalization. I regard the cohort model as a plausible research question the authors might recognize, not as a positive case established by one of their named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not identified a named computational result to mirror. The paper’s theorem concerns when the model makes a common wrong answer more likely than the correct one; it says nothing about computational complexity. The reported error reduction and EM accuracy are experimental results, not complexity results. LazySusan and EM are proposed algorithms, but the paper establishes no complexity classification for either. In fact, Section 4.4 says efficient solution of the infinite-horizon, dynamically growing POMDP is open, then uses bounded lookahead. The proposed batch-sizing problem is therefore a new problem inspired by the paper, not a continuous version of one of its computational results.

The best version of that proposal still loses the paper’s central decision: after each response, request another worker or stop, based on the ordered history and updated belief. Polling a mass of workers in one batch removes that adaptive control. Preserving it exposes a scaling problem. With \(N\) workers, polling a positive fraction \(x\) costs \(Ncx\) at the paper’s per-job cost \(c\), while the value of answering one task stays bounded. As \(N\) grows, the optimal queried fraction tends to zero; a finite number of useful ballots has vanishing mass. Scaling costs down by \(1/N\) could avoid that collapse, but it changes the paper’s per-response economics. Scaling task value with \(N\) instead describes a growing workload, a different optimization problem.

There is a further mismatch in the worker-tracking version. A worker’s past responses affect the estimate of that worker’s error parameter and thus future tasks. A type that captures this exactly must include the worker’s evolving history; its support can grow with the workload. A fixed, small set of baseline worker profiles avoids that problem only by simplifying away part of the paper’s learning model. Neither version supplies the missing complexity result.

This negative case has a limit: a large marketplace with repeated tasks and standardized worker profiles could support a sensible continuous workforce-allocation model. But that would be continuous crowdsourcing control, and the paper treats each task’s correct answer as latent ground truth rather than choosing among agents’ preferences or interests. It may be a worthwhile neighboring project; the paper does not establish it as a ChoCo mirror. The case against is strongest on that scope and anchoring gap, and weaker as a universal claim that no useful continuous crowdsourcing model could be built.
