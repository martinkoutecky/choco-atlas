The continuous mirror question

Given rational shared production data \(A,p,c\), a finite set of firm types \(T\) with endowments \(b_t\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and common-pool supply \(\rho\) per unit of population, assume \(d(\mu)\le\rho\). Compute rational per-firm payoffs \(u_t\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_t=v^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mu)\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\alpha_tu_t\ge v^{\mathrm{opt}}(\alpha)\) for every coalition mass vector \(\alpha\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\), where \(v^{\mathrm{opt}}(\alpha)=\operatorname{val}(\alpha;\min\{d(\alpha),\rho\})\), and \(\operatorname{val}(\alpha;z)\) and \(d(\alpha)\) are defined by the paper's production LP with aggregate private endowment \(\sum_t\alpha_tb_t\).

The model it lives in

Firms share the paper's technology, prices, and resource cost; each type \(t\) has per-firm private endowment \(b_t\) and mass \(\mu_t\). A coalition is any mass vector \(\alpha\le\mu\), the common-pool supply scales as \(\rho\) per unit mass, and the decision variables are type-level per-firm payoffs \(u_t\). An optimal dual solution for the grand-coalition production LP provides the natural certificate.

The objection that survived

The paper gives no runtime or complexity claim, so the computational character rests on treating its constructive dual LP proof as a computable result.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 18's optimistic-game core result when aggregate demand is at most the common-pool supply; it leaves the scarcity, pessimistic-game, and known-allotment partition-function cases aside.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest case is narrow. This paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires: no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifies a problem as tractable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest foothold is Theorem 18, proved here: for the optimistic LPP game \((N,v^{\mathrm{opt}})\), if the grand coalition’s optimal common-pool demand satisfies \(d_N\le r\), then its core is nonempty. Its proof constructs a core allocation from an optimal dual solution. That is an existence result with a constructive LP proof, not a complexity result, so I would not present it as a qualifying computational anchor.

As a prospective lead mirror of Theorem 18, I would ask for a Continuum Optimistic-Core Allocation. There are many small firms with the paper’s shared technology \(A\), prices \(p\), and resource cost \(c\). A type records a firm’s complete private-resource endowment \(b_t\). The input gives the finite support \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a common-pool supply \(\rho\) per capita. Thus a coalition containing mass \(\alpha_t\) of type \(t\) has private resources \(b(\alpha)=\sum_t\alpha_tb_t\). Define \(\operatorname{val}(\alpha;z)\) by the paper’s production LP for that resource bundle and common-pool allotment \(z\), and let \(d(\alpha)\) be the least allotment attaining the coalition’s maximum value. Its optimistic worth is
\[ v^{\mathrm{opt}}(\alpha)=\operatorname{val}\bigl(\alpha;\min\{d(\alpha),\rho\}\bigr). \]

Under the promise \(d(\mu)\le\rho\), the problem is: output rational per-firm payoffs \(u_t\), one for each type, such that
\[ \sum_t\mu_tu_t=v^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t\alpha_tu_t\ge v^{\mathrm{opt}}(\alpha) \quad\text{for every }\alpha\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]. \]
These are efficient, coalition-rational payoffs for every measurable coalition, including ones that split a type’s mass. The dual solution used in Theorem 18 supplies a natural certificate: give each firm of type \(t\) the payoff induced by its endowment and the dual resource prices. Solving the relevant rational LP should take polynomial time in the production dimensions, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. That is my expected Class A answer for this *prospective* problem, not a complexity claim made in the paper.

The regime is plausible: a large sector of firms can share a standardized production process and market prices while differing only in a small number of recurring resource bundles. Then the firm count \(n\) is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of endowment types, and the common-pool supply scales with sector size. This mirrors the paper’s own structure closely: its producers already share technology and prices, and their resource bundles are the source of heterogeneity. It continuizes the population; scaling the pool per capita is the accompanying normalization.

The scope is only Theorem 18’s optimistic-game, non-scarce-grand-coalition result. A natural next question is whether core existence or allocation remains tractable when \(d(\mu)>\rho\), including for the pessimistic game or the partition-function game when allotments are known. I would expect the stated lead problem to be tractable because the paper’s proof already builds its allocation from an LP dual.

The weakness is substantial: the paper never frames core allocation as a computational problem or analyzes its complexity, and this easy-regime dual construction is already available for finite firms. So the mirror may be recognizable to the authors and makes a clean high-multiplicity optimization question, but it does not provide the programme’s desired test of whether discrete hardness dissolves. Theorem 18 is the strongest honest foothold I see; it is not a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 18 proves that the optimistic game’s core is nonempty when \(d_N\le r\), using an optimal dual solution to construct a core allocation. The other main results likewise concern existence or stability. None states a computational problem or analyzes its complexity. The proposed task of outputting a type-level allocation is therefore an added algorithmic problem, not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper.

The endowment-type mirror is nevertheless well formed: firms could share technology and prices, differ among a small set of resource bundles, and face a common pool scaled to the sector. A mass-core allocation would be a sensible question. I would not reject it on multiplicity grounds. But it moves the work into computation for cooperative production games; the paper itself is about core existence, not the complexity of computing interventions or outcomes in social choice. The scarcity or unknown-allotment cases could motivate further research, but they would change the target rather than supply a missing computational result to continuize.

This negative case is narrow: the continuum question may be worthwhile to that neighboring field. Its weakness is that the paper’s dual construction gives a natural starting point for studying it. Still, under ChoCo’s stated criterion, a plausible continuous population is not enough when the paper offers no computational result to anchor the mirror.
