The closest candidate is Theorem 5.5, proved here: a nontrivial linear neutral cardinal welfare function satisfies IIA exactly when ballots have two composition levels. But that is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. The same is true of Theorem 5.9, also proved here, which characterizes when positional methods satisfy the strong majority criterion. Neither meets the required anchor standard.

A plausible population model would take each partially ranked ballot \(b\in C^\lambda\) as a voter type, with \(\mu_b\) the fraction of the electorate submitting it. For a positional rule with weight vector \(w\), candidate \(X\)’s score would be \(\sum_b \mu_b w(b(X))\). In a large electorate with relatively few distinct ballot types, one could ask whether the rule satisfies IIA or another criterion over all such distributions. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 5.5 characterizes when a neutral linear welfare function satisfies IIA; Theorem 5.9 does the same for the strong majority criterion. The other main results are also structural or axiomatic. None studies an algorithmic problem or classifies its complexity. Under ChoCo’s scope, that is decisive: a continuous-population complexity project would need to introduce a new computational problem, not continuize a result this paper establishes.

The proposed society of ballot types is sensible for a high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s real-valued profiles make that connection natural. But asking whether a rule satisfies IIA or another criterion over those distributions remains an axiomatic question. The paper’s geometric proofs also range over signed profile vectors and rotations of the full Euclidean space, while a society is a normalized distribution of nonnegative masses. That difference complicates a direct mirror, though it is not the main objection: even a clean restatement on the population simplex would still be outside the programme’s computational focus.

A stronger computational proposal could ask for a minimum-cost mass transfer that creates an IIA violation, or for a smallest-support witness. Those may be worthwhile questions. But the paper defines neither problem nor its costs, inputs, or objective, and proves no computational result about them. They would be new algorithmic extensions inspired by the paper, not continuous mirrors of its results. I can’t rule out that such extensions deserve study; I don’t see an anchored continuous-computational mirror here.
