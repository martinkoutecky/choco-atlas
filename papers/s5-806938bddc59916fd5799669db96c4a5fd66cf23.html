The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\) rankers, a finite set of behavioral types \(\mathcal T\), rational population masses \(\mu_t\), and unknown type-specific response laws \(q_t(i,j)\), design an adaptive policy over horizon \(T\). Each comparison samples a fresh hidden type according to \(\mu\), then returns a preference drawn from that type's law, giving aggregate preference probability \(p^\mu_{ij}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_tq_t(i,j)\). Assuming \(p^\mu_{ij}\ne \tfrac12\) for distinct rankers, bound the policy's expected cumulative regret relative to an aggregate Copeland winner, using the paper's regret definition.

The model it lives in

A society of session types \(\mathcal T\) carries masses \(\mu_t\); each type has a pairwise response law \(q_t\). The learner selects ranker pairs and observes responses from fresh hidden sessions, so its aggregate matrix is \(P^\mu\). The objective is low expected cumulative Copeland regret over \(T\) rounds.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers SCB's expected-regret guarantee in Theorem 13. It leaves CCB's regret results and the empirical comparisons unclaimed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a finite high-multiplicity cohort sampled without replacement, under what conditions does SCB retain a regret guarantee comparable to the iid population model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s online ranker-evaluation problem. The paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result; its named computational anchors are regret guarantees. The best anchor is Theorem 13, proved in the paper for Scalable Copeland Bandits (SCB, Algorithm 3).

The regime I have in mind is a large search platform evaluating \(K\) rankers across millions of user sessions. Sessions fall into \(\tau\) stable behavioral cohorts—perhaps defined by query intent and interaction pattern—with \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of sessions. A type is complete for this task: its members share the same stationary response law for every ranker pair. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of sessions in that cohort.

Here is the continuous problem: Continuous-Population Copeland Dueling Bandits. An instance consists of \(K\) rankers, a horizon \(T\), a finite type set with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and fixed type-specific pairwise response laws \(q_t(i,j)\), where \(q_t(i,j)+q_t(j,i)=1\). The learner does not know those laws; it can query a pair of rankers, after which a fresh session is sampled from the population and returns its preferred ranker. The sampled type is hidden. Thus the aggregate preference probability is
\[ p^\mu_{ij}=\sum_t \mu_t q_t(i,j). \]
Assume \(p^\mu_{ij}\ne \tfrac12\) for distinct rankers. At each discrete round, a policy chooses a pair to compare. Its solution is an adaptive policy whose expected cumulative regret is small, where the regret of comparing \(a_i\) and \(a_j\) is
\[ 2\,\mathrm{cpld}_\mu(a^\star)-\mathrm{cpld}_\mu(a_i)-\mathrm{cpld}_\mu(a_j). \]
Here \(a^\star\) is an aggregate Copeland winner and \(\mathrm{cpld}_\mu\) is normalized Copeland score under \(p^\mu\).

Theorem 13 states that, for a Copeland bandit instance with no pairwise ties, SCB’s expected regret is
\[ O\!\left(\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K \frac{H_i\bigl(1-\mathrm{cpld}(a_i)\bigr)}{\Delta_i^2}\log T\right), \]
and hence is also
\[ O\!\left(\frac{K(L_C+\log K)}{\Delta_{\min}^2}\log T\right). \]
In the theorem’s notation, \(\Delta_{ij}=|p_{ij}-\tfrac12|\), \(H_i=\sum_{j\ne i}1/\Delta_{ij}^2\), \(\Delta_i=\max\{\mathrm{cpld}(a_1)-\mathrm{cpld}(a_i),1/(K-1)\}\), \(\Delta_{\min}=\min_{i\ne j}\Delta_{ij}\), and \(L_C\) is the largest number of losses of any Copeland winner. The paper proves this result, deriving it from its arm-identification bound with the squaring trick.

I expect this continuous problem to be tractable in the paper’s regret sense. Each observed comparison is Bernoulli with mean \(p^\mu_{ij}\), so the mixture-of-types oracle has exactly the aggregate preference matrix used by SCB. The theorem’s bound carries over unchanged. The paper’s ranker-evaluation motivation—human pairwise feedback, click behavior, and interleaved comparisons—makes this population interpretation plausible to its authors: it gives a concrete source for their stationary preferences while preserving their arms, feedback, Copeland objective, and regret measure.

That inheritance is an immediate answer to the mirror question, not a novelty collision. The paper treats \(P\) as primitive; it does not formulate the user population as a distribution over behavioral types. The continuous question is well-posed even though its regret guarantee follows directly from the paper’s result.

The mirror covers SCB’s regret guarantee only. The horizon remains discrete, and the population is the continuous object. It does not extend the arm set or make the outcome space continuous.

The weakest point is that the type distribution disappears once it is aggregated into \(p^\mu\). SCB does not use \(\tau\), the masses, or type-specific structure, so this mirror supplies a population-level interpretation but no new continuous-optimization problem or complexity landscape. A more ambitious follow-up would ask whether visible cohort labels or type-targeted sampling improve the regret bound, and how the result relates to a large but finite cohort sampled without replacement. That would be a meaningful next question, but it is a modification of the paper’s feedback model rather than the cleanest mirror of Theorem 13.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 13 is the proponent’s sole anchor, and its proposed population disappears exactly when the paper’s preference matrix is formed. For each pair,
\[ p^\mu_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_tq_t(i,j). \]
If a fresh, hidden type is sampled at each comparison, the observed outcome is Bernoulli with that mean—the paper’s original oracle. The regret and SCB’s guarantee depend only on the resulting matrix \(P^\mu\), not on \(\tau\), the masses, or the type-level laws. Conversely, any preference matrix in the paper can be represented with one type. So this mirror gives Theorem 13 a plausible story about where its stochastic preferences come from, but supplies no population-dependent computational problem for the theorem to solve. The paper’s ranker-evaluation setup already estimates and simulates aggregate pairwise preferences from sampled queries and clicks; the proposed hidden mixture changes their interpretation, not what the learner observes or computes.

Making types visible or letting the platform choose which cohort to query is the strongest way to make the masses matter. But then the question becomes a type-conditioned or contextual dueling-bandit problem: the learner estimates several preference matrices and combines them using cohort weights. That may be a useful extension, but it changes the feedback and action model rather than furnishing a continuous-population mirror of SCB’s result. With finitely many cohorts, its added structure is a finite-context learning problem; the proposed anchor gives no reason to expect the continuous-optimization machinery ChoCo targets to be relevant.

The negative case is weakest at the motivating scenario. Large ranker-evaluation platforms plausibly do have many sessions in recurring behavioral cohorts, so the population story is sensible. If that story alone makes the reformulation worth recording, Theorem 13 survives as a mirror. My objection is narrower: in the stated mirror, the society is latent and fully aggregated before the computational question begins, so it is difficult to see what continuizing the population adds to this particular result.
