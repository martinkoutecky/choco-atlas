The case FOR (proponent)

A credible continuous setting is a large organization with many agents in a fixed set of roles: for example, workers grouped by skill, where agents in the same role have identical contribution opportunities. Let \(K\) be the roles and \(\mu_i\) the population share in role \(i\). A coalition is represented by its mass vector \(q\), with \(0\le q_i\le\mu_i\); its worth depends on the participation fractions \(y_i=q_i/\mu_i\). This makes population mass, rather than individual headcount, the natural object.

One precise follow-on problem is Continuous Shapley-Core Membership. Give a rational TU game \(g\) on the \(K\) roles, with \(g(\varnothing)=0\), and extend it to participation fractions by its multilinear extension
\[ F(y)=\sum_{S\subseteq K}g(S)\prod_{i\in S}y_i\prod_{j\notin S}(1-y_j). \]
The worth of coalition mass \(q\) is \(F(q_1/\mu_1,\ldots,q_K/\mu_K)\). Assign the role-\(i\) bloc the Aumann–Shapley amount
\[ A_i=\int_0^1 \frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i}(t\mathbf 1)\,dt. \]
The question is whether this allocation is in the continuum core: does
\[ \sum_i \frac{q_i}{\mu_i}A_i \ge F(q_1/\mu_1,\ldots,q_K/\mu_K) \]
hold for every feasible \(q\)? A solution is “yes,” or “no” with a violating coalition mass vector. Because the slack is multilinear, if it is negative anywhere on the box, it is negative at a corner. Thus it suffices to check coalitions consisting of whole role blocs. The \(A_i\) are exactly the finite game’s Shapley payoffs, so this construction gives a precise discrete-to-continuous dictionary for the core-stability question. With an explicit table of \(g(S)\), the check takes time polynomial in that table’s size; that does not imply a bound polynomial in \(K\) for a succinctly represented game.

The nearest named result is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that \(Sh(v)\in C(v)\) if and only if \(v\) decomposes as a linear combination of singleton unanimity games, with coefficients \(\alpha_i=Sh_i(v)\), plus a nonnegative linear combination of the games \(\tilde v_{B_k}^{-}\) associated with minimal balanced collections \(B_k\). This is a structural characterization of the Shapley-in-core property. It is not a complexity result or an algorithmic encoding. The paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to a parameterized complexity class. So, under your anchor rule, there are no eligible anchors; the problem above is a plausible computational follow-on from Theorem 4, not a complexity result this paper establishes.

The soft spot is the extension choice: the authors study arbitrary finite TU games, whereas this mirror assumes worth depends only on role participation fractions and uses a particular continuum extension. That is a meaningful high-multiplicity regime, but it narrows their model. The strongest positive claim is therefore that their core-stability question has a natural, exact population-continuum analogue—not that this paper has already charted its computational complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result. The paper’s Theorem 4 characterizes when the Shapley value belongs to the core by giving a decomposition; Theorems 1 and 3 and the corollaries likewise describe cones and their generators. None states a complexity bound or algorithmic classification. So there is no computational result here whose complexity landscape a continuous mirror could chart.

The proposed role model is coherent, but it adds structure the paper does not supply. A finite TU game gives worths at whole coalitions; it does not specify worths for fractional participation. The multilinear extension chooses those values, and a different mass-worth function \(V(q)\) could agree on every whole-role coalition while behaving differently between them. The proposed corner check shows that this particular choice reduces membership to the finite game’s coalition checks. That makes it a well-defined follow-on, but its computational content comes from the chosen extension and input representation, not from a computational claim in the paper.

A more general version could specify a sequence of symmetric games with many agents per type, converging to a worth function \(V(q)\), and ask whether the limiting Shapley-like allocation is in the continuum core. That is a plausible continuous computational question. But it would study a new family of games: this paper does not specify the type symmetry, scaling law, or limiting worth function needed to define it.

So the negative case is strong against calling any of these a mirror of a computational result in this paper. It is weak against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from the paper’s subject. Homogeneous populations with worth depending on type masses are a sensible regime, and a carefully specified model could be worth studying. I would not claim the universal negative is established.
