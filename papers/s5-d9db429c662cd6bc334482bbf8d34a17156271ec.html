The continuous mirror question

Given bipartite worker and slot types \(I\) and \(J\), allowed pairs \(E\), nonnegative rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), and rational surpluses \(w_{ij}\), choose matching masses \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) subject to \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\nu_j\). Let \(W\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\). Compute payoff rates \(u_i,v_j\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_i\mu_i u_i+\sum_j\nu_j v_j=W\) and \(u_i+v_j\geq w_{ij}\) for every allowed pair, with rates zero for nonessential types. Among these continuous core imputations, find one minimizing the largest essential-type rate, one maximizing the smallest essential-type rate, and one minimizing their spread.

The model it lives in

A capacitated bipartite matching market over finitely many types, where \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) are type masses, \(x_{ij}\) is mass matched between types, and \(u_i,v_j\) are per-unit payoff rates. The matching LP and its dual define the continuous core; fairness optimizes the maximum, minimum, or spread of rates across essential types.

What the mirror covers

Covers the three assignment-game fairness computations in Theorem 19. It leaves the paper’s other game characterizations and the max-flow fairness results in Theorem 20 outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • When masses are normalized discrete counts, do the continuous fairness optima agree with the symmetrized discrete optima?
  • How do essential types and fairness optima behave when a type is split into finer types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is Theorem 19, proved in this paper. It says that in the assignment game, an optimal solution to the displayed min-assignment LP is a min-max fair core imputation; an optimal solution to the max-assignment LP is a max-min fair core imputation; and an optimal solution to the equitable-assignment LP is an equitable core imputation. The theorem’s core set \(C(I)\) consists of core imputations arising from optimal dual solutions; for the assignment game, the paper’s dual characterization makes that the whole core. This is an algorithmic anchor: the paper supplies LPs that compute the underlying fair allocations, rather than merely encoding some other problem.

A plausible population regime is a large placement or hiring market. Each worker and vacancy slot can take part in at most one match, but many workers share a profile and many slots share a profile. A type records the attributes that determine compatibility and the surplus from each possible match. There may be hundreds of thousands of worker and slot units but only a modest number of types. That is close to the paper’s own motivation of matching categories of students to schools, while keeping the underlying question—the division of gains from matching—intact.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Fair Assignment-Core Selection. The input is finite sets of worker types \(I\) and slot types \(J\), allowed pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\), nonnegative rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) with total mass one, and rational match surpluses \(w_{ij}\) for \((i,j)\in E\). A solution specifies payoff rates \(u_i,v_j\) per unit of mass of each type. Aggregate matching is described by masses \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\nu_j\); let \(W\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\) over these matchings.

A payoff-rate vector is in the continuous core if \(\sum_i\mu_i u_i+\sum_j\nu_j v_j=W\), all rates are nonnegative, and every coalition is protected: for every choice of submasses \(0\leq a_i\leq\mu_i\), \(0\leq b_j\leq\nu_j\), its payments \(\sum_i a_i u_i+\sum_j b_j v_j\) are at least the maximum matching surplus those submasses could generate. In this assignment setting, that condition is equivalently expressed by \(u_i+v_j\geq w_{ij}\) for every allowed pair, together with the total-payment equality. The action variable is mass matched between types; no individual receives a fractional job. The continuous variables describe how a large cohort divides across possible matches.

Call a type essential if its entire mass is used in every maximum-surplus matching. The problem asks for three core payoff-rate vectors: one minimizing the largest rate among essential types, one maximizing the smallest such rate, and one minimizing the difference between the largest and smallest. Rates for nonessential types are zero, as in the assignment-game result. These are precisely the three fairness criteria in Theorem 19, translated to per-unit population mass. I would expect this mirror to be tractable by linear programming: compute \(W\), identify essential types by optimizing their matched mass over the maximum-value face, then solve the three fairness LPs. The expected running time is polynomial in the numbers of types and allowed pairs and the rational input bit length.

I think the authors would recognize this as their assignment-game question in a high-multiplicity regime. A type preserves every attribute that affects the problem, and its mass replaces a count of otherwise indistinguishable agents. The matching surplus, coalition-stability condition, and fair division of matching gains all remain. The mass-weighted total-payment constraint is the necessary co-modification when the population is represented by proportions.

The main weakness is that this is an extension of an already LP-tractable assignment game, not a case where discrete hardness dissolves. Also, the precise bridge between fairness over individual agents and fairness over type-level payoff rates deserves proof, especially under type refinement or unequal replication. Those limitations keep the case modest; they do not make the mirror a different problem. The paper’s related-work discussion mentions hardness results for core membership and the nucleolus, but those are cited results rather than numbered computational results of this paper, so I would not use them as anchors. Further questions include whether the three fairness optima agree with the symmetrized discrete optima when masses are \(n_t/n\), and how the answers change when a type is split into finer types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible attack on the proponent’s anchor, Theorem 19, is that its continuous mirror appears to be a direct capacitated version of the paper’s LPs. With worker and slot types, masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), and matching masses \(x_{ij}\), the primal is
\[ \max \sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}x_{ij} \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_j x_{ij}\leq\mu_i,\quad \sum_i x_{ij}\leq\nu_j,\quad x_{ij}\geq0. \]
Its dual gives payoff rates \(u_i,v_j\), with \(u_i+v_j\geq w_{ij}\) and total payments \(\sum_i\mu_i u_i+\sum_j\nu_jv_j=W\). The fairness LPs then follow by optimizing the maximum or minimum rate over essential types. Coalition protection for every choice of submasses follows from those pairwise dual inequalities. For rational masses, this is an ordinary polynomial-size LP in the listed types and allowed pairs.

That makes this a modest mirror: it seems to inherit the paper’s optimization structure directly, rather than expose a new computational obstacle. But that is a complaint about how much new machinery the answer may require, not a sound objection to asking the question. The populations in the proposed placement market can have many agents per type; rates preserve per-agent fairness; and the capacity-weighted equality is the natural mass version of total profit. Unequal masses do not break the model, and the authors’ category-based matching applications make the scenario recognizable.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The best negative case is that the result may be an immediate extension of Theorem 19, but under the programme’s stated standard, a well-posed mirror with a straightforward answer still counts. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is not supported here.
