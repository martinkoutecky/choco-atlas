The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind this programme requires as an anchor. Its theorems analyze equilibrium outcomes and popularity ratios; they do not classify an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. So I cannot honestly give a theorem-backed continuous computational question per anchor.

The closest results are Theorem 7, proved here: in plurality, the asymptotic best achievable ratio is at most \(2\), while the worst achievable ratio is unbounded. Theorem 8, also proved here: in approval, \(3/2 \leq \underline{\mathcal R} \leq \overline{\mathcal R} \leq 2\). Proposition 6 shows that with maximum out-degree two, the additive gap is unbounded for both rules. These are meaningful anchors for a study of equilibrium quality, but none asserts a complexity result.

There is a plausible *future* population-level angle: a large peer community choosing one member as chair or prize winner, with confirmation patterns shared in cohorts. A continuum confirmation network could represent the population and ask how popularity ratios behave. But the paper’s strategic problem depends on each voter being a distinct candidate who prefers himself, and on votes arriving in a finite order with a last voter and tie-breaking. In a nonatomic population, a single voter has zero effect on the vote totals and there is no last voter; grouping voters into strategic blocs would change the agents and their incentives. That makes the proposed continuum a new game-theoretic model, not a strong mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The positive case is therefore limited: the paper’s network setting makes a continuum extension imaginable, but it does not supply the computational anchor needed to make that extension a ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The first problem is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational result. Theorem 7 bounds best and worst equilibrium popularity ratios in plurality; Theorem 8 bounds them in approval; Proposition 6 shows an additive gap can grow without bound. They characterize outcomes, not the complexity of an algorithmic problem. One could newly ask how to compute achievable winners or optimize these ratios, but that would introduce a computational question the paper does not study.

The stronger obstacle is the population model. In this paper, every agent is also a candidate, prefers himself above everyone else, and acts at a particular place in a public voting order. Those features make identity relevant even for agents with the same confirmation pattern: each has a different self-candidate, and position can change strategic incentives. Treating them as one voter type therefore loses information the equilibrium question uses. Including identity and position in the type restores that information, but makes the support grow with the population rather than giving a high-multiplicity instance.

That problem affects each result, including the closest candidate for a mirror, Proposition 6. Its graph construction groups some agents by neighborhood, but those groups are not complete types under the paper’s incentives: their members remain distinct candidates with distinct positions. Theorem 7’s bad plurality equilibria likewise depend on ordered agents playing different strategic roles. Theorem 8’s approval construction uses a sequence of distinct roles and ballots over named candidates. Replacing each group with a mass that acts as one bloc could preserve some patterns, but it changes the game: blocs are no longer individuals who may win for themselves, and a bloc’s joint ballot is not the paper’s sequential individual vote.

The best rescue would be a large community choosing a representative of a cohort, or a continuum of voters choosing among a fixed set of candidates. Either could support a sensible population model. But both remove the paper’s defining feature that voters and candidates are the same set. A continuum of individually named candidates has another problem: each candidate has zero mass, so the paper’s popularity of the elected individual is not directly a type-level quantity. Adding candidate identity back into the model avoids that collapse only by giving up the high-multiplicity gain.

So the proponent’s equilibrium-quality angle is imaginable as a new line of work, but the paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and its equilibrium results resist a faithful high-multiplicity encoding. That is a strong case against spending ChoCo’s effort on this paper as a continuous computational mirror. It is not a proof that no useful related model can be devised; it is a judgment that the plausible rescues change the strategic object the paper studies.
