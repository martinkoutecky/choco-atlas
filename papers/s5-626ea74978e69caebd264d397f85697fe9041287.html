The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(\Theta\), nonzero valuation vectors \(v^\theta\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^3\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita supplies \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^3\), and an integer bundle cap \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^3\), decide whether and construct a rational allocation table \(x_{\theta,b}\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^3\) with \(b\le B\). Require \(\sum_b x_{\theta,b}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,b}x_{\theta,b}b=q\), and any prescribed fixed masses receive their specified bundles. Require envy-freeness: whenever \(x_{\theta,b}>0\) and \(x_{\theta',b'}>0\), \(v^\theta\cdot b\ge v^\theta\cdot b'\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support society of valuation types with rational masses, fixed per-capita supplies, and integral bundles bounded by \(B\); the decision variables are the masses \(x_{\theta,b}\) assigned to each type-bundle pair.

The objection that survived

The proposed support enumeration takes time polynomial in the bundle-menu size, which can be exponential in the input length when the cap \(B\) is binary encoded; moreover, Theorem 3's separator algorithm does not directly transfer to mass split among bundles.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's exact three-type envy-free allocation and partial-allocation extension question. It does not transfer the paper's separator proof or running-time guarantee, and it excludes chores and mixed valuations because the formal model specifies nonnegative valuation vectors.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(|\Theta|\), is the search problem polynomial in the binary input length, including the bundle cap \(B\), or only polynomial in the bundle-menu size?
  • What is the complexity when \(|\Theta|\) varies, and can the finite bundle cap be removed while preserving a well-posed population model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is Three-Type High-Multiplicity Envy-Free Extension. The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: given \(n\) agents with non-degenerate additive valuations, \(m\) items in three types, and a partial allocation to some agents, it finds an envy-free allocation extending that partial allocation, if one exists, in time \((nm)^{O(\sqrt n)}\). I read its valuation domain as the one specified in Section 2: nonzero vectors in \(\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^3\), using rational inputs for a computational formulation. The paper’s abstract also claims mixed valuations, but its formal definition restricts valuations to nonnegative vectors, so I would not include chores or mixed valuations in this mirror without clarification from the authors.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance has a finite set \(\Theta\) of agent types, each with a nonzero valuation vector \(v^\theta\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^3\), and rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\). It gives per-capita supplies \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^3\) and an integer bundle cap \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^3\). Each individual receives an integral bundle from
\[ \mathcal{B}=\{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^3: b_t\le B_t\text{ for each }t\}. \]
The cap preserves the finite-instance bound that nobody can receive more units of a type than exist in the whole inventory. The instance may also pin specified masses of agents to specified bundles, mirroring the paper’s partial allocations.

The question is whether there are rational masses \(x_{\theta,b}\ge 0\) assigning each type’s population to bundles such that \(\sum_b x_{\theta,b}=\mu_\theta\), the fixed masses receive their prescribed bundles, and \(\sum_{\theta,b}x_{\theta,b}b=q\). The allocation is envy-free exactly when, for every \(x_{\theta,b}>0\) and every \(x_{\theta',b'}>0\),
\[ v^\theta\cdot b\ \ge\ v^\theta\cdot b'. \]
A solution is the finite table of masses \(x_{\theta,b}\). Thus each unit of mass still receives an indivisible bundle; only the type-level averages can be fractional.

This is a population relaxation of the paper’s problem: for a finite instance, set \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/n\), \(q_t=m_t/n\), and \(B_t=m_t\). Discrete allocations embed by assigning mass \(1/n\) to each agent’s bundle. The mirror allows those type masses to be divided more finely, while preserving the paper’s three item categories, additive utility comparisons, exact envy-freeness, and partial-allocation extension. Types must include every feature relevant to the instance; in particular, fixed and free populations cannot be merged carelessly. This is a plausible regime for large provisioning systems with many users sharing a small number of utility profiles over three resource tiers. The paper itself studies a high-multiplicity setting, which supports this regime’s legitimacy rather than creating a novelty collision.

For the intended regime of many agents and relatively few types, I expect this problem to be Class A when \(\tau=|\Theta|\) is fixed, though I do not claim polynomial dependence on variable \(\tau\). The reason is that fractional masses turn the assignment counts into LP variables once the bundles in use are chosen. In three dimensions, each type’s distribution over bundles can be replaced by one supported on at most four of its original bundles while preserving its average bundle; removing other bundles cannot introduce envy. So the support search need only consider \(O(\tau)\) bundles, followed by an LP over their masses. With \(|\mathcal{B}|=\prod_t(B_t+1)\), this suggests an \(|\mathcal{B}|^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) algorithm, polynomial for fixed \(\tau\). Whether the dependence on \(\tau\) can be improved, or whether the unrestricted-\(\tau\) problem is hard, remains open. The paper contains no numbered hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer claim; its numbered main result is algorithmic.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s exact three-type allocation and extension problem, not every result or claim in the paper. Its soft spot is the integral bundle menu: fractional population masses do not make bundles divisible, and the EF condition depends on which bundles occur at all. The bounded bundle universe also retains a finite-instance parameter; removing that cap in a genuine scaling limit may change the problem. Those are substantive questions for the mirror, but they do not undermine the central analogy: the continuous object here is the population, while each member of that population still receives an integral bundle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 3: it is a named computational result for exact envy-free allocation, including extension of partial allocations. So the “no computational result” objection does not apply.

The strongest case against is that the theorem’s tractability story is tied to a finite set of individually represented agents. Its geometric construction treats agents as vertices of an envy graph and uses a separator of size \(O(\sqrt n)\). In a population model, the natural types are valuation vectors, and a type’s mass may be split among several bundles. That no longer gives one graph vertex per agent or the separator argument used here. The paper also makes no assumption that many agents have identical valuations. Its “high multiplicity” is principally the repeated item types, not repeated agents.

That undercuts any claim that Theorem 3’s algorithm or its structural explanation transfers to the continuous model. The proponent’s further claim of tractability is also unestablished: support selection is combinatorial, and enumerating bundles up to a binary-encoded cap \(B\) can take time exponential in \(\log B\). The proposed \(|\mathcal B|^{O(\tau)}\) bound is not polynomial in the input length as stated.

But neither point defeats the mirror itself. A society of repeated user profiles sharing three resource valuations is plausible; cohort masses and per-capita supplies give it a coherent finite-support model. Each person can still receive an integral bundle, while the cohort is divided among bundles. Fixed allocations can likewise be represented by pinned cohort masses. That is a legitimate continuous-population question, even if the paper’s separator algorithm says nothing about its answer. The item multiplicity in the paper does not make this population mirror already done.

So the negative case is weak where it must be strongest: it shows that the theorem’s technique may not survive continuization, but that is not a reason to reject a well-posed computational question. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
