What the mirror covers

No result receives a continuous computational mirror; Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 6.1 are equilibrium claims, while Propositions 2.1 and 2.2 concern aggregate risk aversion.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a computational follow-on, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s results. A plausible high-multiplicity regime would have many consumers sharing one of a relatively small number of complete types \(c=(u,e)\), where \(u\) is utility and \(e\) is the initial endowment. The mass of each type would be its population share. A natural computational task would be to find a market-clearing price and an allocation that gives each type a utility-maximizing bundle. With indivisible goods, the type-level average allocation may be fractional even though each consumer’s bundle is integral; that fits the programme’s allowance for co-modifying a model when population becomes continuous.

But the paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor that question. Theorem 4.1, proved here, says that under assumptions A0–A5 every strategic equilibrium has almost all agents leave in finite time with a bundle maximizing utility at a common Walrasian price. Theorem 6.1, also proved here, says that, given a Walrasian allocation and the stated maximizer condition, some strategy profile supports it as a strategic-equilibrium outcome. Both are equilibrium equivalence and existence results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. Propositions 2.1 and 2.2, proved here, connect concavity properties of utility-function covers to weak and strong aggregate risk aversion; they are not computational results either.

So I cannot give a qualifying continuous problem per anchor without inventing an anchor the paper does not provide. The most favorable evidence is that the paper’s equivalence theorems offer structure for a later algorithmic study of equilibrium computation. The main weakness is decisive for this task: the paper already takes the population to be a continuum, and it does not formulate or analyze the computational question that would make that continuum a mirror in ChoCo’s sense.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a continuous mirror starts with the missing anchor: the paper proves no computational complexity result. Theorem 4.1 characterizes strategic equilibrium outcomes; Theorem 6.1 shows that Walrasian allocations can be supported; Propositions 2.1 and 2.2 relate preference conditions to aggregate risk aversion. None classifies an algorithmic problem. Under ChoCo’s criteria, that is decisive.

The strongest proposed follow-on is to compute a market-clearing price and utility-maximizing allocation for a finite set of types \(c=(u,e)\). That could be worthwhile research, but it is not a mirror of a result in this paper. The paper already models an atomless population and describes its state through distributions over agent characteristics. Replacing named individuals with type masses would restate a feature of the model, while adding computation would pose a new question the paper never studies.

That distinction doesn’t mean the computational question is already solved: the equilibrium theorems are not algorithms or complexity classifications. But a tractable input model would need to specify how utility functions are represented, how divisible and indivisible goods are encoded, and which aspects of the infinite-horizon strategic game are to be computed. Those choices could define a valuable project; they do not supply a continuous mirror anchored in this paper. The negative case is therefore strong for this paper, though it does not rule out useful computational work built on its model.
