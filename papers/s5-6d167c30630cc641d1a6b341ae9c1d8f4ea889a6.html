What the mirror covers

The proposed mass model covers truth-tracking outcomes for PDW and CDW; it does not mirror Theorem 1's ordering characterization or Theorem 2's stochastic asymptotic results as computational findings.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of the paper’s large-population analysis, but it fails the programme’s required anchor test: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 compares PDW and CDW pointwise; Theorem 2, proved here, says that under its independent-error assumptions PDW is correct with probability tending to one, while CDW is correct with probability tending to one exactly when the individual’s conclusion competence \(v_g(v)\) exceeds \(1/2\). These are substantive results, but neither classifies a problem as tractable or hard, nor gives an algorithm. So neither can honestly serve as the required computational anchor.

The plausible regime is a large standing panel judging a fixed set of \(m\) issues, with many people sharing one of a relatively small number \(\tau\) of complete judgment vectors \(z\in\{0,1\}^m\). Let \(\mu_z\) be the fraction holding judgment vector \(z\), and let \(s^\star\) be the true premise vector. A precise candidate is Limit PDW/CDW Truth Tracking: given \(m\), a deduction rule \(g\), \(s^\star\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\), compute both continuous-majority outcomes and report which, if either, equals \(g(s^\star)\). For each issue \(j\), PDW applies \(g\) to the vector whose \(j\)-th coordinate is the majority judgment under \(\mu\); CDW takes the majority under \(\mu\) of the values \(g(z)\). Fix a tie rule as part of the instance. This asks the same truth-tracking question, with type mass replacing individual votes. The paper’s independent-error model gives the particular distribution
\[ \mu_z=v^{m-\|s^\star,z\|}(1-v)^{\|s^\star,z\|}, \]
so Theorem 2 provides the natural link to the continuum limit.

For an explicitly given support, this candidate is straightforward to compute by summing masses and evaluating \(g\). That makes it a weak computational contribution: the paper’s interesting condition \(v_g(v)>1/2\) concerns a distribution potentially spanning all \(2^m\) judgment vectors, and the paper does not analyze the complexity of evaluating that condition from a compact description of \(g\). The authors might recognize the finite-support problem as the deterministic population limit of their model, but I cannot claim they would recognize it as a computational problem they studied.

The main weakness, then, is decisive for this programme: the continuous-population connection is credible, but the paper supplies no qualifying complexity result to anchor a continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s results do not supply the computational anchor the programme needs. Theorem 1 characterizes a pointwise ordering between PDW and CDW. Theorem 2 gives an asymptotic truth-tracking condition and an expression for \(v_g(v)\). Neither asks for an algorithm or classifies computational complexity. The paper’s main contribution is about which procedure is more accurate, not how hard it is to compute a procedure’s outcome.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity setting: a large standing panel could contain many people with the same judgment vector. So the objection is not that mass is meaningless. But the proposed task—compute the two outcomes from a rational mass distribution \(\mu\)—adds a computational question to the paper. With the stated support-list input, it amounts to summing masses by issue, applying \(g\), and comparing the results. If instead one asks whether CDW’s asymptotic competence exceeds \(1/2\), the paper’s iid model gives a compactly described distribution over all \(2^m\) judgment vectors. Making that a complexity problem requires a succinct representation of \(g\); the resulting task is weighted model counting for that representation, which the paper neither specifies nor studies.

The continuous limit is credible, but it does not expose a computational problem already present in the paper. Theorem 2 has already analyzed the population-size limit; turning its probabilities into exact masses changes the representation without introducing a paper-grounded algorithmic question. That is the strongest case against the mirror. It is not airtight: the panel scenario is natural, and one could build a worthwhile complexity problem around succinct deduction rules. But that would be a new computational extension, rather than a continuization of a named computational result in this paper.
