The continuous mirror question

Given finite buyer types \(I\) and seller types \(J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\ge0\) with \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\), values \(v_{ij}\), budgets \(b_i\), and reserves \(r_j\), choose common type prices \(p_j\) and mass flows \(x_{ij}\ge0\). Require \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i\), \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\), and \(x_{ij}>0\Rightarrow r_j\le p_j\le\min\{b_i,v_{ij}\}\); if seller type \(j\) has leftover mass, require \(p_j=r_j\). Require core stability: whenever \(x_{ij}>0\), \(v_{ij}-p_j\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) for every \(k\) with \(p_k<b_i\); if type \(i\) has unmatched mass, require \(0\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) for every such \(k\). For a rational threshold \(K\), does such an outcome exist with \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j)\ge K\)?

The model it lives in

A two-sided assignment market with buyer-type mass \(\alpha_i\), seller-type supply \(\beta_j\), type-specific values, budgets, and reserves, and common category prices. The decision variables are mass flows \(x_{ij}\); each unit of mass receives one indivisible item, and the objective is surplus from trade over core-stable outcomes.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3’s surplus-threshold decision problem for repeated applicant and room types with common category prices; it leaves unrestricted seller-specific instances, the incentive-compatibility result, and the iterative-auction guarantees outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For unrestricted type counts, is the rational surplus-threshold decision problem tractable or continuum-specifically hard?
  • Does fixing the number of types permit an algorithm parameterized by \(|I|+|J|\) and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I would defend is a mass version of Maximum Surplus Budget Constrained Stable Bipartite Matching, the problem in Theorem 3. That theorem is proved in this paper: given \(n\) buyers and \(n\) sellers, buyer budgets \(b_i\), seller reserves \(r_j\), values \(v_i(j)\), and an integer \(k\), decide whether there is a core outcome \(\mu\) with \(\sum_i (v_i(\mu(i))-r_{\mu(i)})\ge k\). Theorem 3 says this decision problem is NP-complete. Its proof reduces Maximum Independent Set on cubic graphs to the surplus-maximizing core problem.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large housing market with many applicants and rooms. Applicants fall into a moderate number of types, each with the same complete value profile over room categories and the same hard budget. Rooms fall into categories with common reserve values, and many units belong to each category. There may be millions of applicants and rooms but far fewer applicant and room types. A type mass is a fraction of that side of the market; a matched mass \(x_{ij}\) means that fraction of buyer type \(i\) each receives one indivisible room of type \(j\). This continuizes the buyer and seller populations and their supplies. It does not give anyone a fractional room.

Here is the decision problem I would hand to a prover, which I would call \(\mathrm{MBSBM}_\infty\). The instance has finite buyer types \(I\) with masses \(\alpha_i\), values \(v_{ij}\), and hard budgets \(b_i\); and seller types \(J\) with supply masses \(\beta_j\) and reserve values \(r_j\). The binary-rational masses on each side sum to one, and the other numerical data are binary rationals. Use a common posted price \(p_j\) for units of seller type \(j\). A feasible assignment is a mass flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\). A matched mass must pay at least the reserve and no more than its buyers’ budgets or values: if \(x_{ij}>0\), then \(r_j\le p_j\le\min(b_i,v_{ij})\). If any supply of type \(j\) is left unmatched, its price is pinned to its reserve, \(p_j=r_j\).

Call the outcome core-stable if no positive-mass buyer cohort and seller type can block it. Concretely, for each used assignment \(x_{ij}>0\), the assigned buyers must get at least as much utility as from any seller type \(k\) whose price is strictly below their budget: \(v_{ij}-p_j\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) whenever \(p_k<b_i\). Any unmatched mass of buyer type \(i\) must have \(0\ge v_{ik}-p_k\) for every such \(k\). These conditions capture the paper’s pairwise core notion: when \(p_k<b_i\), a buyer who strictly preferred type \(k\) could offer the seller a small price increase and make both better off. The question is whether there is a feasible core-stable \((x,p)\) with
\[ \sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in J}x_{ij}(v_{ij}-r_j)\ge K, \]
for a given rational threshold \(K\). This is the paper’s surplus-from-trade objective, aggregated over mass.

This is a question about their assignment market: unit-demand buyers, hard budgets, seller reserves, pairwise core stability, and maximum surplus among core outcomes. The posted-price assumption is natural for repeated units of a standardized room category, and gives a precise market regime to study. It does restrict the paper’s more general ability to price individual sellers differently, so this mirror covers Theorem 3’s central problem in a homogeneous market regime; it does not claim to cover every instance of that theorem. It also does not try to continuize the paper’s incentive-compatibility result or its iterative auction.

For unrestricted numbers of types, I would tentatively expect this mirror to be continuum-specifically hard. Fractional matching flows remove the discrete allocation choices that the paper’s reduction exploits, so Theorem 3’s NP-hardness does not transfer by itself. But the type-level value matrix can still carry graph structure, and each seller price can lie on either side of many buyer budget thresholds. Those affordability choices remain combinatorial even when the agents are nonatomic. A new reduction would have to show that these choices preserve hardness despite fractional flows. A further question is whether the problem is tractable or fixed-parameter tractable in \(|I|+|J|\), with the type count and input bit length explicit.

The weakest point is the connection between the paper’s hard instances and a genuine high-multiplicity regime. Its cubic-graph gadgets use many distinct valuation profiles; the proof notes repeated budgets, but equal budgets alone do not make agents the same type. The number of full buyer and seller types may therefore grow with the constructed instance, and fractional allocation may erase the independent-set choices that make the discrete problem hard. That is a real limit on what can be claimed from Theorem 3. It does not make the mirror artificial: large assignment markets with repeated applicant and supply types are sensible objects, and deciding whether surplus maximization over their core outcomes becomes tractable is exactly the computational question this programme is meant to expose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 3’s hard instances do not exhibit high multiplicity. Its reduction builds a separate buyer and seller profile for each graph gadget, with values tied to specific neighboring gadgets. Under ChoCo’s definition of type, equal budgets are not enough: agents must be identical across every feature the problem uses. The proposed model therefore cannot treat those gadget agents as a few repeated types.

There is a sensible repair: a housing market with repeated applicant profiles and interchangeable rooms. But that gives a restricted, block structured market with category prices, whereas the theorem’s input permits distinct values for every buyer–seller pair and prices for individual sellers. To represent that full structure, types would have to preserve the distinctions that make the gadgets work. To keep few types, the mirror must instead study a different market regime. The proponent acknowledges this restriction; it is a real gap between the theorem and the proposed mirror, though the housing regime could still be worth studying on its own.

The common price for each seller category also narrows the model. A more faithful version could allow identical sellers to receive different prices, represented by mass at different prices. That seems possible, but it would add another outcome dimension without giving the paper’s theorem any high multiplicity support. Neither objection rules out a worthwhile project; they weaken the claim that this is a strong mirror of Theorem 3.

That is the honest limit of the negative case. The paper has a named computational result, and repeated applicants matched to repeated room categories are a plausible population. I can argue that the proposed mirror changes the instance regime and is not directly supported by the NP-completeness reduction. I cannot establish that no such regime is sensible or that its computational question is not worth asking.
