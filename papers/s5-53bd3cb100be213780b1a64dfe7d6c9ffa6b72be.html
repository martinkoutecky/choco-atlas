The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous mirror here, but the paper does not supply an anchor that meets your stated rule. Its numbered results are Theorem 1, proved here, which says ALG-GENERAL is equivalent to ALG-IDENTICAL when agents have identical valuations; Theorem 2, proved here, which gives a condition under which moving one good strictly increases NSW; and Theorem 3, proved here, which gives a condition for a strictly improving swap. None classifies a problem as hard or tractable. The paper’s APX-hardness statement is unnumbered, and the \(1.061\)-approximation guarantee is credited to Barman et al. [15], rather than proved as a numbered result here. So I cannot honestly present a compliant per-anchor question.

The closest numbered result for a positive case is Theorem 2: if good \(j\) moves from agent \(i\) to agent \(k\), with current utilities \(V_i,V_k\), and values \(v(i,j),v(k,j)\), then NSW strictly increases when
\[ V_i v(k,j)-V_k v(i,j)-v(k,j)v(i,j)>0. \]
That result is proved here and informs the paper’s neighborhood search. It gives a concrete link to allocation changes, though it is not a complexity result.

A credible setting for the mirror is a large pool of cloud-resource users, with many users sharing one of a small number of complete valuation types over standardized resource classes. Let \(T\) be those types, \(\mu_t\) the fraction of users of type \(t\), \(J\) the resource classes, \(q_j\) the available supply of class \(j\) per unit of population, and \(v_{tj}\) the additive value to type \(t\). Let \(\mathcal B\) be the finite set of permitted integral bundles. For \(b\in\mathcal B\), write \(u_t(b)=\sum_{j\in J}v_{tj}b_j\). A decision variable \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) users who receive bundle \(b\). The continuous NSW allocation problem is
\[ \max_{x\ge 0}\ \exp\!\left(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}x_{t,b}\log u_t(b)\right) \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}x_{t,b}=\mu_t\quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\quad(j\in J). \]
The population is continuous; each user’s bundle remains integral. As the number of users and repeated resource units grows with these proportions fixed, the model is the high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s NSW allocation problem. It keeps the additive utilities and geometric-mean objective, while scaling supplies per capita as the continuum requires. A regime with millions of users and relatively few valuation types is plausible, and the paper itself motivates NSW with computing-resource allocation. Authors could reasonably recognize this as their problem under high multiplicity.

The prover’s question would be whether an \(\epsilon\)-optimal solution to this Continuum NSW Configuration Allocation problem can be found in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau=|T|\), \(|J|\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), or whether hardness remains. I would expect tractability when the bundle menu or its pricing problem is manageable; for unrestricted bundles with variable \(|J|\), I would suspect continuum-specific hardness may remain in the pricing step. That boundary is also a natural further question: which bundle families admit efficient pricing, and when does the continuous optimum coincide with the limit of the discrete optima?

The main weakness is formal: this candidate is not anchored by a numbered complexity result, as requested. Its substantive weakness is that the mirror uses repeated resource classes, while the paper allows arbitrary distinct goods. That is a real restriction, but it describes a credible instance regime and preserves the paper’s allocation objective.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject this as a mirror of this paper, though the case against the proposed model is weaker than the case against its anchor. The proponent is right that the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational complexity result. The APX-hardness claim is background; the \(1.061\)-approximation is credited to prior work. The paper’s Theorem 1 is an equivalence between two greedy algorithms, while Theorems 2 and 3 give conditions for a particular move or swap to improve NSW. None supplies the computational result the proposed complexity question is supposed to mirror.

The configuration model is a reasonable high-multiplicity question in its own right. But it does not continue Theorem 2’s local-move result. In that model, moving mass between bundles changes the objective according to the log utilities of those bundles, subject to aggregate resource constraints. The theorem’s condition instead compares two named agents’ current utilities and their values for one particular good. Preserve a single-agent move in a continuum and its mass is zero; replace it with a positive mass transfer and the cross-agent condition has become a different optimization problem. The same mismatch applies to Theorem 3’s swap condition.

Nor does Theorem 1 rescue the connection: an equivalence of two finite algorithms under identical valuations is not a complexity classification. A continuum version would need a newly specified allocation procedure and would at most ask whether that procedural equivalence lifts to the chosen mass model. The paper’s EDA results are experimental comparisons, not a proved complexity guarantee.

So the strongest negative case is that the proposed configuration LP may be worth studying, but it is not anchored to a named computational result of this paper. I cannot honestly claim that the cloud-resource regime is implausible or that its continuous NSW question is worthless in itself. The negative case is strong on the paper-specific fit and weak on the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous population model exists.
