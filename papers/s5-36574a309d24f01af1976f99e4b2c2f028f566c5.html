The continuous mirror question

Given a bipartite type graph \(G=(I,J,E)\), positive rational type masses \(a_i,b_j\) with \(\sum_i a_i+\sum_j b_j=1\), and rational surpluses \(w_{ij}>0\), let \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) be a matching mass flow with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq a_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq b_j\), and let \(W^*\) be its maximum total surplus. Call a type essential if its full mass is matched in every maximum-surplus flow. A core payoff vector consists of nonnegative per-agent payoffs \(p_i,q_j\) satisfying \(p_i+q_j\geq w_{ij}\) for every edge and \(\sum_i a_i p_i+\sum_j b_j q_j=W^*\). For a common denominator \(D\) of the masses, list \(Da_i\) copies of \(p_i\) and \(Db_j\) copies of \(q_j\) for essential types, sort the list in nondecreasing order, and compute a core payoff vector whose list is lexicographically maximal.

The model it lives in

A two-sided assignment market with finitely many types carrying rational population mass, matching mass flows subject to type capacities, and per-agent core payoffs. The objective lexicographically maximizes the sorted payoff list with each essential type’s payoff repeated according to its mass.

What the mirror covers

Covers the leximin core imputation result in Theorems 8 and 9; leaves the leximax and min-spread algorithms and the nucleolus comparison aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mass-weighted leximin core payoff vector be computed in time polynomial in \(|E|\), the number of types \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), without expanding the masses into clones?
  • Can the paper’s structural characterization of essential agents and its primal-dual updates be adapted directly to unequal rational type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a narrow case for a continuous leximin core imputation of a two-sided assignment market. Imagine a large labor market with many workers and positions: workers share a small number of skill profiles, positions share a small number of job profiles, and two people of the same type have the same surplus from every possible match. The population may be enormous while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types stays modest. A type’s complete description includes its side of the market and its full surplus profile against the other side’s types.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. The input is a bipartite type graph \(G=(I,J,E)\), rational type masses \(a_i,b_j>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-match surpluses \(w_{ij}>0\). A matching is a mass flow \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq a_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq b_j\). Let \(W^*\) be the maximum total surplus. Call a type essential if its full mass is matched in every maximum-surplus flow.

A type-level core imputation assigns per-person payoffs \(p_i,q_j\geq 0\), with \(p_i+q_j\geq w_{ij}\) on every edge and
\[ \sum_i a_i p_i+\sum_j b_j q_j=W^*. \]
The question is to compute a core imputation that leximin-maximizes the payoffs of essential agents, counting each payoff according to the mass of its type. Precisely, take a common denominator \(D\) for all masses, form a list with \(D a_i\) copies of \(p_i\) for each essential \(i\) and \(D b_j\) copies of \(q_j\) for each essential \(j\), sort it in nondecreasing order, and lexicographically maximize that list. The solution is the exact per-type payoff vector; the matching flow and \(W^*\) provide its market certificate. Counting mass matters: giving each distinct type one vote would change the fairness question.

The named anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper. It says Algorithm 1 runs in strongly polynomial time, specifically \(O(mn^3)\), where \(m\) is the number of edges and \(n\) the number of agents. Theorem 8, also proved here, supplies the correctness claim: Algorithm 1 terminates with a leximin core imputation by clock time \(M/2\), where \(M\) is the largest edge weight. Together these establish an efficient algorithm for the finite assignment-game leximin problem that this mirror generalizes. The paper offers no comparable hardness result for this assignment-game fairness problem, so an algorithmic anchor is the right lead.

I expect the mirror to be tractable, in Class A, with a type-compressed algorithm polynomial in \(|E|,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. Rational masses give a formal bridge to finite high-multiplicity instances: multiplying by \(D\) creates integer numbers of identical agents, while the payoff list above counts exactly those agents. The assignment game’s matching structure and the paper’s primal-dual approach make a compressed algorithm plausible. The authors should recognize this as their problem: it keeps the same assignment surplus, coalition stability, and individual leximin fairness, while replacing unit populations by type masses and matching flows.

This case covers the paper’s leximin result, not its leximax or min-spread results, and it does not treat Theorem 6’s comparison with the nucleolus as a complexity anchor. The weakest point is the complexity gap: Theorem 9’s bound depends on the number of individual agents. Expanding rational masses can produce \(D\) agents, with \(D\) exponentially large in \(L\). The theorem therefore does not itself give polynomial time in the compressed input. A type-level algorithm must also handle mass-weighted fairness and balance payoff changes by type mass. That is a real algorithmic question, and the proposed mirror makes it precise.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proponent has not inherited Theorems 8 and 9’s running-time bound: \(O(mn^3)\) is polynomial in the number of named agents, while a rational-mass instance can expand to exponentially many clones. The mirror needs its own analysis in the type count and mass bit length. But that gap is the computational question the mirror poses; it does not defeat the question.

The proposed type model survives the stronger objections. With rational masses, scale them by a common denominator \(D\) and make that many identical agents of each type. Their aggregate matching is exactly a mass flow. The core constraints and total surplus scale with \(D\). Since the clone instance is symmetric within each type, its unique leximin core imputation must give identical payoffs to clones of the same type. Thus the mass-weighted payoff list preserves the paper’s individual leximin objective rather than discarding agent identity. A plausible large labor market supplies a high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 8’s correctness result together with Theorem 9’s strongly polynomial algorithm is a genuine computational anchor for this mirror ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11437)).

I can’t honestly make the universal negative case persuasive. The unresolved compressed-input bound is a substantive task, but the mirror itself is well-posed, preserves the paper’s stability and fairness questions, and has a credible population scenario. Any claim that it merely continuizes the outcome or loses individual fairness would miss the clone-equivalence argument.
