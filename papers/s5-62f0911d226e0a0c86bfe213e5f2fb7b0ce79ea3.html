The case FOR (proponent)

The honest limit is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind you asked for: no numbered theorem says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The nearest named result is Appendix Lemma 1, proved in this paper. It is a game-theoretic characterization, not a complexity result, so it cannot serve as a qualifying anchor.

Lemma 1 says: a player whose preferred policy is the status quo does not compromise on either policy or budget; majority players propose their preferred policy without transfers in all nine preference-and-status-quo cases; and minority players’ choices depend on the status quo. When it is at least as close to the minority preference as to the majority preference, the minority proposes its own policy without transfers (cases 2, 3, 6, and 9). When it favors the majority, the minority may pay the cheapest-to-compensate majority member, or switch to the majority policy if that transfer is too costly (cases 1, 4, 7, and 8). In cases 4 and 5 a middle policy can also be optimal: in case 5 without transfers, and in case 4 with a positive transfer to the cheapest majority member.

The concrete positive case I would still put before the authors is a continuous version of that policy-plus-budget proposal problem. Imagine a large party membership assembly deciding on one of three discrete policy endorsements and a per-capita fund for local chapters. A type records a member’s preferred policy and their money-equivalent disutility for each alternative, including the status quo. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the listed types, with many members but relatively few distinct types. A proposer offers a policy and targeted per-capita payments; the fund \(B\) scales with membership. Thus the population is continuous while the policy menu remains discrete.

Here is a precise problem. Given finite support \(T\), masses \(\mu_\theta\), policy disutilities \(d_\theta(y)\), status quo \(y_0\), a vote quota \(q>1/2\), per-capita budget \(B\), and proposer disutility \(d_p(y)\), choose a policy \(y\in\{L,M,R\}\) and fractions \(r_\theta\in[0,1]\) of each type to compensate. A voter of type \(\theta\) accepts if the payment offsets any extra policy disutility relative to the status quo. Define \(\Delta_\theta(y)=\max\{0,d_\theta(y)-d_\theta(y_0)\}\). Types for which \(d_\theta(y)\le d_\theta(y_0)\) support the proposal without payment. The problem is to maximize \(B-C-d_p(y)\), where \(C=\sum_{\theta:d_\theta(y)>d_\theta(y_0)}\mu_\theta r_\theta\Delta_\theta(y)\), subject to \(C\le B\) and enough mass voting yes:
\( \sum_{\theta:d_\theta(y)\le d_\theta(y_0)}\mu_\theta+\sum_{\theta:d_\theta(y)>d_\theta(y_0)}\mu_\theta r_\theta\ge q. \)
The solution is the proposed policy and the compensated mass by type. This is a bloc-scale, per-capita version of the paper’s proposer trading off its remaining budget against policy utility, with the same disagreement payoff and majority constraint.

For each of the three policies, the cheapest way to obtain the remaining votes is to compensate dissenting types in order of \(\Delta_\theta(y)\), partially paying the last type if needed. So this particular problem looks tractable (Class A): sorting the \(\tau\) listed types and comparing three policies takes polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). That is my derived algorithmic expectation, not a result proved in the paper. The model keeps policy indivisible and makes the population the continuous object.

The regime I find most credible is a large membership assembly with repeated preference-and-intensity profiles and a fund allocated among local chapters. Its headcount could be large relative to the number of complete types, while retaining the paper’s ideological choice, heterogeneous status-quo losses, budget transfers, and majority approval. The authors could reasonably recognize the coalition-cost question as theirs. The mirror covers the proposal and compensation subproblem behind Lemma 1; it does not mirror the paper’s experimental findings about induced preferences, voting thresholds, or agreement rates.

The weakest point is also substantial: the paper studies three named players, each able to propose, with one randomly selected proposal put to a vote. My mirror uses a large electorate and a proposer with a per-capita budget account. That is a defensible scale-up of the proposal problem, but it changes the institution, and the paper gives no computational anchor to show the authors already treat such a scale-up as their problem. Further questions would include whether the tractability survives type-specific payment costs, how the continuous solution approximates indivisible transfers in finite populations, and whether one can compute equilibrium when proposal power is endogenous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers one named result that could be used as an anchor: Appendix Lemma 1. It characterizes equilibrium proposals across a finite set of three-player cases; it does not state a computational problem or a complexity result. There is no complexity landscape here for continuization to chart. The paper’s contribution is experimental: it measures how naturally elicited political preferences shape bargaining in triads.

The proposed mass-transfer model is a reasonable new optimization problem, but it changes the game in ways that matter to that anchor. In the paper, each of three players submits a proposal, one is selected at random, and the players vote using thresholds they set in advance. The proposed model instead gives a designated proposer a per-capita fund and lets it buy enough support from a mass of voters. That captures coalition cost, but it removes the contest among proposers and replaces the paper’s voting behavior with a stipulated acceptance rule. The party assembly scenario makes this a plausible problem to study; it does not make it the computational version of Lemma 1.

A faithful scale-up would keep the three-seat committee and draw its members from a population distribution over complete types. Then one could aggregate results across committees. The distribution is continuous, but each bargaining interaction remains a finite triad; the resulting question concerns sampling committee compositions, not a society bargaining as a mass. If instead every member of an atomless population can propose and one proposal is selected uniformly, an individual proposer has probability zero of selection. Restoring meaningful proposal power requires changing the institution—for example, appointing an agenda setter or selecting among a finite number of representatives.

The negative case is strongest on the gap between the paper’s actual object and the proposed computational task, rather than on any claim that a large assembly is implausible. A large membership assembly could make a mass model worth studying, but the paper does not pose that institution or a computational question about it. So the proposed mirror does not survive as a mirror of a named computational result. The broader claim that no authors could find a worthwhile continuous follow-up is harder to establish: the assembly model may be a sensible new direction, even though the case that it belongs to this paper is weak.
