The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named computational anchors, so there is none to defeat. That is the paper’s central obstacle: its main claims are empirical and conceptual, not results about the complexity or algorithms of a computational problem.

The strongest candidate is the typology’s claim that results-oriented deliberation tends to produce viable projects, while process-oriented deliberation better ensures participants’ voices are heard. A continuous model could assign residents to preference or need types and measure the mass whose priorities appear in the resulting projects. But that would not mirror the claim. “Voices heard” depends on who speaks, when, and how facilitators and other participants respond; a type’s mass cannot represent those interactions. Adding a model of discussion, influence, and facilitator behavior would formalize a new research question, not continuize a computational result in this article. Nor does the claim specify an objective or decision rule that could supply an algorithmic problem.

The strongest alternative is to model project selection. Let \(\mu\) describe residents’ project preferences, impose a budget constraint on candidate projects, and ask which portfolio maximizes support—or how much mass must change preferences to alter the winner. That is a sensible computational question for participatory budgeting. But the article does not analyze such a rule or ask how to compute its output. Its central account is how small committees create and screen projects under bureaucratic constraints, and how facilitation shapes that process. A model of the final electorate would study a neighboring PB problem, not the paper’s result. A model of committee deliberation would have to preserve the small groups, distinct roles, attendance, and interpersonal dynamics that make the paper’s explanation work; treating those actors as interchangeable mass would discard the mechanism under study.

There is a real weakness in claiming that no scenario could produce a useful continuous PB model: city residents can naturally be described by preference shares, and continuous questions about final project votes may be worthwhile in their own right. But that possibility does not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper. The bridge from its findings to computation requires supplying the rule, objective, and formal problem the article never studies. On the programme’s terms, that is too large a step to count as an article-level mirror.
