The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for a ChoCo anchor. Its formal results concern equilibrium and payments, not whether a problem is in \(P\), hard, or fixed-parameter tractable. So I cannot honestly supply a qualifying continuous problem per anchor.

The closest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in this paper. Theorem 1 says that, for an estimate \(\delta=\delta^\ast+\beta\), choosing \(\alpha>R/(n\delta)\) makes truth-telling a strict equilibrium when \(\beta\leq0\), and a \(\beta R/(n\delta)\)-approximate equilibrium when \(\beta>0\). Theorem 2 says that the expected relative saving in that truthful equilibrium is at least \(P(1)-1/(n\delta)\). These are incentive and savings guarantees, not complexity classifications.

If an unanchored neighboring question is useful, the most plausible one is First-Order Type-Mass PTSC Calibration. Consider a large QoS-monitoring platform with \(N\) clients and a small support \(T\) of types, each type specifying clients’ binary signal and peer-belief distributions, PTSC prior estimates, and refund contract. The instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, with \(N\gg |T|\). Reports remain binary; the outcome is the mass reporting \(0\), and each client receives the paper’s outcome-dependent refund and PTSC side-payment.

Set \(\alpha_N=\lambda/N\). For each type, compare the expected utility from truth-telling with each nontruthful deterministic reporting strategy, and take the limit of \(N\) times that utility difference as \(N\to\infty\). The question is to find the infimum \(\lambda^\ast\) for which every type has a strictly positive first-order truth-telling margin, and the limiting relative saving under truth-telling. Under the paper’s positive-correlation assumptions, this is plausibly tractable: the incentive conditions reduce to finitely many rational inequalities across the \(\tau=|T|\) types. Theorem 1 supplies the nearest support for the calibration question, and Theorem 2 for its savings output. This candidate covers those equilibrium and savings claims only; it does not cover the experimental section or the paper’s other extensions.

A plausible regime is repeated, standardized service checks with many clients sharing a limited number of network, device, and signal-correlation profiles, and a common SLA refund rule. The paper already studies large \(n\) and defines its outcome as a fraction, which helps the analogy. The proposed mirror would make the *population* a rational distribution over complete client types; it would not treat the already fractional outcome as the continuization.

The main weakness is fundamental: in an ordinary atomless population, one client’s report changes the aggregate outcome by zero, so the outside incentive that the mechanism must overcome disappears. The \(1/N\) scaling and first-order payoff retain the finite-agent incentive effect, but they introduce an asymptotic criterion the paper does not study. Even if authors accepted it as a sensible extension, the resulting finite-type calibration appears computationally easy and lacks the required complexity anchor. This is a possible research lead, not an anchor-compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the decisive starting point: this paper gives no named computational problem whose complexity it classifies. Theorem 1 is an equilibrium condition, Theorem 2 a payment bound, and Theorems 3–4 extend those incentive and savings claims. Their proofs may involve calculation, but the paper poses no worst-case algorithmic question. Its experiments do not supply one either. A continuous computational problem would have to be added from outside the paper’s results.

There is a plausible population regime. Repeated QoS checks could involve many clients sharing a small support of types, with each type specifying signal beliefs, correlation, and refund terms. The fractional outcome \(o_w\) does not itself make the population continuous, and this setting can make type masses meaningful. The difficulty is that the paper’s key incentive depends on an individual’s ability to change that outcome. With \(n\) agents, one report changes it by \(1/n\), which gives the outside incentive a marginal effect of order \(R/n\). In an atomless society, one report changes the aggregate by zero.

That changes what the paper’s scaling result measures. Its sufficient scale is \(\alpha>R/(n\delta)\). If \(\alpha\) stays positive in the continuum, the peer-consistency reward can still favor truth-telling, but the outcome-dependent incentive has disappeared; the model has reduced to peer prediction without the feature this paper analyzes. If \(\alpha=\lambda/n\), the side-payment and each agent’s payoff margin also vanish. Multiplying the margin by \(n\) recovers a first-order coefficient, as the proponent proposes, but that coefficient retains the finite agent’s \(1/n\) influence as an extra asymptotic object. It is not an individual incentive in the atomless game.

The proposed calibration limit therefore seems mathematically coherent, but weak as a ChoCo problem. Under the paper’s assumptions, it reduces to a scalar threshold, essentially \(\lambda>R/\delta^\ast\), or the maximum of the corresponding ratios across types. The limiting saving is the type-mass average \(P(1)\). Both are computable directly from the support in \(O(\tau)\) operations. Keeping finite \(n\) gives the paper’s existing threshold and bound; optimizing a fixed binary-report payment menu would add finitely many incentive constraints and lead naturally to a small linear program. A harder formulation could be made by enlarging the mechanism family, report space, or belief-learning problem, but its complexity would come from those added choices, not from continuizing this population.

The same problem defeats the strongest alternative anchors in the paper. A positive mass \(f\) of honest agents is a natural continuum parameter, but the denial-equilibrium result again depends on the finite agent’s marginal effect on the aggregate. With unscaled peer rewards, the outcome-dependent conflict has vanished; with \(1/n\)-scaled rewards, the finite-agent correction returns. The honest-agent and savings extensions do not yield a separate computational problem.

So the negative case is strongest on research fit, not on whether one can write a sensible continuous model. The high-multiplicity QoS scenario is credible, and a first-order asymptotic incentive analysis might be worthwhile in mechanism design. I do not see a way to rule out every such reformulation. But the paper itself offers no computational result to mirror, and its best rescued candidate is a direct calculation that preserves finite-agent pivotality through an added scaling convention. That is too thin to justify a continuous computational mirror for this programme.
