The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror to. The unnumbered theorem in §3.2 claims Condorcet consistency, independence of binary comparisons, monotonicity, and Pareto consistency. Those are axiomatic properties, not results about complexity or algorithms. The paper itself says that studying the method’s computational complexity is future research. I therefore cannot honestly supply a numbered complexity anchor or restate one as required.

The strongest positive foothold is an unanchored population version of the proposed voting rule. Let a type \(t\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}^C\) specify one voter’s grades for all candidates, and let rational masses \(\mu_t\) sum to \(1\). For each candidate \(c\), eliminate \(c\) if at least half the mass assigns it grade \(1\); otherwise compute its weighted mean \(a_c=\sum_t\mu_t t(c)\) and mean absolute deviation \(d_c=\sum_t\mu_t|t(c)-a_c|\). Return the remaining candidates with lexicographically maximal \((a_c,-d_c)\), treating unresolved ties as co-winners. That last convention makes the question well-defined, though the paper’s instruction to repeat the process in a tie is not specified enough to reproduce exactly.

This is plausible for a large electorate using a shared grading rubric, where many voters give the same complete grade vector and \(N\gg\tau\). The decision is which candidate or candidates win; the rule first maximizes average grade, then minimizes deviation. Winner determination appears to take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), by aggregating the grade masses and computing these quantities. The authors would likely recognize the weighted rule as their method applied to population proportions.

But that is a natural continuous restatement of a voting rule, not a mirror of a computational result proved in the paper. It covers winner determination only, and the rule’s tie handling remains underspecified. That is the weakest point—and it leaves no valid anchor-based positive case under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result to mirror. Its §3.2 theorem claims axiomatic properties; it gives no algorithm or complexity bound. Section 4 explicitly leaves computational complexity as future work. A continuous study could be a new contribution, but it would not be a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proponent’s unanchored winner-determination question is the strongest candidate. It has a plausible population story: many voters may use the same grading rubric and give the same complete grade vector. Represent each grade vector by a type with rational mass. For each candidate, aggregate the mass at each grade to find a weighted median, then compute the mean and mean absolute deviation. With support size \(\tau\), this takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. The paper’s tie procedure is underspecified, but a natural tie convention repairs that without changing the population model.

That makes the question coherent, but it does not supply the missing anchor. A richer question—such as the minimum mass transfer needed to change the winner—could be worth studying, but it adds an intervention problem and transfer costs that the paper neither analyzes nor claims as a result. It would be a new problem inspired by the proposed rule, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational contribution.

The negative case is therefore strong on scope, but weak as a universal claim. I cannot honestly argue that no useful continuous-population question could be asked about this rule: the large-electorate regime is sensible, and the rule has a natural mass version. What the paper does not offer is a computational result for such a question to mirror.
