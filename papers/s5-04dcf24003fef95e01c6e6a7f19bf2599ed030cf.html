The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are about social-choice properties and impossibility: for example, Proposition 2.1 proves properties of L.P.R., and Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper, rules out a procedure satisfying non-disappointment and the Condorcet winner criterion for four or more alternatives. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The same is true of the other named propositions and corollaries. So there are no anchors here of the kind your brief requires.

The closest honest positive lead is a population version of computing the L.P.R. outcome, based on Proposition 2.1—but it is not a qualifying anchor. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking of the \(m\) candidates, and let the input be a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) supported rankings, with binary rational masses. Many residents sharing a ranking type is a plausible regime; the decision variable is none, and the task is to return the L.P.R. winner set: repeatedly tally the mass ranking each remaining candidate last, eliminate the candidate or candidates with the largest tally, and return those eliminated in the final round. The population version preserves the rule’s question directly. A straightforward implementation appears polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), so I would expect this computing task to be tractable, but the paper does not establish that complexity result.

The stronger-sounding lift of Theorem 3.1 would ask whether a social choice function on distributions over rankings can satisfy both the Condorcet winner criterion and non-disappointment. That is a natural population-level question, and the paper’s impossibility witness is a finite electorate that can be represented as a rational distribution. But it remains an axiomatic existence question, not the computational complexity question ChoCo studies.

So the positive case is limited: the paper offers a sensible continuous-population setting around L.P.R., but no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.1 and the numbered claims and propositions concern axioms and social-choice properties; none states a complexity bound for a computational problem. The paper defines L.P.R. and proves which properties it satisfies, but does not study the complexity of computing it. That leaves no result here for ChoCo’s complexity programme to mirror.

The L.P.R. outcome is the proponent’s best computational lead, and a continuous population of people with shared rankings is a sensible model. But computing the outcome is just repeated tallying: in each of at most \(m\) rounds, scan the \(\tau\) supported rankings, find each ranking’s last remaining candidate, and sum the corresponding masses. This takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). A stronger version might ask for the least costly mass transfer that changes the winner or creates social disappointment. That could be a worthwhile optimization problem, but it adds a cost model and an objective absent from the paper. It is a new research question inspired by L.P.R., not a continuous mirror of a result the paper establishes.

The strongest lift of Theorem 3.1 asks whether a social-choice function over distributions can satisfy both the Condorcet-winner criterion and non-disappointment. That remains an axiomatic existence question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Turning it into a search or robustness problem requires specifying how rules are represented, or adding a rule and a cost for changing the population. The theorem itself supplies none of that; its finite-profile witness does not turn the result into an algorithmic task.

So the negative case is strong that this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the large-electorate, few-ranking-types regime is entirely plausible, and a costed robustness problem around L.P.R. might merit study on its own. That possibility does not rescue the proposed mirrors as continuizations of this paper’s results.
