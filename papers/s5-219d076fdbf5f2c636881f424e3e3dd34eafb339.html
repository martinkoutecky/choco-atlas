The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of slot types \(D\) and a finite support of complete patient types \(t=(r,e)\), where \(r\) is a strict ranking over \(D\), \(e\in D\) is the initially endowed slot type, and the rational masses \(\mu_t\) sum to one, let \(q_d=\sum_{t:e(t)=d}\mu_t\). Find a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,d}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_d x_{t,d}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,d}=q_d\) that admits no blocking coalition: no selected submass can reallocate only its initially endowed slots so every member weakly improves and positive mass strictly improves. Determine whether the resulting aggregate assignment is unique.

The model it lives in

A one-specialty referral market with a continuum of patients and finite support over rankings and initial slot endowments. Slot supplies scale with patient mass; every patient receives one indivisible consult, while \(x_{t,d}\) records the fraction of type \(t\) assigned slot type \(d\).

The objection that survived

The mirror requires genuinely interchangeable, per-capita-scaled appointment slots, while the paper's motivating market gives patients preferences over individually named doctors.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's core membership and uniqueness claim; it leaves Theorem 1's DSIC claim, TOAM-IComP, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a core assignment and its uniqueness be computed in time polynomial in the number of slot types, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does a type-level top-trading-cycles procedure preserve core existence and uniqueness when masses split across types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. The closest named computational anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the paper: for the full-preference allocation problem, the allocation returned by TOAM is a core allocation and is the unique core allocation. Here, “core” means no coalition can trade its initially assigned doctors so every member is at least as well off and one is strictly better off. The paper’s \(O(kn^2)\) running-time claim appears in its prose, not in a numbered complexity theorem. I would use Theorem 2 as an algorithmic allocation anchor, without presenting it as an in-P result.

The lead mirror is Continuous Type-Core Allocation. For one specialty, let \(D\) be a finite set of interchangeable doctor-service types, and let \(r\) range over strict rankings of \(D\). An agent’s complete type is \((r,e)\): their ranking and the type \(e\) of the doctor they initially receive. The input is a finitely supported distribution of rational masses \(\mu_{r,e}\), summing to one. The initial supply of doctor type \(h\) is \(q_h=\sum_r\mu_{r,h}\).

A solution is a rational allocation matrix \(x_{r,e,d}\geq 0\), where \(x_{r,e,d}\) is the mass of type-\((r,e)\) patients assigned a doctor of type \(d\). It must allocate all patient mass and respect supply: \(\sum_d x_{r,e,d}=\mu_{r,e}\), and \(\sum_{r,e}x_{r,e,d}=q_d\). A coalition may select any submass from these assignments and reallocate only the doctor mass its members initially owned. The allocation is in the core if no such coalition can make every selected patient weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off, according to their rankings. The problem is to find a core allocation and determine whether its aggregate allocation matrix is unique. There is no scalar welfare objective; the target is core stability and, in the analogue of Theorem 2, uniqueness.

This has a credible high-multiplicity setting: a large e-health platform coordinating weekly referrals from many hospitals, with many patients and consult slots but a much smaller set of recurring patient rankings and doctor-service profiles. Doctor types could capture specialty, language, availability, and consultation mode; patient types capture rankings over those profiles and the initially assigned slot. As the number of hospitals and slots grows, the mass distribution can remain supported on relatively few complete types. The model treats each unit of mass as receiving an integral consult; fractional values in \(x\) describe what fraction of a patient type receives each doctor type.

I expect this problem to be Class A, though that is a research conjecture, not a consequence of the paper’s theorem. With finite support, checking whether a proposed \(x\) has a blocking coalition can be expressed as a finite mass-reallocation feasibility problem. The substantive question is whether core computation and uniqueness can be handled in time polynomial in \(|D|\), the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), without expanding rational masses into individual patients. A type-level TTC or flow algorithm is a plausible route. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s full-preference allocation result, category by category; it does not try to continueize the paper’s experiments or its incomplete-preference mechanism.

The weakest point is the doctor-type abstraction. The paper ranks named doctors, and a patient may value a particular physician’s reputation or identity. Grouping physicians into service types is faithful only in a platform where the relevant choices really are interchangeable consult slots or recurring profiles. If named-doctor distinctions drive the problem, the support may grow with the population and the high-multiplicity regime loses its force. Retaining initial endowments in the type definition and using a concrete cohort-based referral scenario makes the mirror recognizable as their core-allocation problem, but it does not remove that limitation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s anchor is only partly computational. Theorem 2 says TOAM’s output is the unique core allocation; the \(O(kn^2)\) bound appears in the surrounding prose, not as a complexity result. So the paper does not give ChoCo the usual target of a named theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. That weakens the case for prioritizing this mirror, though it does not rule out studying core computation.

The deeper obstacle is what the core is about. Each patient initially owns a particular doctor, and blocking trades reallocate those doctors among their owners. In the paper’s setting, doctors are scarce, individually identified experts. If a larger patient population shares a fixed pool of such doctors, each doctor’s supply vanishes relative to the population, so a mass allocation cannot represent the one-to-one matching the theorem concerns. If the doctor supply grows with the population, the model must replicate doctors or their appointment capacity. Keeping doctor identities then tends to make the patient types depend on a growing pool of named experts, undermining the hoped-for small support. Grouping doctors into interchangeable profiles can restore a compact type space, but it changes the object: patients rank service profiles, and the platform reallocates quantities of profile-level slots rather than particular doctors. The proposed type-core formulation makes that move explicit, but it does not preserve the identity-sensitive market that motivates the paper.

There is a plausible escape: a high-volume referral platform with recurring patient preference types and interchangeable appointment slots defined by specialty, language, and availability. That is a sensible continuous matching problem, and the authors might recognize it as a broader version of their model. It is also the best counterexample to a categorical negative verdict. Its weakness is that it depends on slots being genuinely interchangeable and scaling with demand; the paper instead emphasizes expert doctors whose identities matter and whose availability is scarce.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit and priority, not impossibility. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous core-allocation mirror in every scenario. The paper’s own framing leaves a narrow, credible typed-slot version open; the objection is that this version changes the resource being allocated enough that its connection to Theorem 2 is uncertain.
