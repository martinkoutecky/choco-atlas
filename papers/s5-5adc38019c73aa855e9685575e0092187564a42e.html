What the mirror covers

The attempted mirror targets only Lemma 3's linear-regret lower bound; it leaves the SBB and GBB regret guarantees, grid lemmas, and constrained-bandit result without proposed continuous-population questions.

The case FOR (proponent)

The only plausible lead I see is narrow: the paper’s Lemma 3, in the global budget balance section, says that for every algorithm in the class of globally budget balanced algorithms considered there, some joint valuation distribution \(\mathcal P\) forces regret at least \(\Omega(T)\). This is the paper’s own lower-bound claim, not a cited result. The supplied text gives its construction and intuition, and says a formal proof is in the appendix; that proof is not included in the excerpt. This is a learning lower bound, not an NP-hardness or other standard computational-complexity result.

A possible mirror is Finite-Type Global-Budget Learning. Take a known finite universe \(\Theta\) of complete one-market profiles \(\theta=(s,b_1,\ldots,b_n)\), with rational valuations of bit length at most \(L\). The society is a fixed rational mass vector \(\mu\) over \(\Theta\), with support size \(\tau\); the learner knows the support but not the masses. Each round samples a profile from \(\mu\). The learner chooses prices \((p_t,q_t)\), receives the paper’s asymmetric feedback, and earns that profile’s gain from trade and revenue. The benchmark is
\[ \operatorname{OPT}_\mu=\max_{p,q\in[0,1]}\operatorname{GFT}_\mu(p,q) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \operatorname{Rev}_\mu(p,q)\ge 0. \]
The question is whether a policy can guarantee sublinear regret against this benchmark while keeping cumulative realized revenue nonnegative with high probability, uniformly over \(\mu\). A solution is such a policy and its regret and revenue guarantees, or a lower bound ruling them out.

The regime would be a very large platform running many parallel one-seller, \(n\)-buyer trading opportunities, with many repeated valuation profiles: \(N\gg\tau\). Mass means the share of opportunities with each complete profile. This preserves the paper’s joint buyer-seller valuations, price actions, GFT objective, and GBB constraint. I would expect this finite-support version to be tractable, likely Class A: with the support known, the seller and buyer valuation thresholds are known, so the arbitrarily narrow, unknown interval in Lemma 3’s construction is no longer hidden in the price space. The unknown masses still need to be learned, and the exact rate and revenue guarantee are part of the question.

This mirrors only Lemma 3’s GBB price-learning problem. It does not cover the paper’s constructive regret bounds or its feedback-graph results. The weakest point is the population unit: mass here is over repeated market profiles, not individual voters or traders. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a platform’s batch of markets, but the authors may see it as a cohort of trading instances rather than a continuous society. Also, making the support known changes the source of hardness: the mirror tests whether the lower bound survives when multiplicity is aggregated but the profile thresholds are exposed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 3 is the only serious anchor, but it is a lower bound on online learnability, not computational complexity. Its \(\Omega(T)\) concerns how many rounds of partial feedback are needed when the valuation distribution is unknown; it says nothing about the running time of an algorithm on an explicit input. The other named results are also regret guarantees, feedback lemmas, or an auxiliary bandit theorem. None asserts the complexity of a computational social choice problem of the kind ChoCo is set up to continuize.

The proposed “Finite-Type Global-Budget Learning” does not bridge that gap. Its types are complete market profiles \((s,b_1,\ldots,b_n)\): each bundles a seller and a group of buyers, along with their joint information. Those are types of market opportunities, not types of individual agents. If the types are instead individual seller and buyer valuations, their masses alone do not preserve the joint distribution, buyer correlations, or the order statistics that determine trade and feedback. Retaining that information requires returning to profile types.

There is also a mismatch with ChoCo’s input model. The mirror makes the profile support known but the masses unknown. ChoCo’s society is supplied with its rational masses. With those masses supplied, the proposed unknown-distribution learning task is absent; with masses hidden and inferred from samples, the task is again statistical learning over rounds. Likewise, the paper’s GBB benchmark uses expected revenue, while the algorithm must manage realized cumulative revenue as it explores. Replacing rounds with a mass vector removes that temporal exploration and budget-management problem. Keeping it requires keeping the sequence of rounds, which makes time and sampling the central objects rather than a continuous agent population.

A static optimization over a known finite distribution of market profiles could be posed, but it would be a new question generated from the paper’s benchmark, not a mirror of Lemma 3’s result. The case against is strongest on that distinction: the paper studies learning from an unknown distribution over time, while ChoCo’s stated target is computation on a supplied continuous society.

The negative case is not airtight as a modeling claim. A platform with many repeated market opportunities could make mass over profiles sensible, and that could be worthwhile work on online constrained learning. But it continuizes the population of opportunities and preserves the paper’s time axis; under ChoCo’s present scope, no computational-result anchor survives.
