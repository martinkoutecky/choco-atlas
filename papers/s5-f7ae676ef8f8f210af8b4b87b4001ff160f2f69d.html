The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) resources of capacity \(1\), a finite support \(T\) of Leontief or satiable Leontief types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), per-capita demand vectors \(r_t\), and entitlements \(e_t>0\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t e_t=1\), compute a type-homogeneous allocation \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\). Require \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}\le 1\) for each resource \(j\), each \(x_t\) to be parsimonious, and for each type \(t\), either \(u_t(x_t)\) to be maximal or some resource \(j\) to satisfy \(\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s x_{sj}=1\) and \(x_{tj}\ge e_t\). Is this computable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the total binary input length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with finite support \(T\) over complete types \(t=(u_t,e_t)\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), and its members receive per-capita bundle \(x_t\), so total use of each resource is weighted by \(\mu_t\). In the market formulation, use \(\tau\) representative consumers with budgets \(\mu_t e_t\), aggregate bundles \(z_t=\mu_t x_t\), and utilities \(U_t(z_t)=u_t(z_t/\mu_t)\). There is no welfare objective; the task is to meet feasibility and the BBF conditions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4 for Leontief and satiable Leontief types, and the selected Leontief, \(L_1\)-norm case of Theorem 1. It does not establish the same computational guarantee for the paper's broader perfectly complementary utility class or address its incentive questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an encoding-preserving reduction and bit-complexity proof for exact BBF computation in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\) for rational Leontief and satiable Leontief types.
  • Determine which succinctly represented perfectly complementary utility types beyond Leontief preserve polynomial-time BBF computation after support compression.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest affirmative case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s resource-allocation problem. The population becomes a distribution over complete agent types; divisible resources and fairness remain exactly the objects the paper studies. This is a credible extension because the paper itself motivates the model with jobs and software agents competing for CPU, memory, bandwidth, and other resources.

Consider a scheduler serving a very large number \(N\) of recurring jobs over a long horizon. A type records the job’s full resource preferences and entitlement. For example, it might specify a Leontief demand vector \(r_t\) and entitlement \(e_t\). If there are \(\tau\) such profiles, with \(N\gg\tau\), write their population shares as rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(\sum_t\mu_t e_t=1\), and normalize resource capacity to one unit per unit mass. The total capacity for \(N\) jobs then scales with \(N\), as it should in this regime. An allocation \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^m_+\) is the per-job bundle for type \(t\); feasibility is \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le 1\) for each resource \(j\). A common bundle within each type is natural because its members have identical preferences and entitlements. Long-run service rates also make fractional bundles plausible in a scheduler.

This makes the *population* continuous: the input society is \(\mu\), and the algorithms work over its \(\tau\) supported types. The paper’s resources are already divisible, so that is not the proposed continuization. Clearing denominators gives \(n_t=N\mu_t\) identical agents of type \(t\), with capacities and entitlements scaled consistently. The mass representation can therefore describe a huge cohort without listing every job. The authors’ own computer-systems motivation makes this scenario recognizable as their problem.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in the paper using the Fisher-market computation of Codenotti and Varadarajan [3]. It states that a polynomial-time algorithm computes a Bottleneck-Based Fair allocation for every setup with Leontief or satiable Leontief utilities.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous BBF for Leontief Types. Its input is the rational type distribution \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), rational Leontief demand vectors \(r_t\) (optionally with the paper’s satiation caps), entitlements \(e_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t e_t=1\), and \(m\) resources of unit capacity. For each type, find a per-capita bundle \(x_t\), feasible in aggregate, such that \(x_t\) is parsimonious and either the type is satiated or there is a bottleneck resource \(j\) with \(\sum_s\mu_s x_{sj}=1\) and \(x_{tj}\ge e_t\). That is the paper’s BBF condition stated for a society of types and masses.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. One can aggregate each type into a representative market consumer with budget \(\mu_t e_t\), aggregate bundle \(z_t=\mu_t x_t\), and utility \(U_t(z_t)=u_t(z_t/\mu_t)\). For Leontief utilities, this is still a Leontief market with coefficients scaled by \(\mu_t\); the market computation then has \(\tau\) consumers rather than \(N\). The resulting BBF condition for the representative consumer is exactly the per-capita condition above. The paper’s theorem supplies the algorithmic anchor; the type aggregation gives the compressed high-multiplicity formulation.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that the paper’s algorithm outputs the \(\|\cdot\|\)-fair allocation under given entitlements for every continuous, strictly monotonic, perfectly complementary utility compatible with that norm. The surrounding text says the algorithm is polynomial for a broad subclass and strongly polynomial for Leontief utilities.

This gives Continuous GRF\(_1\): use the same rational type instance and feasible bundles, take Leontief utilities and the \(L_1\) norm, and find the type-homogeneous allocation that is fair under the paper’s Definition 10 when each type is expanded into \(N\mu_t\) identical agents for a common denominator \(N\). The fairness levels are \(\|x_t\|_1/e_t\); the expanded list repeats each level \(N\mu_t\) times. This defines the population version directly from the paper’s fairness notion. I also expect Class A here: the paper’s water-filling algorithm can process identical agents as a cohort, with masses weighting resource use. For an active set of types, the next common fairness level is determined by the first resource exhausted; for Leontief demands, the allocation step has a closed-form weighted capacity calculation.

Theorem 4 is my lead because it states an explicit polynomial-time result for a named fairness criterion. Theorem 1 broadens the case to a different fairness concept and a wider utility class. The paper offers no named hardness result, so there is no discrete hardness anchor here to dissolve or transfer. The further questions are whether the compressed algorithms have bit complexity polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the binary input length; how far the BBF computation extends beyond Leontief types; and whether the paper’s incentive questions remain meaningful for cohorts.

My weakest point is that these are high-multiplicity algorithmic extensions, not a new continuum-specific optimization engine. The masses weight aggregate feasibility, while the decisions are per-type resource bundles; this is not a mass-transfer problem or an exponential-column LP. Also, the original paper already gives polynomial algorithms in the number of individually listed agents, so the main computational gain is representing repeated profiles compactly, with running time tied to \(\tau\) and \(L\) rather than to \(N\). That limits the novelty claim, but it does not make the mirror a different problem: the paper’s agents, preferences, resources, and fairness tests all survive in the type-distribution model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about research value, not whether these mirrors can be defined. Both anchors seem to yield a routine compression of an already tractable allocation problem. For Theorem 4, a type of mass \(\mu_t\) can be represented by one market consumer with budget \(\mu_t e_t\), aggregate bundle \(z_t=\mu_t x_t\), and utility \(u_t(z_t/\mu_t)\). This preserves the market equilibrium and BBF conditions. For Theorem 1, the water-filling steps can likewise weight each type’s resource use by \(\mu_t\). In a finite-support model, neither mirror appears to introduce a new optimization structure.

That case does not defeat the better versions of either anchor. Jobs or tenants with recurring, shared resource profiles give a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and per-capita bundles preserve the individual fairness guarantees. Theorem 4’s market formulation and Theorem 1’s fairness algorithm are named computational results to mirror. The paper’s systems motivation makes it credible that the authors would recognize these population versions as their problem. If jobs instead have unique deadlines, locations, or demands, the fixed-support regime may be a poor fit—but that objection disappears for stable cohorts with a small number of shared profiles.

So the negative case is weak under the programme’s criteria. The mirrors may be straightforward and yield little new algorithmic machinery, but that is a claim about their likely payoff, not a fundamental reason the continuous-population questions are unworthy. I cannot honestly defeat either anchor in a way that rules out all plausible scenarios.
