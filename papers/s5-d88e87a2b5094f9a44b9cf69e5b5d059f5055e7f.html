The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of Proposition 18, second bullet. It states that, given an aggregation game with arbitrary goals, deciding whether there exist a transfer plan and a Nash equilibrium of the resulting game whose collective outcome satisfies every player’s goal is \(\Sigma^2_p\)-hard. The paper states the result here, but its proof derives the hardness from a Boolean-games result cited as Wooldridge et al., Proposition 6, together with the paper’s translation into aggregation games.

I would call the continuous problem Mass Efficient Negotiation Existence. Its instance is a finite set \(T\) of complete voter types and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type specifies its goal formula \(\gamma_t\), payoff function \(u_t\), and any role the aggregation rule uses. A rationally specified mass aggregator \(\bar F_\mu\) maps the ballot chosen by each type to a collective ballot; issue-wise mass quotas are a natural special case. The type’s members act as a binding bloc and cast one ballot \(b_t\in\{0,1\}^m\).

A transfer plan is a finite table of nonnegative rates \(\theta_{t,s}(b)\), contingent on the type-ballot profile \(b\), paid from type \(t\) to type \(s\) per unit of recipient mass. Type \(t\)’s payoff per member at \(b\) is

\[ u_t(b)+\sum_{s\ne t}\mu_s\theta_{s,t}(b)-\sum_{s\ne t}\mu_s\theta_{t,s}(b). \]

Preferences retain the paper’s priority: satisfying \(\gamma_t\) comes first, and payoff breaks ties. The question is whether there are a transfer table \(\theta\) and a profile \(b\) such that no type can improve by switching its bloc’s ballot, and \(\bar F_\mu(b)\) satisfies \(\gamma_t\) for every type with positive mass. A solution is the pair \((\theta,b)\).

A plausible regime is a large electorate organized into party caucuses, union chapters, or other delegations whose members share goals and payoffs and can be bound by a representative’s ballot. There may be many people but relatively few complete types; \(\mu_t\) records each bloc’s share of the electorate. Utility transfers are then rates across bloc populations. This gives the authors a recognizable question about negotiation engineering a goal-satisfying equilibrium, while making the society itself a distribution over types.

For the broad mass-aggregator version, I expect hardness to transfer. A finite instance embeds by giving each original player a type of mass \(1/n\), letting the mass aggregator reproduce the original aggregator on those types’ ballots, and scaling per-pair transfer rates by \(n\). The resulting type-level deviations and payoffs reproduce the original game, so Proposition 18’s existence problem is a special case. That embedding has \(\tau=n\); it does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is fixed or small. Whether the hardness persists for issue-wise majority, restricted goals, or genuinely low-support societies is a further question, as is how runtime depends on \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length.

The mirror covers this one equilibrium-and-efficiency existence question. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s full equilibrium-selection theory. The principal weakness is the bloc assumption: a standard atomless voter cannot change a majority outcome by a unilateral vote, so ordinary individual Nash equilibrium loses the pivotal incentives central to the paper. Treating each type’s mass as a coordinated actor preserves those incentives, but changes the deviation concept. Organized delegations make that a plausible setting; authors focused strictly on independent individual voters might reject it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 18, second bullet, is the proponent’s only anchor, and it is a real computational result. The trouble is preserving its strategic actors while making the population continuous.

In the proposed model, one ballot \(b_t\) controls all of type \(t\)’s mass. That makes each type a bloc that can commit its members to the same ballot. The paper’s players are individual voters: each chooses a ballot, and transfers are commitments one voter makes to other voters. Shared goals and payoffs make voters the same type; they do not make them a coordinated player. The finite embedding shows the distinction: to reproduce the original game exactly, it assigns a separate type to each original voter, so \(\tau=n\). It establishes a relabeling, but no compression of a repeated-type population.

Preserving independent decisions gives a different continuous model. A type must be allowed to split its mass across ballots. In an atomless society, a single voter’s deviation changes no ballot mass and cannot change the collective outcome. With zero transfers, every measurable ballot assignment is therefore a Nash equilibrium, including any assignment whose aggregate satisfies the goals. The existence question reduces to whether such a mass assignment is feasible; the negotiation mechanism no longer selects it. Restoring the paper’s transfer mechanism would require representing commitments between individual voters, and a transfer table over types and ballot profiles does not encode those relationships.

The delegation scenario is the strongest repair: parties or unions can bind their members to a representative’s ballot, and representatives can negotiate over transfers. That is a coherent weighted voting game. It also makes the bloc assumption substantive: the strategic players are organizations, and their voting weights reflect membership. If the authors accept that change of player, the mirror may be worth studying. I would present it as a delegation model, though, rather than as the high-multiplicity version of their individual-voter negotiation game.

So this anchor does not establish a worthwhile population continuization of the paper’s own mechanism: keeping voters independent removes pivotality in the limit, while preserving pivotal blocs changes who acts and negotiates. The negative case is still not airtight. An author who regards binding delegations as a natural instance of their negotiation problem could accept the mirror; I cannot honestly rule out that scenario.
