What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only direct verification of \(\mathrm{EJR}^+\) over approval-type masses; the axiomatic characterizations, witness framework, and earlier polynomial verification result remain without a computational mirror from this paper.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified one. The paper supports a natural continuous-population model, but it contains no qualifying computational anchor: none of its numbered Theorems, Lemmas, Propositions, or Corollaries asserts membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, parameterized tractability, or similar. Its only computational statement is unnumbered: that PJR+ and EJR+ can be verified in polynomial time, attributed to earlier work. Thus, strictly speaking, this is not yet a fully anchored ChoCo paper.

The best structural anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that if a proportionality notion \(f\) satisfies independence of losers, monotonicity, robustness to fully satisfied voters, independence of approval swaps, and lower quota for party-list instances, then \(f\subseteq\mathrm{EJR}^+\); moreover, \(\mathrm{EJR}^+\) itself satisfies all five axioms. This is the lead mirror, although it is axiomatic rather than computational.

A concrete continuous version would be the following.

Let \(C\) be a finite candidate set, \(k\) the committee size, and let the voter types be approval ballots \(T=2^C\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_A)_{A\in T}\), where \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of voters with approval set \(A\). The decision variable remains an integral committee \(W\in\binom{C}{k}\); only the population is continuized.

Call \(W\) continuously EJR+-valid if, for every \(c\notin W\) and every \(\ell\in[k]\), the mass of voters who approve \(c\) but approve fewer than \(\ell\) members of \(W\) is strictly below the \(\ell\)-seat quota: \(\mu(\{A:c\in A,\ |A\cap W|<\ell\})<\ell/k\).

The problem is:

“Continuous EJR+ Verification.” Given \((C,k,\mu,W)\), decide whether \(W\) satisfies this condition. A negative certificate is \(c\notin W\) and \(\ell\in[k]\) for which the displayed mass is at least \(\ell/k\).

This is a faithful high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s EJR+ question. A type is a complete approval description, and mass means the fraction of the electorate with that description. If all masses are rational, clear denominators using a population size divisible by \(k\); the resulting finite election has exactly the same EJR+ condition. The continuum therefore does not discard the paper’s group-based notion or replace committees by fractional outcomes.

The regime is plausible for the paper’s own civic-platform motivation: millions of users select among a fixed set of candidates or policy proposals, but many users follow recurring approval templates produced by parties, organizations, issue coalitions, or standard questionnaires. Thus \(n\gg\tau\), with \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}\mu|\) moderate. Proposition 1 supplies a useful sanity check: on party-list societies, all the paper’s notions coincide with lower quota, and the finite party masses \(n_x/n\) translate directly to \(\mu_x\).

I would expect Continuous EJR+ Verification to be in Class A for an explicit type distribution: scan every \(c\), \(\ell\), and type \(A\), requiring polynomially many rational-mass comparisons. Further questions include whether the full axiomatic characterization in Theorem 4 survives under mass-preserving profile transformations, whether a satisfying committee can be constructed efficiently, and how the continuous PJR+ analogue behaves.

The weakest point is decisive: this computational question is newly extracted from the paper; Theorem 4 itself does not establish a complexity result. So I would defend the existence and plausibility of a continuous mirror, but I would not claim that this paper, by itself, supplies the programme’s required named computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong because the paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 4 is an axiomatic characterization: it identifies EJR\(^+\) from lower quota and four invariance axioms. None of the paper’s numbered results establishes polynomial-time solvability, hardness, approximation, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic optimization problem. The statement that PJR\(^+\) and EJR\(^+\) can be verified in polynomial time is unnumbered and attributed to earlier work. Thus the paper does not provide the kind of computational result whose high-multiplicity behaviour ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed Continuous EJR\(^+\) Verification is mathematically legitimate, but that legitimacy is also its limitation. For each \(c\notin W\) and \(\ell\in[k]\), one simply computes

\[ M_{c,\ell} = \sum_{\substack{A\subseteq C\\c\in A,\ |A\cap W|<\ell}}\mu_A \]

and checks whether \(M_{c,\ell}<\ell/k\). This is the finite verification test with voter counts replaced by weighted sums. With an explicit support of \(\tau\) approval types, it is a direct scan in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length. It requires neither continuous optimization nor a separation oracle, and it exposes no new population-level computational phenomenon.

The exact denominator-clearing argument makes the problem a compressed representation of the finite test, not a result about a limiting society. That is not a defect in the model: the high-multiplicity regime is sensible, especially for a platform with recurring approval templates. But it means that the proposed question is an input-encoding reformulation of a known verification predicate, not a continuization of a computational theorem proved by this paper.

The axioms do not rescue the anchor. In the continuous setting, lower quota becomes the ordinary comparison \(\lfloor k\mu_x\rfloor\) on party masses; independence of losers, monotonicity, approval swaps, and robustness can all be restated as mass-preserving profile transformations. The proof of Theorem 4 then remains a combinatorial implication between profile properties. It contains no optimization over mass, no algorithmic bottleneck, and no complexity landscape to investigate. Proposition 1 is even less promising: on party-list societies it reduces to lower-quota arithmetic,

\[ |W\cap C_x| \ge \min\!\left(|C_x|,\left\lfloor k\mu_x\right\rfloor\right), \]

so it supplies a naturality check, not an independent computational mirror.

The strongest attempted rescue would be to ask for a satisfying committee rather than merely verify one. That could be a worthwhile new study of weighted committee construction, but it is not a continuous mirror of a named result here. The paper itself gives no complexity classification for that task, and the committee remains an integral \(k\)-subset of \(C\); only the voter data have been compressed into masses. Any substantive theorem would therefore be new work about committee selection, not a continuization of this paper’s contribution.

Likewise, giving \(\mu\) a succinct representation could make the mass query difficult, but then the difficulty comes from evaluating a succinct probability distribution over approval sets. It is not caused by continuizing the population. Making committees fractional would produce a potentially richer problem, but that continuizes the outcome space and leaves the paper’s scope.

So I would not object that the proposed electorate is implausible, nor that high multiplicity is somehow inappropriate. The regime is admissible. The problem is that every faithful version is a routine weighted restatement, while every version with real computational substance introduces a new selection, succinctness, or outcome-space problem absent from the paper. The honest negative conclusion is therefore not that no related continuous problem could ever be interesting; it is that this paper supplies no worthwhile ChoCo mirror under the programme’s computational-anchor standard.
