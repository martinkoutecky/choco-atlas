The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is conditional. This paper has a very natural high-multiplicity mirror, but it has no named computational-complexity theorem of the kind the programme normally requires: no result is stated as NP-hard, polynomial-time, FPT, W[1]-hard, or similar. Its named results are axiomatic characterizations, implementation, and impossibility theorems. So, under the anchor rule read strictly, there is no admissible computational anchor.

The best substantive candidate is nevertheless Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that a bargaining solution \(f\) satisfies no benefit of randomization, convexity, Pareto optimality, invariance to positive coordinate rescaling, weak symmetry, and weak independence of irrelevant alternatives if and only if it is the relative utilitarian solution \(f^{\mathrm{RU}}\), where \(f^{\mathrm{RU}}(S)=\arg\max_{u\in S}\sum_i u_i/m_i(S)\) and \(m_i(S)=\max_{v\in S}v_i\).

A precise population mirror would be Continuous Relative-Utilitarian Selection. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu\), a set \(A\) of feasible bargaining alternatives, and for each type \(t\) a normalized vNM utility \(u_t:A\to[0,1]\), with disagreement utility \(0\) and \(m_t=\max_{a\in A}u_t(a)>0\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\). The task is to return an alternative

\(a^\star\in\arg\max_{a\in A}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,\frac{u_t(a)}{m_t}\),

or, if ties matter, the entire set of maximizers. Randomization may remain available exactly as in the paper: for a lottery \(\lambda\), evaluate its expected type utilities, without making the outcome space itself the continuous object.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. If \(n_t\) individual agents share type \(t\), then the finite objective divided by population size is exactly

\(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{u_i(a)}{m_i}=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\frac{n_t}{n}\frac{u_t(a)}{m_t}\).

A plausible regime is a large class-action settlement, union membership, or mass arbitration: millions of claimants, but only a few dozen or hundred complete utility types determined by claim category, household situation, risk attitude, and valuation of the settlement menu. The type must include every bargaining-relevant attribute; otherwise this would not be high multiplicity. The alternatives can remain indivisible settlement packages or other discrete social options.

The authors should recognize this as their problem rather than a weakened surrogate. Their own RU rule is an equal-per-agent sum of normalized utilities; replacing repeated individual terms by their limiting masses is precisely the high-multiplicity relaxation. The mirror is not “continuous because lotteries are allowed”; it is continuous because the population is represented by \(\mu\).

For an explicitly listed alternative set, the problem is plainly in Class A: evaluate the objective and select a maximum. With a succinct alternative domain, the interesting question becomes whether the pricing problem \(\max_{a\in A}\sum_t\mu_tu_t(a)/m_t\) is tractable. If the underlying allocation or alternative domain has a polynomial optimization or separation algorithm, the continuous problem should inherit it. If that domain is already NP-hard to optimize over, hardness transfers from the alternatives, not from population multiplicity. A continuum-specific Class C result would require the masses or type aggregation themselves to create hardness, and nothing in this paper suggests that.

The mirror could also ask whether population versions of the paper’s six axioms characterize this weighted integral rule, with weak symmetry interpreted through measure-preserving relabellings of agents. That is a natural theorem-generation question, but it is axiomatic rather than computational and should not be presented as the paper’s existing result.

The paper’s Theorem 2 gives a constructive implementation of strictly RU-optimal alternatives, but I would not make it the lead anchor. Its game relies on individual unilateral deviations, a finite approval sequence, and a first rejecting player. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass and cannot generally alter the proposal distribution, while a continuum has no canonical first rejecting agent. A faithful mirror would need organized type-level bargaining blocs or a mediator aggregating positive-mass reports, which is an additional modelling choice. Proposition 2’s impossibility of full implementation for two agents reinforces that this strategic mirror has a genuine boundary.

My weakest point is therefore clear: the paper does not specify an input encoding, an algorithm, or a complexity question. The continuous selection problem is strongly faithful to its central RU rule, but the claim that it inaugurates a new complexity landscape would have to be supplied by ChoCo rather than extracted from this paper. I would defend this as a sensible Class A candidate for a continuous mirror of Theorem 1, but not as a paper already containing a qualifying computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection comes before modelling: this paper contains no admissible computational anchor. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization; Propositions 1–3 and Proposition 2 are impossibility or independence results; Theorems 3 and 4 are further axiomatizations; and Theorem 2 is an SPE implementation theorem. None states a complexity bound, algorithmic problem, reduction, approximation result, or parameterized classification. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, there is therefore no computational result to continuize.

The proponent’s proposed mirror of Theorem 1 is mathematically coherent, but that is not enough. With repeated agents of identical complete utility type, the identity

\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{u_i(a)}{m_i} = \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\frac{u_t(a)}{m_t} \]

is correct. I grant that this is a sensible high-multiplicity welfare problem. But the paper never supplies a computational problem whose high-multiplicity version this is. The alternative set \(A\), its encoding, and the utility representations are completely unrestricted. If \(A\) is explicitly listed, the task is just scanning finitely many alternatives. If \(A\) is succinct, the interesting difficulty is whatever optimization problem has been hidden inside \(A\); that is a new problem supplied by ChoCo, not a computational consequence of Theorem 1. The masses merely become coefficients in one linear objective. No pricing problem, exponential-variable LP, separation question, or other characteristic computational structure comes from the paper.

Nor does the population formula preserve the theorem’s actual axiomatic content. The original bargaining problem has one utility coordinate per bargaining participant, and symmetry, Pareto efficiency, weak IIA, and normalization are stated on the full vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Aggregating repeated coordinates into type masses replaces that object with a weighted social-welfare problem. A new measure-theoretic axiom system could certainly be written, but that would be a new axiomatic programme—and axiomatic continuization is explicitly outside ChoCo’s scope.

Theorem 2 does not rescue the case. It is not a computational-complexity result, and its mechanism has no canonical atomless limit. The construction relies on named players making unilateral proposals, a finite approval order, and a single rejection being decisive. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass and cannot be represented by a change in the population distribution; there is no canonical “first rejecting player.” Treating each type as a representative gives a finite \(\tau\)-player game, not a continuum. Requiring a positive-mass rejection changes the game, while allowing a measure-zero rejection to remain decisive makes the outcome depend on identity and ordering information discarded by \(\mu\). Any viable repair therefore introduces a new bargaining institution rather than continuizing this implementation.

The same point applies even more sharply to Proposition 2: its impossibility is specifically a two-player strategic construction. Replicating the two players produces a population welfare model; preserving the strategic proof keeps the problem finite. Neither is a continuous mirror of the stated result.

The honest limitation of this negative case is that the proponent has identified a genuinely sensible high-multiplicity version of relative-utilitarian selection. I cannot honestly claim that such an object is meaningless in every economic scenario. But under the programme’s strict requirement for a named computational result, it is not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper: the only plausible population model is an externally invented optimization problem, while the paper itself contributes no computational landscape to chart.
