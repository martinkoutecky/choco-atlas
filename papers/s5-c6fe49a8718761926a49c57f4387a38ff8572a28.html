The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I believe is a narrow mirror: admissible dynamic-coalition synthesis for a large, typed robot fleet. The paper’s named computational anchor is Theorem 2 (proved here): the strategy for \(P_1\) synthesized by Algorithm 2 is admissible for \(P_1\). The paper gives a polynomial running-time bound for that algorithm in the size of the explicit product game. It has no named hardness result to anchor a continuous-hardness question, so this is an algorithmic lead.

A plausible regime is a warehouse or municipal fleet with thousands of robots but relatively few complete types: for example, type records encode capability, available actions, temporal-goal preferences, and relevant operating limits. A type is complete in the paper’s sense: robots grouped together are indistinguishable in everything the model uses. The system state records fractions of robots in each finite task or monitor state. A dispatcher acts as the distinguished leader. At each round it may invite a mass of one type into a temporary coalition and prescribe a joint action; the remaining robots act under their own admissible policies. Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the fleet of type \(t\), with support size \(\tau\) much smaller than the robot count. Inviting a mass rather than one named robot is a deliberate co-modification: it makes coalition control meaningful at population scale.

I would hand a prover the following problem, Fluid Dynamic-Coalition Admissibility (FDCA). An instance contains a finite set \(T\) of robot types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); finite action sets, local task states, and temporal-goal preference preorders for each type; a distinguished leader with its own actions and preorder; and rational transition tables specifying how action flows update the population state. Write \(x_{t,q}\) for the mass of type \(t\) in local state \(q\). Each round, the leader chooses a type to invite and a joint action for an invited mass; the remaining mass is assigned feasible individual actions. These choices are represented by nonnegative flow variables whose sums cannot exceed the available mass in each state. Atomic propositions are given by finite, rational predicates on the population state, and goals are scLTL formulas evaluated along agents’ traces.

The question is: synthesize a finite-memory leader policy that chooses the invited type, coalition actions, and mass flows so that each invitation is rational for the invited type and the leader’s policy is admissible. As in the paper, admissibility rules out a policy strictly dominated under the relevant worst-case comparison with other agents’ strategies. The leader’s objective is partial-order based: minimize its worst achievable outcome rank over all follower policies, among policies with rational coalition proposals. A solution is a finite-memory controller, described by rational polyhedral guards over \(x\) and rational flow rules, that makes those choices and attains the best such worst-case rank. This asks about the underlying synthesis problem, not an encoding.

I expect FDCA to be Class A in the stated finite-type, rational polyhedral setting. The paper’s construction repeatedly computes rank layers and checks whether a coalition action can enforce a transition into a target set. In FDCA, the corresponding choices are mass flows over finitely many types, states, and actions; feasibility and rationality checks look like linear optimization or separation problems. The central question is whether the rank and predecessor regions stay representable with polynomially many bits and pieces. The problem would generate useful follow-ups: is the running time polynomial in the product-state size, \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\); can a continuous solution give a high-multiplicity controller a rounding or preprocessing guarantee; and where do the exact polyhedral regions become too complex for the LP approach?

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 2 concerns a finite product game with a leader choosing a coalition with at most one other player. FDCA gives the leader a divisible cohort and makes the state an aggregate mass vector. That is not a literal limit of the theorem’s game graph. Still, it preserves the distinctive problem the authors solve—jointly choosing temporary coalitions and control actions under temporal preferences, with coalition acceptance and leader admissibility as constraints. Their own motivation includes robot coordination, and their model explicitly allows a player to represent a unified group. That makes a large typed fleet a credible setting in which they could recognize this as their problem’s continuous form. My case rests on that one synthesis result; it does not claim that the rest of the paper has already been continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2: the paper gives an algorithm for synthesizing an admissible strategy for \(P_1\), and claims polynomial running time in the explicit product-game size. So the negative case cannot rest on a lack of computational results. It rests on whether that result has a meaningful population-continuous version.

The paper’s agents are strategic units, not individual robots awaiting aggregation. Assumption 1 even says a player may represent a unified group. That puts a fleet’s physical multiplicity inside a player, while the game itself has a finite set of strategically distinct players. Scaling the fleet therefore need not create the kind of many-agent regime that the paper’s algorithm addresses. The robot story is plausible, but it does not by itself make the game’s society a distribution over interchangeable strategic agents.

The closest limit that preserves the game’s coalition rule has a sharper problem. \(P_1\) may form a coalition with at most one other player. If that partner is one of \(N\) interchangeable agents, its mass tends to zero as \(N\) grows. Keeping a positive-mass coalition means changing the coalition rule. That change is not just a new way to encode the same choices: it requires defining when a divisible cohort accepts a proposal and how admissibility applies to its members.

FDCA makes that change by allowing an invited mass, and by giving the leader and followers flow choices within a type and state. But the source game has deterministic strategies: agents with the same relevant history choose the same action. Splitting such a cell’s mass across actions requires randomization or a way to distinguish its members; inviting only part of it similarly adds a selection mechanism absent from the model. Those choices may be reasonable for a fleet, but they create new strategic semantics. The resulting synthesis problem is a coherent candidate for mean-field control or game theory; it is a more substantial redesign than a population-scale instance of Theorem 2.

A stricter version could invite whole, identical cohorts and keep deterministic actions. That avoids the split-flow issue, but leaves a finite set of cohorts acting as the paper’s players. A more expressive fluid version can preserve mass-scale coalitions, but then its rationality and admissibility definitions, state representation, and synthesis algorithm all need to be developed for that new model. The paper’s product-game result does not supply that bridge.

I would therefore reject FDCA as a demonstrated continuous mirror of this paper, while keeping open that the authors might recognize cohort coalitions as a worthwhile extension. That is the negative case’s weak point: the paper is explicitly motivated by robots and permits group-level players, so a carefully specified fleet model could persuade them. But the case for has not shown that Theorem 2’s distinctive problem survives the move to a continuous population without replacing its coalition and strategy semantics.
