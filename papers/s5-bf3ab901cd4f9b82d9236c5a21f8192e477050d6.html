The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many complete agent types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), indivisible goods \(G\), monotone valuations \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\), and baseline per-capita supplies \(q_g\), define \(u_t\) as the largest value supportable for a unit mass of type \(t\) by an aggregate distribution over integral bundles under \(q\). Find \(y\ge0\) and masses \(z_{t,B}\ge0\) minimizing \(\sum_{g\in G}y_g\), subject to \(\sum_B z_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(z_{t,B}>0\Rightarrow v_t(B)\ge u_t\), and \(\sum_{t,B\ni g}z_{t,B}\le q_g+y_g\) for every \(g\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity course or appointment allocation model with finitely many valuation types, population masses \(\mu_t\), integral bundles \(B\), fractional type-level assignment masses \(z_{t,B}\), per-capita baseline supply \(q\), and minimized extra-copy mass \(y\).

The objection that survived

The finite-\(N\) bridge is not fully formalized: the mirror measures extra copies per capita, whereas the theorem counts integral total copies, and it does not prove that \(u_t\) is exactly the limit of the paper's finite MMS benchmarks.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the exact MMS-with-copies algorithm and bounds of Theorem 3.4. It leaves the paper's lower bounds, additive and submodular special cases, approximate MMS theorems, and \(1\)-out-of-\(d\) results untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the zero-augmentation continuum allocation round to \(N\) agents, and what additive MMS loss or extra integral copies are necessary?
  • For succinct valuation representations, can the type benchmarks \(u_t\) and the bundle-column LP be computed efficiently?
  • Which supply normalization makes the finite-\(N\) total-copy bounds converge to the per-capita objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity course-allocation or appointment-allocation version of the paper’s exact MMS-with-copies problem. The agents are a very large population of students or patients, while the number of complete valuation/eligibility profiles is small: \(T\) is finite, \(|T|=\tau\ll N\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents of type \(t\). A type includes everything relevant to the allocation, so agents of one type have identical valuations, eligibility constraints, and MMS benchmark.

The necessary co-modification is to describe supply per capita. Let \(G\) be the finite set of indivisible goods and \(q_g\) the baseline mass of copies of good \(g\) available per unit population. Opening an additional seat or appointment contributes extra-copy mass \(y_g\). Each individual agent still receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\), with at most one copy of any good. Only the aggregate allocation is fractional: \(z_{t,B}\) denotes the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(B\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper. It states that for every instance with \(n\) agents, \(m\) goods, and monotone valuations, there is a \((t,k)\)-allocation achieving every agent’s MMS while using at most \(t\le m/e\) total extra copies and at most \(k\le\min\{n-1,3\ln m/\ln\ln m\}\) copies of any individual good. Moreover, given an MMS partition for every agent and any \(\beta\in\mathbb N\), the paper gives an efficient randomized algorithm returning such an allocation with probability at least \(1-e^{-\beta}\). This is a constructive result proved here, not a result cited from elsewhere. The paper does not contain a numbered NP-hardness or complexity-classification theorem, so there is no hardness anchor to prefer.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

CMMS\(_\infty\)-Augmentation. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), a distribution \(\mu\), goods \(G\), baseline per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and a monotone valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) for each type. Let \(u_t\) be type \(t\)’s baseline continuum-MMS: the largest value \(u\) for which a unit mass of identical type-\(t\) agents can each receive an integral bundle of value at least \(u\) using only supply \(q\). Find a measurable allocation and extra-copy vector \(y\ge0\) minimizing \(\sum_{g\in G}y_g\), subject to every type-\(t\) agent receiving value at least \(u_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B\ni g}z_{t,B}\le q_g+y_g\) for every good \(g\).

Equivalently, this is the finite LP over bundle columns \(z_{t,B}\): \(\sum_Bz_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(z_{t,B}=0\) whenever \(v_t(B)<u_t\), and \(\sum_{t,B\ni g}z_{t,B}\le q_g+y_g\). The bundles themselves remain indivisible; only the mass assigned to different bundles is fractional.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. It still asks how much extra supply is needed to give every agent her original MMS, and it still measures augmentation by duplicated goods. The scenario is natural: a university has a large recurring population of students in a small number of curricular profiles, or a hospital has many patients in a small number of treatment-preference types. Extra copies are extra seats, slots, or appointments. The paper itself motivates copies through course allocation and market-clearing error, so this interpretation should not look artificial.

The continuous mirror has a particularly clean answer. For every type \(t\), take a baseline MMS witness \(z^t_B\) using supply \(q\), with every used bundle satisfying \(v_t(B)\ge u_t\). Define the society-wide allocation by \(z_{t,B}=\mu_t z^t_B\). Then every type receives its MMS, and for every good \(g\),

\(\sum_t\sum_{B\ni g}z_{t,B}=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{B\ni g}z^t_B\le\sum_t\mu_t q_g=q_g.\)

Thus \(y_g=0\) for every good. The continuum population eliminates the conflict between type-specific MMS witnesses by mixing them across the mass of each type. No agent receives a fractional good or a fractional bundle.

I would therefore expect this mirror to be Class A at the allocation layer: exact MMS requires zero augmentation in the continuum, and the construction is explicit once each type’s MMS witness is known. This is stronger than Theorem 3.4’s \(m/e\) bound, but it is not claiming that the paper’s finite theorem becomes a polynomial-time algorithm for arbitrary valuation representations. Computing the MMS benchmarks or witnesses may still be difficult; the exponential bundle LP has a pricing problem over goods, and hardness driven by the goods dimension could survive. That would be Class B behaviour, not continuum-specific hardness.

The main further questions are quantitative rather than existential: how much extra supply is needed to round the continuum allocation to a finite \(N\)-agent allocation; what additive loss occurs when type masses are not exactly representable; and whether the \(m/e\) and \(O(\ln m/\ln\ln m)\) bounds describe the finite-rounding cost. One can also ask what happens if each type is required to receive one common bundle rather than a distribution over bundles, but that restriction would remove the essential high-multiplicity effect.

The weakest point is the benchmark interpretation. The paper starts from one finite set of named goods and an \(n\)-way MMS partition, whereas the mirror uses per-capita supply and the corresponding continuum MMS. A sceptical reader could regard that as a change of fair-share notion. The answer is that the supply scaling is forced by making the population continuous, and the individual allocation remains integral. It is precisely the high-multiplicity regime of repeated course seats or appointment slots. I would not anchor on Theorem 3.1 as a second result: its lower-bound construction has essentially one distinct valuation type per agent, so \(\tau=n\), which is the opposite of this proposed regime. My positive case is therefore deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 3.4’s exact MMS-with-copies problem in a genuinely many-agents/few-types market, and argues that the paper’s discrete augmentation barrier dissolves there.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor fails at the model boundary. Theorem 3.4 is about collisions between finitely many named agents’ choices of bundles from one finite inventory. A population continuum forces a choice: retain that inventory, in which case MMS degenerates, or scale the inventory with population, in which case the copy objective is convexified away.

With the paper’s literal supply—one copy of each \(g\in G\)—let \(N>|G|\). Every partition of \(G\) into \(N\) bundles contains an empty bundle, so for every normalized monotone valuation,
\[ \mu_i^N(G)=0. \]
Thus the faithful high-multiplicity limit has zero MMS and zero required augmentation. This is not merely the claim that the answer becomes easy; the benchmark that Theorem 3.4 protects has disappeared.

The proponent’s per-capita supply is the natural escape, but it changes the issue in a decisive way. If a type-\(t\) witness \(z^t\) uses baseline supply \(q\), then the proposed aggregate allocation \(z_{t,B}=\mu_t z^t_B\) satisfies
\[ \sum_{t,B\ni g} z_{t,B} \le \sum_t \mu_t q_g = q_g. \]
Hence \(y=0\) for every valuation class, not because of a theorem about MMS or copies, but because the allocation polytope has been convexified. Each type is allowed to use a fraction of the entire inventory it would have used if the whole population had that type.

That is exactly the conflict Theorem 3.4 studies being removed. In the paper, one chooses one integral bundle for each named agent; overlapping choices require physical copies. In the proposed model, each type can distribute its mass over all safe bundles, and the distributions are averaged. The balls-into-bins argument, the \(m/e\) bound, and the \(O(\ln m/\ln\ln m)\) per-good bound have no surviving object to analyze.

There is also no faithful normalization of the copy count. If \(q_g\) means one baseline copy per agent, then a normalized population of mass one represents \(N\) physical copies of \(g\). A single extra physical copy has mass \(1/N\), so the paper’s \(O(m)\) additional copies vanish in the continuum limit whenever \(m\) is fixed. Treating one unit of \(y_g\) as one extra copy instead means adding \(O(N)\) copies to the original instance; that is a new resource-augmentation regime.

The obvious repairs do not help. Requiring every type to receive one common bundle would restore collisions, but identical agents in the paper are not required to receive identical bundles; this is an artificial type-level restriction. Requiring integer copy counts leaves a nonatomic population unable to receive positive-mass allocations from finitely many atoms. Asking for an exact finite-\(N\) realization of the aggregate allocation could be worthwhile, but that is an integral rounding or discrepancy problem, not the continuous mirror proposed here.

Computing each \(u_t\) or an MMS witness may remain difficult, but that is the single-type MMS problem. It is not the computational content of Theorem 3.4’s resource-augmentation result. The proposed LP therefore yields either a degenerate benchmark or the definitional identity \(y=0\); it does not produce a nontrivial continuous-population problem corresponding to the paper’s theorem. I would reject this anchor, while acknowledging that a separate finite-rounding study could be valuable.
