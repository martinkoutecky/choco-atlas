The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot supply a qualifying anchor from this paper. The supplied text has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Its central findings are empirical: the authors sample 24,518 redistricting plans and find the NC2012 and NC2016 plans atypical, while the Judges plan is comparatively typical. The footnote about Markov-chain convergence is not a numbered complexity result. So the set of required anchors is empty, and there is no honest way to state one continuous problem per anchor.

The strongest positive case is an unanchored mirror of the paper’s ensemble analysis. Represent North Carolina by its VTD polygons, with each polygon a type carrying its location, county, population mass, and the election and demographic data used by the paper. There are about 2,692 such spatial types but millions of residents, a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A plan is a partition of the state into 13 connected districts; each point of population mass receives one district, while population, vote, and minority totals are computed from the mass assigned there. Impose the paper’s population, compactness, county-splitting, and VRA criteria, and extend its score function \(J\) to these partitions.

Call the resulting question Atomless Gerrymandering-Ensemble Evaluation: given that spatial instance, a candidate plan, \(\beta\), and an explicit computable reference measure \(Q\) over admissible partitions, compute or approximate the distribution of Democratic seat counts under
\[ \Pi_\beta(dD)=\frac{e^{-\beta J(D)}Q(dD)}{Z_\beta}. \]
A solution could be the seat-count probabilities, or an approximation to them within a specified total-variation error; the same ensemble could report ordered district vote-share distributions and the candidate plan’s tail rank. This retains the paper’s central question—how a plan’s election outcomes compare with an ensemble of plausible plans—while making population mass the continuous object. The paper itself acknowledges that its plan distribution is a modeling choice, so specifying \(Q\) is recognizable in spirit. The mirror would cover the ensemble comparisons, not establish that a chosen ensemble is uniquely neutral.

I would leave its complexity class open. Continuous masses alone do not simplify the connected geometric partition problem, and the paper provides no hardness result to transfer or algorithmic result to mirror. The weak point is that a reference measure over continuous partitions needs a concrete, defensible construction—perhaps a limit of the VTD-based sampling scheme—and different constructions could change the answer. If that measure and its relation to the authors’ finite ensemble were made precise, this would be a plausible research extension. It remains an extension without a qualifying anchor in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no qualifying anchor. It reports empirical findings from a finite ensemble of VTD assignments; it states no numbered theorem or other result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its MCMC discussion describes a sampling procedure and eventual convergence, but gives no mixing-time bound or approximation guarantee. So the proposed ensemble evaluation is an unanchored extension, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

At the paper’s existing support, treating residents as atomless mass does little by itself. A plan still assigns each VTD to one of 13 districts, and the reported vote and demographic totals are already sums over VTD data. To make continuization substantive, districts must be allowed to cut through VTDs. The authors did examine finer splits to achieve more exact population balance and report that they barely change the election results. That does not rule out every finer spatial model, but it weakens the case that population atomlessness adds a new question to this analysis.

The stronger version would represent North Carolina as a spatial population density, allow connected regions to form districts, and estimate the seat-count distribution under a reference measure on those partitions. That is coherent, and millions of residents make the high-multiplicity regime plausible. But the computation then depends centrally on the reference measure \(Q\). The paper’s score \(J\) does not define one: the proposed \(\Pi_\beta\) also needs \(Q\), and different mesh refinements or boundary-generation processes can induce different distributions and tail ranks. The paper itself recognizes distribution choice as a modeling decision. Making a particular continuous \(Q\) precise could be worthwhile, but it would make the main research problem how to define and compute an ensemble over geometric partitions. It would not supply a computational question already asserted or classified by this paper. Local engineering has the same issue: a continuous analogue needs a chosen notion of “nearby” plans, such as moved population mass or boundary displacement.

So the best negative case is that the proposed mirror either leaves the paper’s finite VTD assignment model essentially intact, or introduces a new geometric sampling problem whose central choices are not fixed by the paper. That is a strong reason not to count this paper as an anchored continuization target. It is weaker as a universal claim: a carefully specified atomless redistricting model could still be a worthwhile project in its own right.
