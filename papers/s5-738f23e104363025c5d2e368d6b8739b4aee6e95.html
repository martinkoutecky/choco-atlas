The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational type masses \((\mu_a)_{a\in A}\) and \((\nu_b)_{b\in B}\), and a rational initial perfect matching flow \(x^0\) with row and column sums equal to those masses, decide whether a finite sequence of mass-divorce moves reaches a stable flow. Each type has a strict ranking over acceptable types on the other side. A move chooses types \(a\in A\), \(b\in B\) and current partner types \(b'\in B\), \(a'\in A\) such that \(b\succ_a b'\), \(a\succ_b a'\), \(x_{ab'}>0\), \(x_{a'b}>0\), and \(a'\) and \(b'\) are mutually acceptable; for some rational \(\delta>0\), it transfers mass \(\delta\) from \((a,b')\) and \((a',b)\) to \((a,b)\) and \((a',b')\), leaving a nonnegative flow. A flow is stable if for every acceptable pair \((a,b)\), either no mass of \(a\) is matched below \(b\), or no mass of \(b\) is matched below \(a\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided matching market represented by interchangeable preference types and rational population masses. The state is a matching flow; each decision step moves equal mass through a blocking pair and its displaced partners, and the question is whether finitely many such steps reach type-level stability. There is no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

A mass move of arbitrary size is a chosen batching rule: the paper's divorce acts on four named agents, so it does not uniquely prescribe how a type-flow transition should work.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the reachability decision underlying Theorems 1 and 3, plus the move-count restriction in Theorem 2; it leaves Theorem 4's combined-parameter algorithm and the PSPACE discussion unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does allowing types to split across partner types make mass-divorce reachability tractable, or does the decision problem remain hard as the number of types grows?
  • Can every reachable stable flow be reached by a sequence whose length is bounded in the input size and the support size?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see keeps the paper’s central question intact: can a matching reach stability through a sequence of divorces? What changes is that the market is represented by masses of interchangeable types, and a divorce can move a positive amount of mass.

Take two sets of types, \(A\) and \(B\), for the two sides of a matching market. A type specifies an agent’s side and a strict ranking of acceptable types on the other side. Agents of the same type are interchangeable for everything the problem models; they are indifferent among individuals within a counterpart type. Each side has total mass \(1\). The input gives the rational mass of each type and an initial perfect matching as a rational flow \(x^0_{ab}\), where \(x^0_{ab}\) is the mass of type \(a\) matched to type \(b\). Perfect initial matchings are a restriction, but the paper’s hardness constructions use them and its divorce operations preserve them.

A mass-divorce move chooses types \(a,b\) who prefer each other to their current partner types \(b',a'\), respectively. If at least \(\delta>0\) mass is matched along both \((a,b')\) and \((a',b)\), and \(a'\) and \(b'\) find each other acceptable, the move transfers \(\delta\) from those two pairs to \((a,b)\) and \((a',b')\). The transfer preserves the mass of every type: each person still receives an integral partner, while the type-level allocation can be fractional. A final flow is stable if there is no pair of types with positive mass on worse assignments on both sides who would prefer to match each other. A solution is a finite sequence of valid moves ending in such a stable flow.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large hiring or placement market in which applicants share a small number of credential profiles, positions share a small number of role profiles, and both sides rank profiles rather than individuals. Millions of participants might then be represented by a small support of types, \(\tau\), with binary-rational masses and matching flows. The mass-flow representation does not continuize the outcome each person receives; it continuizes the population and the aggregate assignment. It also asks the paper’s computational question about divorce reachability, rather than an equilibrium question about population dynamics.

I would anchor the case on three results, all proved in this paper:

  • Theorem 1: DivorceSM for incomplete and strict preferences is NP-hard. The paper proves this by reduction from CLIQUE. This is my lead anchor because it concerns the paper’s central decision problem directly.
  • Theorem 2: DivorceSM is W[1]-hard with respect to the total number \(\kappa\) of allowed divorces. This is also proved here, using the paper’s reduction framework.
  • Theorem 3: DivorceSM remains NP-hard even if each preference list has constant length. This is proved here by reduction from a restricted 3SAT variant; the construction in the paper has preference lists of length at most eight.

For Theorem 1, the continuous question is Mass Divorce Reachability: given the type masses, type rankings and acceptability, and an initial perfect matching flow, is there a finite sequence of mass-divorce moves to a stable flow? I would provisionally expect this to be Class C, hard for continuum-specific reasons. The source result establishes no hardness transfer: a type-level mass can be split, whereas the reduction’s selector agents are discrete. But the order of moves and the requirement to reach stability through the specified operation remain computational constraints in their own right. Further questions include whether a successful sequence can always be shortened to a bound polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, and how this reachability problem compares with simply finding a stable flow.

For Theorem 2, the corresponding problem is \(\kappa\)-Bounded Mass Divorce Reachability: the same input, with the question restricted to sequences of at most \(\kappa\) mass-divorce moves. I would lean toward continuum-specific parameterized hardness in \(\kappa\), but this is less secure than the lead: a move can choose its mass, so the discrete reduction’s combinatorial choices may not survive unchanged. The natural follow-up is whether the problem is FPT in \(\kappa\), or whether the choice of type-level move sequences already gives W[1]-hardness.

For Theorem 3, the question is Bounded-List Mass Divorce Reachability: the Mass Divorce Reachability problem with every type ranking at most eight acceptable counterpart types. I would again expect Class C hardness, if hardness survives at all. Constant-length rankings make the type-level setting easier to picture, but do not remove the path requirement. The key follow-up is whether bounded preference length yields tractability once the individual population is divisible, or whether reachability remains hard as the number of types grows.

I think the authors could recognize these as continuous analogues of their problem: the divorce transition, its acceptability condition, and the stable endpoint are all retained. Type-based matching also has a natural connection to the paper’s mention of fractional matchings under preferences. That related work is supporting context, not a collision: it does not already ask whether a stable outcome is reachable by divorce operations from an initial matching.

My weakest point is the co-modification of strict individual rankings into strict rankings over types, with indifference among individuals within a type. That is necessary for a genuinely high-multiplicity regime with interchangeable agents, but it is not literally the paper’s strict-preference model. And because masses are divisible, the paper’s NP-hardness and W[1]-hardness do not automatically transfer. The positive case is that the mass-divorce questions preserve the paper’s distinctive computational object—reachability under its divorce rule—and put it in a credible large-population regime. Whether that is the right analogue is ultimately for the authors and a referee to judge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed model replaces the paper’s transition system with a different one. In the paper, a divorce is an operation on four particular agents: a blocking pair forms, and their particular partners are paired. The resulting reachability question is about paths through matchings of identifiable people. A type-level flow instead permits a mass \(\delta\) to move between type pairs. It must choose which portions of each type participate, and it may split what was one role in the paper’s gadgets among several outcomes. That is a coherent flow-reconfiguration problem, but there is no canonical limit operation that preserves the paper’s individual divorce rule.

That distinction is sharpest for Theorem 1. Its continuous counterpart would be Mass Divorce Reachability in a profile-based hiring or placement market. This is the lead anchor, and the setting is plausible: many applicants and positions may share profiles, while rankings over profiles and an initial matching flow are natural input. But the counterpart depends on treating “a mass of agents divorces” as one move, with arbitrary positive mass, and treating type-level stability as the endpoint. Those choices define the new system; they are not forced by taking the paper’s population to a limit. The paper’s reduction uses selector agents and graph-specific acceptability to enforce choices among named roles. In a mass model those roles can be split. That does not count against the question merely because it may change the hardness result; it does make the proposed transition rule a modelling decision, rather than an evident continuization of the theorem.

Theorem 2 inherits that issue and adds a parameterization problem. In the paper, \(\kappa\) counts atomic divorces. In a mass version, one move might transfer a vanishing fraction or almost the entire available mass. Counting moves then measures rounds of type-level intervention, not how much of the society changes. One can define that parameter, and a large placement market could plausibly care about intervention rounds. But the result would be about a chosen operational convention. Charging by transferred mass would answer a different question, while fixing a minimum transfer granularity would reintroduce a discrete scale. This weakens the case for treating the W[1]-hardness theorem as a particularly faithful or valuable mirror.

Theorem 3 is the hardest anchor to dislodge. Bounded preference lists remain meaningful for profile types, and the same mass-flow reachability question with lists of length at most eight is easy to state. The objection is again that the list bound does not resolve the underlying change in the move: the original theorem bounds the length of individual agents’ lists, whereas the mass model bounds a profile’s list and allows arbitrary divisions of that profile’s mass. That makes it a restriction of the new model, but not a more faithful bridge to the paper’s result.

These objections do not establish that the type-flow model is uninteresting. A large market with interchangeable worker and position profiles can supply a sensible scenario, and the model does continuize the population rather than merely the outcome. Nor does the cited work on fractional matchings already answer reachability by divorces. The proponent’s model also addresses the main concern about individual outcomes by treating the flow as an aggregate assignment of agents who still receive integral partners.

So the negative case is weak where it needs to be strongest. It can argue that the three anchors share one modelling choice—what counts as a mass divorce—and that the resulting problem is less directly tied to the paper than the proposal suggests. It cannot honestly show that no profile-based scenario would make that choice worthwhile. I would be cautious about claiming the paper has no continuous mirror; the more defensible criticism is that its strongest proposed mirror needs the authors’ endorsement of a specific mass-transition rule before these theorems can be treated as its anchors.
