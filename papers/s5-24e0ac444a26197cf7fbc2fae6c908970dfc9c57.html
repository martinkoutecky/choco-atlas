The continuous mirror question

Given schools, a finite support of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita capacities, and efficiently represented substitutable, LAD, acceptant choice rules over applicant-mass vectors, determine whether the induced legal set of mass allocations has a student-optimal member and compute it. Define blocking by testing whether a type with mass assigned below school \(a\) in one allocation could have an infinitesimal tagged applicant admitted by \(a\), with another allocation assigning positive mass of that type to \(a\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic school-choice market with type masses \(\mu_t\), allocation variables \(x_{t,a}\) including an outside option, per-capita school capacities, and mass-based choice rules that specify both bulk choices and responses to an infinitesimal tagged applicant. Legal allocations satisfy the paper's internal nonblocking and external blocking conditions; select the student-optimal member using typewise stochastic dominance.

The objection that survived

With per-capita normalization, a finite market can have room for one lower-priority student while its limit has no residual capacity; the infinitesimal applicant test does not restore that disappearing slack.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns Theorem 3's equivalence between the student-optimal legal assignment and sEADA under acceptant choice rules. It leaves the paper's lattice and rural-hospitals results, strategy-proofness result, and assignment-with-contracts extension unaddressed, and does not claim convergence from finite markets.

Open questions for a prover

  • For the stated mass-based choice rules, does a unique legal set exist, and does mass sEADA compute its student-optimal member?
  • How does the continuous result relate to large finite markets when individual capacity slack vanishes under normalization?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper’s best positive case is narrow. It has no named theorem stating a standard complexity result such as NP-hardness or membership in P. Its closest algorithmic anchor is Theorem 3, proved here: when every school has an acceptant choice function, the student-optimal legal assignment coincides with the outcome of the simplified efficiency-adjusted deferred-acceptance mechanism, sEADA. The theorem does not state a complexity bound. Given efficient school-choice computations, the described finite-market procedure has polynomially many deferred-acceptance proposals and removal rounds, but that is an inference from the procedure, not a complexity theorem in the paper. So this is a defensible constructive anchor only if the reader accepts algorithmic identification as enough; there is no hardness anchor to mirror.

My lead mirror would be Atomless sEADA for acceptant school choice. A type \(t\) records a student’s complete strict ranking of schools and every feature used by school choice rules. The instance gives a finite set of schools, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita capacities, and type-level choice rules \(C_a\) over applicant-mass vectors. Require those rules to be substitutable, satisfy the law of aggregate demand, and be acceptant: school \(a\) chooses all applicants below capacity \(q_a\), and \(q_a\) units when at least \(q_a\) apply. Include an efficient finite description of each rule and its response to an infinitesimal tagged applicant.

A solution is an allocation \(x_{t,a}\geq 0\), including an outside option, with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\); each school’s assigned mass is chosen by its rule, and no type is assigned to an unacceptable school. Extend the paper’s blocking notion to these allocations: allocation \(u\) blocks \(v\) if some positive mass of type \(t\) is placed at school \(a\) in \(u\), that type has positive mass assigned below \(a\) in \(v\), and \(a\) would admit an infinitesimal tagged applicant of type \(t\) from its applicant mass in \(v\). A legal set has the paper’s two properties: allocations outside it are blocked by one inside it, and allocations inside it do not block one another. The requested output is the student-optimal member of this set, with typewise preference compared by stochastic dominance over each type’s school ranking.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for large centralized school or college assignment: many students share rankings, eligibility categories, and other choice-relevant attributes, so the population is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported types. Capacities and reserves naturally scale with cohort size, and the continuous question asks for population shares. A district that relies on unique lottery numbers would not fit this regime; those numbers would have to be part of the type and could make \(\tau\) large.

The expected direction is tractable (Class A) when \(C_a\) has an efficient finite description: the sEADA steps appear amenable to operating on type masses rather than named students, perhaps with a finite breakpoint algorithm or a continuous optimization formulation. That expectation is open. The prover should determine whether mass deferred acceptance and the repeated under-demanded-school removals terminate, whether the resulting allocation is legal and student-optimal, and the running time in \(m,\tau,L\), and the choice-rule encoding size. A further deliverable is the relation between this answer and large finite markets: whether discrete sEADA outcomes converge to the continuous allocation, and whether rounding yields useful discrete assignments.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s algorithmic identification under acceptant choice functions. It does not claim that the paper’s lattice, rural-hospitals, or strategy-proofness results carry over, nor does it address non-acceptant choice rules. Its weakest point is the transition from named students to mass allocations: the paper’s legality definition depends on which assignments are possible, while a continuum can erase one-person changes and makes infinitesimal blocking a modeling choice. The tagged-applicant rule makes that choice explicit, but it is also an added modeling commitment. If the authors would not recognize that mass-level blocking rule as their legality question, this mirror fails.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection starts with what Theorem 3 actually establishes. It says that, under acceptant choice functions, the student-optimal legal assignment coincides with sEADA. It does not state a complexity result, define an input encoding for choice functions, or ask for a runtime bound. Computing that assignment is a possible follow-up, but the paper’s contribution is a mechanism-identification theorem, not a computational problem whose complexity the continuous version would chart.

The proposed atomless sEADA also has trouble preserving the paper’s distinctive object: legality turns on whether an individual student can be assigned to a school in some assignment. Consider a school with capacity \(k\), \(k-1\) higher-priority applicants, and lower-priority applicants. In the finite market, one lower-priority student can be admitted. With \(2k\) students, the corresponding masses converge to a market where higher-priority applicants exactly fill the school’s per-capita capacity, and no positive mass of lower-priority students can be admitted. The finite legal possibility has disappeared at the limit. A deterministic finite choice rule must also decide which lower-priority individual gets that seat; representing that distinction can split the group into more types, while treating the group fractionally changes the individual assignment rule.

The proposed fixes each change the question. Requiring a positive mass to block can miss a student’s individual legal claim. Testing an infinitesimal tagged applicant preserves a one-person probe, but adds an agent outside the mass allocation and requires a new rule for how that probe interacts with the school’s choices. Either may define a sensible continuum matching model. Neither follows from the paper’s legal-set theorem, and neither alone supplies a finite-to-continuous dictionary for individual legal standing.

That is a reason to reject this as a mirror of Theorem 3, not proof that atomless school choice is uninteresting. The negative case is weak against the broader claim: large districts with repeated preference and eligibility profiles are a natural high-multiplicity setting, and an anonymous mass version of sEADA could be worthwhile in its own right. What the paper does not give is a computational-complexity anchor for that project, or a direct way to carry its individual notion of legality into the continuum.
