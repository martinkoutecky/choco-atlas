The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an eligible computational anchor, so I can’t honestly state a continuous problem per anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition that classifies a problem as tractable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, and it gives no running-time or hardness result for its algorithms.

The closest named results are proved in the paper, but they concern auction properties. Proposition 3.2 says that with i.i.d. valuations, VCG with the colluding group and \(|\mathsf C|\) additional non-colluding bidders achieves expected welfare at least that of an efficient, possibly untruthful, mechanism on the original \(\mathsf N\cup\mathsf C\). Theorem 4.3 says that C-PoP with its dynamic-programming oracle is ex-post DSIC for all bidders, including colluders, when the groups are correctly specified. Theorem 4.7 says that under the known i.i.d. valuation-distribution assumption, C-PoP’s expected welfare is at least V-PoP’s, which is at least VCG’s welfare on the non-colluders alone. Those are welfare and truthfulness guarantees, not complexity results; describing a DP oracle does not establish its computational complexity.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind a *new follow-up*: a large procurement or wholesale platform with many suppliers drawn from a small set of valuation and collusion-classification types. A population model could represent each joint type by its fraction of the supplier pool and scale item supply with the market. C-PoP’s common price and sequential allocation make that mass description natural. But replacing finite bid ranks with type masses or quantiles would be a new model, and the paper does not establish that its truthfulness proof or oracle carries over. Its valuation distributions and classifier errors describe uncertainty about a finite set of bidders; they do not themselves continuize the population.

That is the strongest positive case I can make while respecting the anchor requirement: the mechanism suggests a credible population-level extension, but the paper supplies no qualifying named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive under ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 3.2 compares expected welfare across auctions; Theorem 4.3 establishes dominant-strategy truthfulness; Theorem 4.7 gives expected-welfare guarantees. None classifies a computational problem by complexity or analyzes an algorithm’s running time. The dynamic-programming oracle suggests computations, but the paper gives no complexity theorem, and its arbitrary valuation distribution \(F\) has no finite input representation. A continuous model could be built around these results, but it would not supply the missing computational anchor.

Proposition 3.2 offers the clearest welfare mirror. One could represent valuation and collusion classes by rational masses, scale item supply with market size, and compare welfare per capita. Keeping the paper’s fixed \(r\) instead makes the limit degenerate for bounded valuations: as the bidder population grows, the winners’ values approach the top of the support, and both sides of the comparison approach the same welfare. Corollary 4.8 already identifies this kind of fixed-supply convergence for C-PoP. Scaling \(r\) with the population could preserve a nontrivial allocation question, but it changes the recruitment comparison and still yields a welfare theorem, not a computational one.

Theorem 4.3 has a plausible atomless version, but its strategic content shifts. With individual bidders of measure zero, one bidder cannot affect the aggregate bids that set the price; truthfulness then reduces largely to the posted-price cutoff. To retain strategic influence, a positive-mass cohort would have to act as a player. That changes the theorem’s bidder-level DSIC question into a question about coordinated mass deviations. The paper’s colluders already act as a coalition, but its non-colluders are individuals; a continuum extension must specify which coalitions can alter the price-setting population. That is a new strategic model, and still no complexity result.

For Theorem 4.7, a strong follow-up would optimize expected welfare over prices in a market with finitely many rational valuation and classification types and capacity proportional to market size. The supplier-pool scenario is plausible as a high-multiplicity setting; I would not reject it on multiplicity grounds. But it requires a new capacity regime and a mass-level price problem. With arbitrary \(F\), computation also needs an input model the paper never supplies. The resulting study could be worthwhile market-design research, but it would establish a new problem rather than mirror one of this paper’s computational results.

So the negative case is strongest on scope: the paper contains meaningful mechanism and welfare results, yet no qualifying computational anchor. Its weakness is equally clear: a continuous auction model may be sensible and interesting in its own right. The case against is that the proposed models are new research questions, not continuous mirrors of the paper’s stated computational contribution.
