What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers the unnumbered winner computation in §2.3.1; it does not cover the tie-breaking algorithm, STV, proportional ranking, or axiomatic criteria. No eligible numbered computational result anchors any of these.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no eligible numbered computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its computational content is unnumbered: §2.3 gives the pairwise tally and Floyd–Warshall algorithm, with runtime \(O(NC^2+C^3)\); §5.3.8 gives runtimes for the tie-breaking version. The numbered corollaries and claims—such as Corollaries (4.1.14) and (4.1.15)—establish structural properties of winners, not a complexity classification.

A plausible population model would take complete rankings as types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\) as population shares, and ask for the Schulze potential winners computed from the induced pairwise margins. Large membership elections with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings would make that a sensible mirror. But its anchor would be the paper’s unnumbered algorithm description, so it cannot meet the required named-result standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The “no eligible computational result” claim is too strict. The paper’s abstract promises an \(O(C^3)\) winner algorithm, and §2.3.1 gives the Floyd–Warshall procedure and total runtime \(O(NC^2+C^3)\); §2.3.2 gives a candidate-specific \(O(C^2)\) check. These are explicit computational results even though they are not packaged as numbered theorems.

A direct mirror takes a distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types and computes \(p_\mu(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]\), then runs the same strongest-path calculation on the induced pairwise margins. With support size \(\tau\), the arithmetic-operation count is \(O(\tau C^2+C^3)\), with rational bit complexity also depending on the input bit length. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for the large membership elections and referendums described in the paper. It gives the discrete-to-continuous dictionary directly: a finite profile with counts \(n_t\) is represented by \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). The lift may be straightforward, but a straightforward answer is still an answer to a well-posed computational question.

There is a stronger nearby question than simply recomputing the winner: how much voter mass must move between ranking types to change the Schulze winner? One can define transfers \(x_{t,t'}\), charge a cost such as Kendall distance, and ask for the minimum total cost that makes a target a winner. That is a population-level robustness problem tied to the paper’s winner computation, not a fractional-outcome or noise-model reformulation. The paper’s discussion of resolving ties and its references to manipulation work make the connection especially natural.

So the absence of a numbered complexity theorem is a limitation of the paper’s presentation, not a decisive reason to reject a mirror. The narrow winner-computation mirror may be a modest contribution, and I cannot make an honest universal case that no scenario is worthwhile: both the high-multiplicity input model and a mass-based robustness question remain credible.
