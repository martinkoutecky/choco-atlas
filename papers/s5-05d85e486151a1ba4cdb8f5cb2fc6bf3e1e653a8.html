The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses of requester and qualified-device types, each with an integer capacity and an explicitly listed DMR marginal bid or ask schedule, compute the type-level traded task mass, allocations, and payments under QUAD’s split-market pricing rule. Split each type’s mass between the two arenas; find clearing prices allowing fractional participation by marginal mass when needed; use each arena’s price to determine eligible trade in the other, with ties resolved by a fixed rule.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete requester and device types, with rational mass, category, capacity, marginal bid or ask schedule, and device qualification status. Aggregate allocations are masses of task units matched between requester and qualified-device types; individual units remain integral, while marginal type mass may be fractional at a clearing threshold.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the second fold of QUAD: allocating homogeneous sensing tasks among devices that have already passed the quality screen. There is one suitable named computational anchor in the paper, and it is an algorithmic result rather than a hardness result.

Lemma 5.1, proved in this paper, states that QUAD is computationally efficient. Its proof analyzes the mechanism’s category loop, randomized IoT-QDBC quality selection, market splitting and price search, demand and supply calculation, and winner determination and payment. The authors claim a per-category running time of \(O(n_i^3+m_i^3)\), and hence polynomial time when the number of categories is constant. That is the full anchor I would rely on: the paper claims the complete QUAD mechanism runs in polynomial time. The proof does not give a clear encoding-sensitive bound for capacities, marginal-value schedules, or the number of price increments, so I would not treat the stated bound as a finished bit-complexity analysis.

I would formulate the mirror as Quality-Filtered Type-Mass QUAD Clearing. In each task category, the input is a finite list of requester and device types with rational masses. A requester type specifies its share of the population, its integer capacity \(q_t\), and its nonincreasing per-task bid schedule \(b_{t,1}\ge\cdots\ge b_{t,q_t}\). A device type specifies its mass, capacity, nondecreasing ask schedule \(a_{u,1}\le\cdots\le a_{u,q_u}\), and a quality-qualification flag produced by the preliminary assessment. The type is complete: agents grouped together have the same category, capacity, bid or ask schedule, and qualification status. A unit of mass means a fraction of the market; a type of mass \(\mu_t\) and capacity \(q_t\) represents \(\mu_t q_t\) task units. Individual bundles remain integral, while the aggregate allocation can be fractional.

The question is to return the exact rational prices, traded task mass, and type-level allocations produced by the split-market rule. Split each type’s mass equally between two arenas. For one arena, at price \(p\), compute demand as the total mass of requester units with bids at least \(p\), and supply as the total mass of qualified device units with asks at most \(p\). Choose a clearing price, allowing fractional participation by the marginal type mass when a discrete jump would otherwise skip equality. At the other arena, use that price to select eligible units, maximizing traded mass and resolving ties by a fixed rule among equal marginal bids or asks. Return a feasible matching \(x_{t,u}\) of requester-unit mass to qualified device-unit mass, together with payments at the crossing price; then reverse the arenas. The decision variables are the \(x_{t,u}\), and the objective in each arena is maximum traded task mass subject to capacities, qualification, and price eligibility.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for municipal sensor procurement: many devices carry the same sensor configuration and offer the same capacity and ask schedule, while recurring task providers submit batches of the same kind of measurement jobs under one of a few bid schedules. The number of agents can be very large while the support size \(\tau\)—the distinct complete types—remains moderate. The paper itself already groups agents by task category and assumes homogeneous tasks and decreasing marginal returns, so this move preserves its market structure. Replacing counts with mass also fits its multi-unit setting: the mechanism allocates task units across a large population, rather than choosing a few uniquely named people.

I expect this post-screening mirror to be Class A, tractable. With explicitly listed marginal schedules, aggregate demand and supply change only at the finitely many bid and ask breakpoints. Sorting those breakpoints and handling marginal mass at equality gives an exact algorithm polynomial in the number of listed marginal units, \(\tau\), and the rational input bit length. That is a proposed continuous result, not something Lemma 5.1 already proves. The direct bridge is the type-weighted version of the paper’s DMR multi-unit market-clearing core.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the allocation-and-pricing component of Lemma 5.1, with the quality screen supplied as part of each device type. It does not claim a continuum analogue of IoT-QDBC’s randomized peer-ranking procedure, or carry QUAD’s truthfulness, individual-rationality, and budget-balance proofs into the mass model. Further questions include whether the screening stage itself has a credible type-level limit, whether the mass mechanism retains strategic guarantees, how its outcomes round to large finite markets, and what changes when capacities are binary-encoded or valuations are not DMR.

The soft spot is the quality screen. QUAD selects individual devices using peer rankings, and compressing that operation into a qualification flag may hide behavior that matters when agents have identical auction schedules but different peer evaluations. The case survives as a focused mirror of QUAD’s second fold: the authors explicitly treat quality selection as a first phase and then run the double auction among the selected devices. A prover could analyze that market directly without claiming that the whole two-phase mechanism has already been continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The affirmative case’s only anchor is Lemma 5.1, which claims polynomial time for the complete QUAD mechanism. The proposed model supplies a quality-qualification flag as input and computes only the second fold. That leaves out IoT-QDBC, the first fold that selects the quality devices, so it does not mirror the computational claim the proponent named.

Preserving that fold requires representing more than the economic schedules and a qualification flag. IoT-QDBC samples individual devices and has peers rank those devices. A faithful type must therefore describe how an evaluator ranks sampled candidates. If those rankings refer to individual devices, the type space grows with the population and may have support close to the number of devices. If rankings instead refer to device types, devices in the same type become exchangeable, and the screening rule changes: the model replaces rankings over individual peers with rankings over types. Either way, the qualification flag skips the central selection process.

A more careful continuous model could represent the distribution of evaluator behavior and calculate each type’s probability of passing the screen. That would be a coherent new model, but its selection kernel and its input representation need to be specified. A simpler quality threshold or top fraction would also be coherent, but it would replace the paper’s random-panel Borda procedure. Neither is established by the proposed mirror, and each asks the authors to adopt a different quality mechanism before the second-fold market can be analyzed.

The second-fold market itself is plausible as a high-multiplicity object: repeated procurement could involve many devices with the same complete bid, ask, capacity, and quality status. But that is a conditional market-clearing problem, with the output of QUAD’s distinctive quality stage handed in as input. It may be worth studying on its own; it does not carry the full Lemma 5.1 anchor. The lemma’s stated runtime also lacks an encoding-sensitive bound for capacities, valuation schedules, and price increments, while its price loop assumes demand and supply reach equality. Allowing fractional marginal mass can make the clearing question well-defined, but that repairs the claimed computation as well as continuizing it.

So the strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror preserves the easy-to-state market while removing the stage that makes QUAD quality-aware. A full mirror must model identity-linked peer rankings or replace them with a new type-level screening rule. The negative case is weakest precisely at the isolated second fold: the paper presents it as a separate component, and authors could reasonably recognize its mass version as a useful question. I cannot honestly rule out that narrower mirror; I can argue that it does not preserve the named computational result.
