The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror on. Theorems 1 and 2 establish EFX existence or nonexistence for particular numbers of agents and goods; Theorems 3 and 4 establish further existence guarantees. The SAT and SMT experiments help prove those claims, but the paper does not name a result classifying the complexity of an underlying problem. Lemma 2 gives an allocation-improvement guarantee, but it cites the Plaut–Roughgarden algorithm and is not a complexity result. So I can’t honestly offer a qualifying anchor under the requested rule.

The closest subject-matter anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: for \(n\ge3\) agents and \(m\ge n+5\) goods, there is a fair division instance with submodular valuations that has no EFX allocation. The three-agent, eight-good counterexample is found with SAT solving; the paper extends it to the stated range of \(n\) and \(m\). This is a theorem about fairness existence, not computational complexity, so it can motivate a mirror but cannot serve as the required anchor.

A concrete high-multiplicity question motivated by it is Continuous EFX Feasibility. An instance has a finite goods catalog \(G\), a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(s_g\) for each good \(g\). Choose \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\), such that
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni g}x_{t,B}=s_g. \]
The question is whether such an allocation can satisfy EFX for every recipient: whenever \(x_{t,A}>0\) and some positive mass receives \(B\), require \(v_t(B\setminus\{g\})\le v_t(A)\) for every \(g\in B\). A solution is the mass assignment \(x\); each person still receives an indivisible bundle, while a type’s mass may be distributed across bundles.

A plausible regime is a large population of households receiving copies of a standardized catalog, with many households sharing the same full valuation and relatively few valuation types. The paper’s three-agent, eight-good example suggests a test case with three valuation types, equal masses, and supplies scaled to represent repeated copies of each good. Authors could recognize this as a high-multiplicity version of their EFX feasibility question: the fairness condition and integral bundles remain, while the population and inventories scale. The mirror would cover the EFX nonexistence question behind Theorem 2, not the paper’s results on relaxations.

My tentative prediction is continuum-specific hardness: choosing which bundles have positive mass is still combinatorial, and feasibility is only linear once that support is fixed. But the paper establishes no such hardness, and its discrete counterexample does not transfer automatically: a continuous type mass can split across bundles, which may remove the obstruction.

That is the weak point too. Theorem 2’s extension relies on a few special agents and goods assigned at individual granularity; those features may not survive the mass model. This is a plausible problem for the authors to study, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 settle existence questions for specified numbers of agents and goods; the SAT and SMT encodings are proof methods for those claims, not complexity results about an input problem. Theorem 2 is the proponent’s closest candidate, but it says that some finite instances lack an EFX allocation. It gives no algorithmic question or scalable complexity claim. On the programme’s stated criteria, that is a decisive mismatch.

The proposed repeated-catalog model is coherent: complete valuation types receive mass, goods have per-capita supplies, and each person still gets an integral bundle. It is a plausible high-multiplicity fair-division problem, and neither the use of types nor the scaling of supplies is itself a valid objection. But it is a new feasibility problem. The paper gives no discrete-to-continuous dictionary for it, and its finite counterexample does not supply a computational result that this model could mirror. Adding a complexity classification would create a research question adjacent to the paper, not continuize one of its results. The same applies to a stronger mirror based on Theorem 3’s tEFX or EF1\&EEFX existence guarantee: those are also structural existence theorems, not computational results.

That leaves the negative case weak on the broadest claim. A repeated-catalog EFX problem could be sensible and worth studying on its own; the paper’s lack of a complexity theorem does not show otherwise. I would argue against treating this paper as a good computational mirror for ChoCo, not that no continuous EFX question in any scenario could be worthwhile.
