The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), a listed support \(T\) of strict rankings, binary rational masses \(\mu_t \ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(\kappa\), is there a strict total order \(\sigma\) on \(C\) such that \(K_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_{\mathrm{KT}}(t,\sigma)\le\kappa\)?

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete ranking \(t\); the society assigns it mass \(\mu_t\). The decision variable is an aggregate order \(\sigma\), and the objective is its population-average Kendall–tau distance from the ranking types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s Kemeny Score result and Theorem 2’s winner, precedence, and recognition results on the equal-mass slice. It leaves Theorem 3’s Kendall–Tau Center result outside the proposed case.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the precise complexity bounds for the variable-rational-mass score and optimal-order queries as functions of \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think there is a credible mirror here, with Theorem 1 as the lead. The society is a finite support distribution over strict rankings of the candidate set: a type is one complete ranking, and its mass is the fraction of the population holding it. Given rational masses \(\mu_\pi\), an aggregate ranking \(\sigma\) has average Kendall–\(\tau\) loss

\[ K_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{\pi}\mu_\pi\,d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,\sigma). \]

The problem, which I would call Continuous Kemeny Score, is: given the candidates, the listed ranking types and their binary rational masses, and a rational threshold \(\kappa\), is there an aggregate order \(\sigma\) with \(K_\mu(\sigma)\le\kappa\)? A solution is such an order; the optimization form asks for one minimizing \(K_\mu\).

This is a society-continuity mirror: rankings and the aggregate remain discrete, while the population becomes mass. It fits a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large electorate divided into three stable preference blocs, each sharing one complete ranking. The number of people can be large while the number of types is three. Ranking is a complete type for this problem because Kemeny uses no other voter attributes.

Theorem 1, proved in this manuscript, is the lead anchor: Kemeny Score is NP-complete for exactly three unweighted full rankings, even when every candidate pair is split 2-to-1 among them. The proof also gives hard instances whose majority tournaments have majority dimension exactly three. For each such profile \(\Pi\), set \(\mu_\pi=1/3\) for its three rankings. Then \(K_\mu(\sigma)=K_\Pi(\sigma)/3\), so the original threshold question with threshold \(\kappa\) is exactly the continuous question with threshold \(\kappa/3\). The all-2-to-1 condition becomes a society in which each pairwise comparison has mass \(2/3\) on one side and \(1/3\) on the other. Thus the mirror is already hard with \(\tau=3\) and equal masses.

The expected direction is hardness transfers (Class B). The decision problem retains the candidates, aggregate orders, and loss being optimized; the population normalization just changes total loss into per-capita loss. Replicating the three voters equally many times gives an arbitrarily large finite electorate with the same society and the same optimal aggregates. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the candidate set, which is exactly the kind of hardness the continuization programme expects to survive. This also rules out an algorithm fixed parameter tractable in \(\tau\) alone unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\): the hard instances already have \(\tau=3\).

A second, related anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. On all-2-to-1 profiles of exactly three unweighted rankings, Kemeny Winner, Kemeny Unique Winner, Kemeny Possible Precedence, and Kemeny Necessary Precedence are \(\Theta_2^p\)-complete; Kemeny Consensus Recognition and Unique Kemeny Consensus Recognition are coNP-complete. Every hard instance has a directed triangle in its majority tournament, so its majority dimension is exactly three.

The continuous mirror is the same mass-weighted Kemeny objective, with these queries: whether a designated candidate is first in some optimal order, or in every optimal order; whether one candidate precedes another in some, or every, optimal order; and whether a supplied order is optimal, or uniquely optimal. The equal-mass, three-type instances reproduce the theorem’s restricted problems exactly, so those complexity classifications hold on that slice. For the full rational-mass versions, the theorem gives the corresponding hardness lower bounds; I would establish their precise upper complexity classes separately rather than assume that the unweighted theorem settles them. These questions remain about the authors’ underlying Kemeny optima, rather than about an encoding used to compute them.

The mirror covers the Kemeny Score result and the winner, precedence, and recognition results in Theorems 1 and 2. I would leave Theorem 3’s Kendall–Tau Center result outside this case: a continuous “maximum distance from any positive-mass type” version is definable, but its objective depends only on the support and ignores the masses. That is a weaker population mirror.

The soft spot is the regime itself. Three large blocs with identical complete rankings are plausible—for example, a large electorate following three stable party or institutional preference orders—but restrictive. And the central hardness result does not show that continuity creates tractability; it shows that this mirror stays hard. I still think that is a worthwhile positive case for the programme: it is a concrete, recognizable continuous-population problem, and it identifies a boundary where continuization does not remove hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror adds little new structure to the paper’s central problem. For Theorem 1’s hard instances, assigning mass \(1/3\) to each ranking gives exactly the same optimal orders and rescales every score by \(1/3\). The reduction, feasible aggregates, and source of hardness all remain untouched. Theorem 2’s winner, precedence, and recognition questions likewise reuse that same set of optimal orders. The paper proves hardness for three ranking records; the continuous proposal turns those records into three types with equal mass.

That critique weakens when tested against the programme’s purpose. A large electorate split among three stable preference blocs is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and ranking is a complete type for Kemeny aggregation. The mirror with arbitrary rational masses also asks a broader question than the equal-mass slice. Since a common denominator can be large relative to the binary input, representing the society by its type masses is a meaningful computational model. The paper’s hardness at \(\tau=3\) would establish that this model stays hard even with constant support. That is a relevant complexity boundary, even if the answer follows from a short reduction.

I cannot make a sound case that defeats either anchor. Theorem 1 survives in the natural mass-weighted Kemeny model, and Theorem 2’s queries are well-defined over the same population distribution. The objections available here—weak novelty of the equal-mass transfer, or the restrictive nature of three identical-ranking blocs—do not show that no plausible high-multiplicity scenario exists. The negative case is therefore weak. If the reader requires every proposed anchor to fail, both anchors survive.
