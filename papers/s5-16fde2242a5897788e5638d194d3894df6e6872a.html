The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational mass vector \(\mu\), promised click mass \(\rho\), residual type demands \(d\), and a type-symmetric polymatroid \(P_\mu=\{X\ge0:X(S)\le f_\mu(S)\ \forall S\subseteq T\}\) induced by per-capita keyword and slot data, compute exactly for each \(t\in T\) the type-mass clinch \(\delta_t=\max_{X\in P_{\rho,d}}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}X-\max_{X\in P_{\rho,d},\,X_t=0}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}X\), where \(P_{\rho,d}=\{X\ge0:\rho+X\in P_\mu,\ X\le d\}\), and determine whether the computation and its payments admit a consistent finite-clone lifting for \(N\mu_t\) identical advertisers.

The model it lives in

Advertisers are types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\), values \(v_t\), budgets \(B_t\), keyword interests, and quality factors; \(X_t\), \(\rho_t\), and \(d_t\) are aggregate click-mass variables, \(P_\mu\) is a per-capita-scaled AdWords polymatroid, and the objective is to compute the exact residual maximum defining \(\delta_t\), with optional clone-consistent payment lifting.

The objection that survived

The opponent correctly observes that type-level clinching is not the sum or limit of individual clone clinches and that individual truthfulness becomes non-pivotal in an atomless society; this blocks treating the mirror as a transferred theorem for the full auction.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the type-level version of Lemma 3.5 and can support a scaled AdWords implementation, but it does not establish Theorem 3.14's full truthful auction or mirror the paper's mechanism impossibilities.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(f_\mu\) for the scaled AdWords market be represented and evaluated in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, rather than hidden in an oracle?
  • Does the type-level clinching path have an exact finite event representation and polynomial bit complexity?
  • For every denominator \(N\), does it lift to the symmetric auction with \(N\mu_t\) clones, with convergent allocations, payments, and individual incentive interpretation?

The case FOR (proponent)

Yes: the paper supports a credible continuous mirror, with Theorem 3.14 as the lead anchor. The paper has no named NP-hardness result, so I would not invent a hardness mirror; the strongest case is an algorithmic Class A mirror.

The natural regime is sponsored search with many advertisers but few advertiser profiles. A type \(t\) records everything relevant to the auction: value per click \(v_t\), public budget \(B_t\), interested keywords \(\Gamma(t)\), and, where relevant, quality factors. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types. For example, a large advertising market may contain \(N\) advertisers but only \(\tau\) recurring campaign profiles, with \(N\gg\tau\). Keyword-query and slot supply must scale with \(N\), or equivalently be specified per capita; otherwise a fixed inventory would make the large-population limit degenerate.

The continuous object is not a fractionalization of the outcome space: the paper already auctions divisible clicks. What becomes continuous is the advertiser population. If \(N\mu_t\) is integral, type \(t\) represents \(N\mu_t\) identical advertisers. Their aggregate click allocation is \(X_t\), and their common per-advertiser allocation is \(X_t/\mu_t\).

My lead problem is Continuous Polymatroid Clinching Auction. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), values and budgets, and a type-symmetric polymatroidal allocation environment

\[ P_\mu=\left\{X\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{T}:X(S)\le f_\mu(S)\text{ for every }S\subseteq T\right\}, \]

where \(f_\mu\) is a monotone submodular rank function, supplied explicitly or through the same value-oracle model used in the paper. In the AdWords specialization, \(P_\mu\) is the per-capita aggregate click-allocation region induced by the keyword and slot data.

The decision variable is the amount of click mass promised to each type during an ascending-price process. If \(\rho\) is the current promised allocation and \(d\) is the aggregate demand vector, define

\[ P_{\rho,d}=\{X\ge0:\rho+X\in P_\mu,\ X\le d\}. \]

Type \(t\) may clinch

\[ \delta_t=\sup\{z\ge0:P_{\rho,d}^{\,t}(z)=P_{\rho,d}^{\,t}(0)\}, \]

namely, the largest allocation to \(t\) that does not reduce the feasible demand set of the other types. The process updates \(\rho_t\), remaining aggregate budget \(\mu_tB_t\), and prices until it terminates. A solution is the final allocation \(X\), aggregate payments \(P_t\), and a finite description of the price and clinching events, satisfying:

  • \(X\in P_\mu\);
  • each type’s total payment is at most \(\mu_tB_t\);
  • every positive-mass advertiser receives nonnegative utility;
  • the induced rule is truthful under the clone interpretation; and
  • the outcome is Pareto-optimal among feasible allocations and payments.

Truthfulness is not interpreted as a vacuous deviation by an atomless individual. The rule must be clone-consistent: for every denominator \(N\), expanding \(\mu\) into \(N\mu_t\) identical advertisers must produce a symmetric finite auction, and a one-advertiser misreport must be tested by the corresponding \(1/N\) change in the reported mass vector.

The anchor is Theorem 3.14, proved in this paper. It states that, for every polymatroidal environment, Algorithm 1 together with the clinching rule of Definition 3.2 has all the paper’s desirable properties: truthfulness, individual rationality, budget feasibility, and Pareto-optimality. The computational support is Lemma 3.5, also proved here: in a polymatroid, the clinched amount equals the difference between the maximum total feasible residual allocation with and without the relevant player, and this quantity can be computed efficiently using submodular minimization. The AdWords application is connected by Theorem 4.2, which proves that the AdWords feasible-allocation region is itself a polymatroid.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, subject to a genuine implementation proof. The paper’s structural reason for tractability survives type aggregation: clinching is still characterized by polymatroid rank differences, and the pricing problem is still submodular minimization. The expected gain is that the computation depends on \(\tau\), the number of advertiser types, rather than \(N\), the number of named advertisers. The remaining proof obligations are nontrivial: polynomially encode the price path, establish bit-complexity bounds, show that the aggregate rank oracle is efficiently computable from the AdWords description, and prove exact clone lifting and rounding.

This is author-recognizable because the model preserves their actual object: budget-constrained advertisers, divisible clicks, polymatroidal feasibility, ascending prices, clinching, truthfulness, and Pareto optimality. It does not replace the auction with welfare maximization or with a fractional allocation problem unrelated to their mechanism.

I would not claim that the whole paper has been continuized. Theorem 5.3 and Theorem 5.11 provide useful boundary questions: the impossibility for general polyhedral environments, and the impossibility of truthful, Pareto-optimal, budget-feasible auctions with decreasing marginal values. But those are structural impossibility results, not NP-hardness anchors. My weakest point is therefore that Theorem 3.14 is a mechanism-design theorem rather than an explicit polynomial-time complexity classification. The positive mirror is nevertheless substantial: it turns the paper’s polymatroid auction into a genuine high-multiplicity computational problem whose natural expected answer is a type-level submodular algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchor is not actually a computational result of the paper. Theorem 3.14 is a correctness theorem for a finite-bidder mechanism; Theorem 4.2 is a structural characterization of a feasible allocation polytope. Lemma 3.5 is the only serious computational candidate, but it computes one clinching subroutine in a submodular-function oracle model. It gives neither a finite encoding of Algorithm 1’s continuously ascending price path nor a polynomial-time bound for the complete exact auction. Thus “Continuous Polymatroid Clinching Auction” would be a new research problem inspired by the paper, not a continuization of a named complexity result.

More seriously, the proposed type aggregation does not preserve the paper’s clinching operation. For a finite clone group \(G_t\), the original \(\delta_i\) is the largest amount bidder \(i\) can receive without reducing the demand set of every other bidder, including the other members of \(G_t\). A type-level \(\delta_t\) preserves only the demand of other types. These are different operations. With \(N\) identical bidders competing for a uniform supply, an individual cannot clinch while the remaining \(N-1\) clones still require the supply; after merging them into one type-player, that same population can immediately clinch the residual supply. The aggregate feasible region may remain a polymatroid, but the price path, payments, and strategic meaning of clinching do not.

The nonatomic limit makes this problem sharper. A single advertiser has measure zero and cannot affect aggregate demand, so individual truthfulness becomes vacuous. A report by an entire type is instead a coalition deviation, which is stronger and different from the paper’s dominant-strategy condition. The proposed “clone consistency” does not repair this inside the continuum: it asks for a compatible family of finite-\(N\) mechanisms, together with convergence, tie-breaking, payment, and rounding theorems. That could be worthwhile finite-size research, but it is not supplied by Theorem 3.14.

Scaling click supply with population avoids the legitimate degeneracy of fixed inventory, but it also introduces a new sequence of AdWords environments or a per-capita slot-supply curve. If \(f_\mu\) is simply supplied as an oracle, the problem is just the paper’s theorem applied to \(\tau\) aggregate players; the population has disappeared into the oracle. If \(f_\mu\) is derived from clones, proving efficient derivation and clone-consistent payments is precisely the missing substantive work. Theorem 4.2 establishes feasibility, not that this strategic aggregation is valid.

Theorem 5.3 and Theorem 5.11 do not rescue the proposal: they are mechanism impossibilities, not computational classifications, and their force also depends on individual budget exhaustion and pivotality, both of which change in an atomless society.

This is a strong reason to reject the proposed mirror as an established ChoCo anchor: it is an attractive new mechanism-design direction, but the paper does not furnish the claimed continuous computational result, and its central strategic object is not invariant under type aggregation. The negative case is not universal, however. A carefully specified finite-clone limit with scaled supply and a proved convergence theorem could be a worthwhile mirror. I therefore cannot honestly claim that every possible scenario fails; only that this paper does not presently support the proponent’s Class A conclusion.
