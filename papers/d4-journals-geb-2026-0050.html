The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that a continuous-population model of streaming is plausible: let a type be a user’s complete stream profile \(x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{N}\setminus\{0\}\), and let \(\mu_x\) be that profile’s share of users. Then an index built from per-user contributions \(d\) has aggregate importance \(J_i(\mu)=\sum_x\mu_x d(i,x)\), with rewards normalized to the platform’s total subscription revenue. A large platform could have many users but relatively few distinct profiles.

But this paper provides no named computational result to anchor a continuous computational mirror. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–3 characterize families of indices through axioms; they do not establish tractability, hardness, or an algorithm for a computational problem. Computing \(J_i(\mu)\) for a specified \(d\) is just aggregation, not a mirrored result from the paper. A continuous recharacterization of those axioms would be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. So there is no defensible prover-ready problem per qualifying anchor here. The missing computational anchor is the decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to continue. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–3 characterize which indices satisfy axioms and how those families intersect. None poses an algorithmic problem or gives a complexity result. The proposed distribution over user profiles is a coherent population model, but \(J_i(\mu)=\sum_x\mu_xd(i,x)\) is an aggregation formula, not a computational question anchored in one of those results.

The strongest computational extension would be to ask how quickly one can calculate rewards for a finite-support distribution of profiles. For a specified, efficiently evaluable \(d\), that amounts to evaluating \(d\) once per supported type and summing the weighted contributions. For the Shapley-induced family, the per-type contribution may itself require a Shapley-value computation. The paper gives no computational representation of the games \(v^{j,x}\), so a complexity problem there would require adding a representation and encoding assumptions. It would be a new algorithmic study of those games, with population mass used to aggregate its outputs.

There is also a type-space tension. A complete user type must retain the stream vector over all artists, whose entries are unbounded integers; coarser categories such as genre or listening pattern can discard information the indices use. Exact profiles could repeat in some platform regimes, but the paper specifies neither a bounded type universe nor a computational task that benefits from repeated profiles.

This makes the negative case strong for the paper’s actual results: none supplies a prover-ready computational mirror, and the obvious reward-calculation extension does not fill that gap. The universal claim remains weaker. A future paper could define a worthwhile optimization or decision problem over profile distributions. That would need additional objectives, representations, and results; it would be a new computational question inspired by this paper rather than a continuous mirror of its characterizations.
