The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational distribution \(\rho\) on shares \(x_i\in[0,1]\), with \(\pi=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[X]\in(0,1)\), and target interim probabilities \((Q(a),Q(b))\), does there exist an anonymous rule \(q_i\in[0,1]\) at each support point such that \(Q(a)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Xq(X)]/\pi\) and \(Q(b)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[(1-X)q(X)]/(1-\pi)\)?

The model it lives in

Each state is a unit-mass continuum society with two complete preference types and masses \(\mu^x=(x,1-x)\); a common prior \(\rho\) over states supplies the aggregate uncertainty. The decision variables are the rule values \(q(x_i)\), and the question is feasibility for the requested interim allocation pair.

The objection that survived

The prior \(\rho\) over population shares is an added input beyond ChoCo’s default single known society, and the paper does not establish that its authors would accept this common-shock regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s reduced-form implementability characterization and proposes a continuum Rawlsian counterpart to Proposition 1; it leaves the paper’s OBIC, unanimity, and prior-condition results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the hinge characterization remain exact for arbitrary, possibly non-finitely supported, priors over population shares?
  • Can finite-agent exchangeable implementation polytopes be shown to converge to this continuum feasibility region with quantitative bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror exists, but it needs one careful change of regime. In a single deterministic continuum society with type masses \((\pi,1-\pi)\), an anonymous rule sees the same aggregate in every state, so both types receive the same lottery: \(Q(a)=Q(b)\). That collapses the paper’s two-dimensional reduced-form question. The stronger mirror keeps the population continuous in every state and lets the aggregate composition vary under a common prior.

For example, take a large referendum electorate with two complete types: voters who rank \(a\) over \(b\), and voters who rank \(b\) over \(a\). Each possible state is a unit-mass continuum society with fraction \(x\) of type \(a\) and \(1-x\) of type \(b\). Let \(X\sim\rho\) be the uncertain aggregate share, where \(\rho\) has finite rational support and \(\pi=\mathbb E[X]\in(0,1)\). There are many agents and just two types; within each type, agents are indistinguishable. An anonymous voting rule is \(q(x)\in[0,1]\), the probability of choosing \(a\) when the reported type-\(a\) mass is \(x\). The outcomes remain the two discrete alternatives.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that under an exchangeable prior over \(n\) finite-agent profiles, with \(B(k)\) the probability of exactly \(k\) type-\(a\) agents, target interim probabilities \((Q(a),Q(b))\) are implementable by a symmetric voting rule if and only if, for every \(j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}\),
\[ j(1-\pi)Q(b)-(n-j)\pi Q(a)+\sum_{k=j}^{n}(k-j)B(k)\ge 0, \]
and
\[ (n-j)\pi Q(a)-j(1-\pi)Q(b)+\sum_{k=0}^{j}(j-k)B(k)\ge 0. \]
This is a characterization of the underlying implementation problem, not a complexity-class result.

The mirror is Continuum Symmetric Reduced-Form Implementation. Given \(\rho\) and a target pair \((Q(a),Q(b))\), determine whether there is a measurable anonymous rule \(q:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) such that
\[ Q(a)=\frac{\mathbb E[Xq(X)]}{\pi}, \qquad Q(b)=\frac{\mathbb E[(1-X)q(X)]}{1-\pi}. \]
A solution is the rule’s probabilities at the support points of \(\rho\), or a certificate that none exists. Its exact characterization is, for every \(c\in[0,1]\),
\[ -\mathbb E[(c-X)_+] \le (1-c)\pi Q(a)-c(1-\pi)Q(b) \le \mathbb E[(X-c)_+]. \]
These are the paper’s two families of inequalities with the quota fraction \(j/n\) replaced by a mass threshold \(c\). With finite rational support, the problem also has a direct linear-program formulation with one variable \(q(x_i)\) per support point. I expect this mirror to be tractable.

A second anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. Among monotone voting rules, the ex-ante Rawlsian rule maximizes
\[ \min\{\pi Q(a),(1-\pi)(1-Q(b))\}. \]
The proposition says that an optimum is a convex combination of qualified-majority rules with quotas \(j^*\) and \(j^*+1\), where
\[ j^*=\max\left\{j:\sum_{k=j}^{n}B(k)\ge 1-\pi\right\}. \]
It gives the resulting interim probabilities as
\[ Q_R(a)=\frac{j^*(1-\pi)+\sum_{k=j^*}^{n}(k-j^*)B(k)}{n\pi}, \qquad Q_R(b)=\frac{(n-j^*)(1-\pi)-\sum_{k=j^*}^{n}(k-j^*)B(k)}{n(1-\pi)}. \]

Its mirror, Continuum Rawlsian Voting, takes \(\rho\) as input and chooses a nondecreasing \(q\) to maximize
\[ \min\left\{\mathbb E[Xq(X)],\ \mathbb E[(1-X)(1-q(X))]\right\}. \]
An optimal rule is a threshold: choose \(a\) with probability \(1\) above some \(c\), probability \(0\) below it, and possibly randomize at \(c\). Choose \(c\) so that
\[ \rho(X>c)\le 1-\pi\le \rho(X\ge c); \]
if there is an atom at \(c\), set \(q(c)=(1-\pi-\rho(X>c))/\rho(X=c)\). This is the continuous counterpart of mixing adjacent quotas. I expect it to be tractable by sorting the support points and finding the cutoff.

The fit is supported by the paper’s focus on exchangeable priors and its large-economy section. In a continuum society, the population objective has a direct interpretation: expected masses of satisfied type-\(a\) and type-\(b\) voters. The threshold rule is also something the authors could plausibly recognize as their quota rule expressed in population shares.

The weakest point is the prior over aggregate compositions. The ChoCo base model usually gives one society \(\mu\), while this mirror gives a prior \(\rho\) over continuum societies \(\mu^x=(x,1-x)\). It is a natural continuum analogue of exchangeability, especially for infinitely extendible exchangeable priors, but it is an added regime restriction. Also, an individual has zero mass in the continuum, so the paper’s strategy-proof interpretation of monotonicity does not carry over literally; the Rawlsian mirror preserves monotone population responsiveness, not a nontrivial individual incentive constraint. If the authors reject this prior over population states, the reduced-form mirror loses its two-dimensional content.

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. These anchors are its implementation characterization and optimization result, rather than complexity-class claims. Useful next questions are whether broader exchangeable-prior classes retain the hinge characterization, how finite-\(n\) solutions approximate the continuum rules, and how the mirror changes with more than two alternatives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a scope boundary. ChoCo’s base object is one known society \(\mu\). In a deterministic atomless society with type-\(a\) mass \(\pi\), an anonymous rule sees only that fixed aggregate. It gives both types the same lottery, so \(Q(a)=Q(b)=q(\pi)\). Theorem 1’s two-dimensional implementation question collapses to a diagonal, and Proposition 1’s objective reduces to choosing \(q\) to maximize \(\min\{\pi q,(1-\pi)(1-q)\}\), whose optimum is \(q=1-\pi\).

The proponent’s stronger mirror restores the lost content by adding a prior \(\rho\) over population compositions. That is mathematically coherent: each state is a continuum society, and conditioning on a voter’s type gives the stated interim probabilities. But it adds uncertainty over societies to ChoCo’s single-society input. Continuizing the population does not itself require that extra prior. If the programme’s scope excludes random population states, the proposed mirror is outside its central model.

That boundary is not decisive, though. The paper already studies exchangeable priors, and the paper’s large-economy discussion makes a continuum of aggregate compositions a plausible limit regime. Theorem 1 is also a named characterization of a concrete feasibility problem, even though it does not state a complexity classification. Its continuous counterpart is not merely an axiomatic question: with finite support for \(\rho\), it is an LP over the rule’s values at the support points. I cannot honestly rule out that the authors would recognize this as their implementation problem.

Proposition 1 has a similar weakness in the deterministic case, but its stronger mirror also survives the main objection. In a continuum, individual reports have zero effect on aggregate mass, so monotonicity no longer carries the paper’s strategyproofness meaning. Yet the Rawlsian optimization over population-responsive rules remains sensible; under \(\rho\), a threshold can balance the expected satisfied masses. That is a different incentive interpretation, not a fundamental failure of the optimization mirror.

So the negative case depends on a strict reading of scope: one fixed society only, with no prior over societies. Under that reading both anchors simplify sharply. Under the proponent’s common-shock regime, both have credible continuous versions. I don’t see an honest argument that defeats those stronger versions across all scenarios; the case against is therefore weak.
