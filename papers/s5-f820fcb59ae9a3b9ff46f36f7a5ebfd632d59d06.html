What the mirror covers

Theorem 2’s large-network approximation supports the population regime; Theorems 1 and 3 analyze equilibrium selection and convergence. None states a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The regime itself is a strong fit for high multiplicity: a large social network can have many users but only two broad blocks, with interaction rates and initial opinion types shared within each block. Theorem 2, proved here, shows that in a dense two-block stochastic block model, agents’ opinions eventually lie close to the corresponding two-agent system’s equilibrium with probability tending to one. That is good evidence that a population-level model is sensible.

But this paper has no named computational complexity result to anchor the requested case. Its nearest results are analytic: Theorem 1 characterizes when the two-agent dynamics reach consensus or persistent disagreement; Theorem 2 proves the stochastic-block approximation; and Theorem 3 proves convergence under a symmetric influence matrix. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

A continuous-population version asking for the limiting opinions of mass in two blocks would be natural, but it would restate the paper’s mean-field analysis. The paper does not pose the missing computational question, such as the complexity of computing or controlling outcomes for a distribution of agent types. So the positive case is that the population regime is plausible and Theorem 2 supports its mean-field interpretation; there are no eligible anchors, and therefore no anchor-specific continuous problems to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is decisive: this paper states no computational problem and proves no computational complexity result. The proponent names Theorems 1–3, but correctly observes that they are analytic results, then supplies no continuous computational question for any of them. On the programme’s terms, there is no computational anchor here to continuize.

Theorem 2 is the best evidence that a population regime makes sense: a large two-block network is approximated by a two-agent dynamical system. That supports high multiplicity, but the result concerns mean-field approximation and limiting opinions, not algorithms for a society represented by a distribution over types. Treating the two representative agents as masses would produce a population version of the same analytic dynamics, not a missing computational problem. This is not a novelty collision; it simply does not identify computational work for the programme.

Theorem 1 classifies the equilibria of a two-agent ODE from the initial opinions and model parameters. Theorem 3 proves convergence using a Lyapunov function. Their natural population versions ask about equilibrium structure and convergence—the kinds of analytic questions the programme distinguishes from computational complexity. A richer type space could include initial opinion, platform strength, and network position, but then the paper’s two- or three-block approximation no longer supplies a computational task over those types.

The strongest improved proposal would be to choose a platform policy that keeps limiting population polarization below a threshold, given a distribution of initial opinions and network types. That could be a worthwhile new research question. But this paper fixes the platform behavior and studies the resulting dynamics; it defines no policy objective or intervention problem. Adding one would extend the model into optimization or control, rather than mirror a computational result in the paper. The authors themselves identify welfare, incentives, and interventions as future research.

So the proponent’s evidence establishes that a high-multiplicity setting is plausible, not that this paper offers a worthwhile continuous computational mirror. The negative case is strong for a mirror *of this paper within ChoCo*. It is weaker as the universal claim that no computational project could ever grow out of the model: policy optimization or more general network dynamics might warrant one, but that would be a different project.
