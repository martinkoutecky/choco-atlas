The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many institutions and a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type fixes vertical eligibility, a laminar horizontal eligibility set, preferences over institution-category contracts, and a merit tier at each institution, with capacities and reservation minima given as rational cohort shares, compute an assignment \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\), eligibility, capacities, and reservations. Does a fractional generalized deferred-acceptance process always compute an assignment with no positive-mass blocking reassignment under the fractional hierarchical one-to-all choice rule and no unjustified envy under weak merit priorities, and what is its complexity in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), the number of horizontal types, and input bit length?

The model it lives in

A finite-support population distribution over complete applicant types; assignment variables \(x_{t,a}\) allocate each type's mass across acceptable institution-category contracts and the outside option. Capacities and hierarchical reservation minima scale with cohort mass, and the task is to find a stable, justified-envy-free assignment rather than optimize a separate objective.

The objection that survived

The paper assumes strict individual priorities, while aggregation into repeated types needs merit ties, and atomless individual strategy-proofness is vacuous; the mirror therefore does not preserve that part of Theorem 3.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's stable and justified-envy-free allocation guarantees; it leaves aside nontrivial individual strategy-proofness, the merit-undominated characterization, fairness results, and the soft-reserve extension.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fractional generalized deferred acceptance terminate with a stable, justified-envy-free assignment for every such finite-support instance?
  • Can it be implemented in polynomial time in the support size, institutions, horizontal types, and input bit length?
  • Which nontrivial incentive notion, if any, should replace individual strategy-proofness for coordinated deviations by positive mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s deferred-acceptance result, but there is an important limit: the paper contains no named theorem classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. The closest anchor is Theorem 3, proved here using a cited result about generalized sequential choice rules: under the aggregate choice rules \((\mathcal C_s^h)_{s\in S}\), the generalized deferred-acceptance mechanism \(\Phi^h\) is stable, strategy-proof for individuals, and eliminates justified envy. The paper gives an explicit procedure for computing its outcome, so this is a constructive algorithmic result; its statement does not analyze running time. A polynomial bound for the finite procedure follows from the proposal process and choice-rule implementation, but should not be presented as a complexity theorem proved by the authors.

The plausible regime is a large public-university admissions cycle. Applicants are numerous, while many share a full profile: vertical category, nested horizontal eligibility, preferences over institution-category pairs, and a merit-score tier. Thus \(n\), the number of applicants, can greatly exceed \(\tau\), the number of distinct profiles. Capacities and reservation minima are given as rational shares of the cohort. The mass representation is honest here: each person still receives one seat or no seat, while a profile’s aggregate assignment can be fractional. Splitting a merit tier at a capacity cutoff is a necessary co-modification, since members of that profile are indistinguishable.

The best candidate mirror is Continuous Hierarchical Deferred Acceptance. An instance consists of a finite institution set, vertical categories and laminar horizontal eligibility sets; a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); and rational per-capita capacities and horizontal quotas. Each type \(t\) fully specifies vertical eligibility, its horizontal set \(\rho(t)\), its strict preferences over acceptable institution-category contracts, and its merit tier at each institution. Apply the paper’s open-first vertical precedence and hierarchical one-to-all choice rule fractionally, allowing a type’s mass to be split at a cutoff.

The question is to compute an assignment \(x_{t,a}\ge0\), where \(a\) is an acceptable contract or the outside option, with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\), that respects eligibility, capacities, and reservations; is stable against any positive-mass reassignment to contracts the affected applicants prefer, when institutions apply the fractional hierarchical choice rule; and eliminates justified envy under weak priority comparisons, so equal-merit members of a split tier do not count as having justified envy. The expected outcome is Class A: with finite support, a mass version of deferred acceptance appears to need only finitely many type-contract proposals, suggesting time polynomial in \(|S|\), \(\tau\), the number of horizontal types, and the input bit length. That is a proposed result, not one established in this paper.

This mirror covers Theorem 3’s stable, envy-free allocation result. It does not attempt to continuize the paper’s separate characterization of the unique merit-undominated choice rule, its fairness theorem, or its soft-reserve extension. Further questions include proving a polynomial bound for mass deferred acceptance, relating its output to discrete markets with \(n_t/n=\mu_t\), and bounding envy or quota violations when a mass assignment is rounded to integral seats.

The weakest point is that the paper’s strategy-proofness result is about individual agents, while an individual in an atomless population has zero influence; that part of the theorem can become vacuous. The type-level mass problem also requires ties and fractional cutoffs the paper does not study. So the setting is a credible population-continuous extension of the authors’ matching problem, but the strict anchor test remains a problem: Theorem 3 is a constructive mechanism theorem, not a named complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 3 establishes properties of a mechanism: stability, individual strategy-proofness, and elimination of justified envy. The deferred acceptance procedure is explicit, but the paper does not pose or classify a computational problem. Asking for a running-time bound for that procedure would be an efficiency analysis of an algorithm the paper already gives, not a continuous mirror of a complexity result. A mass version could be an interesting new market-design question, but that is a different contribution from the computational gap ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed mirror also loses one of Theorem 3’s central claims. In an atomless society, a single applicant has zero mass, so changing their reported preferences cannot affect the outcome. Individual strategy-proofness is then vacuous. Treating an entire type’s mass as the reporting agent gives the type a coordinated deviation; that is a different incentive concept. Stability and envy can still be studied for mass assignments, but they do not preserve the full result the affirmative case chose as its anchor.

There is a related obstacle to its high-multiplicity story. The paper assumes strict institutional rankings of individuals. Under that assumption, applicants who share preferences and reservation eligibility can still differ in merit priority, which matters to the choice rule and to justified envy. The proposed “merit tier” and fractional cutoff replace those strict priorities with ties. That is a plausible variant for some admissions settings, but it changes a central input to the theorem rather than merely aggregating interchangeable applicants. A continuous score distribution is another possibility, but it leaves the paper’s finite-support type model and requires a different computational input framework.

The strongest countercase is therefore narrow: the mass deferred-acceptance proposal is conceivable, but it is not a continuation of a computational result in this paper, and preserving the theorem’s individual incentive claim conflicts with making individuals negligible. The negative case is not airtight. Large applicant cohorts with genuinely shared profiles and tied merit scores could support a worthwhile fractional matching study; it would just be a new model-design project rather than the computational mirror this paper supplies.
