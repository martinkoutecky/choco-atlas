The strongest candidate for a better mirror is voting power in a population with many repeated weight classes. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: people with the same voting weight could be treated as one type. But the measures at the heart of the paper ask whether one individual can change an outcome. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass, so changing their vote cannot change the aggregate; individual pivotality collapses. Treating an entire type as a coordinated bloc preserves nonzero influence, but changes the subject from an individual’s power to a group’s power. Rescaling individual influence to recover a limit would require additional choices about quota scaling, random voting, and normalization. Those could motivate a new asymptotic study, but they do not provide a continuous computational mirror of a result in this paper.

That leaves a strong case against a mirror *of this paper*: its subject is not a computational problem, and its central notion of influence loses its individual unit in the continuous limit. The broader claim that no worthwhile project could ever connect continuous populations and voting power would be too strong; a carefully specified study of limiting influence might be valuable. But it would be a follow-on to the paper’s topic, not a continuization of one of its computational results.
