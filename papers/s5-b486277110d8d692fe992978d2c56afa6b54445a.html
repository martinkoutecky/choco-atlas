What the mirror covers

The proposed article-mass lift covers only Theorem 4.1's IA–SC incompatibility; it supplies no computational task, and the paper's other results and discussion provide no computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population interpretation I can defend is to treat papers in a large citation corpus as the agents. A type records a paper’s source journal and its full citation vector across the journal set; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of papers of type \(t\). The induced per-paper citation matrix is
\[ c_{ij}(\mu)=\sum_{t:\,s(t)=J_j}\mu_t a_t(i), \]
where \(a_t(i)\) is the number of citations a type-\(t\) paper makes to \(J_i\). A plausible regime is a large corpus with many papers but relatively few repeated citation profiles, so the number of papers is much larger than the support size \(\tau\).

The closest anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: *there is no scoring procedure that is both invariant to aggregation and self-consistent.* Its proof uses the paper’s four-journal example and its three-journal version after merging \(J_3\) and \(J_4\); both examples lie in the stated balanced, unweighted, loopless, extremal domain. In the four-journal case, self-consistency forces \(J_1\succ J_2\); invariance to aggregation carries that ordering to the merged problem, where self-consistency forces a tie, a contradiction.

The corresponding candidate is Article-mass IA–SC existence. Its domain is all finite-support rational article populations of the kind above. The question is whether one scoring rule, depending on the induced journal citation matrix, can satisfy the paper’s aggregation-invariance and self-consistency axioms throughout that domain. A solution would be such a rule; a negative certificate would be a finite set of populations and journal merges whose axiom constraints contradict one another. Theorem 4.1 supplies a certificate: take four article types, one for each source journal, each with mass \(1/4\), and give the type from \(J_j\) citation vector \(4(C_{1j},\ldots,C_{4j})\), using the theorem’s matrix \(C\). These are integer citation vectors and induce exactly \(C\). Replicating each type arbitrarily many times gives a high-multiplicity corpus with four types. Merging journals \(3\) and \(4\) induces the theorem’s reduced matrix. So the impossibility survives this population representation.

That is a real, if narrow, positive case: the citation matrix can be understood as an aggregate of many same-type agents, and the theorem’s witness fits a small-support population exactly. The weakest point is also decisive for ChoCo: this remains an axiomatic existence question. Theorem 4.1 does not classify the complexity of computing a ranking or solving an algorithmic problem, and the paper contains no named result asserting tractability, hardness, or another computational classification. Thus I cannot offer a qualifying computational anchor or assign this candidate to the programme’s tractable / hardness-transfer / continuum-specific-hard trichotomy. The candidate may be a useful population reinterpretation of the theorem, but it is not a ChoCo computational mirror under the stated criteria. Further axiomatic questions include how small a population witness can be and whether the contradiction persists under approximate versions of the two axioms.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is the proposed Article-mass IA–SC existence question. Its population model is plausible: papers can be agents, citation profiles can be types, and the four-type construction reproduces the theorem’s matrix exactly. Replicating those types gives a high-multiplicity corpus. I would not object that this lift fails, or that a surviving impossibility result is uninteresting.

The problem is that the lift is still an axiomatic existence question, not a computational one. Theorem 4.1 quantifies over scoring procedures and shows their axioms are incompatible; it gives no input-output task whose complexity could be classified. The four-type witness certifies the axiom conflict, but does not turn it into an algorithmic problem over populations. One could define a new task—say, minimize a chosen measure of IA–SC violation, or verify the axioms for an encoded scoring rule—but that requires new choices of objective and representation. It would be a follow-up, not a mirror of the result the proponent anchored on. The paper has no other named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo computational mirror, while being candid that the broader claim—no worthwhile continuous follow-up in any scenario—is stronger than the evidence supports. The article population is a sensible regime; the failure is that this paper’s result gives ChoCo no computational question to classify.
