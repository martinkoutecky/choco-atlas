The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchor-backed positive case under your criteria: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class.

The closest results are Theorem 1 (proved here), which says that with non-overlapping HR traits the four stated axioms characterize the 2SMG choice rule; Theorem 2 (proved here), which characterizes the single-category meritorious horizontal choice rule by maximal HR accommodation, no justified envy, and non-wastefulness; and Theorem 3 (proved here), which says that with overlapping traits the four axioms characterize the 2SMH choice rule. These are axiomatic characterizations, not complexity results. The paper does describe a matching-based procedure, and says the alternative one-to-all convention is computationally hard in general, but neither supplies a numbered complexity result for me to use as an anchor.

The most promising *unanchored* mirror would concern large public recruitment cohorts: represent applicants by mass over vertical category, HR traits, and merit classes; choose mass for each position category, with an HR matching assigning each selected unit to at most one protected trait. But the paper assumes distinct merit scores, so grouping many applicants into a small number of complete types is not a clean fit. I would not present that as a prover-ready mirror of one of the paper’s named computational results: there is no qualifying result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes 2SMG, Theorem 2 characterizes the single-category meritorious horizontal rule, and Theorem 3 characterizes 2SMH. Each is an axiomatic result about which choice rule satisfies specified properties; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s observation that the alternative one-to-all convention can be computationally hard is not a formal complexity result. Its chosen one-to-one convention instead uses maximum bipartite matching as a tool. So none of the three theorems supplies a ChoCo-style complexity anchor.

The best candidate for a new question would be a large recruitment cohort, with applicants represented by mass over category, HR traits, and merit, and position supplies scaled with the population. One could ask how to compute the resulting mass allocation. But exact merit is central to the paper: it assumes distinct scores, and says ties are broken to produce a strict ranking. Since merit is part of an applicant’s complete type, preserving that ranking gives each applicant a distinct type. The support then grows with the applicant count; the proposed compression does not arise. Combining applicants into merit bands would make repeated types possible, but would discard distinctions the paper uses to define its choices and envy comparisons.

A nonatomic merit distribution creates a different problem. An individual has zero mass, so replacing one selected person with one rejected person leaves the mass allocation unchanged. The paper’s no-justified-envy test, which turns on whether that individual replacement reduces HR accommodation, no longer expresses the original condition. Replacing a positive mass of one type with another could define a sensible new condition, but it would be a type-level axiom for fractional cohorts, not a continuization of the paper’s individual-level characterization. Scaling positions and quotas with population size is an allowed co-modification, but it does not resolve this change in what the axioms test.

There may be a worthwhile market-design project in extending these rules to large cohorts. The weakness in the negative case is that such an extension could preserve their policy purpose and interest the authors. But it would be a newly formulated mass-allocation problem, not a continuous mirror of any of the paper’s three named results. On the evidence here, none of the proposed anchors survives as a ChoCo computational mirror.
