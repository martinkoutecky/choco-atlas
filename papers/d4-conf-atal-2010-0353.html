The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the central problem: none of the paper’s named results is a computational result in ChoCo’s sense. Theorem 1 proves convergence under myopic play; Theorem 2 proves a Nash equilibrium; Theorem 3 characterizes equilibrium payoffs. None poses an algorithmic problem whose complexity or exact, approximate, or parameterized solution could be charted. The paper’s continuous-action extension still has a finite population, so it does not supply that missing anchor either.

Theorem 1 is the strongest possible candidate for a mirror. A natural version would replace the finite goal list with a distribution over goal types and let each type receive updates in a population sweep. The finite partition points \(i/n\) then have a natural continuous counterpart: uniform mass on \([0,1]\). For a finite-support input distribution, finding the resulting median is a sorting and scan over the \(\tau\) goal values. That may be a sensible analytic limit of the convergence theorem, but it does not expose the algorithmic landscape ChoCo is meant to study; any harder computational question would depend on an added representation or schedule absent from the paper.

Theorem 2 does not fare better under that limit. In an atomless population, one individual has measure zero, so a unilateral change cannot affect the aggregate path. The Nash condition therefore loses the strategic force it has in the finite game. Letting an entire goal type coordinate a deviation could restore influence, but that changes the question from individual Nash equilibrium to coalition stability.

Theorem 3 depends on a finite payoff vector indexed by players and on punishments triggered by a named player’s deviation. A continuum replacement would need a new payoff object and a new enforcement model; measure-zero deviations again make individual enforceability vacuous, while type-level deviations are coalitional. That could motivate a different game-theoretic project, but it is not a computational mirror of the stated folk theorem.

So the strongest continuous reading of this paper is a plausible mean-field analysis of its dynamics. That is the honest limit of the negative case: such an analysis might be worthwhile in a programme about continuous-agent games. But under ChoCo’s computational remit, the paper supplies no qualifying computational result, and its most natural continuous extensions either reduce to a simple quantile calculation or require changing the strategic object.
