What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper: it classifies no problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise. The closest usable anchor is Proposition 1, proved here in Appendix A.3. It is an analytic result that gives a closed-form capacity computation, rather than a complexity classification. If “computational result” is restricted to a stated complexity result, then the paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

Proposition 1 says that, with heterogeneous action sets \([0,R_i]\), full information aggregation holds under its stated incentive conditions: writing \(D(\eta)=(1-\eta)/(1+(n-1)\eta)\), \(0\)-type players have \(A_i=b_i-\tilde b(\eta)<0\), and \(1\)-type players have \(A_i>0\), the lower-bound condition is \(\tilde b(\eta)-b_{\min}\le D(\eta)\,2/(n+2)\); each \(0\)-type must satisfy \(\tilde b(\eta)-b_i\ge D(\eta)(n/(n+2)-R_i)\); and each \(1\)-type must satisfy \(b_i-\tilde b(\eta)\le D(\eta)(R_i-n/(n+2))\), with at least one of the latter conditions strict. Here \(\tilde b(\eta)=\frac{\eta}{1+(n-1)\eta}\sum_j b_j\). The proposition then gives the minimal upper bounds: \(R_i=n/(n+2)+(b_i-\tilde b(\eta))/D(\eta)\) for either type. The paper’s own emphasis on these minimum action sets makes this its strongest possible anchor for a population mirror.

The candidate is Mean-Field Minimum-Capacity FIA. Its input is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over bias types \(b_k\), with masses \(\mu_k\) summing to one, and an aggregate-interdependence parameter \(\lambda\). The regime would be a large alliance of comparable operational units or agencies: many units, but relatively few distinct preference types, with members of a type sharing the same bias and signal technology. Mass \(\mu_k\) is the fraction of units of type \(k\). Each unit chooses a per-capita action in \([0,R_k]\), receives the paper’s quadratic payoff for matching its biased state, and publicly reports its private signal. Use the mean-field scaling \(\eta_N=\lambda/(N-1)\), which keeps total cross-unit influence finite as the population grows. The limiting joint action for a type-\(k\) unit is \(\phi_k=(x_k+\lambda\bar x)/(1+\lambda)\), where \(\bar x=\sum_k\mu_k x_k\).

The question is: compute the componentwise smallest capacity profile \(R_k\) that permits full information aggregation and lets each type achieve \(\phi_k=\theta+b_k\) for every \(\theta\in[0,1]\); if no such profile exists, report infeasibility. Let \(\bar b=\sum_k\mu_k b_k\). Under truthful public reporting, the continuum of conditionally independent signals reveals \(\theta\) through its aggregate signal share. The action policy that implements each type’s ideal joint action is \(x_k(\theta)=\theta+(1+\lambda)b_k-\lambda\bar b\). Thus the lower action bound is feasible exactly when \((1+\lambda)\operatorname{ess\,inf} b-\lambda\bar b\ge0\), and the pointwise minimal capacity is \(R_k^*=1+(1+\lambda)b_k-\lambda\bar b\). Equivalently, this profile minimizes total per-capita capacity \(\sum_k\mu_kR_k\) among profiles that implement those ideal actions. Computing it takes \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations, where \(\tau\) is the support size, so I would expect this mirror to be tractable.

This covers only Proposition 1’s minimum-capacity/full-information question, not the whole paper. It is plausible as the paper’s problem because it keeps its central objects—public communication, heterogeneous biases, interdependent actions, and the capacity needed for aggregation—and the proposed output is precisely the kind of minimum action set the proposition studies. A natural next question is how quickly the finite-\(N\) capacity profiles converge to this limit.

The weakest point is that the finite model’s truth-telling conditions are strategically pivotal: one player’s report can change others’ actions. In an atomless population, one report has zero mass, so those individual incentive constraints disappear; truth-telling is at best weakly optimal. The mirror therefore retains the capacity needed to implement the desired actions, but not the proposition’s finite-population mechanism for making truthful communication incentive-compatible. The scenario is also less direct for sovereign states, which are few; the case depends on authors accepting many comparable alliance units as legitimate players.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is real, but it is not a computational result: Proposition 1 gives closed-form incentive conditions and minimum action bounds for a finite-player equilibrium. The paper never defines a problem of computing an equilibrium or capacity profile and analyzes its complexity. The proposed \(O(\tau)\) procedure computes a new mean-field formula; it does not mirror a complexity question posed by the paper. That is the strongest scope objection under ChoCo’s computational remit.

The proponent’s formula does have a precise connection to the proposition. With \(N\) players, \(\eta_N=\lambda/(N-1)\), and average bias \(\bar b\), the finite expressions converge to \(\tilde b_N\to\lambda\bar b/(1+\lambda)\), \(D_N\to1/(1+\lambda)\), and \(R_i\to1+(1+\lambda)b_i-\lambda\bar b\). The lower-bound condition also converges to the one stated in the mirror. So the capacity profile is not an arbitrary re-modelling; it is a clean limit of the finite result.

The difficulty is what that limit does to the result’s central mechanism. In Proposition 1, a player’s report can change other players’ beliefs and actions, which is why capacity constraints determine whether truth-telling is incentive-compatible. In an atomless population, an individual report has zero effect on aggregate reports and actions. Truth-telling is therefore weakly optimal alongside misreporting, while the truthful population’s signal share reveals \(\theta\) in the limit. The proposed question becomes whether capacities allow the desired actions once the state is statistically identified. That is a sensible mean-field feasibility question, but it no longer tests the proposition’s strategic link between capacities and truthful communication.

The strongest repair would retain pivotal senders—for example, model many comparable operational units alongside a few influential alliance blocs. But those blocs remain atomic strategic players, and the result would concern a hybrid game. Treating a whole positive-mass cohort as able to deviate together instead changes the equilibrium concept to one with coordinated group deviations. Either could be interesting, but neither is the high-multiplicity mirror proposed here. The paper’s motivating players are sovereign states with distinct preferences, information, and capacities; many interchangeable agencies or units are conceivable, but require a substantial change in who makes decisions and how communication works.

I cannot honestly establish that no such scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror. The finite-to-continuum capacity limit is credible, and a suitable organizational setting might make it useful. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no computational-complexity anchor, and its most promising population limit removes the very individual incentive constraints that make Proposition 1 an information-aggregation result.
