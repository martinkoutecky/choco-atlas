The continuous mirror question

Given rational buyer-type masses μ_b summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\) summing to \(1\), and rational valuations \(v_{bi}\), find a componentwise least price vector \(p\) satisfying the listed lattice-linear price constraints, together with an allocation \(y_{bi}\geq 0\) such that \(\sum_i y_{bi}=μ_b\), \(\sum_b y_{bi}=q_i\), and \(y_{bi}>0\) only when item type \(i\) maximizes \(v_{bi}-p_i\) for type \(b\); report infeasibility if no such price and allocation exist.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity exchange with complete valuation types carrying rational masses and item categories with per-capita supplies. The decision variables are category prices \(p_i\) and type-to-category match masses \(y_{bi}\); the objective is the componentwise least feasible price vector, with the matching masses certifying market clearing.

The objection that survived

The paper’s iteration bound depends on the price range and minimum advancement, so it does not itself give polynomial time in the binary input length; the proposed LP argument also depends on establishing the weighted-market formulation and least-price property.

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends the constrained market-clearing price application using Theorem 1 and Lemma 7; it leaves the stable-matching, shortest-path, and concise-representation results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does weighted Hall feasibility for rational type masses and supplies remain meet-closed, yielding a least price vector under lattice-linear price constraints?
  • Can the least constrained price vector and a clearing allocation be computed in time polynomial in the number of types, item categories, constraints, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor here is Lemma 7, proved in this paper. It says that the market-clearing predicate on price vectors is lattice-linear. In full: whenever the current price vector’s demand graph has no perfect matching, there is an item \(j\) such that every componentwise larger price vector that leaves \(j\)’s price unchanged still has no perfect matching. The proof uses Hall’s theorem to find a minimal over-demanded set and shows that each item in it has this property. The paper then uses this result with its predicate-detection algorithm to compute a least constrained market-clearing price vector. Theorem 1, also proved here, supplies the algorithmic bridge: when each advancement on a forbidden coordinate is at least a fixed \(\delta>0\), LLP returns the least feasible vector below \(T\), if one exists, in at most \(\sum_i \lceil T[i]/\delta\rceil\) parallel iterations.

A qualification: the paper has no numbered result that classifies market clearing as polynomial-time or hard. Lemma 7 is the closest named result for a computational mirror: it establishes the structure the paper’s algorithm uses, rather than a standard complexity classification. My lead is a continuous, high-multiplicity version of Constrained Market Clearing Price.

Call it Least Type-Mass Market-Clearing Prices. An instance has bidder types \(b\in\mathcal B\), with rational masses \(\mu_b\) summing to \(1\); item types \(i\in\mathcal I\), with rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\) also summing to \(1\); and rational valuations \(v_{bi}\). A bidder type is the complete valuation vector over item types. Prices \(p_i\) lie in \([0,\max_b v_{bi}]\), with an optional finite list of rational linear price constraints that are lattice-linear, such as \(p_i\ge p_j\). Let \(D_b(p)=\arg\max_i(v_{bi}-p_i)\). The question is whether there is a price vector satisfying the constraints and a mass allocation \(y_{bi}\ge 0\) such that
\[ \sum_i y_{bi}=\mu_b,\qquad \sum_b y_{bi}=q_i,\qquad y_{bi}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ i\in D_b(p). \]
If so, return a componentwise least such \(p\), together with a clearing allocation \(y\); otherwise report infeasibility. Here \(y_{bi}\) is the share of the whole population of type \(b\) receiving item type \(i\). Each actual buyer still receives one indivisible item: fractional values describe the distribution of matches within a type.

The regime is a large exchange for standardized goods: millions of unit-demand buyers, but only a moderate number \(\tau=|\mathcal B|\) of distinct valuation profiles and a moderate number of item categories. Supplies scale with the population. The same finite type description repeats, while counts grow. That is a plausible setting for the paper’s problem: it retains buyers’ valuations, demand at posted prices, market clearing, and the least-price objective. The co-modification to per-capita supply is needed to let item availability grow with the buyer population.

I expect this version to be Class A for the stated linear price constraints. The weighted assignment problem has a transportation LP; its dual describes supporting prices. One can optimize over the dual optimal face, imposing the listed price constraints, and recover a clearing allocation by complementary slackness. If a least feasible price vector exists, minimizing the sum of its coordinates selects it. This suggests exact time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|\mathcal I|\), the number of constraints, and input bit length \(L\). That algorithm and its bound would be new results for the mirror; Lemma 7 motivates the extension but does not prove them.

My weakest point is the change from \(n\) individual items and a perfect matching to item categories with scaled supplies and a mass matching. The type-level version needs a weighted Hall argument for the analogue of Lemma 7, and arbitrary lattice-linear constraints need not fit the LP formulation above. The mirror covers the paper’s market-clearing-price result, not its stable-matching or shortest-path results. It opens further questions: does the forbidden-item argument extend to rational masses, which broader lattice-linear constraints preserve tractability, and how tight is the relationship between this problem and its finite high-multiplicity instances?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat the market-clearing anchor. The strongest case against it is that Lemma 7 is a structural result, not a complexity classification, and Theorem 1’s iteration bound depends on \(T\) and \(\delta\), so it does not by itself give a polynomial-time bound in the binary input length. The paper’s market-clearing section therefore gives a less direct computational anchor than a theorem classifying a problem as tractable or hard.

But that does not defeat the better mirror. Take a large exchange with many buyers sharing a moderate number of complete valuation types, and item categories whose supplies grow with the population. Type masses and supplies define a mass-allocation problem at posted prices. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the population is the continuous object. Prices and demand still do the work they do in the paper; the supply adjustment is the co-modification the programme allows.

Nor does the fractional allocation make this merely outcome-space continuity. With rational masses and supplies, scale by a common denominator to obtain a finite market of replicated types and integral item counts. The transportation constraints have an integral polytope, so a feasible mass allocation corresponds to an integral matching in that replicated market. Neither individual identity nor one-person granularity is essential to the question.

The weighted Hall argument and the resulting algorithm might turn out to be familiar or straightforward through linear programming. Under the programme’s rules, that would not make the question unworthy: a known answer outside the literature, or a tractable answer, changes what needs proving but does not invalidate a sensible mirror. I see no basis here for claiming it has already been answered as a continuous computational problem.

So the negative case is weak. Its real point is about contribution: the paper itself does not supply a strong complexity classification for this anchor, and a mass-market version may yield little new theory. But the anchor survives as a well-posed computational question, and I don’t see an honest fundamental objection to its continuous mirror.
