The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case supplies no positive computational anchor. Its central observation is the strongest argument against a mirror: the paper reports experimental findings, such as 25 of 28 decision makers favoring the more politically similar recipient, and compares intervention effects. The most plausible continuization would treat repeated allocation cases as a distribution over complete types for the allocator and two recipients. That is a sensible way to aggregate many hiring or award decisions; the political profiles in this experiment already come from a finite set. But aggregating cases yields population rates for favoritism and intervention uptake. It does not produce a computational question the paper answers.

One could add a policy problem: choose interventions across a large population to minimize favoritism subject to a budget, for example. That requires specifying a new objective, costs, and response model. With the paper’s measured response rates, the resulting choice may be a small decision table or linear program. A richer model of strategic responses could become a substantial project, but its computational core would be newly introduced; none of the paper’s findings anchors it.

The negative case is strong for the programme’s notion of a mirror. It cannot prove that no useful adjacent project could be built in any scenario.
