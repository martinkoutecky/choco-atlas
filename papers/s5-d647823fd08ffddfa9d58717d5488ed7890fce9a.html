What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors cover Theorems 1–3; the paper's other results and empirical estimates are not computational anchors either.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is that this paper’s voting rules already act on aggregates of ballot types. In a large election, one could represent the society by rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) over rankings, with a small support of commonly shared ballots. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s focus on histograms makes the representation natural.

But I cannot identify a named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The paper’s theorems characterize privacy leakage; they do not classify the complexity of computing it or of another underlying problem. The closest results are:

  • Theorem 1, proved here: for finite \(U\) and finite \(\Delta\subseteq\Pi(U)\), the histogram rule on \(n\) i.i.d. votes is \((\epsilon,\delta,\Delta)\)-DDP when \(\epsilon\ge 2\ln(1+1/(p_{\min}n))\), with \(\delta=\exp(-\Omega(np_{\min}\min(2\ln 2,\epsilon)^2))\).
  • Theorem 2, proved here: for two candidates and any \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), the \(\alpha\)-biased majority rule’s exact \(\delta\) is the maximum over \(p=\pi(a)\), \(\pi\in\Delta\), of \(\Theta\!\left(n^{-1/2}\left[(p/\alpha)^\alpha((1-p)/(1-\alpha))^{1-\alpha}\right]^n\right)\). It is \(\Theta(n^{-1/2})\) if \(\Delta\) contains a distribution with \(p=\alpha\), and exponentially small otherwise.
  • Theorem 3, proved here: for fixed \(m\), finite \(\Delta\), and generalized scoring rules satisfying monotonicity, local stability, and canceling-out, the exact privacy parameter is \(\Theta(n^{-1/2})\) when \(\Delta\) contains the uniform distribution, and exponentially small when \(\Delta\) contains no unstable distribution.

These are substantive results, especially Theorem 3’s dichotomy, but none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. I would not count them as computational anchors under the brief’s requirement.

There is also a modeling obstacle to turning them directly into continuous-population questions. The paper’s privacy guarantee uses randomness from a finite sample of \(n\) i.i.d. votes and conditions on one voter’s vote. An exact mass distribution \(\mu\) removes that sampling randomness; changing the report of a zero-mass individual leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, making that privacy question degenerate. Keeping finite \(n\) preserves the paper’s setting, while allowing positive-mass changes would define a different privacy notion.

So the paper offers a plausible high-multiplicity representation, but no prover-ready continuous computational problem tied to a qualifying named result. Computing the exact privacy parameter as a complexity problem could be a follow-on direction; it would be a new question, not an anchor supplied by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that none of these three results states a computational problem. Theorem 1 bounds privacy parameters; Theorem 2 gives a two-candidate asymptotic formula; Theorem 3 proves an asymptotic dichotomy for a class of voting rules. The exhaustive calculations for small elections are experiments, not a complexity classification. There is no named algorithmic problem here whose continuous-population complexity ChoCo could chart.

That remains true even though the paper offers a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Its \(\pi\) is a distribution over ballot types, and a large election drawn i.i.d. from \(\pi\) is a sensible regime. But the results concern the random *finite sample* of \(n\) individual votes and the privacy of changing one vote. The randomness of that sample produces the boundary probabilities that determine \(\delta\). Replacing the realized electorate by its exact mass distribution \(\mu\) removes those fluctuations. The continuous histogram is just \(\mu\); changing an infinitesimal voter leaves it unchanged. A positive-mass change instead asks about a group’s effect, not the paper’s individual-privacy question.

The strongest mirror for Theorem 1 would be to ask for the exact DDP or eDDP of the histogram when the ballot proportions are continuous inputs. But if those proportions describe the actual continuum society, the histogram reveals them exactly and the individual-level comparison vanishes. If they describe the sampling law for a finite election, the mirror retains the finite-sample model that generates the theorem and has not continuized the population. Theorem 1’s bound is already stated in terms of that law and \(n\); a complexity study of evaluating its parameters would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 2 makes the mismatch especially clear. In the continuum majority model, the winner is fixed by whether \(\mu(a)\) crosses \(\alpha\). At the threshold, changing a zero-mass voter does nothing; changing a positive mass makes this a group-sensitivity question. The theorem’s \(n^{-1/2}\) versus exponential behavior comes from the finite binomial sample near or away from the threshold. The proposed continuous input \(\pi\) is the sampling distribution, and the two-candidate result already reduces its effect to a one-dimensional formula. Turning that formula into a complexity problem would not supply the missing population-continuous computational task.

For Theorem 3, one could keep a continuous ranking distribution \(\mu\) and ask how a GSR’s winner changes near its score-boundary hyperplanes. That is a coherent continuous analysis, but the paper’s result is about the probability that a finite sample lies near those boundaries, conditional on one individual’s vote. In an exact continuum society that sampling probability disappears. Adding a prior over possible societies could restore randomness, but it changes the model’s source of uncertainty and asks about privacy under uncertainty over whole populations. That might be an adjacent project; the paper gives no computational result to anchor it.

So the paper’s distributional model supports the claim that large, type-aggregated electorates are sensible. It does not yield a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of its results: the candidate continuous reformulations either erase the individual privacy question or preserve the finite-sample question that produced the original bounds. The negative case is strongest on that scope distinction; it is not a claim that computing exact privacy parameters for variable-size elections could never be an interesting problem.
