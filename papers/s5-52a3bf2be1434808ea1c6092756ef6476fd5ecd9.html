The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-conforming positive case: the paper has no numbered result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified by computational complexity. Its closest results are Proposition 8, proved here, which characterizes absorbing states of the anonymous Boolean threshold process by conditions on the numbers of active neighbors at inner and outer frontier nodes; and Proposition 9, also proved here, which says absorbing classes are fixed singletons or cycles of nonempty states whose sets are pairwise incomparable by inclusion. Those are results about the dynamics, not the computational complexity of a problem.

A plausible *prospective* mirror is Block-Mean-Field Cascade. Consider a population divided among finitely many roles \(R\), with rational role shares \(\lambda_r\), degrees \(d_r\), thresholds \(q_r\), and a rational matrix \(P\) describing the share of each role’s neighbors drawn from each other role. A type is a role together with its active or inactive status; its mass is the fraction of the whole population in that state. If \(x_r(t)\) is the active fraction within role \(r\), then each agent in role \(r\) samples \(d_r\) neighbors afresh each round, independently, according to \(P\). Write \(p_r(t)=\sum_s P_{rs}x_s(t)\). The next active fraction is

\[ x_r(t+1)=\sum_{j=\lceil q_r d_r\rceil}^{d_r} \binom{d_r}{j}p_r(t)^j(1-p_r(t))^{d_r-j}. \]

Given the rational model and rational initial fractions \(x(0)\), the decision question is whether \(x_r(t)\to1\) for every role \(r\). A solution is a yes or no answer. This asks a computational question about long-run cascade behavior, using the paper’s threshold mechanism and its concern with eventual diffusion.

The regime is a large population of agents in a few repeated network roles: \(N\) is much larger than \(|R|\), and the type support is at most \(2|R|\). The mirror is plausible for random-mixing settings because role, threshold, and neighborhood composition give agents a shared description, and proportions are the natural quantities. Its weakest point is substantial: fresh random mixing replaces the paper’s fixed network, and an initially finite active set has zero mass in the continuum. This model instead starts from a macroscopic seed. The authors might recognize it as a mean-field version of their cascade question, but it does not preserve their arbitrary-network analysis.

I would leave this candidate’s complexity direction open. The paper’s propositions don’t establish whether the limiting-cascade decision problem is tractable, inherits discrete hardness, or is hard for continuum-specific reasons. Questions about the existence of nontrivial fixed points, periodic limits, and the minimum seed mass needed for a cascade follow naturally, but none is anchored by a numbered computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does not prove a computational complexity result to mirror. Its main theorems classify long-run behavior of a stochastic process on a fixed, countably infinite graph. Proposition 8 characterizes when a configuration is absorbing; Proposition 9 describes the possible forms of absorbing classes in the deterministic case. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem.

One could turn Proposition 8 into a computational question, such as whether a given configuration is absorbing or whether a graph admits a nontrivial absorbing configuration. But checking a supplied configuration on a finite graph is just a local scan. Searching for one could be a more substantial optimization problem, yet that would be a new problem built from the characterization, not a continuous mirror of a complexity result in this paper. Proposition 9 has the same gap: cycle existence or behavior could be studied computationally, but the proposition itself gives no complexity boundary to continue.

The proposed Block-Mean-Field Cascade is the strongest candidate, because it gives a concrete algorithmic question and mass dynamics. Its recurrence is coherent for agents in finitely many roles who redraw neighbors independently each round. But that changes the paper’s defining structure. The paper studies repeated local updates on one fixed, bounded-degree network. In the candidate, fresh random mixing replaces that network, and a finite seed must be replaced by a seed of positive mass: in an atomless population, finitely many initially active agents have mass zero.

That gap is not repaired by simply tracking more role-level fractions. On a fixed sparse graph, equal active fractions can have different local arrangements and therefore different next-step behavior. For example, under a threshold rule, a cluster and a scattered set of the same size can expose different numbers of vertices to enough active neighbors. To preserve that information, a type description must include local configurations; after each round, predicting those configurations can require a larger neighborhood description. There is no evident fixed, finite support that closes the dynamics while retaining the paper’s arbitrary-network question.

Other continuum limits trade away a different part of the model. A dense graph limit gives agents neighborhoods of positive mass, but drops the bounded-degree setting. A sparse limit preserves finite local neighborhoods, but each such neighborhood has zero mass in an atomless population, so aggregate role shares do not encode the pointwise patterns governing updates. Either could motivate a new diffusion model, but neither is a population-only continuation of the one analyzed here.

A richer version of the candidate—perhaps a finite-type random network with macroscopic seeds and a decision problem about convergence—might still interest the authors. The case against is weaker there: it is a real computational question. But its answer would be about long-run behavior in a resampled mean-field model, not a complexity question raised by this paper’s results. On the evidence in the paper, the continuum mirror would require replacing the fixed network, the seed regime, or both; the proposed computational question does not overcome that mismatch.
