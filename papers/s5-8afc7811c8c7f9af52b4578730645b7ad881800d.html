The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a defensible positive computational mirror under the stated anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational result. Its main results establish when conditional numerical representations exist, when they can be continuous, and how conditional versions of von Neumann–Morgenstern and Debreu results work. Those are representation theorems, not complexity classifications or algorithms for a computational problem. The paper does not compute with an LP, MILP, or other encoding that could supply an anchor.

The nearest tempting anchor is the theorem that a conditional preference order has a numerical representation if and only if it has a conditionally countable order-dense subset. But that is an existence characterization, not a result that a decision or search problem is in \(P\), hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Turning it into a representability-testing problem would require choosing a finite input encoding and asking a new question the paper does not study.

The population interpretation also fails the paper’s own plausibility test. Its probability measure describes uncertainty over states, and its random variables and lotteries are objects being evaluated by a single decision maker. They are not masses of agents or voter types. A distribution over decision makers could be devised, but that would introduce a social aggregation problem absent from the paper, with no named result to anchor it.

So there are no qualifying anchors, no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover, and no direction—tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specific—to predict. The weakest point is decisive: the paper studies axiomatic decision theory rather than computation over a population. That does not make its use of probability or conditional outcomes a novelty collision; it means the supplied results do not support the requested continuous-population computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point in the case for a mirror is also where it stops: the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Its theorems characterize when a conditional preference order has a numerical representation, when that representation can be continuous, and when it can be affine or robust. None defines a computational input or task, let alone classifies its complexity.

The nearest candidate is the conditional Debreu theorem: a representation exists exactly when there is a conditionally countable order-dense subset. One could pose a new computational problem about deciding representability or constructing a utility from a finite encoding of a preference relation. But the paper supplies no such encoding, and choosing one would determine the computational question rather than translate the theorem. A population distribution does not fix that gap: repeating agents with the same conditional preference leaves the theorem unchanged, while combining agents with different preferences requires an aggregation rule the paper never defines. Either way, the population does no work in a mirror of this result.

The paper’s probability space and conditional random variables do not supply a hidden population axis. They represent states of the world, uncertain outcomes, and information available to a single decision maker. A measure over states is not a mass distribution over agents. Reinterpreting states as voter types would change the meaning of the model and the question being asked. The paper is already continuous in its treatment of states and outcomes, but that is not a continuous-population treatment or a novelty collision.

One could build a worthwhile computational study of aggregating conditional preferences across a population. The honest weakness in this negative case is that such a project might be valuable on its own. But it would need a new social choice problem, an aggregation model, and computational results; none is anchored in this paper. Under the requested scope, the proponent’s decision to name no qualifying anchor is decisive.
