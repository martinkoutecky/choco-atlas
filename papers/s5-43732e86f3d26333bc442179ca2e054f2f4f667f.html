The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many worker types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), monotone non-positive valuations \(v_\theta\), interval chore kinds \(J\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), let \(\mathcal I\) be the integral interval-feasible bundles and choose masses \(x_{\theta,B}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,B\ni j}x_{\theta,B}\le q_j\). Decide or compute an allocation whose positive-support bundles satisfy pairwise \(\mathrm{EF1}\), and in which every chore with residual supply cannot be added to any positive-mass bundle without an interval conflict.

The model it lives in

A configuration model over worker types and integral interval-feasible bundles, with population mass \(x_{\theta,B}\), per-capita chore supplies \(q_j\), and no welfare objective beyond simultaneous \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and maximality.

The objection that survived

The two-agent swapped-schedule argument provides no algorithmic guarantee for a continuum supporting many bundles, whose \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and maximality constraints are support-sensitive disjunctions.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3 and its adjacent maximal-schedule construction; it leaves the \(\mathrm{EFX}\) and Pareto-optimality impossibilities, arbitrary-agent existence results, and dichotomous special-case algorithms untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the additive or dichotomous version be solved in polynomial time through configuration-LP separation over interval-feasible bundles?
  • For arbitrary monotone value-query types, is the high-multiplicity problem continuum-specifically hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s interval-scheduling problem, anchored in Theorem 3, the result labelled \(\texttt{TwoAgentsEFoneInterval}\) (the supplied source cross-reference is \(\Cref{thm:Two_Agents_EF1_Interval}\)).

The theorem is proved in this paper, not merely cited: for any CISP instance with two agents, monotone valuations, and an arbitrary interval conflict graph, it gives a polynomial-time algorithm, assuming value-query access, that returns an \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and maximal schedule. The appendix proves it through the polynomially constructible adjacent maximal-schedule sequence of Lemma \(\texttt{AdjacentSequenceInterval}\), together with the adjacent-schedule lemma. This is the paper’s strongest anchor because it preserves arbitrary interval conflicts and general monotone valuations.

I would mirror it with the following problem, Continuous EF1-Maximal Interval Scheduling.

There are finitely many worker types \(\Theta=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), with rational population masses \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). A type is a complete description of a worker’s valuation over feasible chore bundles, including any eligibility or skill information. Each type \(\theta\) has a monotone non-positive valuation \(v_\theta\).

There is also a finite catalogue \(J\) of recurring chore kinds. Chore kind \(j\) has an interval \([s_j,f_j)\) and a rational per-capita supply \(q_j\). Thus a population of size \(N\) has approximately \(Nq_j\) indistinguishable copies of chore \(j\). Copies remain indivisible: one worker receives either zero or one copy of a chore kind, and a worker’s bundle must contain pairwise non-overlapping intervals. Repeated copies of the same interval simply create the corresponding interval-graph clique.

Let \(\mathcal I\) be the family of feasible integral bundles. The decision variable is

\[ x_{\theta,B}\ge 0, \]

the mass of type-\(\theta\) workers receiving the integral bundle \(B\in\mathcal I\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{B\in\mathcal I}x_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta \]

for every \(\theta\), and

\[ \sum_{\theta}\sum_{B\ni j}x_{\theta,B}\le q_j \]

for every chore kind \(j\).

The residual supply of \(j\) is

\[ r_j=q_j-\sum_{\theta}\sum_{B\ni j}x_{\theta,B}. \]

The allocation is maximal if, whenever \(r_j>0\), no bundle \(B\) with positive assigned mass can accept another copy of \(j\) without creating an interval conflict. It is \(\mathrm{EF1}\) if, for every two positive-mass bundles \(B\) and \(B'\) belonging to types \(\theta\) and \(\theta'\), respectively, either \(B=\varnothing\), or there is some \(j\in B\) such that

\[ v_\theta(B\setminus\{j\})\ge v_\theta(B'). \]

The problem is to find such an \(x\), or report that none exists. There is no welfare objective: the objective is simultaneous \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and maximality.

This is a genuine population continuization. The workers are the continuous object; bundles and chores remain integral. A rational solution has an exact finite interpretation: choose a common denominator \(D\), create \(D\mu_\theta\) agents of type \(\theta\), \(Dq_j\) copies of chore \(j\), and assign \(Dx_{\theta,B}\) agents to bundle \(B\). Conversely, every finite allocation induces these masses. Maximality and \(\mathrm{EF1}\) correspond exactly under this normalization.

A plausible regime is a large workforce of interchangeable employees—say, hospital or municipal staff—with only a few role types, while the number of recurring task copies grows proportionally with the workforce. The regime has \(N\gg\tau\): many workers share each complete valuation type, while the interval catalogue and the number of types are moderate. This is not the paper’s story merely rewritten with fractional chores; every individual worker still receives an indivisible, conflict-free bundle.

I would expect the additive or dichotomous version to be a Class A candidate. The aggregate assignment constraints form a configuration LP, and pricing over interval-feasible bundles is closely related to weighted interval scheduling. The real question is whether the \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and maximality conditions can be enforced with polynomially many relevant configurations or a suitable separation oracle. For arbitrary monotone value-query valuations, a continuum-specific hardness boundary remains quite plausible.

The mirror covers only Theorem 3 and its adjacent-sequence proof. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s \(\mathrm{EFX}\) or Pareto-optimality impossibility examples, nor the arbitrary-agent existence results.

The weakest point is genuine: Theorem 3 concerns exactly two named agents, whereas the proposed mirror has a mass of workers and potentially many bundles in its support. The paper’s algorithm does not automatically solve that stronger problem. The defence is that the interval-conflict, monotone-chore, \(\mathrm{EF1}\), and maximality structure is preserved exactly, while the high-multiplicity regime is natural and has a precise finite-to-continuous dictionary. The mirror is therefore a credible research question, but not a theorem already latent in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor, \(\texttt{TwoAgentsEFoneInterval}\), does not survive continuization as the same computational result. Its substance is not merely “interval conflicts plus \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and maximality”; it is the fact that there are exactly two bundles. The proof relies on red–blue coloring, a path from a schedule to its swapped schedule, and an envy switch between two adjacent schedules. None of these is a population-level argument.

There are only two plausible ways to lift the theorem.

If the population is genuinely continuized, then replicating the two agents produces many workers. A feasible allocation is now a distribution over many bundles \(B\), with constraints such as

\[ \sum_{B}x_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta. \]

The required fairness condition becomes pairwise \(\mathrm{EF1}\) for every pair of bundles with positive support. A type may be split across many bundles, so the two-agent swap argument disappears. Even with only two worker types, there may be arbitrarily many supported bundles. The theorem’s adjacent-schedule lemma gives no implication for this setting: it proves \(\mathrm{EF1}\) from a transition between two allocations of two named agents, not from a configuration mixture.

The proposed configuration LP also does not preserve the paper’s algorithmic structure. The feasibility constraints are linear, and additive pricing over interval-independent bundles may indeed resemble weighted interval scheduling. But \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and maximality are support-sensitive disjunctions:

\[ x_{\theta,B}>0,\ x_{\theta',B'}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bigvee_{j\in B} \left[v_\theta(B\setminus\{j\})\ge v_\theta(B')\right], \]

while maximality contains implications involving \(r_j>0\) and every supported bundle. These are not the linear winning conditions or the adjacent-sequence structure that make the two-agent theorem work. The resulting problem may be interesting, but it is a new many-agent fair packing problem, not a continuous version of the named theorem.

Alternatively, one can force every worker of each type to receive the same bundle, so that there remain only two effective bundles. That preserves the theorem’s two-agent structure, but then the continuum has no substantive role: the masses merely weight two already-existing agents, while the bundle problem and the \(\mathrm{EF1}\) comparison remain finite and two-sided. Allowing a type to split across bundles immediately returns to the many-agent problem above. Thus the anchor faces a basic dichotomy: preserve the theorem and lose genuine population continuization, or preserve genuine population continuization and lose the theorem’s defining structure.

The per-capita chore catalogue does repair one obvious degeneration—without recurring chore supply, almost all workers would receive nothing—but it does so by changing the problem into a weighted coloring/configuration problem. That is a legitimate co-modification, yet it reinforces rather than resolves the mismatch: the paper schedules a finite set of chores on two machines, whereas the proposed model schedules recurring chore supply across a continuum of machines.

The negative case is therefore strong against this particular anchor: no natural high-multiplicity interpretation retains both the continuous-population object and the content of \(\texttt{TwoAgentsEFoneInterval}\). It is not strong enough to establish the universal claim that no continuous formulation of interval chore scheduling is worthwhile. A one-type, high-multiplicity model with recurring tasks could be a worthwhile new ChoCo problem; it simply is not supported by the paper’s two-agent theorem.
