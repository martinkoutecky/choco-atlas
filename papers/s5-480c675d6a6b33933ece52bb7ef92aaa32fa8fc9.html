The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow possible extension of the paper’s fixed-point work. It does not meet your anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

The nearest results are Proposition 3.1 and Proposition 3.2, both proved here. Proposition 3.1 says that when \(m=2k-1\), for each positive integer \(C\) and all sufficiently large \(k\), the finite weighted voting system with weights \((a,b,\ldots,b)\), where \(b=(k-C)/(2k^2-k)\) and \(a=1-mb\), is a non-trivial Shapley–Shubik fixed point; it also says these are all the non-trivial fixed points of that form. Proposition 3.2 gives the corresponding classification for \(m=2k\): for each positive \(C\) and sufficiently large \(k\), \(b=(k-C)/(2k^2+k)\), \(a=1-2kb\), and all non-trivial fixed points of that form are obtained this way. These are structural classifications of finite-player systems, not computational-complexity results.

A plausible, but unanchored, continuous extension would study a large committee with two voter types of positive population fractions \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). Give each type a per-capita vote-weight parameter \(x_A,x_B\), normalized by \(\alpha x_A+(1-\alpha)x_B=1\). For each \(N\), form a finite committee with those type proportions, scale each member’s weight by \(1/N\), and compute the finite Banzhaf or Shapley–Shubik index. The question would be to characterize the type-weight vectors for which \(N\) times each type’s per-member index converges to that type’s per-capita weight. A solution would be the full set of such normalized vectors, for each index. This retains the paper’s fixed-point question and gives population mass a clear meaning; a large organization with many interchangeable members of each type is a plausible regime.

The difficulty is that the propositions do not establish this limit or classify fixed points with positive mass for both types. In their stated families, there is only one \(A\) voter, so the \(A\)-type fraction tends to zero as \(m\) grows. The paper’s real-valued weights are also continuous vote weights on a finite set of players, not a continuous population. Thus this is a reasonable direction to ask the authors about, but it is not a mirror of an anchored result under the requested standard. Propositions 2.1 and 2.4 do not fill the gap: they concern disagreement between indices for certain divisor voting systems and equality of winning-coalition counts for specified systems, respectively, not the complexity of computing either quantity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 3.1 and Proposition 3.2 classify fixed points in finite weighted voting systems; they give formulas, not a complexity result or an algorithmic problem. Proposition 2.1 compares two indices for certain divisor systems, and Proposition 2.4 counts winning coalitions. Those are number theoretic and combinatorial results, not computational questions. The divisor systems also have one player per distinct divisor, so enlarging \(n\) does not create repeated voter types in the way a high-multiplicity regime requires.

The fixed-point propositions are the closest candidates, but their nontrivial families collapse in the proposed limit. Each has one \(A\)-player and \(m\) \(B\)-players, so the \(A\)-type’s population mass is \(1/(m+1)\). In Proposition 3.1, its total vote weight is \(a=C/k\); in Proposition 3.2, it is \(a=(2C+1)/(2k+1)\). Both tend to zero as \(k\) grows, while the \(B\)-type takes essentially all the population and vote weight. The limit therefore has just one type and only the trivial fixed point.

The proponent’s stronger version—two types each with a positive population fraction—is a sensible question to explore, but it changes the regime that the propositions classify. It replaces a single distinguished player by a macroscopic group. To define the limiting indices, one must also specify finite-population approximants and normalize the per-player power, which otherwise tends to zero. That could lead to a new asymptotic study of power indices. As stated, though, it is a limit and fixed-point classification question, not a computational complexity or algorithm question anchored in this paper.

So the case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror is strong: none of its results supplies the computational problem the programme is meant to continuize, and the closest fixed-point families lose their nontrivial feature in the direct population limit. The universal claim that no worthwhile project could grow from the paper is weaker. A separate high-multiplicity study of power-index computation may be worthwhile; it would be a new computational direction, rather than a mirror of the results reported here.
