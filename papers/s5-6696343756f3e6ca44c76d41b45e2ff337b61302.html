The case FOR (proponent)

There is one important qualification: the report has no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a complexity result. Its strongest computational anchor is the claim in §3.5.2 that STV manipulation is NP-hard, cited as reference [2]. I’ll use that imported result, rather than present it as proved by Durand, Mathieu, and Noirie. If “of this paper” excludes results it cites, then this report has no eligible numbered anchor.

The precise anchor is Theorem 1 of Bartholdi and Orlin (1991), cited as [2] in the report and proved in their paper. It says that EFFECTIVE PREFERENCE under STV is NP-complete: given candidates, a distinguished candidate \(c\), and the sincere strict, complete rankings of the other voters, decide whether one voter can submit a ranking that makes \(c\) win. Their reduction’s combinatorics lie in the alternatives and elimination process. The report invokes this hardness in §3.5.2, where it discusses testing whether STV can be manipulated. [The ChoCo paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.6455), [Bartholdi and Orlin’s paper](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/cs286r/courses/fall11/papers/BO91.pdf).

The lead mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous STV Route Manipulation. Its instance is a finite set \(C\) of candidate routes and a finite set \(T\) of carrier types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type records the complete information relevant to the vote: its utility vector over the routes, its route participation, its sincere ranking, and the ballots it is allowed to submit under the route protocol. Compute the sincere STV winner \(v\), using a fixed tie rule; the report’s route model breaks elimination ties by candidate index and splits a ballot equally when its top surviving preference is tied.

For a proposed route \(c\), choose manipulated mass \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) only for types whose true preferences rank \(c\) above \(v\). Let \(x_{t,\rho}\ge 0\) be the mass of type \(t\) that submits admissible report \(\rho\), with \(\sum_{\rho}x_{t,\rho}=z_t\). The remaining mass \(\mu_t-z_t\) votes sincerely. The question is whether there exist such mass allocations for which STV elects \(c\); a solution is the report-mass allocation and its resulting elimination sequence. This is a decision problem, so its objective is feasibility: can a beneficial coalition change the winner? It preserves the paper’s central objects—carriers, route alternatives, their financial preferences, and strategic STV ballots—while making the action variable a population mass.

A plausible regime is a large interconnection or service-composition marketplace with many interchangeable providers participating in route selection for a standard demand and a fixed menu of route templates. Group providers only when their utilities, route participation, and ballot constraints match exactly. In repeated wholesale roles with common tariffs, the number \(N\) of providers could be in the thousands while the number \(\tau\) of distinct complete types is in the tens or hundreds. That is a proposed regime, not the report’s 38-carrier experiment. The report itself names service composition involving many providers as a future use case; it also explicitly lets manipulators split votes fractionally in its stronger Exhaustive Ballot bound. That makes a mass allocation recognizable as an extension of machinery the authors already use, though not as a result they establish. [Report, §3.5.2 and future work](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.6455).

I would tentatively expect this question to be Class B, hardness transfers, because the cited reduction’s difficulty is driven by the candidate agenda and elimination order, not by needing a large number of distinct voters. But that is a conjecture for the continuous problem, not a consequence of Theorem 1: fractional cohorts can distribute reports across rankings, and the prover must show that this extra flexibility does not turn a no-instance into a yes-instance. Natural follow-ups are whether the problem is polynomial for fixed \(m\), whether it admits an algorithm parameterized by \(m\) or \(\tau\), and whether discrete elections with high multiplicity approximate the continuous answer as their type counts scale.

This mirror covers the report’s STV manipulation question in §3.5.2. It does not claim that the report’s empirical STV-versus-range figures—such as the reported manipulability rates or welfare comparisons—carry over to a continuum; those are experimental findings, not numbered complexity results.

The weakest point is the gap between the anchor and the mirror: Bartholdi–Orlin’s theorem concerns one indivisible strategic ballot, whereas this question lets a positive-mass type split its reports. The report’s own numerical setting also has one voter per carrier and only 38 carriers, so it is not itself a high-multiplicity regime. Still, the proposed model asks the same strategic question about the same route choice and voting rule; its uncertain complexity is the research question, not a reason to replace it with an easier but different problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with the anchor: this report proves no numbered computational-complexity result. Its one precise complexity claim is imported from Bartholdi and Orlin. Their Theorem 1 concerns whether a single voter can submit an effective preference that elects a favored candidate; the report cites that result in §3.5.2 as background to its STV procedure. The report’s own contributions are empirical comparisons on a 38-carrier network, including bounds from tests that can be inconclusive—not a theorem about STV manipulation complexity. So the proponent’s anchor is real prior art, but it is not a computational result of this paper. [The report](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.6455), [Bartholdi and Orlin](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/cs286r/courses/fall11/papers/BO91.pdf).

The proposed mirror also changes the cited problem substantially. Bartholdi–Orlin ask whether one voter can choose a ballot. The mirror lets a positive mass of a type coordinate, and lets that mass split among reports. It further uses the report’s route-specific restrictions on which paths a carrier may rank, and its treatment of indifferent paths. That may be a sound continuous coalition-manipulation problem, but it is not a high-multiplicity version of the theorem the report cites.

A stronger formulation could minimize the mass or cost of a beneficial coalition needed to make a route win. With a finite route menu and finitely many complete types, fixing an elimination sequence gives linear constraints on report masses; one could examine the resulting feasibility problem across elimination sequences. That makes a coherent new question. It still does not turn the report’s empirical results into a complexity theorem, and the report supplies no large population of repeated carrier types to ground it: its voters are specific network nodes whose route participation and utilities depend on their positions.

The best counterexample to a universal negative is also clear. A marketplace with many providers, a fixed menu of service compositions, and repeated provider roles with identical utilities and ballot constraints could give a sensible continuous STV coalition problem. The report itself suggests service composition as a future use case. That scenario could be worth studying, but it would mirror the paper’s generic coalition-manipulability metric, not a named computational result established here.

So the case against is strong for the claimed anchor and the paper’s specific experiment: the anchor is imported, the proposed problem is a different coalition problem, and the tested carriers are not a high-multiplicity population. I cannot honestly extend that to “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario.” A continuous service-composition version remains plausible; the negative case is weakest exactly where it must be universal.
