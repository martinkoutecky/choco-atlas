The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot identify a named computational result in this paper that meets the required anchor standard. Theorem 1 characterizes dominant-strategy implementation by UPE; Theorem 3 gives a necessary condition for Nash implementation; Theorems 4–6 give sufficient conditions or special cases. Propositions 1 and 2 show that particular voting-rule SCFs fail Nash implementation. These are results about whether mechanisms implement social choice functions, not results about computational complexity or algorithms.

The nearest positive lead is Proposition 1, proved here: for \(m\ge3\), the extended and recursive SCF versions of plurality, instant-runoff, and Borda fail to be Nash implementable, for every tie-breaking rule and jury configuration. But it is not a computational hardness result, so using it as an anchor would overstate what the paper establishes.

A plausible setting for a population model would be a large evaluation system whose experts share complete types: an honest ranking and a preference function over candidate rankings, subject to the designer’s known impartiality and favoritism constraints. Mass would be the fraction of experts of each type. That is conceivable, though requiring identical preference functions makes the regime narrower than simply having many reviewers.

The mirror then runs into a basic modeling problem. In a genuinely nonatomic population, a single expert’s report changes no mass, so unilateral deviations cannot affect a mechanism whose outcome depends on the aggregate reports. Treating each type’s mass as a coordinated bloc would restore consequential deviations, but changes the paper’s game: identical experts become a coalition. An approximate or finite-player limit could be studied, but neither is a computational problem anchored by a named result in this paper.

So the honest positive case is weak: the setting suggests a possible population model, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and its implementation theorems do not by themselves yield a continuous computational question that its authors would recognize as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the programme’s own gate: this paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes dominant-strategy implementation; Theorems 3–6 give necessary or sufficient conditions and special cases for Nash implementation; Propositions 1 and 2 give nonimplementation results for particular voting-rule SCFs. None studies worst-case complexity or gives an algorithm with a running-time bound. Proposition 1, the proponent’s nearest candidate, is a game-theoretic impossibility result, not a computational anchor.

A large pool of reviewers sharing complete types—judgments and the preference information relevant to the mechanism—is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. That makes the proposed scenario stronger than a small committee example. The obstacle is that the paper’s central object is individual strategic implementation. If the continuous mechanism depends on the distribution of reports, one reviewer’s deviation changes no mass and no outcome. Every report is then a best response, so a nonconstant social choice function cannot be implemented across all equilibria. Giving a positive-mass type the power to deviate as a bloc restores influence, but changes the players from individual experts to coordinated coalitions. Keeping named experts influential instead makes identity relevant and prevents them from being compressed into interchangeable mass.

One could invent a computational question about deciding whether a mass-based rule satisfies a continuous analogue of WUPE. But the paper supplies no canonical encoding of the rule, no continuous equilibrium notion, and no algorithmic task of that kind. Those choices would define a new project; they do not turn Proposition 1 or the implementation theorems into computational results. The negative case is strongest within ChoCo’s stated scope: a sensible high-multiplicity story exists, but this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and the most natural atomless version erases the individual deviations its results concern.
