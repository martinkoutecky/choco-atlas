The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\lambda_t\) over finitely many advertiser portfolio types \(t\), value vectors \(v_t\in[0,1]^m\), a horizon \(H\), and unknown but fixed positive CTR vectors \(\theta_t\), construct a finite-support policy and history-based payment rule with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,H\), and the input bit length. The policy may use an individual’s report, the distribution of reports, and observed clicks; each individual deviation has zero mass and must still receive report-dependent treatment. Require stochastic truthfulness for every \(\theta\), total assignment mass at most one per stage, and per-capita welfare strictly above uniform allocation whenever \(M_2^2\ge\sigma M_1^2\) for some \(\sigma>1\) and \(\max_{t,j}v_{t,j}\theta_{t,j}>\epsilon>0\), where \(M_q=\left(\sum_t\lambda_t\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m(v_{t,j}\theta_{t,j})^q\right)^{1/q}\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of advertisers with mass \(\lambda_t\) for each portfolio type, shared unknown CTRs within each type, report-dependent allocation policies, per-stage assignment mass at most one, click feedback, and payments computed from the observed history.

The objection that survived

With per-capita supply and shared CTRs, vanishing exploration can yield many observations at negligible per-capita cost, so the mirror may lose the paper’s central learning challenge; the proponent acknowledges this but does not establish that the no-simulation constraint remains meaningful.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.3(a)’s stochastic-truthfulness and welfare-advantage guarantee over uniform allocation for skewed instances with at least two agents. It leaves the general payment transformation and the ex-post impossibility results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does pooling click observations by type preserve a nontrivial no-simulation and truthfulness constraint under per-capita supply scaling?
  • Can a finite-support policy and payment rule attain the stated welfare guarantee with polynomial running time?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the paper’s multi-parameter bandit mechanism, where a large population of advertisers competes for a scarce stream of impressions. I would anchor on Theorem 6.3(a), proved in this paper using its allocation rule ALL and the transformation in Theorem 3.1. It says: when every value-per-click is at most \(1\), every CTR is positive, and the instance is \(\sigma\)-skewed with \(\sigma>1\) and \(\max_j v_j\mu_j>\epsilon>0\), then for every \(\delta\in(0,1)\) the constructed mechanism \(M_\delta\) is stochastically truthful; moreover, on every instance with at least two agents, some choice of \(\delta\) makes its expected welfare strictly exceed that of uniform random allocation. Here skew is measured by the first and second moments of the products \(v_j\mu_j\), as defined in the paper.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is an ad marketplace with many advertisers using standardized campaign portfolios, such as franchisees choosing among the same finite set of ad formats. A type is an advertiser’s complete portfolio: its ad formats and value-per-click vector. Its mass is the fraction of advertisers with that portfolio. The number of advertisers \(N\) is large, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct portfolios is small. As a deliberate per-capita scaling, give the market \(N\) impressions per stage over a fixed number \(H\) of stages. Each format has a fixed CTR shared by its type, unknown to the mechanism; individual clicks are independent Bernoulli trials. This keeps each advertiser’s expected service meaningful as \(N\) grows.

Call the resulting question Mass-MAB-Truthful-Advantage. An instance gives \(m,H\), a finite set of portfolio types with rational masses \(\lambda_t\) summing to \(1\), and value vectors \(v_t\in[0,1]^m\); the CTR vector \(\theta\in(0,1]^{\tau m}\) is an unknown environment parameter, not a mechanism input. A policy assigns each advertiser probabilities of receiving each format at each stage, based on its own report, the distribution of reports, and observed clicks. Feasibility requires total assignment mass at each stage to be at most \(1\), the normalized per-capita supply. A deviating advertiser’s report must still affect its own allocation rule even though its deviation has zero mass in the population distribution. Payments must be computable from that run’s observed history alone.

The problem is to construct such a policy and payment rule that are stochastically truthful for every CTR vector and whose per-capita welfare beats the uniform-allocation benchmark on every nonuniform, skewed instance with \(\sigma>1\) and \(\max_{t,j}v_{t,j}\theta_{t,j}>\epsilon\). For the benchmark, uniform allocation selects uniformly from the population of advertiser-format pairs. With common portfolio size \(m\), its per-stage welfare is \(M_1\), where
\[ M_q=\left(\sum_t\lambda_t\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m(v_{t,j}\theta_{t,j})^q\right)^{1/q}, \]
and skew is \(M_2^2\ge \sigma M_1^2\). A solution would be a finite-support policy and payment algorithm, with a runtime bound in \(m,\tau,H\), and the input bit length, plus proofs of feasibility, truthfulness, information-feasibility, and the claimed welfare guarantee. The paper does not give this continuous result or a polynomial-time bound for it; those are part of the proposed problem.

I tentatively expect the weighted allocation part to be Class A: the finite-support moments and allocation constraints have a direct type-mass form, and the paper’s structural argument may survive weighting. The mirror covers only the stochastic-truthfulness and welfare-advantage result of Theorem 6.3(a), not the paper’s general payment transformation or its ex-post impossibility results. I would not treat Theorem 5.1(c) as a hardness anchor: it is an ex-post approximation impossibility, not a complexity classification, and its single-advertiser bound need not persist in a population limit.

The weak point is substantial. Scaling impression supply with the population makes aggregate click observations nearly deterministic, so the learning problem may become easier in a way the finite-market theorem does not capture. Also, atomlessness makes an advertiser’s effect on aggregate reports vanish; the mirror must retain individual report-dependent treatment to keep truthfulness meaningful. Those choices make this a credible continuous extension of the paper’s mechanism-design question, but not a direct limit theorem. The paper is also closer to algorithmic mechanism design than to canonical computational social choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6.3(a) is a weak anchor for ChoCo: it proves a welfare advantage for an explicit mechanism, but does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. ALL and the payment transformation are given constructively; the theorem asks whether their welfare beats a benchmark, not how hard it is to find an optimal policy or solve a worst-case problem. A continuous analogue could be a sound mechanism-design question, but it would not chart the complexity landscape this programme is meant to study.

There is also a real modeling tradeoff in the proposed mirror. In the paper, each ad is an individual bandit arm with its own unknown CTR. The proponent makes CTRs common within a portfolio type so repeated advertisers can be grouped. But with \(N\) copies of each positive-mass type, exploring a vanishing fraction \(\alpha_N\) of the population still yields \(\Theta(N\alpha_N)\) click observations per type-format. Taking \(\alpha_N\to0\) while \(N\alpha_N\to\infty\) makes those CTRs arbitrarily well known at vanishing per-capita exploration cost. The limit therefore removes the uncertainty that makes the paper’s MAB and no-simulation setting difficult. Keeping only \(O(1)\) total observations instead means serving a vanishing fraction of the population; per-capita welfare and individual impact vanish in the limit. And if CTRs remain idiosyncratic by advertiser, they must distinguish types, so the support grows with \(N\) and the proposed finite-support high-multiplicity regime is lost.

One could repair the model by asking for the best truthful policy at finite \(N\), or for its convergence rate as \(N\) grows. That may be worthwhile work, but it is a new asymptotic mechanism-design problem. With finitely many shared CTRs, the limiting model can learn them nearly for free; the paper’s strict improvement over random allocation then no longer represents the original informational challenge. The finite-\(N\) version retains that challenge, but is precisely the discrete high-multiplicity problem rather than a new continuous complexity result.

I would not claim this proves that no continuous model of strategic learning could be valuable. A model of type-level learning with finite-sample guarantees might be. The negative case is narrower but stronger: the proponent’s anchor either loses its defining uncertainty in the continuum, leaves the high-multiplicity regime, or becomes a welfare-guarantee question without the computational classification ChoCo targets.
