The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) schools and \(\tau\) student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), type-level school preferences and priority tiers, a master-list ordering of the tiers, and a rational downward-closed feasible occupancy polytope \(\mathcal F\), compute the exact aggregate assignment \(x_{tc}\) produced by staged GDA. At each stage, a supplied polynomial-time rule chooses a rational mass limit \(\delta_k\) such that the feasible residual occupancy vectors within that limit form a polymatroid; determine the runtime in the input bit length, \(\tau\), \(m\), the polytope description, and the number \(K\) of stages.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population of student types carrying mass \(\mu_t\); variables \(x_{tc}\) give the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(c\), with unmatched mass recorded separately. The task is exact outcome computation under staged, priority-respecting GDA and per-capita occupancy constraints; it has no separate optimization objective.

The objection that survived

In the paper's strict-ranking model, each student's master-list position and college priorities can distinguish that student, so complete types may be singletons; the proposed tiered priorities and mass-stage semantics require a model extension.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns Theorem 10's conditional outcome-computation result. It does not cover the strategyproofness, fairness, weak nonwastefulness, Pareto-frontier theorems, or the experimental comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the stage masses be selected efficiently from a succinct description of \(\mathcal F\)?
  • Can the aggregate proposal process be bounded polynomially in \(m\), \(\tau\), the input bit length, and \(K\)?
  • Which incentive and fairness guarantees, if any, hold for the tiered mass mechanism?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I see is narrow: a continuous version of computing the outcome of MS-GDA in a large school-placement market. The type is a complete student profile: preferences over schools, the student’s master-list priority tier, and the priority weights that each school assigns to that profile. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students of type \(t\). A type-level assignment \(x_{tc}\) records how much of that mass goes to school \(c\); it represents a partition of the type’s students, so each student still gets one school. School capacities and distributional quotas are expressed as a feasible set of per-capita occupancy vectors.

A plausible regime is a large annual placement system with many students but relatively few combinations of preference, eligibility, and priority profiles. Several hundred thousand students might share a few hundred such types. Master-list tiers and school priorities are part of the type, so grouping students does not discard information the mechanism uses. Ties among people within a type can be broken arbitrarily if that cannot change the aggregate assignment; otherwise the type must be refined.

My lead anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper. It says that, if computing the constraint function \(f\) and choosing the stage size \(d\) can each be done in polynomial time, MS-GDA runs in polynomial time. The proof bounds the number of stages by \(n\), since each \(d\geq 1\), and uses the cited \(O(T(f)n^2)\) bound for GDA, where \(T(f)\) is the time to compute \(f\). The theorem is therefore conditional, and its original input is an explicit finite market. The continuous question below is a proposed analogue of that result, not a result the paper already proves.

I would call it Continuous MS-GDA Outcome Computation. An instance has \(m\) schools and \(\tau\) student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one; each type has the preference and priority data just described. It also has a rational, downward-closed polytope \(\mathcal F\subseteq[0,1]^m\) of feasible school-occupancy vectors, a master-list ordering of the types, and a polynomial-time rule for choosing rational stage masses \(\delta_k>0\). After earlier stages produce occupancy vector \(b^k\), the rule must choose \(\delta_k\) so that
\[ \left\{v\geq 0:\ b^k+v\in\mathcal F,\ \sum_{c=1}^m v_c\leq\delta_k\right\} \]
is a polymatroid. The rule must process all remaining student mass in polynomially many stages.

The question is: compute the exact aggregate assignment \(x_{tc}\) produced by running staged GDA on those mass slices, where each type’s unassigned mass proposes in its preference order and each school tentatively keeps maximum-priority feasible mass under the stage’s polymatroid constraint. A solution is the rational assignment, with unmatched mass, together with enough stage information to verify that it follows those GDA choices and respects \(\mathcal F\). The expected direction is tractable, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), the polytope description size, the number of stages \(K\), and the cost of the supplied stage rule. At each proposal step, school choice is a linear optimization over type masses. Establishing a polynomial bound on the aggregate proposal process is part of the problem.

This keeps the paper’s mechanism recognizable: student preferences, school priorities, the master list, residual constraints, and the GDA subroutine all remain. The co-modification is that integer occupancy vectors become per-capita masses, and the M♮-convex stage condition becomes a polymatroid condition. It mirrors the runtime claim of Theorem 10 only; it does not claim that strategyproofness, ML-fairness, or weak nonwastefulness automatically extend to the continuum.

The main further questions are whether the stage masses can be selected efficiently from a succinct constraint description, whether aggregate computation can preserve meaningful incentive and fairness guarantees, and when the continuous outcome approximates or exactly represents outcomes in large finite markets.

The weakest point is that Theorem 10 establishes polynomial time for an explicitly represented finite market and assumes polynomial-time computation of \(f\) and \(d\). It does not establish a high-multiplicity algorithm. In the mirror, the continuous stage decomposition and the type-level GDA process both need new analysis; a discrete \(d\geq1\) argument alone does not supply it. There is no comparable numbered hardness result in this paper to anchor a hardness-dissolution question, so Theorem 10 is the relevant computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 10 is the right anchor, but its runtime bound does not expose a high-multiplicity regime in the model as written. MS-GDA takes an explicit master list of \(n\) students, and each college has a strict ranking of students. Since the master list places every student at a distinct position, a type that records everything the mechanism uses must record that position. Then \(\tau=n\), so the proposed input with a few hundred priority “tiers” is not a compressed instance of this paper’s model. If the type omits position or college priority, it omits information that can change which students are processed in a stage or accepted by a college.

One could make a sensible variant with tied priority classes and split mass within a class. That is the strongest version of the mirror, and the large-placement scenario makes it plausible. But it changes the strict-priority model and supplies new semantics for cutting a stage through a cohort. Theorem 10 gives no aggregate algorithm or outcome-equivalence result for those semantics. Its polynomial bound is in the explicit population size \(n\); it does not analyze running time in \(\tau\) and the bit length of a compressed population. A continuous runtime theorem would therefore establish a new result about a tiered, aggregate mechanism, rather than continue the theorem’s existing complexity analysis.

There is a second gap in the proposed formulation. The continuous instance is given a polynomial-time stage rule and counts its number of stages \(K\) as a parameter. Those assumptions carry much of the work: the paper’s stages are prefixes of an ordered list of individual students, while a mass-stage rule must decide how to divide type masses and ensure that the aggregate GDA process makes finite progress. Theorem 10 assumes efficient computation of \(d\), but does not solve that choice problem. Providing a rule may make the aggregate computation question well-posed; it also means the mirror is not deriving the paper’s runtime guarantee from a compressed version of its stated input.

That is the best case against this anchor: the paper’s strict individual priorities prevent the proposed compression, and the tiered repair asks for a new mechanism and a new runtime result. It is not an airtight universal objection. Large placement systems with genuinely shared priority classes are plausible, and prior high-multiplicity matching work supports the regime rather than counting against it. A continuous aggregate MS-GDA for such systems could be worthwhile; I would describe it as an extension of this paper’s model, not a direct continuous mirror of Theorem 10.
