The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Proposition 2 says that every profile has at least one Stable Voting winner and that a uniquely weighted profile has exactly one; Propositions 1 and 3–5 characterize the rule or establish voting criteria. None asserts a complexity result for a computational problem. The conclusion explicitly leaves Stable Voting’s computational complexity open.

A plausible *unanchored* mirror is Stable Voting on Rational Societies: given candidates \(C\) and a finite support of \(\tau\) ballot types with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), compute the Stable Voting winner set. For each pair \(A,B\), use the mass-weighted head-to-head margin
\[ d_\mu(A,B)=\sum_t \mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[A\succ_t B]-\mathbf{1}[B\succ_t A]\bigr), \]
then apply the paper’s recursive match ordering and defeat-path rule to those margins. A solution is the set of winners, including ties. A natural regime is a large electorate with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings.

That is a plausible population mirror, but it has no numbered complexity result to anchor it. Every rational society can be scaled to integer ballot counts by taking a common denominator, preserving all margin comparisons and the resulting winners. So the model is sensible as a high-multiplicity computational question; the paper gives no basis to predict whether it is tractable or hard. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to anchor a mirror. Proposition 2 proves existence and uniqueness under a condition; the other propositions establish characterizations and voting criteria. None gives a complexity result, and the conclusion explicitly leaves Stable Voting’s computational complexity open. So the proponent’s case is an unanchored continuation, not a mirror of a result in the paper.

The proposed computation on rational societies is nevertheless a sensible high-multiplicity question: a large electorate with relatively few distinct rankings is plausible. Scaling rational masses to integer ballot counts preserves the margins and winners, though the resulting electorate can be much larger than the encoded input. That is a real compact-input question, and high-multiplicity work supports the regime.

The best objection is that Stable Voting depends only on pairwise margins. From \(\tau\) supported types, those can be computed in \(O(\tau m^2)\) time; after that, the rankings themselves disappear, and the task is computing the rule on a rational weighted tournament. This makes the mirror less connected to the continuous-optimization machinery that motivates the programme. But it does not make the problem ill-posed or uninteresting: the paper itself asks about the complexity of computing Stable Voting, and the compact high-multiplicity encoding could change that analysis. The honest negative case is therefore limited: no established computational result is being mirrored, and the proposed mirror’s fit with the programme is weaker than its fit as a new complexity question. I cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
