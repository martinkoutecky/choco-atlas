Its Theorem 1 says concave TODs are subadditive; Theorems 4 and 5 assert that the negotiation set is nonempty for pure deals in any TOD and for mixed deals in subadditive TODs; Theorem 6 says an optimal mixed-deal procedure in a subadditive two-agent TOD divides available utility equally. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names Theorems 1, 4, 5, and 6, but none is a computational result. Theorem 1 relates concavity to subadditivity; Theorems 4 and 5 establish that certain negotiation sets are nonempty; Theorem 6 characterizes the utility split under optimal negotiation between two agents. They specify no computational problem or complexity bound. The paper’s mention of low computational complexity as a design criterion does not supply one. That leaves no named computational result here for a continuous-complexity mirror to investigate.

The best population version would group agents with recurring task sets and costs, then treat their proportions as masses. That regime is plausible, but it does not rescue these anchors. Extending Theorem 1 to aggregate task demand would study a property of the domain’s cost function, not the computational complexity of a population problem. A mass-based version of Theorems 4 and 5 would ask whether an efficient, individually rational allocation exists among many agents. The paper proves existence for two-agent deal models; it gives no computational target for selecting such an allocation in a population. Those could motivate new work, but they would be newly formulated problems rather than mirrors of the paper’s results.

Theorem 6 is the closest candidate for a stronger mirror: take two large classes of agents, aggregate each class’s utility, and ask whether the optimal negotiation divides the available utility equally. But that either retains two bargaining parties, leaving the population continuum outside the theorem’s central object, or requires a new multi-agent bargaining model whose allocation and utility notions the paper does not define. In either version, the equal-split result is an outcome claim, not a computational classification.

So the objection is not that high multiplicity is implausible; recurring task-and-cost profiles make it quite sensible. The gap is that no choice of population scenario turns these particular results into computational claims. A computational study of high-multiplicity negotiation might be worthwhile on its own, but it would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
