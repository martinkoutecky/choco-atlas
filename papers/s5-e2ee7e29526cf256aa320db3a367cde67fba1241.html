What the mirror covers

The proposed population interpretation covers no computational result; Theorems 7-1 and 7-2 and the paper's other results are axiomatic characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The limited positive case is that this paper has a natural population model waiting in the background. Let a type be a ranking of the alternatives and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of society with ranking \(t\). The paper’s non-Paretian condition has a direct distributional form: for every pair \(x,y\), positive mass ranks \(x\) above \(y\), and positive mass ranks \(y\) above \(x\). The authors themselves motivate \(NP\) partly by large societies with few alternatives, so a high-multiplicity regime is plausible.

But the paper contains no named computational result to anchor a mirror. Theorem 7-1, proved here, says that for all \(m,n\ge 3\), every strategy-proof rule on \(NP(n,m)\) with full range is dictatorial: one named individual’s top-ranked alternative is selected at every profile. Theorem 7-2, also proved here, extends the conclusion to strategy-proof rules whose range has at least three alternatives. These are axiomatic classification theorems; neither asserts a complexity bound or studies an algorithmic problem.

There is also a substantive obstacle to turning that opening into a continuous computational question. A distribution \(\mu\) forgets voter identities, while the theorems’ conclusion singles out one. And in a nonatomic population, one individual’s report changes zero mass, so any rule depending only on \(\mu\) is automatically strategy-proof against unilateral deviations. Requiring a positive-mass group to deviate would restore strategic force, but would change the paper’s notion of strategy-proofness.

So the population-side reinterpretation is intelligible, but it does not yield a continuous computational mirror of a named result in this paper. Under the requested anchor standard, there is no per-anchor problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is prior to any choice of type space: this paper proves no computational result. Theorem 7-1 classifies full-range strategy-proof rules; Theorem 7-2 extends that classification to rules with range of at least three alternatives. Both are axiomatic theorems about which rules can exist, not complexity or algorithmic results. The paper supplies no computational problem, input encoding, or objective for a continuous optimization method to address. One could invent an algorithmic question about a succinctly represented rule, but its complexity would depend on that added representation and would not mirror either theorem.

The proposed distribution over rankings is nevertheless a natural population model. The \(NP\) condition translates cleanly: for every pair of alternatives, positive mass ranks each above the other. The difficulty is that the theorems’ central axiom is unilateral strategy-proofness, and their conclusion identifies a particular individual as dictator. In an atomless society represented only by \(\mu\), one person’s report changes no mass. Any rule based only on \(\mu\) is therefore strategy-proof against unilateral deviations, while a named individual’s ranking is not recoverable from \(\mu\). That makes the direct continuum restatement vacuous on the axiom and ill-defined on the conclusion.

A stronger proposed mirror could let a positive-mass group change its reports. That preserves strategic consequences, but it asks about group strategy-proofness, a different property from the one classified here. Alternatively, retain named individuals as atoms alongside a continuous population. Then individual incentives and dictatorship can be meaningful, but the strategic agents remain a finite identified set; the continuum is background to a new mixed model, not the population continuization of this theorem. Neither repair yields a computational result from the paper.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated computational programme, though not a proof that no worthwhile follow-on research exists. A continuous axiomatic study of group deviations, or a mixed atom-and-continuum model, might be interesting on its own. But those would be new axiomatic projects. The natural population interpretation does not turn Theorems 7-1 or 7-2 into computational mirrors.
