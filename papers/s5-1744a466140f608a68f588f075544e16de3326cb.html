What the mirror covers

The proposed model addresses the finite-sample estimation guarantees in §4 under a smooth worth-density assumption. It does not cover arbitrary characteristic-function games, and §5's coalition-size stratification only motivates a related question about stratifying the continuous rank variable.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-cohort approximation error be bounded under explicit smoothness assumptions, and can the estimator use worth evaluations of \(F\) instead of an oracle for its derivative?
  • Does stratifying the mass or arrival-rank variable improve the sample bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is real but narrow. The paper gives no numbered P/NP or other complexity-class result. Its strongest anchor for a continuous mirror is the unnumbered Theorem in §4.2; no number is printed in the supplied text, so I would not invent one. It states: if the Shapley value \(\phi\) of a player is \(O(r f(n)\ln n)\), where \(n\) is the number of players, \(r\) is the range of that player’s marginal contributions, and \(0<f(n)<1\), then there are constants \(c,d>0\) such that
\[ \Pr(|\hat{\phi}-\phi|\ge\epsilon)\le d\exp\!\left(-\frac{cm\epsilon^2}{\phi}\right). \]
The paper attributes this to a straightforward application of Corollary 4.7 of Vu and omits the proof. Its ensuing estimate is an \(O(\sqrt{r\ln n\,\ln(1/\delta)/m})\) error with probability at least \(1-\delta\); the constants are hidden. This is an estimation guarantee, not a hardness result.

A plausible mirror is Continuous Type-Shapley Estimation. Take a fixed finite set \(T\) of player types and a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). Each type completely captures the player’s role and all attributes that affect the game, so members of a type are interchangeable. A natural regime is a large cooperative platform or production network with many contributors but only a modest number of recurring roles. For example, millions of participants might fall into tens of contribution types.

Let \(F(x)\) be the worth density for a coalition with mass vector \(x\), where \(0\le x\le\mu\). For a cohort of \(n\) players with type proportions \(\mu\), let a coalition \(S\) have worth \(v_n(S)=nF(x(S))\), where \(x(S)\) is its vector of type proportions. Assume \(F\) is smooth enough for the following directional derivative to exist. For a target type \(t\), set \(g_t(u)=\partial_t F(u\mu)\) and \(\psi_t=\int_0^1 g_t(u)\,du\). Under this model, \(\psi_t\) is the limiting per-member Shapley allocation for type \(t\): a member’s random arrival rank tends to a uniform \(u\), and the coalition preceding that member tends to mass \(u\mu\).

The continuous problem’s instance is \((T,\mu,F,t,\epsilon,\delta)\), with oracle access to \(g_t(u)\) and the stated range or moment information. It asks for a randomized estimate \(\hat{\psi}_t\) such that \(\Pr(|\hat{\psi}_t-\psi_t|\ge\epsilon)\le\delta\); a solution is an estimator and its sample bound. Sampling independent uniform \(u_j\) and returning \(m^{-1}\sum_j g_t(u_j)\) directly mirrors sampling random permutations and averaging marginal contributions. In particular, if \(g_t\) has known range \(r\), the paper’s Hoeffding calculation gives \(m\ge\lceil r^2\ln(2/\delta)/(2\epsilon^2)\rceil\). Under the theorem’s additional envelope assumption across finite cohorts, its improved bound gives a smaller dependence on \(r\), with the caveat that the paper does not make the constants explicit.

I expect this mirror to be tractable by randomized sampling—Class A. It covers the paper’s finite-sample estimation guarantees in §4, not the general computation of Shapley values for arbitrary characteristic functions. Section 5’s stratification by coalition size suggests a related continuous question: whether stratifying the rank variable \(u\), or other mass-based summaries, improves the query bound.

The authors should recognize the underlying question: estimate a player’s expected marginal contribution under random arrival. Existing type symmetry is precisely what makes the high-multiplicity regime sensible here. The co-modification is the worth-density model \(F\), which makes type-level mass and marginal contribution density well defined.

The weakest point is that a continuum has no individual player with positive mass. The proposed \(\psi_t\) is a limit of finite-cohort Shapley values, and that limit relies on type symmetry and smoothness. The paper’s theorem does not establish convergence to this limit or control the finite-cohort approximation error. A complete result would need to bound that error and connect the theorem’s constants and assumptions to the continuous model. That limitation makes this a conditional mirror, not a continuization of the paper’s full general-game setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about what information the continuous model needs. The paper’s Shapley value is defined for a characteristic-function game whose coalition values come from an oracle. Even if players fall into a fixed set of interchangeable types, the finite game supplies values only at lattice points: the possible type-count vectors. Those values do not determine a continuous worth density \(F\), or its derivatives. Many smooth functions can agree on the finite game’s lattice and have different derivatives between lattice points. So the proposed target
\[ \psi_t=\int_0^1 \partial_t F(u\mu)\,du \]
is not determined by the paper’s input. The construction \(v_n(S)=nF(x(S))\) resolves that ambiguity by making \(F\) part of the model, but it adds a smoothness and scaling assumption the paper does not make. A mirror would need to justify that model and bound the finite-cohort error; the paper’s results do neither.

The proponent’s stronger formulation is mathematically coherent: many contributors can share a modest set of roles, and a per-member Shapley allocation is a plausible quantity. But with oracle access to \(\partial_t F\), the continuous task is Monte Carlo integration along a one-dimensional path. The paper’s Hoeffding argument transfers directly. That gives a valid continuous guarantee, but it does not carry the paper’s characteristic-function oracle into the continuum; it assumes a stronger oracle that already exposes the marginal contribution density. If the model instead provides only values of \(F\), the mirror needs extra regularity bounds and a way to account for the cost and error of estimating derivatives. The main modelling burden has moved into the new input.

There is no decisive “no computational result” objection: although the paper has no numbered complexity-class theorem, it does give finite-sample estimation bounds and sampling algorithms. Nor does the fact that the continuous bound resembles the discrete one disqualify the mirror; a dull answer can still be worth asking. The negative case is narrower: the paper’s result does not itself establish that its arbitrary-game estimation problem has a well-defined continuous-population counterpart.

That case is not strong enough to defeat every plausible mirror. The role-based, smooth-worth model is a reasonable co-modification, and its per-member limit is recognizable as a Shapley question. The best honest verdict is that the proposed mirror is conditional and requires substantial new modelling, not that no worthwhile mirror exists. The positive analyst’s single anchor survives in that narrow form.
