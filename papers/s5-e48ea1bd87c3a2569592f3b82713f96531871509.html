The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(M\), finite valuation-type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), subadditive valuation oracles \(v_\theta:2^M\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), and \(\psi\ge\max_{\theta\in T}v_\theta(M)\), where one unit of population carries one unit of each item supply, compute a universally truthful randomized posted-price mechanism using polynomially many type-level demand queries. The mechanism must produce masses \(x_{\theta,S}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_S x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S:j\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le 1\), while achieving \(\mathbb{E}[W(x)]\ge\frac{1}{2\alpha(\beta+1)^2}\operatorname{OPT}_\infty-\frac{\psi}{m}\), with \(\alpha=O(\log\!\log m)\) for subadditive valuations and \(\beta=O(\log\!\log m)\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity combinatorial-auction market with valuation types \(\theta\), population masses \(\mu_\theta\), per-capita item supply, integral bundles \(S\subseteq M\), aggregate allocation variables \(x_{\theta,S}\), welfare objective \(\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}v_\theta(S)\), and posted-price mechanisms whose configuration-LP pricing problem is answered by demand queries.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the truthful price-learning and welfare-approximation guarantees in Theorems 1 and 2 for subadditive, XOS, and submodular valuations. It leaves the internal price lemmas, proof details, and concluding approximation open problems without separate mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper's binary-search price-learning argument survive when each round produces fractional sale quantities rather than a sale/no-sale event?
  • What atomless individual or type-level incentive notion preserves the paper's universal truthfulness guarantee?
  • Can the configuration LP and demand-query separation oracle yield a polynomial-time continuous mechanism with the stated approximation?
  • How should the unknown-scale calibration branch of Theorem 2 be formalized over a distribution of valuation types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a Class A, high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s truthful posted-price mechanism. This paper contains no named NP-hardness, coNP-hardness, \(\#\)P-hardness, or parameterized-hardness theorem, so there is no honest hardness anchor. Its useful anchors are algorithmic: the paper asks whether truthful welfare approximation can be made computationally efficient, and that question has a natural continuous-population form.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper rather than cited. It states that there is a universally truthful mechanism for combinatorial auctions with subadditive bidders, using at most \(n\) demand and value queries and polynomial time, whose expected welfare is an \(O((\log\!\log m)^3)\)-approximation to optimum. For XOS, and hence submodular, bidders, the guarantee improves to \(O((\log\!\log m)^2)\). The theorem is therefore not merely about an LP formulation: it asserts an efficient truthful allocation mechanism with a quantitative welfare guarantee.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here. Given \(\psi\geq \max_i v_i(M)\), the paper’s binary-search mechanism \(\mech\) is universally truthful, polynomial-time, and uses at most \(n\) demand queries. Its expected welfare satisfies

\[ \E[\Welfare]\geq \frac{1}{2\alpha(\beta+1)^2} \sum_i v_i(S_i^\star)-\frac{\psi}{m}, \]

where \(\alpha=O(\log\!\log m)\) for subadditive valuations, \(\alpha=O(1)\) for XOS valuations, and \(\beta=O(\log\!\log m)\). Theorem 2 removes the extra assumption that \(\psi\) is known by adding the sampling and second-price branch. I regard Theorem 2 as the lead anchor, but Theorem 1 is the cleaner structural mirror because it isolates the price-learning mechanism itself.

The continuous market I would propose is a large procurement market with \(m\) standardized item classes and many buyers. A type \(\theta\in T\) is a complete procurement profile: a normalized, monotone, subadditive valuation \(v_\theta:2^M\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\), together with any tie-breaking information relevant to the mechanism. The population is a distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{T}\), with \(\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta=1\). Thus \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of buyers having that valuation profile.

The item supply must scale with the population. The simplest version has one copy per unit population of every item class. Equivalently, for a market of size \(q\), there are \(q\mu_\theta\) buyers of type \(\theta\) and \(q\) copies of each item class. Each individual buyer still receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\); only the aggregate assignment is continuous. If \(x_{\theta,S}\) denotes the mass of type-\(\theta\) buyers receiving bundle \(S\), feasibility is

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta \quad\text{for every }\theta, \]

and

\[ \sum_{\theta\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{\theta,S}\leq 1 \quad\text{for every item }j. \]

The welfare is

\[ W(x)=\sum_{\theta\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq M} x_{\theta,S}v_\theta(S), \]

and \(\OPT_\infty\) is the maximum of this quantity over feasible \(x\). This is a continuous population model, not merely fractional outcomes: \(x_{\theta,S}\) records which fraction of the population receives each integral bundle.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is direct. For rational \(\mu\), take \(q\) large enough that every \(q\mu_\theta\) is integral. The continuous allocation is then the normalized limit of allocations in a finite market with \(q\mu_\theta\) repeated buyers of each type and \(q\) repeated copies of each item class. The regime of interest is \(q\gg \tau=|T|\), with \(m\) and \(\tau\) fixed or moderately growing. A plausible example is a national procurement platform with millions of buyers but only a few hundred recurring valuation profiles for standardized product categories.

The first precise mirror is:

\[ \mathsf{CSA\text{-}KnownScale}_\infty. \]

An instance consists of \(M\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), subadditive valuation oracles \(v_\theta\), and a number \(\psi\) satisfying

\[ \psi\geq \max_{\theta\in T}v_\theta(M). \]

The task is to compute a universally truthful randomized posted-price mechanism. For each realization of its random seed, the mechanism must assign feasible masses \(x_{\theta,S}\) and charge each buyer prices independent of that buyer’s report. Its expected welfare must satisfy

\[ \E[W]\geq \frac{1}{2\alpha(\beta+1)^2}\OPT_\infty-\frac{\psi}{m}, \]

with \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) as in Theorem 1. The computation should be polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length, using type-level demand queries rather than one query for every repeated buyer.

This is recognisably the same problem as Theorem 1. The action is still choosing bundles for buyers; the objective is still welfare; the valuation class is still subadditivity; the mechanism is still universally truthful; and the central device is still item prices learned through fixed-price auctions. The only change is that repeated buyers are represented by their mass.

There is also a strong computational reason to expect this mirror to be tractable. The continuous welfare problem has the configuration-LP form

\[ \max\; \sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}v_\theta(S), \]

with the feasibility constraints above. Its dual has variables \(u_\theta\) and item prices \(p_j\):

\[ \min\; \sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta u_\theta+\sum_j p_j \]

subject to

\[ u_\theta+\sum_{j\in S}p_j\geq v_\theta(S) \quad\text{for every }\theta,S. \]

Separating a dual solution asks whether some type has a bundle maximizing

\[ v_\theta(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j, \]

which is exactly a demand query. Thus the exponential family of bundle configurations has a natural pricing oracle already present in the paper. In the continuous setting there is no rounding loss from assigning fractional mass: the mass is genuinely split among many integral-bundle buyers. This is precisely the kind of LP-plus-pricing structure for which continuization should expose useful optimization machinery.

I would expect \(\mathsf{CSA\text{-}KnownScale}_\infty\) to be in Class A. The likely proof would replace the paper’s finite bidder groups \(N_\ell\) by random submasses of the population, run the same fixed-price auctions, and integrate utilities and revenues over types. The paper’s price-learning argument is especially compatible with this: a type’s repeated buyers can be distributed across the binary-search rounds, and all buyers face item prices rather than individualized prices.

The second precise mirror is:

\[ \mathsf{CSA}_\infty. \]

It has the same instance and solution format, but \(\psi\) is not supplied. The mechanism must estimate the relevant full-bundle scale from a calibration subpopulation and then output a universally truthful randomized allocation with polynomial running time and type-level demand/value queries. For subadditive valuations it must achieve

\[ \E[W]\geq \frac{1}{O((\log\!\log m)^3)}\OPT_\infty, \]

and for XOS or submodular valuations,

\[ \E[W]\geq \frac{1}{O((\log\!\log m)^2)}\OPT_\infty. \]

This mirrors Theorem 2, including its unknown-scale issue. The continuous analogue of the paper’s sampling branch is natural: sample a positive-measure calibration population, identify the largest full-bundle value among the sampled types, and use that value to define the candidate price range for the remaining population. Since every positive-mass type is represented by many repeated buyers, calibration is more stable than in a small finite market.

I would also expect this problem to be Class A, although less cleanly than the known-scale version. The configuration LP and demand pricing remain available, and the paper’s use of posted prices is already the right truthfulness technology for a nonatomic population. The further questions are whether the \((\log\!\log m)^3\) guarantee survives exactly, whether the calibration branch can be implemented with only \(O(\tau\log\!\log m)\) type-level queries, and whether the continuous problem admits an exact configuration-LP mechanism with a better approximation factor than the paper’s finite-population construction.

The weakest point is that the paper’s proof uses a genuinely discrete event: each item is either sold or unsold in a particular round. With per-capita supply, a positive mass of buyers may purchase copies of the same item class, so the binary search may observe a sale fraction rather than a single sale/no-sale bit. Likewise, the finite proof sums an additive error over at most \(m\) nonempty optimal bundles, whereas a continuum allocation may involve positive mass from many types. These issues mean that Theorem 1 does not transfer as a formal theorem merely by replacing sums with integrals.

There is a second, narrower truthfulness caveat. The mirror treats individual buyers as atomless strategic agents. A unilateral deviation has zero effect on the population distribution or learned prices, which makes posted-price truthfulness natural. If instead an entire positive-mass type is treated as one strategic coalition, the paper’s truthfulness argument no longer applies directly. That is a real modelling choice, not a technicality.

Even with those concessions, this is a credible mirror. It keeps the paper’s underlying valuation problem, its welfare objective, its demand-query model, its posted-price mechanism, and its truthful-deviation requirement. The continuous modification is exactly the high-multiplicity regime: many buyers share complete valuation types, while item supplies scale with the population. The strongest positive claim is therefore not that the paper secretly contains a hardness result, but that its price-learning mechanism is a natural candidate for a continuous, LP-and-pricing-based Class A theory.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case. The paper passes the anchor gate: Theorems 1 and 2 are named computational results about truthful, polynomial-time welfare mechanisms and query complexity. There is no valid “no computational result” objection.

The strongest attack is that the proposed lift of Theorem 1 is not automatic. The paper’s fixed-price auctions use a finite ordered set of bidders, one copy of each item, and a sale/no-sale event. In a continuum with per-capita supplies, a round may sell only a fraction of an item class, so the binary-search update is no longer the same event. With fixed total supply, rationing and ordering among atomless buyers require a different model. Theorem 2’s calibration branch likewise does not transfer verbatim: a second-price auction among a sampled population is not the same as awarding one indivisible grand bundle to a named bidder.

Those are real defects in the proponent’s claimed transfer, but they do not defeat the better mirror. Per-capita supply is explicitly an admissible co-modification, and repeated procurement profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The mechanism can be reformulated with fractional sale quantities, type-level rationing, or a configuration-based allocation rule. That becomes an open continuous mechanism-design problem rather than an incoherent one.

Indeed, Theorem 1 has an even stronger continuous formulation than the proponent gives. With type masses \(\mu_\theta\) and integral bundles \(S\), the aggregate allocation variables \(x_{\theta,S}\) satisfy

\[ \sum_S x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta, \qquad \sum_{\theta,S\ni j}x_{\theta,S}\leq 1. \]

This is a genuine population-continuization: mass is allocated among integral bundles. Its dual separation problem is

\[ \max_S\left(v_\theta(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\right), \]

which is precisely a demand query. Thus the continuous model naturally exposes the configuration-LP and pricing machinery central to the programme. Rational masses can also be cleared to large finite populations of repeated types, giving the required high-multiplicity bridge.

The remaining objection is strategic rather than computational. For atomless individuals, a unilateral report has zero effect on learned prices, so truthfulness becomes price-taking and partly degenerate. If instead a whole positive-mass type is strategic, one has introduced coalition or type-agent deviations. But that is a modelling choice requiring an explicit incentive notion, not a proof that no worthwhile population model exists.

So the strongest honest negative conclusion is narrow: the proponent has not established that either theorem transfers unchanged, and Theorem 2 is particularly underspecified. That does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 1 survives as a credible Class A high-multiplicity anchor, possibly through a configuration-LP mechanism rather than the paper’s binary-search mechanism.
