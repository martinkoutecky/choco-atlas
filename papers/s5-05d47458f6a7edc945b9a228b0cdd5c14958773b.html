The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete household types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a piecewise-linear demand schedule \(q_t(p)\) for slot \(h\) at fixed prices in other slots, and a per-capita supply cost \(g(z)\), compute the lowest nonnegative price \(p\) with positive trade satisfying \(p z(p)=g(z(p))\), where \(z(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t q_{t,h}(p)\). Return that price and each type’s demand there, allowing mass to split across feasible schedules at ties; return no trade if no such price exists.

The model it lives in

A proxy market with a continuum of households represented by complete preference and demand types carrying masses \(\mu_t\). Each type supplies its demand schedule for one slot while other-slot prices are fixed; the aggregator scales total supply cost with population, so per-capita cost is \(g(z)\). The output is the minimum break-even price and the resulting type-level allocation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s proxy-phase minimum-equilibrium price and allocation for one slot with other-slot prices fixed. It leaves the finite-bidder Bayes–Nash guarantee, the clock phase, rolling-horizon dynamics, and simulations alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions does a bidder-submodularity analogue for type masses preserve the welfare and allocation claims?
  • How hard is it to generate a type’s demand schedule from compact appliance constraints?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, stated and argued in this paper with a proof sketch that uses results cited from earlier work. It says: if the coalition worth function is bidder-submodular and the aggregator computes minimal competitive-equilibrium prices, then the proxy phase implements the auction’s VCG allocation, and straightforward bidding is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium. This is the paper’s only numbered result I would use. It concerns the outcome and incentives of a computation, rather than classifying a problem as polynomial-time or hard; the paper gives no numbered complexity result.

My lead mirror is Continuous Minimum Competitive Equilibrium (C-MCE). Consider an aggregator serving a large cohort of households over a fixed horizon, and clearing one time slot \(h\) in the proxy phase while prices in the other slots, \(\bar p_{-h}\), are fixed. A type is a complete household preference and demand response for those fixed prices: the same feasible schedules and values. The instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational population fractions \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and each type’s piecewise-linear demand schedule \(q_t(p)\) as the price \(p\) for slot \(h\) varies. It also gives the aggregator’s per-capita supply cost \(g(z)\), where \(z\) is energy use per household. This normalization is the needed co-modification: with \(N\) households, total cost is \(N g(z)\), so the break-even unit price does not grow just because the population is larger.

The decision variable is a common price \(p\) and the resulting type-level demand profiles. Aggregate demand per household is \(z(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t q_{t,h}(p)\). The task is to return the lowest nonnegative price with positive trade satisfying \(p z(p)=g(z(p))\), together with each type’s demand at that price. If there is no positive-trade solution, return no trade. For the paper’s demonstration cost shape, \(g(z)=a z^2\), the clearing condition is \(p=a z(p)\). If a type is indifferent among discrete appliance schedules, its mass may be split across those schedules; each household still receives a feasible schedule, while the type’s average demand can be fractional.

This is a plausible population regime: for example, a utility coordinating many households enrolled in standardized energy-management cohorts, with types determined by appliance portfolio, task state, and preference parameters. One might have thousands or millions of households but far fewer exact cohorts. The paper itself studies 1,000 users and three utility representations, and observes that aggregate demand becomes smoother with more users. That supports the regime, though its randomly sampled household parameters would make the exact type support much larger than three. The paper’s interpolation of demand over price is continuity in the demand representation; C-MCE makes the population distribution itself the object.

I expect C-MCE to be tractable for the stated input: aggregate the rational, piecewise-linear type schedules and search their breakpoints. With quadratic per-capita cost, the break-even equation is linear on each demand segment. The expected time is polynomial in the number of types, total breakpoints, and bit length. This is a computational question about the proxy allocation and its minimum competitive-equilibrium price. Under the relevant bidder-submodularity condition, Theorem 1 is the reason that this price-allocation output matters: in the paper’s finite-bidder setting, it implements the VCG allocation. The continuous version would still need its own proof that the corresponding worth condition and allocation claim carry over.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 1’s proxy-phase allocation, not the full staggered online auction. Further questions include whether bidder-submodularity has a useful measure-valued analogue; whether the minimum equilibrium is unique when demand schedules have ties; and how hard it is to generate a type’s demand schedule from compact device constraints. If those constraints encode integer appliance scheduling, that type-level pricing problem may remain hard even when the population is continuous.

The weak point is that the continuum could wash out the theorem’s incentive claim. In a nonatomic population, one household has no effect on price, so its strategic problem is not the finite-bidder Bayes–Nash problem in Theorem 1. Also, this paper’s theorem is not a complexity-class result, so the mirror’s algorithmic question is a new question built around its computed outcome. I would present C-MCE as a credible mirror of the allocation part of the named theorem, while being explicit that it does not continuize the theorem’s truth-telling guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only serious anchor, but it is a conditional mechanism result, not a complexity result. It assumes the aggregator computes minimum competitive equilibrium prices, then relates that outcome to the VCG allocation and a Bayes–Nash equilibrium. The paper gives no worst-case algorithm or complexity classification for a computational problem. Its runtime discussion is a simulation of one implementation.

That matters for C-MCE. With the demand schedules already supplied, the computation is to aggregate them and find a price crossing. For the paper’s piecewise-linear schedules and quadratic per-capita cost, that reduces to solving the clearing equation on each segment. The same calculation applies to a finite list of households; repeated schedules can simply be aggregated. If schedules instead come from compact appliance constraints, generating them may require solving each type’s scheduling problem. That work remains at the type level. C-MCE therefore gives a coherent market-clearing question, but it removes the private reporting and strategic incentives that Theorem 1 concerns, and its easiest formulation adds little computational substance.

A closer attempt would put the theorem’s proxy game over an atomless population. In the usual model, an individual then has no effect on aggregate price. Truthful demand becomes price-taking behavior, rather than the finite-bidder incentive claim proved in the paper. If a whole positive-mass cohort can manipulate the auction, that cohort acts as a strategic player; the model has changed to a finite game among blocs. One could study coalition stability or incentives in a continuum economy, but those would be new questions, not continuizations of the theorem’s stated result.

There is a stronger adjacent possibility: allow each type’s mass to split across its feasible appliance schedules and optimize the resulting allocation with a configuration LP. That could expose a meaningful pricing problem over schedules. Its target, however, is a new welfare-optimization problem; the paper’s theorem concerns a proxy mechanism conditional on its equilibrium prices. This distinction is not a claim that persistent scheduling complexity makes the question uninteresting. It is a claim that the paper’s named result does not supply that computational target.

The negative case weakens at the universal claim. Large populations of households are a natural high-multiplicity regime here, and per-capita costs and type-level average demands are sensible co-modifications. I would reject C-MCE as a strong direct mirror of Theorem 1, but I cannot honestly defend the claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
