The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of complete buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-buyer budgets \(b_t\), the paper's MEC and RAN job-demand parameters, and per-capita supplies \(\bar D^{\mathrm{MEC}}_{m,r}\) and \(\bar D^{\mathrm{RAN}}_c\), compute equilibrium allocations and supporting prices for the continuum Fisher market. Equivalently, solve the type-weighted version of program (4a–4g): maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t b_t\log(u_t)\), with each type's job-capacity constraints unchanged and aggregate capacities \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{t,m,r}\le\bar D^{\mathrm{MEC}}_{m,r}\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{t,c}\le\bar D^{\mathrm{RAN}}_c\); return the optimal type allocations and their supporting prices.

The model it lives in

A continuum of price-taking buyers described by a finite distribution over complete types. Each type specifies its per-buyer budget and MEC/RAN job demands; its representative allocation determines utility under the paper's bottleneck function. Type masses weight both the log-utility objective and aggregate resource use, while dual variables give market prices.

What the mirror covers

  • Formalize the finite replication dictionary between a population with rational type masses and the paper's finite set of buyers, including the correspondence of allocations and prices.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for the convex program (4a–(4g), maximizing \(\sum_s B_s\log u_s\) subject to the MEC and RAN capacity constraints and the paper’s utility constraints), its optimal allocations \(X^*,Y^*\) and corresponding prices induce a market equilibrium for \(\Gamma\). The authors use that program to compute the equilibrium and say it can be solved efficiently with interior point methods. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a formal polynomial-time complexity theorem; the paper contains no comparable named hardness result to prefer instead.

The mirror I believe in is Continuum Market Equilibrium for MEC/RAN Slices. Let \(T\) be a finite set of slice-buyer types with support size \(\tau\), and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of buyers of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type completely specifies the per-buyer budget \(b_t\), the MEC resources needed per job \(d^{\mathrm{MEC}}_{t,r}\), and the RAN resources needed in each cell \(d^{\mathrm{RAN}}_{t,c}\), along with any other characteristic used by the model. The instance also gives per-buyer-population supplies \(D^{\mathrm{MEC}}_{m,r}\) and \(D^{\mathrm{RAN}}_c\). The question is to find an allocation and supporting prices that induce market equilibrium.

For each type, the allocation gives a representative buyer \(x_{t,m,r}\) units of resource \(r\) at edge node \(m\), and \(y_{t,c}\) units of radio resource in cell \(c\). Its service utility \(u_t\) is the maximum concurrent-job capacity under the paper’s bottleneck model: \(j_{t,m}\le x_{t,m,r}/d^{\mathrm{MEC}}_{t,r}\) for every \(r\), \(u_t\le\sum_m j_{t,m}\), and \(u_t\le\sum_c y_{t,c}/d^{\mathrm{RAN}}_{t,c}\). Aggregate allocations must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{t,m,r}\le D^{\mathrm{MEC}}_{m,r}\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{t,c}\le D^{\mathrm{RAN}}_c\). The allocation maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t\log u_t\); the solution includes the resource prices supporting those allocations as an equilibrium. With binary-rational masses and parameters, the natural algorithmic expectation is an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate convex-optimization algorithm scaling with \(\tau\), the numbers of edge nodes, resource kinds and cells, the input bit length, and the requested precision. The paper does not itself prove that bit-complexity bound.

A plausible regime is a large MEC/RAN federation with many independently budgeted tenant-slice instances, such as regional deployments using a small catalogue of service profiles and priority budgets. There might be thousands of buyer instances but only tens of distinct complete types, so \(N\gg\tau\). This is still the paper’s market: each buyer is a service provider choosing resource bundles to maximize its own job utility under a budget. The population being made continuous is the population of those buyers. For \(N_t\) buyers of each type, set \(\mu_t=N_t/N\) and scale physical supply as \(N D\); aggregating identical buyers and normalizing gives the type-level program above. Thus rational type distributions have an exact finite replication, while arbitrary masses extend the model continuously. The expected classification is tractable.

The authors could recognize this as their problem: it retains their buyers, budgets, joint MEC/RAN goods, bottleneck utility, and market-equilibrium objective. It does assume the larger federation has proportionately larger supply, which is a natural co-modification when the deployment grows with its tenant population. The already-divisible resource goods are not the continuization here; the buyer population is. This case covers Theorem 1’s equilibrium-computation result, not the paper’s full efficiency and fairness analysis, alternative allocation mechanisms, or simulations. It also suggests questions about how equilibrium prices and utilities change as \(\mu\) varies, and how accurately a finite empirical population approximates the continuum solution.

My weakest point is the regime: MEC markets may have only a few organizational service providers, and their infrastructure may not grow proportionately with the number of buyers. The mirror is convincing for a federation with many repeated, independently managed slice instances, but it is not a claim that every MEC deployment is a high-multiplicity market.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only strong anchor is Theorem 1, and it is a thinner computational result than the proponent’s framing suggests. It proves that the optimizer of an explicitly stated convex program induces a market equilibrium; the paper does not give a bit-complexity bound for computing that equilibrium. A continuum mirror would replace the service-provider index \(s\) with a support of types \(t\), weight each type’s log utility by its mass \(\mu_t\), and scale the resource supplies per capita. Since every type and its resource requirements are listed explicitly, this introduces no pricing problem over a hidden or exponentially large type space. The resulting program is essentially the same one, with \(S\) replaced by \(\tau\).

That makes the mirror look more like an aggregation of the paper’s existing computation than a new computational landscape. Repeated providers with identical budgets and demand profiles can be represented by a representative provider and a multiplicity weight. A high multiplicity encoding could reduce dependence on the number of providers, but only if the application actually has many independently budgeted providers of repeated types. If multiple “slice instances” belong to one provider, treating them as independent buyers may erase its shared budget and decision-making; if they are separate providers, the scenario must supply a growing shared MEC/RAN infrastructure for them to compete over.

Those are real reasons to doubt the mirror’s payoff, but they do not defeat it. The proposed federation of repeated, independently budgeted tenants is plausible, and scaling per-capita supplies is a sensible co-modification. The paper’s buyers, budgets, goods, and bottleneck utilities all remain intact, with no identity-dependent objective or vanishing quantity. Theorem 1 is a named computational anchor, even though it is not a formal complexity theorem. I therefore cannot honestly make the universal negative case: the best objection is that this mirror may offer little beyond a weighted reformulation of an already explicit convex program. That lowers its likely novelty, but it does not show that the continuous question is ill-posed or unworthy.
