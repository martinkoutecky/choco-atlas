The continuous mirror question

Given a public-budget-regular value distribution \(\mu\), common public budget \(B\), and supply share \(0<\rho<1\), consider \(N\)-agent markets with unit-demand advertisers and \(K_N\) identical slots, where \(K_N/N\to\rho\). Determine whether the multi-unit public-budget clinching auction’s normalized interim allocations and payments have a limit independent of integer rounding, and whether its limiting per-capita welfare is at least \(1/e\) of the optimal BIC per-capita welfare for \((\mu,B,\rho)\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with value distribution \(\mu\) and common public budget \(B\), served by capacity \(\rho\). Each advertiser remains unit-demand; type-level service \(x(v)\) is fractional, with total service at most \(\rho\). Define the limiting clinching rule through finite markets with \(K_N/N\to\rho\), and compare its per-capita welfare with the optimal BIC benchmark under the same distribution and capacity.

The objection that survived

As advertisers become negligible in the limit, individual incentive constraints may change or collapse; the proponent does not specify whether the limit is taken from finite DSIC mechanisms or defined using a nonatomic equilibrium.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper. It says that for public-budget regular i.i.d. agents, the clinching auction’s expected welfare is at least \(1/e\) of the welfare of the optimal mechanism. The paper proves this by comparing clinching to an ex-ante relaxation and establishing the needed allocation bound in Lemma 3.4. This is an algorithmic approximation result, though not a conventional complexity-class result: the paper gives no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem. So this case can support a continuous approximation question, not a claim about hardness dissolving.

I would mirror the result in a large auction market with many advertisers and inventory that grows with the market. A type is an advertiser’s value \(v_t\) for one slot and public budget \(B\); with a common budget, value identifies the type. The population is a unit mass with rational type shares \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). In a finite market of \(N\) advertisers, roughly \(N\mu_t\) have type \(t\), so each positive-mass type contains many nearly identical agents. Let inventory be \(K_N=\lfloor\rho N\rfloor\) identical slots, for a fixed \(0<\rho<1\). Each advertiser still receives either zero or one slot; only the share of each type served is fractional.

Call the question Proportional-Supply Continuum Clinching Welfare. Given \(B\), \(\rho\), ordered values \(v_t\), and regular type masses \(\mu_t\), take the limit of these finite markets and their public-budget clinching auctions. Ask whether the limiting clinching mechanism is well-defined and guarantees at least \(1/e\) of the optimal Bayesian incentive-compatible welfare for that population. More precisely, a candidate solution must give a limiting allocation and payment rule, show that allocations can be implemented with at most mass \(\rho\) served, respect each advertiser’s hard budget, and prove \(W_\mu(C_\infty)\ge \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{BIC}}(\mu)/e\), where \(W_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t v_t x_t\) and \(x_t\) is the truthful interim service probability. The benchmark may know \(\mu\); clinching need not. This targets Theorem 3.5’s welfare guarantee and its prior-independent auction, rather than the paper’s entire revelation-gap programme.

The regime is plausible for ad platforms with a large population of advertisers in a fixed number of value-and-budget classes, and inventory scaling with the number of advertisers. The type masses are the aggregate object; the type-level \(x_t\) records what fraction of a large cohort is served. The supply scaling is a deliberate co-modification to keep competition meaningful as the population grows.

I expect a Class A result: the finite theorem suggests an explicit clinching mechanism, and the paper’s ex-ante comparison offers a route to bounding its welfare. Further questions include whether the limit depends on the sequence of integer roundings, what convergence rate holds in \(N\) and the number of types, and whether the result extends to irregular value laws or heterogeneous budgets.

The weak point is material: Theorem 3.5 is for a single item, while this mirror scales inventory with population. That scaling is allowed by the programme’s scope, but it asks for a new multi-unit extension; the theorem does not imply the \(1/e\) guarantee. Keeping one item fixed as the population grows may instead make the continuous limit degenerate, since each individual’s share of the population and chance of receiving the item vanish. Also, this paper offers no hardness result to test whether continuization removes population-driven hardness. The positive case is therefore for a sensible, potentially tractable continuous approximation problem, not for a complexity-theoretic transformation already established by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.5 is the right anchor to challenge, but its weakness is that it proves a welfare approximation guarantee, not a computational-complexity result. It fixes the clinching auction and shows its expected welfare is within a factor \(e\) of the Bayesian optimum for each public-budget regular i.i.d. distribution. It gives no running-time bound or algorithm for solving an optimization problem from an explicit society. The proposed limit question—whether that guarantee survives—therefore looks like a mean-field mechanism-design analysis, not the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart. Turning it into a problem of computing an optimal mechanism from type masses would be more computational, but it would no longer mirror Theorem 3.5.

There is also a real modeling fork. Keep one item as the number of bidders grows, and the served fraction of the normalized population tends to zero. The theorem’s allocation is then controlled by the highest order statistics: a mass distribution over types does not directly record which individual wins. One can add an extreme-order or winner-type description, but that moves away from the programme’s aggregate mass-transfer object. Scale supply to \(K_N=\lfloor\rho N\rfloor\), as the proponent proposes, and type-level service remains nonzero. That is a coherent large-market question, but it changes the single-item feasibility constraint and the \(1/n\) ex-ante allocation benchmark that drive the paper’s proof. It becomes a proportional-capacity auction problem requiring a new analysis, not a limit of the stated theorem.

Those points make the proposed mirror a poor fit for ChoCo’s computational agenda, but they do not rule it out as worthwhile mechanism-design research. The proportional-supply version is plausible, and the paper’s symmetry and common-budget assumptions make repeated types sensible. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no scenario or re-modelling could yield a worthwhile continuous mirror. The negative case is strongest on programme fit: the named result is a performance bound, and its nondegenerate population limit needs a substantial change of market capacity. The scaled-supply proposal still survives as a potentially valuable adjacent project.
