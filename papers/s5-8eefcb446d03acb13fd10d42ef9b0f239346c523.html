The continuous mirror question

Given a MOCO instance with feasible set \(X\), objective vector \(y(x)\), a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a rational polytope \(\Omega_0\) of normalized type-level preference vectors containing the true profile \(\omega^*\), design an adaptive policy that queries pairwise collective comparisons under \(F_{\omega,\mu}(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\omega_t^\top y(x)\). After each answer restrict the current profile polytope \(\Omega\) to profiles consistent with the answers, and return \(x\in X\) with \(\max_{\omega\in\Omega}[F_{\omega,\mu}(x)-\min_{z\in X}F_{\omega,\mu}(z)]\le\delta\).

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete preference types, each with a normalized unknown tradeoff vector. The decision variable is an adaptive policy choosing project-pair queries and a final project; its required guarantee is regret at most \(\delta\) for every profile still consistent with the collective answers. The aggregate scalarizer is linear in the profile parameters, and precise-profile optimization reduces to MOCO at the aggregate weight.

The objection that survived

The paper does not justify an interpersonal utility scale or explain how a population supplies exact collective comparison answers; the proponent stipulates these assumptions but does not show that intended users would accept them.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the regret guarantee extend formally to the joint type-profile polytope and this collective-query oracle?
  • How do the policy’s query count and computational cost depend on \(|T|\), the number of objectives, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Proposition 1, proved by the authors (its proof is omitted in the paper for space). It says that for every positive threshold \(\delta\), IEEP returns \(x^*\in X\) with \(\mathrm{MR}(x^*,X,\Omega)\le\delta\). IEEP repeatedly optimizes at extreme points of the current preference polytope, asks the decision maker to compare promising solutions, and stops when it finds a solution whose worst-case regret is within the threshold. This is an approximation guarantee for an interactive algorithm, not a polynomial-time result. The paper gives no numbered hardness or complexity-class theorem; its MST experiments are not such a result.

My lead mirror is Continuous Population IEEP for MOCO. Imagine a large city choosing a public transport network from a feasible set \(X\), evaluated on \(d\) objectives such as cost, access, and emissions. The city’s residents fall into a finite set \(T\) of preference types: everyone in type \(t\) shares the same tradeoff vector \(\omega_t\), though the analyst does not know it exactly. The instance gives the rational mass \(\mu_t\) of each type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational polytope \(\Omega_0\) of possible joint preference profiles \(\omega=(\omega_t)_{t\in T}\). The intended regime is millions of residents but relatively few types, so the input describes type shares rather than individuals.

For a project \(x\), let \(y(x)\) be its vector of objective costs and define the society’s stipulated proportional-welfare cost as \(F_{\omega,\mu}(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\omega_t^\top y(x)\). A collective comparison query on \(x,x'\) reports which has lower \(F_{\omega,\mu}\) under the true profile, and cuts the current uncertainty polytope by the resulting linear inequality. Given the MOCO instance, \(\mu\), \(\Omega_0\), and \(\delta>0\), the problem is to design an adaptive query policy that returns a project \(x\) with \(\max_{\omega\in\Omega}[F_{\omega,\mu}(x)-\min_{z\in X}F_{\omega,\mu}(z)]\le\delta\), where \(\Omega\) is the polytope left after the queries. A solution is the policy, its finite comparison transcript, and the returned project meeting that bound. This is a genuine population model: a discrete city with \(n_t\) residents of each type has \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); the continuous input keeps those proportions and makes population size irrelevant to the optimization.

The connection to Proposition 1 is structural. The aggregate objective remains linear in the unknown parameters, and a precise-profile optimization reduces to solving the original MOCO problem at the aggregate weight \(\sum_t\mu_t\omega_t\). Thus the proposition’s extreme-point approach appears to extend to this joint parameter polytope. I would expect the mirror to be Class A when \(d|T|\) is fixed and the precise-weight MOCO problem is efficiently solvable. For unrestricted support size, I would leave the complexity open: Proposition 1 gives no bound on running time or query count, and the joint polytope can have many extreme points. Natural follow-up questions are whether the regret guarantee extends under a formal collective-query model, and how query and computation costs depend on \(|T|\), \(d\), and the input bit length.

I think the authors could recognize this as an extension of their general elicitation framework: the collective objective is still a linear-parameter scalarizer, and the paper’s bibliography includes multi-agent optimization work. But the mirror covers only Proposition 1’s regret guarantee, not the MST experiments or the paper as a whole. Its weakest point is the collective oracle. The paper elicits from one decision maker; here I stipulate that society can answer according to a mass-weighted utility sum. Ordinary majority comparisons need not correspond to that sum or to any single linear scalarizer. If the authors or a referee reject that aggregation model, the population is only a wrapper around their original one-decision-maker problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only serious anchor, but it guarantees regret correctness, not a running-time or query-complexity bound. I can’t dismiss it as non-computational: it is a named algorithmic result. Still, its setting is one decision maker with one unknown scalarizer. It says nothing about representing or eliciting a society’s preferences.

The proposed city model supplies that missing step by stipulating proportional welfare. With \(F_{\omega,\mu}(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\omega_t^\top y(x)\), every project comparison depends only on the aggregate vector \(\bar\omega=\sum_t\mu_t\omega_t\). Thus IEEP sees the projected uncertainty set of possible \(\bar\omega\), not the underlying population profile. This is a coherent extension, but its collective comparison oracle and interpersonal utility scale are extra modeling choices. The paper’s individual preference comparisons do not establish either one. Rescaling one type’s weights can leave that type’s preferences unchanged while changing \(\bar\omega\) and the selected project.

The strongest ordinal alternative is to ask for type-level preferences and aggregate their comparisons by majority. That can produce cycles, so there may be no single scalarizer or preferred solution of the kind Proposition 1 guarantees. A Pareto, fairness, or majority-based objective could repair that, but it would pose a different collective-choice problem. Asking each type separately avoids aggregation, but then the target is a set of type-specific solutions, and the mass shares do no work unless one adds a new objective for allocating queries or valuing outcomes.

So the negative case is that each route either adds a social welfare model the paper does not supply, or leaves Proposition 1’s single decision maker behind. That makes this a weak mirror of the paper’s result and a less direct fit for a population-continuization programme. It is not airtight: a normalized utilitarian model with an aggregate-query oracle is plausible, and the authors may recognize it as an extension of their interactive method. I can’t honestly rule out that scenario; the case against is weakest precisely there.
