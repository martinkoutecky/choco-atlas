A credible regime would be a large campus or dense urban deployment with many MUEs and FUEs but a modest number of repeated radio and service profiles. A type would include everything the model uses: whether the device is an MUE or FUE, its traffic and QoS parameters, subchannel, power limit, D2D eligibility, and its link and interference coefficients to other type classes. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of devices of type \(t\). This keeps the paper’s central ingredients—relay cooperation, leased superframe, and interference between coalitions—while making the population large relative to the number of types.

The unanchored lead would be Continuum Spectrum-Leasing Coalition Formation. Given rational type masses, a type-to-type interaction kernel, and the paper’s service parameters, choose a mass \(z_q\) of copies of each feasible coalition pattern \(q\), where a pattern has one FUE and a finite bundle of MUE types, together with its leasing fractions and transmission controls. The masses must partition the population: \(\sum_q z_q a_t(q)=\mu_t\), where \(a_t(q)\) is the number of type-\(t\) members in pattern \(q\). The requested solution is such an allocation and its resulting payoffs that is undominated under a continuous version of the paper’s recursive-core deviation rule. A prover would also need a formal definition of that rule for residual populations with arbitrary real masses. I would tentatively expect this problem to be hard for continuum-specific reasons: coalition patterns remain combinatorial, and payoffs depend on the surrounding partition through interference. The paper gives no hardness result to support that expectation.

The authors could recognize this as their problem because it preserves the decision they study: which MUEs cooperate with which FUEs, and on what leasing and transmission terms. The weakest point is that their recursive core is defined for a finite set of named players. Extending it to divisible masses, while preserving the same externalities and deviation semantics, is substantive work. Thus the scenario supports a possible mirror, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for it.

Coalition feasibility and payoffs depend on particular D2D links, subchannels, and interference from players outside each coalition. Encoding every player’s links to everyone else in its type makes the number of types grow with the network. A compact type-to-type interaction kernel would avoid that growth only under strong symmetry assumptions; it would replace the paper’s local, identity-specific interaction structure and its strongest-interferer negotiation with an exchangeable model. The continuous recursive-core deviation rule would also need a substantive definition.

That leaves a plausible escape from the negative case: a repeated campus or urban deployment with a small set of radio profiles and symmetric class-level interactions could support a coherent continuum model. The paper does not rule out that regime, and such a model might be worth studying on its own. So the defensible negative is narrower than the requested universal claim: there is no formal computational result here to mirror, and the proposed mirror requires substantial new modeling; I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous problem exists in any scenario.
