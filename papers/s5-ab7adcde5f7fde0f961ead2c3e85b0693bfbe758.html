The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a rational polynomial surplus function \(F\), and an initial state of coalition mass vectors plus singleton mass, determine when every admissible run of the specified fairness-driven split and surplus-driven merge rules reaches a value-preserving invariant set after finitely many updates; characterize such conditions or give an instance with an infinite run.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s finite-time stabilization claim and the split-merge dynamics it analyzes; it leaves the generic finite-state principle, Shapley-value computation, and numerical case study aside.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that, for a finite player set, nonnegative singleton values, the stated fairness-stable split rule, and a lexicographically nondecreasing Lyapunov function that strictly improves whenever a profitable merge occurs, every trajectory enters in finite time the paper’s zero-progress invariant set. That set is claimed to contain Shapley-fair, merge-stable fixed points and finite value-preserving cycles. This is a convergence result for the proposed algorithm, not a conventional complexity classification: the paper gives no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result.

A credible population regime is a large pool of interchangeable agents in a cooperative enterprise or service network: agents share a type when they have the same contribution technology and every other game-relevant attribute. The number of agents can grow while the number of types \(\tau\) stays small. The paper’s ten-player example is not itself evidence for that regime, and its arbitrary named-player games need not be type-symmetric. The mirror applies to the meaningful subfamily where coalition surplus depends only on the masses of its member types.

Here is one precise candidate, Continuous SFMS Stabilization, mirroring Theorem 2. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a rational polynomial \(F:[0,1]^T\to\mathbb R\) with \(F(0)=0\), and an initial coalition structure. A coalition is represented by its type-mass vector \(x\); its total surplus is \(F(x)\). Assume \(F\) is differentiable and has nonnegative standalone rates \(b_t=\partial_tF(0)\geq0\).

Use the replication-limit Shapley value per member of type \(t\):
\[ \phi_t(x)=\int_0^1 \partial_tF(sx)\,ds. \]
This is the limit of the ordinary Shapley value when a coalition with type proportions \(x\) is replicated to many small, otherwise identical agents. It is efficient in the mass sense: \(\sum_t x_t\phi_t(x)=F(x)\).

Represent a state by active coalition vectors \(x^1,\ldots,x^k\) and a singleton pool \(d\), with \(\sum_j x^j+d=\mu\). The pool records agents currently isolated by splits; its surplus is \(b\cdot d\). Define fairness deficit and total surplus by
\[ \Psi=\sum_j\sum_t x_t^j(-\phi_t(x^j))_+,\qquad \Phi=\sum_jF(x^j)+b\cdot d, \]
and use \(V=(-\Psi,\Phi)\) in lexicographic order. A split of \(x\) moves its negative-\(\phi_t(x)\) type mass into \(d\). It retains the remaining coalition only if that residual is itself Shapley-fair; otherwise it moves all of \(x\) into \(d\). A merge combines two active coalitions when \(F(x+y)>F(x)+F(y)\). The mass version also permits a chosen amount \(z\leq d\) of singleton mass to join a coalition \(x\) when \(F(x+z)>F(x)+b\cdot z\), or two selected singleton masses to form a coalition when their joint surplus exceeds their separate standalone surplus. As in the paper, splits are applied before a sequence of profitable merges; the order of merges may vary.

The question is: for which such instances and initial states does every admissible run enter, after finitely many mass updates, the largest weakly invariant subset of states admitting a value-preserving successor? A solution would prove that guarantee under stated conditions, or give an explicit instance and infinite run showing that it fails; in the positive case it should characterize the reached fixed point or finite value-preserving cycle. The corresponding computational follow-up is to find such a reached invariant state and bound the work in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and the initial coalition support.

I would expect the unqualified finite-time guarantee to fail for continuum states: the paper’s proof uses a finite image of \(V\), while a continuous state space can admit infinitely many strict improvements whose gains shrink toward zero. That makes this a continuum-specific obstruction candidate, not transferred hardness—the paper supplies no discrete hardness anchor. A useful next question is whether regularity or a minimum positive improvement gives finite termination, or whether the right result is approximate or asymptotic stabilization.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it preserves their agents, Shapley fairness trigger, surplus-based merges, and SFMS endpoint, while taking the high-multiplicity limit. The principal weakness is that the singleton pool and mass-level merges are genuine co-modifications; exact limiting behavior may depend on how finite-agent singleton merges are scaled. There is also a serious issue in the anchor itself: Theorem 2 assumes Lyapunov monotonicity as A3, while the paper’s Lemma 3 argues that merging does not change \(\Psi\). But a merged coalition can have new Shapley values, so that claim needs justification. Thus the mirror is a worthwhile computational question, not a claim that the paper has already established the finite-population premise needed to solve it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 2, and its result is convergence of a finite-state dynamical system, not a computational complexity result. Its finite-time argument comes from the finiteness of \(\mathcal P(N)\) and the assumed Lyapunov monotonicity in A3. The paper gives no runtime bound, input-encoding model, or complexity classification for finding an endpoint; the discussion of Shapley computation is background, not a named result. The proposed continuous stabilization question preserves that analytic convergence target. Adding a follow-up about how much work it takes to find a state makes a new computational problem, rather than making Theorem 2 itself a computational anchor.

A stronger computational version is imaginable: encode an anonymous cooperative game by a rational function \(F\), then ask whether a specified dynamics reaches a fixed point or cycle, or compute one. But that requires choosing the game representation, merge and split operations, and computational output. Those choices may produce a worthwhile problem, but the paper’s theorem does not select among them. The proponents’ proposed mass-level merges are permissible co-modifications; they also help define a new dynamics whose algorithmic properties the paper never studies.

There is a further weakness in treating Theorem 2 as an established property of the stated algorithm. Its A3 assumption supplies the monotonicity the theorem needs, while Lemma 3’s justification that merging leaves \(\Psi\) unchanged is not generally valid. For example, let \(v(\{1,2\})=2\), \(v(\{1,3\})=0\), \(v(\{2,3\})=10\), and \(v(\{1,2,3\})=3\), with singleton values zero. The fair coalition \(\{1,2\}\) profitably merges with \(\{3\}\), but the resulting Shapley value of player 1 is \(-2\). So the paper’s stated rule can increase the fairness deficit during a merge. That does not make a continuous question uninteresting, but it weakens the claim that the mirror is continuing a proved convergence guarantee for this algorithm.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is inherently implausible here. Anonymous cooperative games with many interchangeable contributors are a sensible regime, and the proposed type-mass model is a credible way to express one. That leaves the negative case narrow: this anchor supports an analytic continuum-dynamics question, while its computational follow-up would be a newly specified problem. The case against rejecting the paper is therefore weak if the authors are willing to treat that follow-up as their problem; the stronger objection is that the proposed mirror does not match the programme’s computational-question criterion.
