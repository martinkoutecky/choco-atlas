The continuous mirror question

Given finite patient-donor types \(\mathcal T\), object types \(\mathcal O\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), an endowment map \(e\), weak preferences \(R_t\), acceptability, and rational weights \(w_{to}\), find \(x_{to}\geq0\) with \(\sum_o x_{to}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t:e(t)=o}\mu_t\) supplied to object type \(o\), and zero mass on unacceptable assignments, maximizing \(\sum_{t,o}w_{to}x_{to}\) or deciding feasibility, subject to there being no positive-mass blocking deviation: no \(q_{to}\leq x_{to}\) and \(z_{t,o,o'}\geq0\) with \(\sum_{o'}z_{t,o,o'}=q_{to}\), \(\sum_{t,o}z_{t,o,o'}=\sum_{t:e(t)=o'}\sum_o q_{to}\), positive support only on \(o'R_to\), and positive mass on some \(o'P_to\).

The model it lives in

A continuum-agent, high-multiplicity Shapley-Scarf exchange economy with typed patient-donor mass: \(x\) is a capacitated transport of indivisible donor units, while \(q\) and \(z\) encode positive-mass blocking and \(w\) selects a maximum-weight strong-core allocation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 5 and the paper's maximum-weight strong-core computation for unbounded exchanges. It leaves the respecting-improvement results, core and Wako-core formulations, bounded-cycle results, and empirical findings without a continuous mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the typed strong core admit a polynomial price, circulation, or separation certificate for rational masses and weak preferences?
  • Can maximum-weight typed strong-core exchange be solved efficiently, and how does complexity depend on the number of types?
  • For bounded exchange length \(k\in\{2,3\}\), does the typed mass model preserve or remove the support-graph hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity kidney-exchange mirror of the paper’s strong-core computation. The paper contains no named theorem proving membership in \(P\) or NP-hardness; its best eligible computational anchor is the formulation result below.

My lead anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper. It states that an allocation \(x\) is in the strong core exactly when its \(0\)-\(1\) assignment matrix \(y\), with every agent receiving one acceptable object and every object assigned once, can be accompanied by prices \(p_i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfying three families of constraints. For every acceptable edge \((i,j)\), the “unbounded stability” constraint is \(p_i+1\leq p_j+n\sum_{k:kR_i j}y_{ik}\); the competitive-equilibrium constraint is \(p_i\leq p_j+n(1-y_{ij})\); and the strong-stability constraint is \(p_i\leq p_j+n\sum_{k:kP_i j}y_{ik}\). Proposition 5 further says that these conditions are sufficient for \((x,p)\) to be a competitive equilibrium. The result is proved here, and the formulation is used in the paper’s computational experiments.

The continuous mirror would be:

Continuous Maximum-Weight Strong-Core Exchange. An instance consists of a finite set \(\mathcal T\) of patient–donor types, a finite set \(\mathcal O\) of donor-object types, rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), an endowment map \(e:\mathcal T\to\mathcal O\), weak preferences \(R_t\) over donor types, and rational exchange weights \(w_{to}\). Type \(t\) represents a mass \(\mu_t\) of otherwise indistinguishable patient–donor pairs: they have the same preference ordering, compatibility relation, clinical attributes, and endowment class. The supply of object class \(o\) is \(\nu_o=\sum_{t:e(t)=o}\mu_t\).

A solution is a transport matrix \(x=(x_{to})\) satisfying

\[ \sum_o x_{to}=\mu_t \]

for every patient type \(t\), and

\[ \sum_t x_{to}=\nu_o \]

for every object type \(o\), with \(x_{to}=0\) whenever \(o\) is unacceptable to \(t\). The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,o}w_{to}x_{to}\), or to decide whether any strong-core solution exists.

The continuous strong-core condition is defined directly from the paper’s blocking notion. A positive-mass coalition may select submasses \(q_{to}\leq x_{to}\) of currently assigned patients. It controls exactly the corresponding mass of their endowed donor objects. A deviation is a reallocation of those objects in which every participating unit of type \(t\) receives an object weakly preferred to its current object, with a positive mass receiving a strictly preferred object. A feasible \(x\) is in the continuous strong core if no such positive-mass deviation exists. The required output is either “infeasible” or an optimal mass matching \(x\) satisfying this condition.

This is genuinely a population continuization, not fractional kidney allocation. Every unit of mass still receives one integral kidney; \(x_{to}\) records only how many otherwise identical pairs receive each object class. The finite object-inventory constraint survives as the column equations, and exchange cycles survive as cycles in the support of the transport matrix.

A convincing regime is a large national or regional kidney-exchange pool containing thousands of patient–donor pairs but only tens or hundreds of repeated clinical types: blood-type and HLA compatibility classes, discretized graft-quality categories, patient priority bands, and common rankings over compatible donor classes. The individual pairs are numerous, while \(|\mathcal T|\) is moderate. This is precisely the regime in which “many named agents” can be replaced by masses without pretending that arbitrary idiosyncratic preferences are identical.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The mirror retains their objects, preferences, exchange cycles, blocking coalitions, competitive allocations, and strong core. It merely replaces the \(0\)-\(1\) assignment matrix by a capacitated circulation over repeated types. The paper’s Section 5 already optimizes transplant weight subject to strong-core constraints, so the objective is not imported from elsewhere.

I would expect this unbounded-cycle version to be Class A. The Quint–Wako procedure described in the paper can be run on type classes, with residual masses handled by bottleneck subtraction and cycle covers replaced by capacitated bipartite flows. When ties create multiple strong-core assignments, choosing a maximum-weight one should become a min-cost-flow or transportation problem inside each relevant absorbing component. Rational masses can be normalized by a common denominator, preserving a finite high-multiplicity dictionary and giving polynomial bit-size solutions.

The main follow-up questions are whether the precise continuous analogue of Proposition 5 has a polynomial price/circulation certificate, whether the result remains polynomial for arbitrary weak preferences and weights, and what happens when exchange cycles are bounded by \(k=2\) or \(k=3\). The latter is especially interesting because the paper itself connects bounded exchanges with stable-roommates and cyclic matching phenomena; continuous mass may remove some multiplicity-driven difficulty but need not remove hardness encoded in the type-support graph.

The weakest point is that the paper’s original objects are named and potentially patient-specific, whereas the mirror makes same-type objects interchangeable and permits type mass to split across several object classes. That is a substantive model change. It is defensible only in the stated high-multiplicity KEP regime, where clinical type—not patient identity—is what determines preferences and compatibility. If the authors regard individualized rankings over named kidneys as essential, this mirror would fail. But under the programme’s definition of high multiplicity, that objection rejects the regime rather than exposing a defect in the continuization itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case turns on the anchor rule, not on claiming that a typed kidney-exchange model is nonsensical. Proposition 5 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it does not really clear the programme’s computational bar. It is an exact mixed-integer characterization of finite strong-core allocations, not a complexity result, algorithm, approximation theorem, parameterized result, or separation theorem. The paper’s experiments show that a solver handles the formulation; they do not establish a worst-case computational claim. The maximum-weight objective in the proposed mirror comes from the experiments, not from Proposition 5 itself.

More importantly, Proposition 5 does not continuize cleanly. Its logic depends on \(0\)-\(1\) indicators. For example,

\[ D_{ij}(y)=\sum_{k:kR_i j}y_{ik}\in\{0,1\}, \]

so in

\[ p_i+1\leq p_j+nD_{ij}(y) \]

the term \(nD_{ij}(y)\) either imposes the price inequality or switches it off with a valid big-\(M\). Replacing \(y\) by mass variables gives \(D_{tj}(x)\in[0,\mu_t]\). A small positive assignment then neither means “zero” nor supplies a valid big-\(M\). Likewise,

\[ p_i\leq p_j+n(1-y_{ij}) \]

forces equality when \(y_{ij}=1\), but its mass analogue does not force equality merely because \(x_{to}>0\).

The exact typed version therefore needs support-sensitive implications such as

\[ D_{tj}(x)=0\Longrightarrow p_{e(t)}<p_o, \]

together with complementary conditions for every positive-support edge. That is a new disjunctive or complementarity model, not the continuous LP or price certificate suggested by the proposition. The proponent’s direct positive-mass blocking definition is a reasonable new model, but it is no longer a mirror of Proposition 5’s result; the paper proves nothing about it.

The strongest rescue is the proposed large KEP with repeated clinical types. That scenario is admissible in principle, and I would not reject it merely because kidneys are indivisible or because prices become type-level. The problem is that it has two incompatible versions. If complete rankings over donor classes are retained, a type is a patient–donor class with a fixed endowment and fixed ranking, and the resulting transport problem is a new capacitated stability problem. The paper’s named-object cycle covers and its \(0\)-\(1\) price encoding have been replaced. If donor-specific preferences are retained instead, then nearly every pair can have a distinct ranking and the supposed high-multiplicity compression disappears.

Allowing a type’s mass to split across several object classes makes the mismatch sharper: agents of one nominal type no longer have the same current allotment. One must reason over assignment cells \((t,o)\), and blocking depends on the positive support of \(x\), not merely on the type data used in the paper. Forbidding such splitting preserves more of the finite model but makes the type block indivisible, thereby sacrificing the continuous population relaxation.

The paper’s central respecting-improvement results offer no second rescue. A named agent has measure zero in an atomless population, so improving that agent has no effect. Promoting an entire positive-mass type is meaningful, but it is a different comparative-static statement, not the paper’s individual improvement theorem. Bounded-cycle variants would likewise be legitimate new typed matching problems, but they would not repair the missing computational anchor.

So the honest negative verdict is narrower than “typed kidney exchange can never be studied continuously.” Such a study could be worthwhile as an independent ChoCo paper. The point is that this paper supplies no qualifying computational result whose continuous-population version it actually mirrors, and Proposition 5’s only plausible anchor loses its defining logical structure under mass substitution. That is enough for a negative assessment under the programme’s standard, though not an airtight impossibility claim about every conceivable housing-market continuization.
