The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. “Discursive Dilemma” names an inconsistency example, not a complexity claim or an algorithmic problem. The paper introduces a general judgment-aggregation model and describes escape routes, but it states no theorem classifying the complexity of computing an aggregate, finding a rule, or repairing a profile.

The best candidate is to take a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete, consistent judgment types and ask whether issue-by-issue majority is consistent. For each agenda item \(\varphi\), its majority mass is \(\sum_{J:\varphi\in J}\mu_J\); the resulting set can then be checked for consistency. That is a coherent population-level question, and the paper’s three-expert example has a direct version with three types of mass \(1/3\). But it is a new computational question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. As stated, its complexity also depends on choices the paper leaves open, especially the logic and how agendas are represented.

A more ambitious repair problem—such as finding the least costly mass transfer that makes the majority set consistent—would need a cost model and objective absent from the paper. It might be a worthwhile project, but it would build a new computational problem around the paper’s topic rather than continuize one of its results.

So the negative case is strong against an anchor-based mirror of this paper: none of its results supplies the required computational anchor. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could grow from the paper. Weighted majority consistency is a plausible follow-on; calling it a mirror is the step I would resist.
