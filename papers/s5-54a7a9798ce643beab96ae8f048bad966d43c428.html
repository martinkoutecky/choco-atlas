What the mirror covers

The proposed anchor concerns Dating’s construction and its strategyproofness, individual rationality, and efficiency; the stable-existence corollary and the impossibility propositions provide no separate computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive foothold is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: the explicitly defined Dating algorithm terminates and yields a strategyproof, individually rational, efficient matching for convex roommate problems. It is constructive, but it asserts no running-time bound or complexity classification. Corollary 1 says every convex roommate problem has a stable matching; the paper attributes this result to Chung and derives it using Lemma 1 and Gale–Shapley. That is an existence result, not a computational one. Propositions 1 and 3 are incentive impossibilities, not computational hardness results.

A plausible adjacent model would take \(\mu\) over finitely many single-peaked preference types in a large roommate market and seek a stable matching measure over pairs of types and single agents. But that would extend the paper’s matching model, not mirror a named computational result in it. A continuous version of Dating raises a further problem: individual strategyproofness is not preserved automatically when an individual has zero mass; replacing it with truthful reporting by positive-mass groups changes the axiom.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper is that it offers no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 1 establishes that Dating is well-defined, individually rational, efficient, and strategyproof. Corollary 1 establishes stable-match existence; Propositions 1 and 3 are impossibility results about mechanism axioms. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Dating is constructive, but its termination proof gives no running-time bound, and the paper does not pose a complexity question about computing its output. Studying that running time could be worthwhile, but it would be a new computational question rather than a mirror of a result the paper establishes.

The best rescue is to model a large population as cohorts along the line, each with a common single-peaked preference type, and compute a mass pairing between types. That is a coherent matching problem. But it does not preserve the paper’s central result. A type-level pairing records partner masses, while Dating’s strategyproofness concerns an individual agent’s report and the partner that named agent receives. In an atomless model, one person’s report does not change the population distribution, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous for an aggregate mechanism. Requiring truthful reports from positive-mass groups would be a different incentive axiom. Keeping individual deviations meaningful instead requires retaining atomic agents and their identities, which gives up the continuous aggregate object.

One could still study stable or efficient mass matchings for those cohorts. The paper’s stable-existence result gives that project a plausible starting point, and the cited continuum matching literature makes the setting credible. But that would be a new algorithmic study of continuous matching, not a computational mirror of this paper’s results. The negative case is strongest against a direct ChoCo mirror; it is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous matching project could arise from the paper.
