What the mirror covers

Theorems 1 and 2 concern statistical properties of certainty; the trust-based neighbor search and preference completion algorithms have no named computational result to anchor a population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a paper-anchored positive case under the stated rule: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary that classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. Algorithms 3 and 4 describe the trust-based neighbor search and preference completion, and the paper evaluates them experimentally, but it gives no complexity result for either.

The named theorems are different kinds of results. Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that with the ratio of rankings preferring \(A\) to \(B\) versus \(B\) to \(A\) fixed, and the number of unordered rankings fixed, certainty increases as the total number of directional rankings increases. Theorem 2, also proved there, says that with the total number of rankings and the number of unordered rankings fixed, certainty decreases as the two directional counts approach equality and increases as they move away from equality. These are properties of the paper’s statistical certainty measure, not complexity classifications.

A plausible setting for a future population version would be a large recommendation service whose users fall into a relatively small number of types, each defined by the same partial ranking and trust value. But turning Algorithms 3 and 4 into a mass-based completion problem would be a proposed extension, not a mirror anchored by a named computational result in this paper. There is therefore no anchor-specific continuous problem—or expected complexity class—to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this is a poor paper to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 concern how the paper’s certainty statistic changes with ranking counts. Neither classifies a computational problem. Algorithms 3 and 4 are computational procedures, but the paper gives no complexity result for them. So there is no named computational result here whose continuous counterpart would supply the expected kind of complexity question.

The certainty theorems do not offer a clean substitute. A continuous society records proportions, while the PCDF certainty depends on the *absolute amount of evidence* as well as its proportions. If counts grow while their ratios stay fixed, the posterior concentrates and \(C^+_{AB}\) tends to \(1\); if counts are normalized to masses summing to \(1\), the result instead depends on treating those masses as fractional sample counts. A more careful model could retain a population-size or effective-sample-size parameter alongside the mass distribution. But then Theorems 1 and 2 become statements about Bayesian evidence accumulation, not computational questions about a continuous society.

The best algorithmic mirror would be a large recommendation service with repeated users who share both a partial ranking and a trust value. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the type-level anchor distances and pairwise evidence could be aggregated by mass. The paper’s fixed-\(k\) neighborhood would sensibly become a fixed fraction of the population; that redefinition is not an objection. But it is a proposed extension of the algorithms, not a mirror of either theorem, and the paper leaves its computational question and complexity measure unstated. Its personalized setting also makes multiplicity contingent: trust must be part of the type, so idiosyncratic trust values can make the support nearly as large as the population.

That makes the negative case credible as a judgment about this paper’s value *as an anchor*: it offers no computational theorem to mirror, and its plausible population version would require framing a new problem around its algorithms. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. Repeated ranking-and-trust cohorts are plausible, so ruling out every such scenario would overstate the evidence.
