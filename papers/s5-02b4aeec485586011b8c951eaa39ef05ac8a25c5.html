The anchor — algorithmic

Certified-path guarantees on bounded-in-degree graphs

Every anchor argued

  • Certified-path guarantees on bounded-in-degree graphs — survived: The opponent argues the result already holds uniformly on finite graphs, but a repeatable bounded-degree network schema supports a recognizable type-mass limit of that result.
  • Average-based delegation can violate DNH — fell: The opponent accepts the positive-mass witness but correctly observes that its continuous version is a mean calculation, and the proposition does not pass the computational-result gate.

The continuous mirror question

Given a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete local-role types, together with a repeatable bounded-in-degree network schema realizing those roles and their competence labels, can the \(T\)-round certified-path rule be defined as a mass routing \(D\) such that it is acyclic, every delegated unit reaches a terminal type with competence gain at least \(\alpha_b-\beta_b\) in each state \(b\in\{0,1\}\), and terminal-load density is at most \(W_T\)?

The model it lives in

Use a measured limit of bounded-in-degree awareness graphs partitioned into finitely many complete local-role types with masses \(\mu_t\), competence vectors, and certified labels. The decision \(D(u)\) is whether unit \(u\) votes or delegates locally; the induced terminal map routes mass to sinks. The task is to construct this routing with the theorem's acyclicity, statewise improvement, and load constraints, rather than optimize an objective absent from the paper. Agents remain nonstrategic, so the limit does not change the solution concept.

The objection that survived

Type masses alone do not encode globally realizable neighborhoods or terminal loads, and supplying a network schema may preserve essentially all the graph information, yielding a limit formulation rather than a computational compression.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the certified-path algorithm's acyclicity, statewise terminal improvement, and bounded terminal-load guarantee; it leaves the intersection and confounded-set mechanisms, the average-competence counterexamples, and the unresolved majority-correctness boundary aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What consistency conditions on the network schema and \(\mu\) ensure a realizable measured limit with the required bounded-degree local neighborhoods?
  • Which finite-replica convergence conditions suffice to pass the terminal routing and load bound to the limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow, credible one: liquid democracy has a natural high-multiplicity regime, and this paper already studies mechanisms whose guarantees depend on the population’s type distribution and local network structure. It offers algorithmic guarantees rather than NP-hardness or an exact/approximation complexity classification, so the mirror would extend its mechanism-design results, not complete a hardness-versus-tractability story.

The supplied text omits theorem and proposition counters. I cite the exact printed titles below rather than inventing numbers.

The clearest lead is “Certified-path guarantees on bounded-in-degree graphs” (proved here). Under \(\alpha_b>\beta_b\), the theorem sets
\[ R=1+\left\lceil 2\min\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha_0-\beta_0},\frac{1}{\alpha_1-\beta_1}\right\}\right\rceil. \]
It proves that every output delegation graph is acyclic; each delegating voter’s terminal sink is at least \(\alpha_b-\beta_b\) more competent in each signal state; and, if maximum in-degree is \(\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}\), every sink’s weight is at most
\[ W_R=1+\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}+\cdots+\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}^R, \]
with \(W_2(H)\le W_R n\). Those are structural guarantees for a bounded-round local mechanism, not an unconditional majority-correctness theorem.

A corresponding continuous problem could be called Type-Mass Certified Delegation. Its input is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete local-role types, with each type recording its competence vector \((p_t^0,p_t^1)\), ordered local neighborhood role, and the labels needed by the mechanism. It also includes \(\pi,\alpha,\beta\) and a repeatable bounded-degree network schema realizing those roles, with maximum in-degree \(\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}\). The regime is, for example, a large DAO or organization with many members in a fixed set of expertise and network roles, where a shared audit or announcement arrives after delegation decisions. The number of people \(N\) grows while the number of roles \(\tau\) stays fixed or moderate.

The task is to construct a bounded-radius delegation rule and its induced terminal mass routing. A solution must be acyclic; for almost every delegating agent \(u\), its terminal agent \(\tau_D(u)\) must satisfy
\[ p_{\tau_D(u)}^b-p_u^b\ge\alpha_b-\beta_b \quad\text{for each }b; \]
and the terminal load density, measured in units of original voters per sink, must be at most \(W_R\). If \(\rho(D)\) is the delegated mass and \(Q_D^b\) the state-\(b\) terminal mean vote mass, the intended analogue of the theorem’s mean guarantee is
\[ Q_D^b\ge \int p^b\,d\mu+(\alpha_b-\beta_b)\rho(D). \]
The certified-path mechanism is a constructive candidate solution to this problem. With a fixed-radius, finite-role network schema, I would expect its induced mass routing to be computable by enumerating local views. Optimizing the delegated mass would be a natural further question, but the theorem does not claim to maximize it.

This mirror’s scope is the mechanism’s statewise improvement and load control. A strictly positive limiting margin above \(1/2\) would imply statewise majority correctness in large replicas; the theorem alone does not ensure that margin. The paper’s bounded-weight concentration argument supplies a route from the structural result to correctness when the statewise margin is large enough. The boundary case, where limiting mean equals \(1/2\), needs separate analysis.

A second useful anchor is “Average-based delegation can violate DNH” (proved here). With equal signal priors, the paper shows that for every sufficiently large \(n\), a complete-graph instance exists where direct voting succeeds with probability tending to one but delegation to a neighbor of strictly higher average competence succeeds with probability \(0.7\). Thus the average-based rule violates ex ante do-no-harm.

Its continuous problem is Mass-Average Delegation Safety: given rational masses over two-state competence types on complete-graph replicas, determine whether delegation to a strictly higher prior-average-competence neighbor satisfies ex ante DNH. A fixed-support witness is type \(A\) with mass \(0.9\) and competence \((0.6,0.6)\), and type \(B\) with mass \(0.1\) and competence \((1,0.4)\), under equal priors. Their averages are \(0.6\) and \(0.7\), so every \(A\)-voter delegates to a \(B\)-voter, while \(B\)-voters stay. Direct voting has mean correctness \(0.64\) in state \(0\) and \(0.58\) in state \(1\), so its success tends to one in both states. Delegated voting succeeds in state \(0\) and fails in state \(1\) with probability tending to one, giving ex ante success \(1/2\). This changes the paper’s witness, but preserves its claim about the average rule while keeping both types at positive mass. For fixed-support complete-graph instances with strict margins, I would expect the safety calculation to reduce to mass and mean calculations; threshold cases at mean \(1/2\) raise further asymptotic questions.

The weak point is that the certified-path result depends on sparse graph structure. A distribution over competence vectors alone cannot encode bounded in-degree, paths, or local information, so the mirror needs network role in the type and a repeatable network schema. If those roles proliferate with population size, the high-multiplicity compression may fail. The second mirror is also a negative safety result, not a complexity classification. The positive case rests most strongly on the bounded-radius delegation problem, and should be presented as a tractable algorithmic-design extension rather than as evidence that the paper already establishes a general continuous complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is not that this paper’s setting cannot be continuized. It can. It is that neither cited result gives a computational problem whose hard part is managing population multiplicity.

The certified-path theorem is the stronger anchor. A good mirror would include network structure in the types: a distribution over bounded-radius rooted neighborhood types, with a realizable network schema. Competence masses alone cannot encode which paths exist or how many voters can reach a sink. But once the network information is included, the theorem already gives its guarantees for every finite graph: acyclicity, statewise terminal improvement, and a sink-load bound. With fixed degree and radius, the per-capita versions are finite sums over local neighborhoods. The continuous statement would largely be an integral restatement of a graph-uniform theorem, not a new population optimization problem.

A more ambitious version could optimize the delegation rule over those neighborhood types. That may be a worthwhile project, but it changes the task: the paper proves guarantees for a particular local rule; it does not ask how to choose the best rule or network. And if the input is only a distribution over local types, it must still encode which combinations of neighborhoods are globally realizable. If it carries the full network to do that, the proposed population compression has not simplified the theorem’s computational content.

The average-based DNH counterexample has a real high-multiplicity interpretation. The proponent’s two-type repair is sound: masses \(0.9\) and \(0.1\) on competence vectors \((0.6,0.6)\) and \((1,0.4)\) give direct-voting means \(0.64\) and \(0.58\), while delegation to the higher-average type gives limiting ex ante success \(1/2\). This avoids the objection that the original distinguished voter vanishes in the limit.

But the repaired mirror asks whether one fixed rule satisfies an asymptotic safety property. The paper already proves that kind of asymptotic counterexample; the mass version is a two-type mean calculation, not an algorithmic question. To make it computationally richer, one might optimize delegation over mass-routing policies. Then the input must also say how mass is spread across individual recipients: sink-load variation affects weighted-majority reliability, and a distribution over competence types does not determine it. A nonatomic mass model also erases finite-vote fluctuations. In particular, DNH ranges over sequences of instances, including means approaching \(1/2\) at different rates; one limiting mass vector cannot preserve that critical-window information without extra finite-size or load-distribution data.

So I would reject both proposed anchors as evidence that this paper merits a continuization project: one lifts a population-uniform local theorem into an integral statement, and the other lifts an asymptotic counterexample into a mass witness. The negative case is still limited. A new optimization problem over mass routing and network structure could be valuable, and large organizations with repeated expertise and network roles are plausible high-multiplicity settings. That would be a promising adjacent question, though, rather than a continuous mirror of the results cited here.
