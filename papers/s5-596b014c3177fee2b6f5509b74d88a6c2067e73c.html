The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its main theorems characterize when matching mechanisms can or cannot satisfy resoluteness, symmetry, stability, and optimality. The paper mentions algorithmic efficiency, and Theorem 8’s impossibility proof uses SAT solving, but neither is a complexity result about a matching problem. The SAT encoding proves an axiomatic impossibility; it does not classify an algorithm’s running time.

Theorem 8 is the closest candidate: for \(n\ge3\), no resolute, \(x\varphi y\)-symmetric, stable mechanism exists. A stronger proposed mirror might model large cohorts as masses and represent their assignments by a coupling \(x_{ab}\) between preference types. That can be a sensible high-multiplicity matching model. But asking whether a stable, symmetric selector exists over that model is still an axiomatic existence question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The type-level formulation also changes what Theorem 8 is about. Its symmetry acts on the identities of individual agents, and its output is a particular bijection. A mass coupling records how much of each type is assigned to each other type; it does not select unique partners. Retaining a unique individual matching requires the identities that the mass representation has discarded. Allowing fractional assignments avoids that problem by changing resoluteness and requires a type-level definition of stability. Either way, a question about computing a stable mass assignment would be a new computational problem, not a continuous version of Theorem 8: the theorem concerns whether a mechanism can satisfy axioms across all profiles, not how to compute an assignment for one society.

The same issue rules out the other named results as anchors. Theorems 2–7 and 9 are also possibility or impossibility statements about mechanism properties, not complexity classifications. None gives the programme a pricing problem, an optimization problem, or a computational boundary to continuize.

The cohort scenario is nevertheless credible. Large groups of applicants and positions with shared preferences could make continuous matching algorithms worth studying. That is the weak point in the negative case: I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous computational matching question exists in any scenario. The stronger, supportable conclusion is narrower: this paper’s contributions do not furnish one. A continuous mass-matching project could be valuable, but it would need an independently motivated computational problem rather than a mirror of these results.
