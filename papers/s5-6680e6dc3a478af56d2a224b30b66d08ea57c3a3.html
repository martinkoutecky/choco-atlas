The continuous mirror question

Given fixed \(k\), a finite support \(T\) of worker types, positive rational confusion matrices \(Q^r\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to one, consider scales \(M\) with \(M\mu_r\in\mathbb{N}\). Each of the \(M\) workers labels each of \(n\) items independently according to their type's matrix, and the true label vector \(y\in[k]^n\) is arbitrary and deterministic. Under the paper's regularity conditions, determine the asymptotic minimax error exponent \(\lim_{M\to\infty}-M^{-1}\log \mathcal R_M\) and give an estimator attaining it, where \(\mathcal R_M=\inf_{\hat y}\sup_{y\in[k]^n}\mathbb{E}[n^{-1}\sum_j\mathbf{1}\{\hat y_j\ne y_j\}]\). Is the exponent \(I_\mu=\min_{g\ne h}[-\min_{s\in[0,1]}\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r\log\sum_{\ell=1}^k(Q^r_{g\ell})^{1-s}(Q^r_{h\ell})^s]\), attained by the per-item MLE?

The model it lives in

Workers are the population; each type \(r\in T\) is defined by its complete confusion matrix \(Q^r\), and its mass is \(\mu_r\). At scale \(M\), there are \(M\mu_r\) workers of type \(r\). For each item, the decision is an estimated label \(\hat y_j\in[k]\), chosen by maximizing the type-aggregated log likelihood. The objective is the worst-case expected fraction of mislabeled items, with asymptotic exponent \(I_\mu\).

The theorem gives no worst-case running-time bound, so this mirror offers limited evidence about the programme's complexity landscape; that limits its payoff but not the well-posedness of the estimation question.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high multiplicity model of the workers. For a standardized annotation task, group workers by their full confusion matrix: workers with the same matrix have the same response behavior for every true label. A platform with \(M\) workers and a small number \(\tau\) of such profiles is a plausible regime, with type mass \(\mu_r\) equal to the fraction of workers of profile \(r\).

The nearest result to anchor this in is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It assumes \(\log k=o(mI(\pi))\), \(|\log \rho_m|=o(\rho_m|\mathcal A_{0.01}|^{1/2})\), and \(|\log \rho_m|=o(\sqrt m I(\pi))\). Under those conditions, it proves that the minimax expected misclassification proportion is \(\exp(-(1+o(1))mI(\pi))\), where \(I(\pi)\) is the minimum, over distinct labels, of their average Chernoff information across workers.

Here is the mirror I would put forward. Call it High-Multiplicity Dawid–Skene Exponent. An instance gives \(k\) labels, a finite support \(T\) of worker types, a rational confusion matrix \(Q^r\) for each type \(r\), and rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to one. At scale \(M\), there are \(M\mu_r\) workers of type \(r\), each independently labeling each of \(n\) items; the unknown true labels are arbitrary and deterministic. The task is to compute the asymptotic minimax error exponent and return an estimator attaining it. The objective is \(\mathcal R_M=\inf_{\hat y}\sup_{y\in[k]^n}\mathbb E[\frac1n\sum_j\mathbf 1\{\hat y_j\ne y_j\}]\). The proposed exponent is \(I_\mu=\min_{g\ne h}\left[-\min_{s\in[0,1]}\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r\log\sum_{\ell=1}^k(Q^r_{g\ell})^{1-s}(Q^r_{h\ell})^s\right]\). The estimator is the per-item MLE, using the type-level response counts.

For fixed \(k\), positive entries in the matrices, and positive mass on types that distinguish the least-separated label pair, the regularity assumptions have a natural high multiplicity version: the number of expert workers grows with \(M\). The expected direction is tractable: the estimator is explicit, and computing the exponent reduces to \(k(k-1)\) one-dimensional convex minimizations. The mirror covers the known-confusion-matrix minimax exponent in Theorem 3.1. It does not extend the paper’s adaptive estimation or EM results.

There is an important limit to this positive case: the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for a ChoCo anchor. Theorem 3.1 establishes a statistical risk rate, not membership in P or a hardness classification. Theorem 4.1’s plug-in guarantee and Theorem 5.1’s majority-vote exponent are also statistical results. So this is a plausible continuous-population version of the paper’s statistical problem, and Theorem 3.1 is its natural source, but it cannot serve as a qualifying computational anchor. The weakness is substantive: the paper already aggregates worker contributions in its exponent, and grouping identical workers mainly rewrites that sum using \(\mu\). There is no discrete hardness here whose dissolution the continuous version could test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The worker type model is the strongest proposed mirror, and it is sensible: in a standardized annotation task, a worker’s full confusion matrix can define their type. With rational type masses \(\mu_r\), the paper’s average Chernoff information becomes a weighted sum over types. That is a coherent high multiplicity statistical model; I would not reject it on the grounds that worker identity matters.

The problem is that Theorem 3.1 is a minimax risk-rate result, not a computational-complexity result. It gives the optimal misclassification exponent for known confusion matrices. Theorem 4.1 and Theorems 4.2–4.3 give statistical guarantees for adaptive estimators, and Theorem 5.1 gives a rate for majority voting. None asks for the complexity of a computational problem or establishes an algorithm’s running time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. The paper specifies an MLE, but its result concerns the MLE’s error, not the cost of computing it. So Theorem 3.1, the proponent’s only anchor, does not meet ChoCo’s stated test for a computational result to mirror.

One could add a decision to recruit or allocate workers by type, perhaps to meet a target error under a budget. That might be a useful continuous crowd-design problem, but the paper fixes the workers’ confusion matrices; it does not optimize over workforce composition. Adding that choice creates a new problem rather than a continuous version of a result proved here. The same is true of turning the plug-in guarantees into a new problem about estimating a distribution over worker types. Those extensions might be worth studying, but they do not supply the missing computational anchor.

Continuousizing the items instead does not repair the mismatch. A distribution over true labels loses the association between each item’s truth and its observed responses; retaining those associations as a function over a nonatomic item space would formulate a different learning problem.

So the negative case is strong for the ChoCo scope: this paper offers no named computational-complexity result to mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim that no continuous mirror could be worthwhile. The worker-population version is a credible statistical continuization, and the article’s asymptotic analysis already makes that direction natural. I would call it adjacent to the programme, rather than a qualifying mirror of this paper.
