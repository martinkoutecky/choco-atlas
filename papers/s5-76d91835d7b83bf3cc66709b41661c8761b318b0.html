The continuous mirror question

Given rational additive valuations \(v_j\) and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\in[0,1]\) for item slots \(j\in J\), decide whether there exists a finite-support mass allocation \(x_b\ge0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\{0,1\}^{J}\) such that \(\sum_bx_b=1\), \(\sum_bx_bb_j=\rho_j\) for every \(j\), and \(x_b>0\Rightarrow\sum_jb_jv_j>0\); equivalently, decide whether the continuous MMS value is positive.

The model it lives in

A homogeneous type \(T=\{t\}\) with mass \(\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\), integral bundle configurations \(b\in\{0,1\}^{J}\), and mass variables \(x_b\); maximize \(z\) subject to the supply marginals and \(x_b>0\Rightarrow v_t(b)\ge z\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem D.2's positive-\(\operatorname{MMS}\) hardness question. It leaves the PTAS results in Theorems 3.3 and 3.4, the non-existence construction, and the auxiliary structural lemmas untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the configuration-feasibility problem for \(z>0\) admit a polynomial-time algorithm or is it NP-hard?
  • Can a strengthened version of the paper's PARTITION reduction survive arbitrary mass mixtures with \(\rho_j=1/n\)?
  • What is the appropriate \(\gamma\)-PO analogue for heterogeneous type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think this paper admits a credible continuous mirror, with Theorem D.2 as the strongest anchor. I would not anchor on the PTAS results: their dependence on a constant number of agents does not transfer cleanly to an atomless population.

Theorem D.2, proved in this paper in Appendix D, says the following. For every fixed constant \(\tau>0\), even when all agents are identical and satisfy
\[ |v(M)|\ge \tau\min\{v(M^+),|v(M^-)|\}, \]
it is NP-hard to decide whether the maximin-share value is strictly positive. The reduction is from PARTITION. Given weights \(e_1,\ldots,e_m\), the construction uses \(m\) goods of values \(e_i\), \(n-2\) additional goods of value \(\beta=1/4\), and two chores of value
\[ -\frac12\sum_i e_i+\beta. \]
The number \(n\) is chosen, as a function of \(\tau\), so that the balance condition holds. Every agent has total value \(n\beta>0\), hence MMS is nonnegative and at most \(\beta\). A PARTITION solution yields an allocation with MMS \(\beta\); conversely, any allocation with positive MMS forces the two chores into separate bundles and yields a perfect partition of the \(e_i\). Thus checking whether MMS \(>0\) is NP-hard even for identical agents under the balance condition.

My mirror is a high-multiplicity version of this exact mixed-manna problem.

There is a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types. A type specifies the additive value of every item slot, including whether that slot is a good or a chore. The society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). For the lead instance, \(T=\{t\}\) and \(\mu_t=1\): a very large population of interchangeable agents with the same valuation vector.

The manna consists of item slots \(J\). Slot \(j\) has a per-capita supply \(\rho_j\in\mathbb{Q}\), interpreted as \(\rho_j\) indivisible copies per unit population. To keep the individual bundle space finite, each agent may receive at most one copy of each slot; hence an individual bundle is an integral vector \(b\in\{0,1\}^{J}\), with value
\[ v_t(b)=\sum_{j\in J} b_jv_t(j). \]
The aggregate supply \(\rho\) may be fractional, but no individual receives a fractional item.

Define the continuous MMS value of type \(t\) by
\[ \operatorname{MMS}_t(\rho) = \max_{p} \min_{b:p_b>0} v_t(b), \]
where \(p\) ranges over distributions on \(\{0,1\}^{J}\) satisfying
\[ \sum_b p_b=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_b p_bb_j=\rho_j \quad\text{for every }j\in J. \]
This is the continuum analogue of partitioning the manna into one bundle per agent: \(p_b\) is the population fraction receiving integral bundle \(b\).

The continuous problem is:

\[ \mathsf{Positive\text{-}MMS}_\infty \]

Given rational \(v_j\) and \(\rho_j\), decide whether there exists a finite-support mass allocation \(x_b\ge0\) such that
\[ \sum_bx_b=1,\qquad \sum_bx_bb_j=\rho_j, \]
and
\[ x_b>0\implies v(b)>0. \]

Equivalently, decide whether \(\operatorname{MMS}(\rho)>0\), and if so return the mass assignment \(x\). The natural optimization extension asks for the largest \(\alpha\) such that every positive-mass bundle receives at least \(\alpha\operatorname{MMS}(\rho)\).

This is a genuine population continuization. If all masses have denominator \(q\), multiplying by \(q\) gives \(q\) agents, \(q\rho_j\) item copies, and \(qx_b\) agents receiving bundle \(b\). Thus rational continuous allocations are precisely high-multiplicity limits of finite allocations. The aggregate variables are fractional only because they describe fractions of agents; every agent still receives an indivisible integral bundle.

A plausible regime is a large standardized workforce, student cohort, or municipal population receiving repeated desirable assignments and undesirable mandatory duties. There may be millions of agents but only one or a few complete valuation types: the same preferences, eligibility, prices, and disutility for each recurring slot. The number of population members is therefore much larger than the number of types. This is exactly the high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that every fair-division instance has it.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. The mirror retains additive mixed valuations, indivisible bundles, the MMS benchmark, and the question of whether every member can receive a guaranteed fair bundle. Only the population count is replaced by mass, with per-capita item supplies added because a continuum population cannot share one finite inventory. That co-modification is forced by the population limit, not a replacement of indivisible outcomes by divisible ones.

My expectation is that hardness transfers, placing this mirror in Class B. The reduction in Theorem D.2 derives its combinatorics from the item weights and the two large chores, not from population multiplicity. The balance condition also survives verbatim after normalization:
\[ |\bar v(\rho)| \ge \kappa\min\{v^+(\rho),v^-(\rho)\}, \]
where \(\kappa\) is fixed and \(\bar v(\rho)=\sum_j\rho_jv_j\). A gap-preserving version of the PARTITION construction should force every positive bundle containing a chore to carry exactly half of the partition weight, even when agents are represented by mass.

The important caveat is also the weakest point: continuum mass permits mixtures of different integral bundle patterns. A fractional mixture might match the per-capita supplies even when no single finite partition does. If that happens, the discrete hardness dissolves, and the problem may instead collapse to a configuration LP. That is not a defect in the mirror; it is precisely the computational question the programme is meant to expose. But the paper itself does not prove that its reduction survives this convexification, so I would present hardness transfer as the expected outcome, not as an established consequence.

The immediate follow-up questions are whether \(\mathsf{Positive\text{-}MMS}_\infty\) is NP-hard or polynomial-time solvable, whether the full \(\alpha\)-MMS problem admits a configuration-LP algorithm, and whether the paper’s \(\gamma\)-PO guarantee has a meaningful extension for heterogeneous type masses. My case covers only the paper’s MMS hardness result, not its entire PTAS-plus-PO programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem D.2 is not really a population result. Its hardness comes from partitioning one finite manna into exactly \(n\) bundles, while all agents are identical. If the manna remains finite and the population becomes atomless, almost every agent receives nothing and the MMS value collapses to \(0\). To avoid that degeneration, the proposed mirror introduces per-capita item supplies and a new configuration problem.

That new problem is not automatically the high-multiplicity version of the theorem. The finite reduction requires \(n\) equiprobable bundles, each containing one copy of every item overall. The proposed \(p_b\) permits an arbitrary mixture of integral bundles with unequal masses. It therefore convexifies the partition constraint: the supply of a slot can be distributed across several bundle patterns. Conversely, if one forces \(n\) labelled cohorts, uniform masses, and the original one-copy structure, one recovers the finite PARTITION instance replicated many times; the continuum then contributes no computational content.

The proponent’s best repair is to set \(\rho_j=1/n\), retain integral bundles, and ask whether the supply vector lies in the convex hull of positive-valued bundles. That is a legitimate and potentially interesting configuration-LP question, but it is no longer Theorem D.2’s problem. The paper’s converse proof uses the existence of exactly \(n\) finite bundles and integer accounting; it does not establish that an arbitrary positive-support mass mixture contains a bundle encoding a perfect PARTITION. The claimed hardness transfer is therefore only a conjecture.

This objection is ultimately weaker than it first appears. The continuous question remains meaningful even if the convexification destroys the reduction, and the authors would plausibly recognise a large homogeneous cohort receiving recurring indivisible assignments as their fair-division problem. There is no identity-sensitive objective, and \(T=\{t\}\) is a perfectly valid high-multiplicity regime. Indeed, the tight accounting in the reduction might be strong enough to force the required half-sum structure even in the mass model; proving that would make this a successful Class B mirror.

So the honest negative conclusion is limited: the proponent has not yet established that their anchor survives continuization, and the faithful alternatives are either degenerate, inert, or a materially different configuration problem. But I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. Theorem D.2 is a named computational result with a natural homogeneous-population interpretation, so this anchor probably survives; the negative case is weak.
