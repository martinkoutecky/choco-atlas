The continuous mirror question

Given finite schools \(S\), finite student types \(T\), rational masses \(\lambda_t\) with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\), rational capacity shares \(q_s\), type preferences \(P_t\), and priority profiles \(\succ,\succ'\) where \(\succ'\) improves \(\succ\) for \(G\subseteq T\), compute an exact mass allocation \(x=(x_{t,s})\) using mass EADA for the profile \(\{\succ_s,\succ'_s\}_{s\in S}\). Require \(\sum_{s\in S\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{t,s}=\lambda_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\), individual rationality, non-wastefulness, M-fairness, and \(G\)-Pareto optimality among feasible M-stable allocations; for a second profile \(\succ''\) with \(\succ'\) more improving \(\succ\) than \(\succ''\), compute both outputs and preserve the stated \(G\)-non-domination relation.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity school-choice market with finite cohort types \(T\), mass vector \(\lambda\), per-capita school capacities \(q\), type-level preferences and priority orders, and decision variables \(x_{t,s}\ge0\). The objective is to compute the exact mass analogue of \(\phi^*\), satisfying M-stability, improved-group Pareto optimality, and responsiveness.

The objection that survived

The mass extension must still prove that splitting positive mass at priority cutoffs preserves EADA's SOSM and responsiveness guarantees; the eventual result could be a routine compression rather than a rich complexity theorem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the constructive core in Proposition 1 and Theorems 1 and 3 under finite types and scaled capacities. It leaves the paper's impossibility result, ancillary applications, and several partial-order edge cases uncontinuized.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can mass EADA be implemented exactly in time polynomial in \(\lvert T\rvert\), \(\lvert S\rvert\), and the input bit length?
  • Do type splitting at priority cutoffs and scaled capacities preserve the SOSM and responsiveness proofs?
  • What rounding or additive guarantees translate the mass solution back to \(N\)-student instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s school-choice problem, with the main anchor being Theorem 3. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity theorem, so this is necessarily an algorithmic rather than hardness-based mirror.

Take a finite set of schools \(S\) and a finite set of complete student types \(T\). A type \(t\) includes everything relevant to the problem: its school preference \(P_t\), its priority-relevant characteristics at every school, its membership in an improved or protected group, and any other parameter used by the mechanism. The society is a rational mass vector \(\lambda\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\lambda_t\) is the fraction of students of type \(t\). School \(s\) has a capacity share \(q_s\), so if the underlying population has \(N\) students, its finite capacity is approximately \(Nq_s\).

A solution is an allocation of the continuum of students to \(S\cup\{\varnothing\}\). Equivalently, it is a matrix \(x_{t,s}\ge 0\), with

\[ \sum_{s\in S\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{t,s}=\lambda_t \]

for every type \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,s}\le q_s \]

for every school \(s\). This is not lottery-based outcome continuity: almost every individual receives one integral school assignment. The matrix records only the mass of identical individuals receiving each assignment.

The multiple priorities are relations \(\triangleright_s\) on types. A mass allocation is M-fair if any priority violation of a type \(t\) assigned below school \(s\), against a type \(u\) occupying \(s\), is reversed by another priority at \(s\). Individual rationality and non-wastefulness are defined in the same way, using positive mass instead of individual membership. Thus M-stability remains the paper’s underlying notion, not a weakened welfare or lottery problem.

The regime is realistic for centralized public-school or college admissions. Imagine millions of applicants divided into cohorts sharing the same complete preference list, score band, geographic or sibling status, minority status, and other priority characteristics. Then \(N\) may be in the millions, while \(\tau=|T|\) is hundreds or thousands and \(m=|S|\) is moderate. The capacities scale with the population, \(q_sN\). This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime; it would not be appropriate for a seven-person committee or for an admissions market in which every applicant has a unique preference and score vector.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper in Appendix A.7. The paper defines

\[ \phi^*(\succ,\succ')= EA\bigl(M(\triangleright'),\succ'\bigr), \]

where \(\triangleright'_s=\{\succ_s,\succ'_s\}\), \(M\) constructs the derived priority relation, and \(EA\) is the efficiency-adjusted deferred-acceptance algorithm. Theorem 3 says that \(\phi^*\) is improved-group optimally M-stable and responsive to improvements. In full: when \(\succ'\) is an improvement of \(\succ\) for a student group \(I'\), the outcome is not Pareto dominated for \(I'\) by any other M-stable matching. Moreover, when \(\succ'\) more improves \(\succ\) than another profile \(\succ''\) for \(I'\), the outcome under \((\succ,\succ')\) is not Pareto dominated for \(I'\) by the outcome under \((\succ,\succ'')\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous Improved-Group M-Stable Assignment. Given \((S,T,\lambda,q,P,\succ,\succ')\), with \(\succ'\) an improvement of \(\succ\) for a type group \(G\subseteq T\), output a mass allocation

\[ x=\Phi_\infty(\succ,\succ') \]

that is M-stable for the two-priority profile and is \(G\)-Pareto optimal among all M-stable mass allocations. Here \(y\) \(G\)-Pareto dominates \(x\) if the two allocations can be realized on the same continuum of agents so that every member of \(G\) weakly prefers their assignment under \(y\), with strict improvement for positive \(G\)-mass. If a second profile \(\succ''\) is supplied and \(\succ'\) more improves \(\succ\) than \(\succ''\), the required outputs must also satisfy that \(\Phi_\infty(\succ,\succ')\) is not \(G\)-Pareto dominated by \(\Phi_\infty(\succ,\succ'')\).

I expect this problem to be Class A: tractable by a mass version of EADA, with running time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of \(\lambda\) and \(q\). The paper’s exact theorem does not establish that complexity bound; that is the continuous computational question. The result remains recognisably the authors’ problem because it retains multiple priorities, M-fairness, affirmative-action improvements, improved-group optimality, and responsiveness. Only the population representation changes.

Theorem 1 is the algorithmic foundation and a second useful anchor. It is proved in the paper in Appendix A.3. It says that if every school priority \(\succ_s\) is a partial order and \(\succ^*\) is a profile of linear-order extensions, then

\[ EA(\succ,\succ^*) \]

is a student-optimal stable matching for \(\succ\): it is stable for \(\succ\), and no other stable matching for \(\succ\) Pareto dominates it for all students.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous EADA-SOSM. Given a finite type set \(T\), mass vector \(\lambda\), school capacities \(q\), type preferences \(P_t\), a partial-order priority profile \(\succ\), and a linear extension profile \(\succ^*\), output the mass allocation

\[ x=EA_\infty(\succ,\succ^*) \]

such that \(x\) is stable for \(\succ\), and no other stable mass allocation Pareto dominates it for the entire population. The expected solution is the compressed EADA process: proposal and rejection quantities are masses, underdemanded schools and rejected type mass are removed round by round, and the output is an exact rational mass allocation.

This is a particularly clean high-multiplicity question because the finite EADA algorithm already works through proposals, rejections, school capacities, and preference truncation. With finitely many types, one can ask whether those operations admit a compressed implementation whose complexity depends on \(\tau\), rather than on the underlying population size \(N\).

Finally, Proposition 1, proved in Section 4, gives the structural reduction on which both questions rest. For a multiple-priority profile \(\triangleright\), the paper defines

\[ m(\triangleright_s) = \Pi\left(\bigcup_{\succ_s\in\triangleright_s}\succ_s\right), \qquad M(\triangleright)=\bigl(m(\triangleright_s)\bigr)_{s\in S}. \]

Proposition 1 says that a matching is M-stable for \(\triangleright\) if and only if it is ordinarily stable for the derived single-priority profile \(M(\triangleright)\). The paper proves this by showing that an unjustified priority violation under one of the original priorities is exactly a justified-or-unjustified violation encoded by \(m(\triangleright_s)\).

The continuous version is:

Continuous M-Stability Reduction. Given \((S,T,\lambda,q,P,\triangleright)\), compute \(m(\triangleright_s)\) on the finite type set and either output an M-stable mass allocation or report that none exists. The correctness requirement is exact equivalence:

\[ x\text{ is M-stable for }\triangleright \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\text{ is stable for }M(\triangleright). \]

On the linear-order subdomain used by Theorem 3, \(m(\triangleright_s)\) is simply the intersection of the two type-priority orders and is acyclic. The natural expectation is therefore a polynomial mass-DA algorithm. With arbitrary partial priorities, the derived relation may contain cycles, just as in the paper’s Example 3, and the continuous problem should explicitly allow a “no M-stable allocation exists” answer.

These mirrors cover only the paper’s core computational content: Proposition 1, Theorem 1, and Theorem 3. They do not claim to continuize every comparative-static or impossibility statement in the paper. They also generate useful boundary questions: whether exact rational mass outputs can always be computed in polynomial time; how rounding behaves when returning to \(N\) discrete students; whether the results survive weak rather than linear type priorities; and whether continuum-specific difficulty appears once the number of types is no longer small.

The weakest point is that Theorem 3 is an algorithmic welfare theorem, not a complexity theorem, and the paper’s EADA result might compress almost mechanically in this finite-type setting. If every applicant has an idiosyncratic preference list, score, or tie-breaker, the high-multiplicity mirror becomes artificial. The positive case therefore depends on the explicit cohort regime above. But within that regime, the mirror is not a change of topic: it asks for the same school-choice assignment, with the same priorities and stability notion, while making the large repeated population the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive at the level of programme fit: none of the three cited results is a computational-complexity result. Proposition 1 is a pointwise definitional equivalence; Theorems 1 and 3 are correctness and welfare guarantees for EADA. The paper calls the mechanism computationally efficient, but gives no complexity theorem, input encoding, high-multiplicity formulation, approximation result, or complexity boundary. Thus a “continuous EADA” would establish a new implementation theorem, not mirror a named computational result of the paper.

Proposition 1 is the weakest anchor. Once types contain all priority-relevant information, \(M(\triangleright)\) is computed on the finite type set exactly as in the discrete model, and the proof of

\[ x\text{ is M-stable}\iff x\text{ is stable for }M(\triangleright) \]

is entirely insensitive to masses. The proposed continuous question therefore adds no population-dependent operation: it merely applies the paper’s relation-theoretic reduction to types and replaces individual assignments by quantities. Cycles and nonexistence remain the same finite priority-graph issue.

Theorem 1 is more plausible, but still not a result to be mirrored. A mass version of EADA would be a compressed execution of the existing proposal-and-rejection procedure: identical types apply in batches, capacities subtract rational quantities, and a type may be split at a cutoff. Proving that this weighted procedure preserves stability and student optimality could be useful, but it is a new algorithmic extension of a finite mechanism, not a continuous counterpart of a complexity claim in the paper. The paper gives no reason to expect a separation problem, an LP, or a nontrivial complexity landscape of the kind ChoCo is meant to expose.

Theorem 3 has the best possible mirror, and this is where the negative case is weakest. If millions of applicants genuinely share complete preference, priority, and affirmative-action characteristics, with capacities scaling proportionally, then the proposed mass assignment is a sensible high-multiplicity school-choice problem. Group Pareto comparisons can be defined by positive mass, and EADA may plausibly admit an exact compressed implementation. That is not invalidated by the fact that the proof is combinatorial or that the result might be easy: an exact high-multiplicity algorithm would still be a legitimate Class A result.

Consequently, I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The paper is a weak source for ChoCo because it contains no named complexity result and its main mathematics may compress mechanically. But the cohort-based version of Theorem 3 survives the negative attack: school choice with repeated applicant types and population-scaled capacities is a genuine high-multiplicity regime, and its continuous computational treatment could be worthwhile. The defensible negative verdict is therefore “poorly anchored and probably modest,” not “no continuous mirror in any scenario.”
