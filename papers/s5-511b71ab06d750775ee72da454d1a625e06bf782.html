The continuous mirror question

Given a finite chord alphabet \(A\), sequence length \(k\), support \(T\subseteq A^k\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational threshold \(q\), does there exist \(W\in A^k\) such that \(\Phi_\mu(W)\ge q\)? Here \(u_{t,j}(W)=1-d_{\mathrm{Jaccard}}(t_j,W_j)\), the values \(u_{t,j}(W)\) are sorted in nonincreasing order as \(u^\downarrow_{t,1}(W),\ldots,u^\downarrow_{t,k}(W)\), and \(\Phi_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{r=1}^k u^\downarrow_{t,r}(W)/r\). Equivalently, find a maximizing \(W\).

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\in A^k\) is a complete proposed chord sequence, \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of contributors, and the output is a sequence \(W\in A^k\) maximizing the mass-weighted proportional satisfaction score.

What the mirror covers

Covers the proportional objective underlying Theorem 1 over a distribution of sequence types. It leaves the genuine 2-gram tradeoff, plurality, Kemeny, Clustered-Kemeny, and the simulation findings aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the claimed PAV hardness be realized by utility patterns induced by the paper’s fixed chord alphabet and Jaccard distance?
  • What algorithms or hardness results hold when the support size \(\tau=|T|\) is bounded?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s proportional aggregation problem. In a large crowdsourced harmonization project for one fixed piece, the agents could be contributors drawn from a library of recurring chord-sequence proposals. A type is one complete proposed sequence \(t\in A^k\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of contributors proposing it. Millions of contributors and hundreds or thousands of distinct proposals is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. It is the paper’s own model with repeated rows grouped into a histogram, not a change to what is being aggregated.

My anchor is Theorem 1. It states that both PAV and PAV with 2-gram are NP-hard. The paper argues this by making its Proportional rule include PAV when the utilities are binary, and then claims the 2-gram result follows by setting \(x_P=1\). The wrapper argument is in the paper; the underlying PAV hardness it invokes is cited from elsewhere, as \(\cite{skowron2016finding}\). The appendix repeats the claim as Theorem 7.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Proportional Harmonization. An instance consists of a finite chord alphabet \(A\), a sequence length \(k\), a support \(T\subseteq A^k\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\). For a proposed output \(W\in A^k\), let \(u_{t,j}(W)=1-d_{\mathrm{Jaccard}}(t_j,W_j)\), and sort these \(k\) utilities in nonincreasing order as \(u^\downarrow_{t,1}(W),\ldots,u^\downarrow_{t,k}(W)\). Define

\[ \Phi_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{r=1}^{k}\frac{u^\downarrow_{t,r}(W)}{r}. \]

The decision question is whether some \(W\in A^k\) satisfies \(\Phi_\mu(W)\ge q\); an optimization solution is a maximizing \(W\). This keeps the paper’s per-agent proportional score intact and replaces its sum over agents with expected score under the type distribution. For a discrete electorate, setting \(\mu_t\) to the fraction of agents with sequence \(t\) gives exactly the same maximizing harmonizations.

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B), provided the paper’s PAV hardness embedding really applies to its stated chord-and-Jaccard model. Every discrete instance can be encoded by rational masses of polynomial bit length, so the continuous formulation does not erase that hardness. The natural follow-up questions are whether the problem becomes tractable for bounded support size \(\tau=|T|\), whether it admits useful approximation or parameterized algorithms in \(k,\tau\), and which restrictions on the chord alphabet or preference types remove the hard cases.

Theorem 1 also claims hardness for the 2-gram variant, but the stated argument uses \(x_P=1\), which removes the 2-gram contribution. The paper’s displayed maximize/minimize conventions for these objectives are also inconsistent. So I would treat that as a claimed endpoint result, not evidence about a genuine tradeoff between representation and musical coherence. I would not make it a second anchor.

The soft spot is the hardness transfer itself: the paper invokes a binary-utility special case, but does not clearly show that the required binary utility patterns are realizable by its fixed Jaccard distances over the chord alphabet. That may leave the theorem’s hardness claim needing a more explicit reduction for the formal musical model. It weakens the predicted complexity result, not the mirror: grouping identical proposed sequences into rational masses is a direct and plausible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ aggregation question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this anchor is that the paper has not convincingly established the result it wants to mirror. Its NP-hardness proof says the proportional rule contains PAV when utilities are binary, but does not show how arbitrary PAV approval patterns arise from the paper’s fixed chord alphabet and Jaccard similarities. The formal objective also has inconsistent maximize/minimize directions. So Theorem 1 is a weak foundation for claiming that this particular musical model has a proved complexity classification.

That does not defeat the mirror. The weighted objective \(\Phi_\mu\) is well-defined even if the theorem’s proof needs repair. And a stronger instance regime is easy to describe: a large community choosing among recurring chord-sequence templates for one standard. Each type is its complete proposed sequence; agents with the same sequence contribute the same satisfaction, and the output can still be a newly assembled sequence. The mass-weighted score is exactly the paper’s aggregate score, with no identity-dependent information lost and no degenerate limit.

One can object that unrestricted sequences may have little repetition, or that a rational histogram is just a compressed discrete profile. But the first is a claim about a particular regime, not every plausible one; the template-choice setting supplies a credible high-multiplicity regime. The second is precisely the high-multiplicity bridge ChoCo is meant to study. Neither shows the question is unworthy.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The source theorem’s proof is vulnerable, but that makes the continuous complexity question more in need of analysis, not less worthwhile. A universal negative case does not survive: the proportional aggregation problem has a sensible continuous-population mirror.
