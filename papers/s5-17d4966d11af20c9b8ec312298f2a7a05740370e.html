The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest substantive mirror is type-aggregated soft load shedding, but this paper has no eligible numbered computational result to anchor it. The experiments use CVX to solve a convex welfare problem, but do not prove a complexity classification. Section 4’s only theorem is unnumbered: it says that some common price \(\lambda\) makes each household’s maximizer of \(U_i(x_i)-\lambda x_i\), subject to \(0\le x_i\le d_i\), also solve the system-wide welfare problem with \(\sum_i x_i=S\). That theorem is proved here, but it is a price-equilibrium characterization, not a numbered result asserting tractability or hardness. I can’t honestly supply the required anchor number.

The corresponding continuous question is nevertheless clear. Consider a utility company serving a large residential population with a small set of repeated household types. A type \(t\) consists of a household’s demand cap \(d_t\) and utility parameters; \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population. Let \(q\) be the available supply per household, so total supply scales with the population. For the paper’s proportional-fair case, take \(U(x)=\log x\). Given rational \(\mu_t,d_t,q\), with \(0<q<\sum_t\mu_t d_t\), find quotas \(y_t\) maximizing

\[ \sum_t \mu_t\log y_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le y_t\le d_t,\qquad \sum_t\mu_t y_t=q. \]

Each household of type \(t\) receives quota \(y_t\); mass records how much of the population that type represents. This is the paper’s underlying SLS allocation question, with the population represented by its type distribution. It is not a continuation of the quota or utility outcome space alone.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable. With a common logarithmic utility, the price condition gives \(y_t=\min(d_t,1/\lambda)\), where \(\lambda\) is chosen to clear supply. Sorting the demand caps and solving the resulting piecewise-linear supply equation gives an exact algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. That is a deduction for the proposed mirror, not a complexity theorem from the paper. The mirror covers the SLS welfare allocation and its price characterization; it does not cover the block-tariff revenue comparisons or the empirical baseline results.

The deployment regime is a utility company serving millions of households with repeated demand and utility profiles—for example, recurring household load classes for a peak window—so \(N\) can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of types. The paper itself motivates utility-scale deployment and mentions clustering, but its experiments use only 34–707 individually listed households and do not establish a small type support. The mirror is most plausible for the stated large-scale deployment regime, rather than as a faithful model of those experimental datasets.

That population-size mismatch, and especially the absence of a qualifying named computational anchor, is the weak point. The type-aggregated problem is a credible continuous form of the authors’ allocation question, but under the requested anchor rule this paper cannot support a formal anchor-based positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against an anchored mirror is that the paper supplies no eligible computational result. Its sole theorem says a common price \(\lambda\) can decentralize the welfare optimum. It is unnumbered and gives no complexity claim. The CVX experiments and runtime plot are empirical reports, not a proved algorithmic result. So the proponent’s proposed type-aggregated SLS problem is a new complexity question built from the paper’s model; it is not a continuous counterpart to a computational theorem in the paper.

The mirror itself is coherent. A utility company serving a large population with repeated household profiles is plausible, and a complete type could include demand cap, utility parameters, tariff, and contract terms. With per-household supply \(q\), the allocation problem becomes \(\max \sum_t \mu_t U_t(y_t)\), subject to \(0\le y_t\le d_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t y_t=q\). Extending this to a distribution over household profiles is also a natural version. But the price theorem then carries over by reweighting the same first-order conditions; it still does not supply the missing computational result. For an atomless type distribution, a complexity question would additionally need a finite representation of the distribution, which the paper does not specify.

The paper’s operational motivation makes the regime credible, so I cannot honestly argue that no scenario could support a worthwhile mirror. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no formal complexity result to anchor one, and its proposed mirror is an extension of an already-formulated welfare optimization problem rather than a continuation of a result the paper proves. The case for rejecting it as an anchored ChoCo target is strong; the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is weak.
