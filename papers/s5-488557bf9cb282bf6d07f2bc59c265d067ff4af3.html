What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-up covers the asymptotic rank and match-type results; it supplies no computational mirror for them.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper studies a very natural large-population regime: two sides of a market each have \(n\) agents, divided into a fixed number of score tiers, with a constant fraction of agents in each tier. That fits settings such as large hiring markets with recurring applicant and employer cohorts.

But the paper has no named computational complexity result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Its main results are probability and asymptotic analysis of deferred acceptance, not classifications such as NP-hardness or polynomial-time solvability.

The closest anchor is Theorem 4.6, proved here. For any men’s tier \(j\), let \(\overline R^M_j\) be the average rank of partners for men in that tier. For every \(\epsilon>0\), as \(n\to\infty\), with probability approaching \(1\),
\[ \overline R^M_j=(1\pm\epsilon) \frac{\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\bm{\alpha}}{\alpha_{\min}} \frac{1}{\bm{\delta}\cdot\bm{\beta}^{-1}} \frac{\ln n}{\beta_j}. \]
This is a meaningful result about the random market’s outcome, but it does not assert a computational complexity claim. The women’s rank and match-type theorems are likewise asymptotic outcome results.

A plausible non-anchor follow-up would take rational tier proportions \(\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\delta}\) and bounded score vectors \(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}\), define the corresponding family of random markets, and ask for the limiting normalized average ranks and match-type probabilities under man-proposing deferred acceptance. The paper already answers that question with formulas. It is therefore an analytic limit-statistics question, not the computational problem ChoCo is looking for; I would not assign it a class in the programme’s complexity trichotomy.

There is also a modelling obstacle to turning the tiers directly into ChoCo types. Agents in a tier share a preference-generating law, but their realized strict rankings over individually named counterparts are sampled independently. Since a type must capture everything the problem uses, the tier is not a complete preference type here. Replacing realized rankings with tier-level preference laws may be a sensible stochastic mean-field model, but it changes the matching problem.

So the positive case is narrow: the market regime is credible and its asymptotics make a natural starting point for a population-level follow-up. The weak point is decisive for this assignment: there is no named computational anchor, and the most tempting follow-up is already answered analytically by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its named theorems characterize random outcomes of deferred acceptance: for example, the men’s average rank concentrates around a formula as \(n\to\infty\). The paper does not classify the complexity of computing, optimizing, or approximating a social-choice problem. Deferred acceptance is the procedure generating the outcome, not a complexity question the paper studies. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, that leaves no computational anchor.

Theorem 4.6 is the strongest proposed anchor, but its natural mass-level reading asks for limiting rank statistics by tier. That is an analytic question about the random market, not a computational one. The same is true of the women’s rank result and the match-type result. The latter already concerns aggregate proportions, so it is the best candidate for a continuum formulation; still, turning it into a computational problem would require adding a task such as optimizing those flows or intervening in the market. That would be a new problem, not a computational mirror of the result.

There is also a deeper modeling obstacle to rescuing the rank anchors. A tier describes an agent’s preference-generating law, but the paper’s DA process uses each realized strict ranking over individually named counterpart agents. Replacing each cohort by mass while retaining those rankings requires a type space of realized preferences over the counterpart identities; that space changes with \(n\) and can be enormous, so the useful high-multiplicity representation does not follow. Collapsing rankings to preferences over tiers is more compact, but discards the individual comparisons that determine proposals, acceptances, and ranks. A continuum stable-matching or flow model could be sensible, but it would study a different object.

So the negative case is strong against these anchors: the aggregate population regime is natural, but the paper’s contribution is asymptotic probability analysis, not computational social choice. It cannot establish the universal claim that no useful continuous computational project exists nearby; matching markets plainly admit other computational questions. But those would need a new objective or intervention, and the paper supplies no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror.
