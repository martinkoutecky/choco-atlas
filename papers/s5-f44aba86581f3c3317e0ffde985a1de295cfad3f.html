The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow: this paper has no named complexity result of the kind you asked for. Theorem 1, proved here, says that a lottery mechanism is strategyproof exactly when it is separation monotonic, separation upper invariant, and separation lower invariant. A separation splits one indifference class into two ordered groups while leaving the rest of the ranking unchanged. Theorem 1 is a useful local characterization, and the paper explains that it can greatly reduce the constraints used to check strategyproofness. But the paper does not classify a computational problem as tractable or hard, or compute with a formulation. So this is a computational foothold, not a qualifying complexity anchor.

The most defensible mirror I can build from it is Population-Weighted Truthful Lottery Repair. Consider a large school-choice cohort with a fixed finite set \(M\) of school programs and a fixed set \(T\) of possible weak rankings. A type is a complete ranking \(R\in T\); a society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with support \(S\) of size \(\tau\). For example, thousands of applicants might fall into a few dozen ranking types over four programs. Given target lotteries \(q_R\) for \(R\in S\), and per-capita capacities \(b_a\) for each program \(a\), choose a lottery \(x_R\in\Delta(M)\) for every possible report \(R\in T\) to minimize
\[ \sum_{R\in S}\mu_R\sum_{a\in M}|x_R(a)-q_R(a)| \]
subject to \(\sum_{R\in S}\mu_Rx_R(a)\le b_a\) for each \(a\), and truthful reporting being strategyproof under first-order stochastic dominance for every true ranking and every possible misreport. A solution is the full table of lotteries \(x_R\). Each applicant still receives one school; the fractional quantities are type-level expected assignments and population shares.

The regime is sensible when applicants with the same ranking are alike in every mechanism-relevant respect, and the cohort is much larger than its number of types. With a nonatomic population, a single applicant’s report does not change the aggregate shares or capacities. They still have a meaningful individual incentive: their report selects their own lottery. This is the clean large-population version of the problem.

Theorem 1 supplies the strongest connection: it gives an exact way to impose the strategyproofness condition using separations. Together with the paper’s Remark 2, which allows responsiveness plus upper and lower invariance, those conditions are linear in the lottery probabilities. The repair objective can also be linearized, so the stated problem is an LP. I would expect tractability when the possible type set \(T\) is explicitly represented, with running time polynomial in that representation’s size, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. That is a consequence for this proposed problem, not a result proved in the paper. Avoiding enumeration of all weak rankings when \(m\) is part of the input is a further question.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as an automated mechanism-design application of their characterization: the introduction explicitly motivates reducing strategyproofness constraints in optimization. But the mirror adds a population-weighted repair objective and capacities; neither is studied in the paper. It covers the strategyproofness decomposition, not the paper’s other claims as a whole. The main soft spot is that the paper’s theorem is about one agent’s report and applies pointwise here; the population makes the design objective and capacity constraints aggregate, but does not make the theorem itself a result about large populations. That makes this a plausible research direction, but a weaker case than a continuous mirror anchored in a named hardness or algorithmic result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch: the paper proves no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes when a lottery rule is strategyproof; it does not give an algorithm, a hardness result, or the complexity of a problem whose input is a society. Remark 1 counts incentive constraints, but a smaller characterization is not itself a complexity classification.

That matters for the proposed lottery-repair mirror. Its target lotteries \(q_R\), repair objective, and capacity limits introduce the optimization problem. The paper supplies a way to express strategyproofness constraints, but those constraints apply pointwise to the rule \(\varphi\); they do not depend on the population distribution \(\mu\). Remove the added objective and capacities, and Theorem 1 is unchanged. So the repair problem is a plausible follow-up, but its population component comes from a new modeling choice, not from a computational result in this paper. That makes it a weak anchor for claiming the paper itself has a continuous mirror.

The better version is hard to dismiss. Optimizing a truthful, capacity-feasible assignment rule for a large cohort is natural in school choice, and the paper explicitly motivates making strategyproofness constraints usable in automated mechanism design. Grouping applicants by complete preference type and weighting aggregate assignments by their masses is coherent. I cannot rule out that the authors would recognize this as their problem.

So the negative case is narrow: the paper offers no direct computational anchor, and the proposed repair imports the optimization objective that makes it one. That undercuts the claim that the mirror follows from this paper’s results. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; the stronger mechanism-design version appears to survive that objection.
