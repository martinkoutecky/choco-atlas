The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Proposition 2, proved here, characterizes pure equilibria by a cutoff \(c\) satisfying \(n(c)=N(s_A(c)+s_B(c))\), \(m(c)=|s_A(c)-s_B(c)|/(s_A(c)+s_B(c))\), and \(c=p(n(c),m(c))\). Theorem 1, also proved here, characterizes equilibrium limits under its assumptions using pivot points; Proposition 6 gives the asymptotic win-probability cases as \(\alpha\) crosses \(2\beta\). These are equilibrium and probability results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. So none can honestly serve as one of the anchors you require.

The nearest prospective mirror would be Issue-Equilibrium Limit Enumeration. Give an instance as rational piecewise-linear support functions \(s_A,s_B\) and a specified polynomial pivotality rule \(p\). Interpret \(s_T(c)\) as the mass of supporters of \(T\) with voting cost at most \(c\); the action is vote or abstain, and equilibrium participation is described by a cost cutoff. For each population scale \(N\), a cutoff must satisfy the fixed-point equation above. The task is to output all limits \(c^*\) of equilibrium-cutoff sequences, together with the corresponding limit of \(\Pr(V_A>V_B)\). A solution is a finite exact description of those limits and probabilities.

The scenario is a large recurring referendum, with many voters drawn from a shared distribution of preference and normalized voting costs. Mass represents fractions of the electorate. Under the paper’s assumptions, this proposed task looks tractable: Theorem 1 reduces candidate limits to \(0\) and support-function pivot points, and Proposition 6 supplies the associated limiting probabilities. Further questions would include complexity in the number of support-function pieces and input bit length, and how the computation changes for other pivotality rules.

That is a plausible new computational question, not a result this paper establishes. Its weakest point is also substantial: the paper’s cost distribution is continuous, while ChoCo’s central finite-type model counts complete cost-preference combinations as types. Encoding the paper’s cost continuum compactly would require extending that finite-support model; discretizing costs could alter the equilibrium behavior. The paper also already analyzes these equilibrium limits mathematically, so the positive case is for computationalizing a continuous-population model it substantially develops—not for claiming a named complexity result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest objection is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Proposition 2 characterizes equilibria by a cutoff equation; Theorem 1 characterizes possible equilibrium limits under stated assumptions; Proposition 6 gives limiting win probabilities for a particular pivotality rule. None poses a computational problem or establishes its complexity. That is a decisive problem for an *anchored mirror*.

The proposed Issue-Equilibrium Limit Enumeration is the nearest candidate, but its input is already the paper’s own society: a distribution over preference and cost, represented by support functions, with elections sampled at each \(N\). The paper then studies those elections as \(N\) grows. Asking an algorithm to enumerate the limits would add an encoding and runtime question to this existing distributional analysis; it would not make a previously discrete society continuous. And the requested limits and probabilities are exactly what the cited theorems characterize. The issue is not that this computation would be easy or that its answer is uninteresting. It is that this is a computationalization of the paper’s existing continuous model, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result.

A finite-type version does not directly rescue that anchor. Grouping voters into rational cost classes would produce the kind of repeated complete types ChoCo studies, but it replaces the paper’s atomless cost distribution with a stepwise one. The paper’s cutoff and limit results rely on smooth support functions; with cost classes, aggregate participation jumps at class thresholds, so those results do not simply carry over. A new pure or mixed equilibrium analysis might be worthwhile, but it would be a new model and a new question.

A stronger adjacent idea would be to choose subsidies by type to make a preferred candidate win robustly across equilibria. That has a meaningful population interpretation, and could be studied with finite cost classes. But the paper does not study interventions. Moreover, the win probabilities at issue depend on finite-\(N\) sampling fluctuations near a tie: replacing the sampled electorate with deterministic continuum masses removes that fluctuation. Keeping the \(N\)-indexed sampling model preserves the probability question, but again leaves the paper’s existing distributional model in place. This could support a separate computational project; it does not supply the missing mirror.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed anchor and against claiming that this paper contains an eligible computational result. It is weaker as a universal claim: a carefully defined intervention or finite-type equilibrium problem could be worthwhile. The honest limit of the case is that such a problem would be a new extension, not a continuization of a computational result in this paper.
