What the mirror covers

The proposed setting does not mirror a qualifying computational result; Theorems 1 and 2 are the named candidates, while the other results likewise concern characterizations, dynamics, or aggregation principles.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a plausible population regime, but this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results characterize stability and convergence; none classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard.

The closest anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: on a connected bipartite board–director graph, for any initial nonempty, closed, convex opinions in the belief simplex and any continuous revision functions satisfying the paper’s Pareto conditions, repeated revision converges to a shared singleton belief in the relative interior of the initial opinions’ convex hull. Theorem 1, also proved here, characterizes stable opinions on a connected graph: they are stable exactly when everyone holds the same unambiguous belief. These are analytic results, not complexity results.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large federation built from many repeated board–director modules, with a small number of complete role and network types. A type would include an agent’s role, connections, production environment, and revision rule; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s population share. But the paper does not analyze such a population limit or compute a solution to a computational problem over it. Its network nodes are finite, and the fact that beliefs are sets in a continuous simplex does not continuize the population.

One could propose a new problem about computing consensus in a type-distribution model, but it would be a follow-on question, not a mirror anchored by a named computational result in this paper. That absence is the case’s weakest point, and it prevents me from honestly supplying the required anchor-backed continuous problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes stable opinions, and Theorem 2 proves convergence under assumptions on the revision functions. Neither asserts the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent identifies this gap too: a problem about computing consensus in a continuous population would be a follow-on question, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 1 is the clearest case. Its conclusion is a geometric characterization: on a connected finite graph, stability means everyone holds the same singleton belief. A type-distribution version would need to preserve the graph property that drives the result. Grouping agents by role or module may erase the links that make the graph connected; in a growing federation, a few bridge directors can keep each finite graph connected while their share vanishes in the limit. Including each agent’s exact memberships in the type description preserves those links, but can make the support size \(\tau\) grow with the population. One can instead posit a finite type-level interaction network with positive cross-type mixing, but that adds a substantive network model the theorem does not establish.

Theorem 2 does not give a cleaner computational anchor. It allows arbitrary continuous revision functions and guarantees convergence without specifying an algorithm or a convergence rate. To ask for a computable answer, one must give those functions an effective finite representation. Restricting them to linear updates on finitely many types produces a consensus calculation on a small matrix, essentially the familiar DeGroot or Markov-chain problem. Allowing richer nonlinear updates could yield a new problem, but its computational content would come from the added representation and interaction assumptions, not from a population continuization of the theorem. Corollaries 1 and 2 and Theorems 3 and 4 inherit the same gap.

A large federation of repeated board–director modules is a plausible high-multiplicity story, so I would not claim that no continuous model could be worthwhile. The negative case is narrower: the paper supplies no named computational result for such a mirror to anchor, and the more promising formulations require new assumptions that determine what can be computed. That leaves room for a good follow-on project, but not a strong case that this paper itself offers a worthwhile continuous computational mirror.
