The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a continuous-population version of STV coalition manipulation with weighted votes. One caveat: this article is a survey and contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition. So it has no anchor that can satisfy the requested numbering rule. Its clearest relevant claim is unnumbered prose in “Manipulation” and “Weighted Votes”: STV manipulation is NP-hard even with a bounded number of outcomes when votes are weighted, attributed to Conitzer and Sandholm (2002). Walsh reports that result; he does not prove it here. I will use it as the best available article-level anchor, without inventing a theorem number.

As the article presents it, the hardness concerns the decision problem of whether a strategic coalition can cast votes so that its desired candidate wins under STV, when vote weights are integers and the number of candidates is bounded. The surrounding discussion describes manipulation as coalition voting. This is a hardness anchor, and the continuous question it suggests is whether that hardness survives when a coalition is divisible into masses.

Continuous STV Coalition Manipulation. Fix a constant candidate bound \(m_0\), STV, and a public deterministic tie-breaking rule. An instance gives a candidate set \(C\), a target \(c^\star\), and rational masses of voters by role and true ranking: sincere voters have mass \(\mu_t\), and strategic voters have mass \(\nu_t\), where every strategic voter’s true ranking puts \(c^\star\) first. The masses sum to \(1\), and the support lists only positive-mass types. Ask whether the strategic population can report ballots that make \(c^\star\) win. A solution is a rational allocation \(y_{t,r}\geq 0\), assigning mass of strategic type \(t\) to reported ranking \(r\), with \(\sum_r y_{t,r}=\nu_t\), such that STV elects \(c^\star\) on the resulting ballot masses. There is no cost objective: as in the underlying manipulation problem, the question is whether a successful report exists.

A plausible regime is a large association, union, or membership body voting among a few nominees. Many members share the same complete preference ranking and the same strategic status; a large coordinated bloc wants its preferred nominee elected. There may be many voters but at most \(m_0!\) ranking types per role, so the number of voters can be much larger than the support size. Treating the bloc’s report as a mass allocation is faithful to the aggregation question: its members can distribute themselves among reported rankings, and the election counts the resulting shares.

I expect this fixed-candidate continuous problem to be tractable. For any proposed STV elimination order, each round’s tally is linear in the allocation variables \(y_{t,r}\): a ballot contributes its mass to its highest-ranked surviving candidate. The proposed order can therefore be checked with linear inequalities, including the fixed tie-breaking rule. With \(m\leq m_0\), there are only constantly many elimination orders to consider; feasibility for each can be decided by linear programming. This is an argument about the underlying manipulation question, not a proposal to study an encoding. It also explains what the mirror tests: weighted-vote hardness may depend on indivisible integer allocations even where the number of preference types is bounded.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the article’s bounded-candidate, weighted-vote STV manipulation claim, not its results on CP-nets, preference elicitation, or other voting rules. It raises further questions about the continuous/discrete relationship: how accurately can a feasible mass allocation be rounded to a finite coalition, what happens near ties, and does tractability persist when the candidate bound is removed or manipulation has prices or costs?

The weakest point is the anchor itself: the survey gives neither a theorem number nor the full formal details of the cited STV variant. Those details would need checking against Conitzer and Sandholm’s paper before claiming an exact match to their reduction. The substantive vulnerability is that a continuous coalition can split mass across reports in ways a finite coalition cannot. That is precisely the relaxation being studied, but it may erase the combinatorics that made the discrete problem hard. It makes this a plausible mirror of the manipulation question, not a claim that Walsh’s survey establishes a continuous analogue or that the continuous and discrete answers must agree.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this is a survey, not the paper that proves the hardness result. Walsh gives no numbered theorem or formal input definition here; he reports that STV manipulation remains NP-hard with weighted votes and a bounded number of outcomes, citing Conitzer and Sandholm. The proposed mirror fills in several details the survey does not specify: a target-first coalition, a particular division into sincere and strategic types, freely divisible reports, and deterministic tie-breaking. That makes it hard to claim this is a continuous mirror of a result established in this paper. A more exact study should anchor itself in the cited primary work.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the question. A large membership body choosing among a few nominees is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Its ranking types are finite, and STV’s outcome depends on their aggregate mass rather than voter identities. For fixed \(m\), each proposed elimination order gives linear tally constraints on the report masses \(y_{t,r}\), with \(\sum_r y_{t,r}=\nu_t\). There are only constantly many orders, so checking feasibility by linear programming is credible. The resulting question—whether weighted STV manipulation’s hardness depends on indivisible coalition weights—is both well-posed and close to the survey’s stated computational interests.

I can make a case against attributing that question to Walsh’s paper as a result of its own. I cannot honestly use that to establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the anchor survives once it is attached to the cited manipulation problem, and the supplied material gives no fundamental modeling obstruction to it.
