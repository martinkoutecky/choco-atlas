The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an eligible computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 proves that worker-quasi-stable matchings form a lattice; Lemmas 1–2 establish closure and give the join; Theorem 2 gives the re-equilibration operator’s properties; Theorem 3 proves existence and a lattice structure for stable matchings; and Theorem 4 characterizes the operator’s fixed point under the law of aggregate demand. These are structural results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified.

The setting could plausibly have a high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large labor market with many workers drawn from a modest number of complete preference types. But that makes a continuous extension *sensible*; it does not supply the named computational result this task requires. The operator’s termination follows from finiteness, with no complexity bound, and the paper does not specify an input encoding for computing its choice functions. So a continuous algorithmic question here would be a new research direction, not an honest mirror of one of the paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not supplied a computational anchor to preserve. Theorem 1 and Lemmas 1–2 establish a lattice and its join; Theorems 2–3 establish properties of an operator and the stable set; Theorem 4 identifies a fixed point under LAD. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Calling the paper’s finite iteration an algorithm does not supply a complexity result: the paper gives no input encoding for firms’ choice functions or running-time bound.

A large labor market with many workers sharing preference and firm-relevant types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But that only makes a continuous model plausible. One could define a mass version of the quasi-stable lattice and ask whether a mass re-equilibration process reaches stability; that would extend the paper’s structural or dynamical results. Or one could ask how efficiently to compute a stable mass matching, but that would be a new computational question. For the paper’s general substitutable choice functions, even stating that question computationally requires an input representation; restricting to a compact family such as responsive preferences supplies one, but still does not turn any theorem here into a complexity result.

So none of the named results offers a ChoCo mirror as framed. The negative case is strong against treating this paper as a source of computational-complexity mirrors. It is weaker as a claim that no continuous study of these markets could be worthwhile: such a study may be valuable in matching theory, but it would pursue a different contribution.
