The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I can’t make a valid case that it proves a complexity result whose continuous mirror should be studied.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here. For an odd committee with each voter’s preferred alternative equally likely, one initial vote, zero money endowment, risk neutrality, rationing by voter \(R_1\), and strictly ordered valuations \(v_1<\cdots<v_n\), it proves that there is a finite threshold \(\lambda_n\geq 1\) such that, if \(v_n\geq\lambda_n v_{n-1}\), an ex ante vote-trading equilibrium exists. Its price is \(p^*=v_{n-1}/(n+1)\); voters \(1,\ldots,n-2\) sell, voter \(n\) demands \((n-1)/2\) votes, and voter \(n-1\) sells with probability \(2/(n+1)\) and demands \((n-1)/2\) votes with probability \((n-1)/(n+1)\). That is a constructive equilibrium-existence theorem, not a result that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. Proposition 1 gives a named welfare comparison, but it is not a computational result either.

The most plausible follow-up would be a high-multiplicity limit of ex ante vote-market equilibrium. Consider a large recurring association deciding a binary issue, with many members drawn from a fixed set of valuation and information types: the number of members grows while the number of types stays small. Given a rational type distribution \(\mu\), replicate its masses to form committees of size \(N\), preserve the paper’s private preference draws and \(R_1\) rationing, and ask whether nontrivial equilibria exist for large \(N\), and what limits their prices, vote allocations, and welfare have. I would expect the answer to be continuum-specific: repeated top valuations and the loss of finite-committee pivotality can change the equilibrium construction. This is a precise research direction, but it is not a computational classification result from the paper, and I would not present it as a valid anchor under the requested standard.

That is also the weakest point of the positive case. The paper’s construction relies on distinct individual valuations and finite-market rationing; a regime with many copies of each type removes the unique top-ranked individuals its result uses. The authors explicitly distinguish their finite market, with aggregate uncertainty, from continuum-agent markets that clear exactly in expectation. And the “continuous” auction in the experiment refers to the trading mechanism, not to a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 explicitly constructs an ex ante equilibrium under special assumptions, including \(p^*=v_{n-1}/(n+1)\); Propositions 2 and 3 extend that construction, and Proposition 1 compares welfare using a closed-form expression. None classifies the complexity of an input problem. One could ask for an algorithm to find equilibria in a broader family of vote markets, but that would introduce a new problem rather than mirror a computational result here.

The strongest proposed mirror is still the right one to examine: take a large committee whose members come from a fixed finite set of complete types, such as pairs \((v,a)\) of valuation and preferred alternative, and study the limit of the market equilibrium. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; grouping voters who share those attributes is not itself a distortion. The difficulty is that the theorem’s market power belongs to particular individuals. It relies on distinct highest and second-highest valuations and makes one voter dictator. With repeated types, those order statistics belong to many indistinguishable people. Treating the entire highest-valuation type as one buyer would make it a coordinated bloc, which changes the paper’s decentralized individual trading model.

In the standard atomless limit, that distinction matters economically: a single voter’s action has measure zero, so it cannot change the aggregate vote or make that voter pivotal. Independent private preference draws and mixed orders also average out, removing the finite market’s aggregate uncertainty; the paper itself contrasts its setting with continuum markets that clear exactly in expectation. Its \(R_1\) rule, which randomly excludes whole voters, has no direct counterpart for infinitesimal agents. A type-mass version could instead let cohorts trade or coordinate, but then the strategic players are blocs rather than the paper’s voters. Preserving finite pivotality means retaining finite committees; preserving mass-level strategic influence means changing the actors or adding coordination or aggregate shocks.

Proposition 1 does not rescue the mirror. A continuous valuation distribution could support a question about limiting welfare, but the paper’s welfare comparison is downstream of its finite-agent equilibrium construction. Assuming a dictator in the continuum and comparing that outcome with majority rule would yield an aggregate welfare calculation, not a result about the paper’s market generating that dictator. Computing equilibria or welfare in a newly specified type-bloc market could be worthwhile in its own right, but it would not be this paper’s continuous computational mirror. The experiment’s “continuous” auction describes the trading mechanism, not a continuous society.

So the negative case is strong as a ChoCo fit: the paper’s named results are analytical equilibrium and welfare results, and the natural population limit removes the individual pivotality on which their market mechanism rests. It is weaker as a literal universal claim: one can invent a related bloc-market model that merits study, but that is a new strategic model, not a continuization of a computational question established by this paper.
