The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of complete utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(ms\), \(m\) teams of mass \(s\), pairwise utilities \(u_{t,t'}\), and rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), compute an allocation maximizing \(\alpha W(x)-(1-\alpha)R(x)\). Here \(x_{t,k}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to team \(k\), each unit of population belongs to one team, and \(R(x)\) is the greatest total uplift of any mass-\(s\) coalition whose members are all weakly better off after leaving their current teams. Determine the exact and approximation complexity of this optimization problem as a function of the input size and parameters.

The model it lives in

An atomless workforce with finitely many complete utility types and rational masses is partitioned into \(m\) teams, each of mass \(s\). The allocation \(x\) determines each type’s utility in its team and total welfare \(W(x)\); \(R(x)\) is the maximum uplift among individually rational breakaway coalitions of mass \(s\). The objective is \(\alpha W(x)-(1-\alpha)R(x)\).

The objection that survived

CTF-MU permits fractional type compositions within teams and breakaway coalitions, while the paper uses indivisible groups of exactly \(s\) workers; the paper establishes no bound on how that changes the optimum or its relation to finite instances.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the formal TFP objective and coalition-uplift model anchored by Theorem 1. It leaves the paper’s algorithmic implementations, computational experiments, and comparisons with benchmark heuristics without a direct analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the exact, approximation, and parameterized complexity bounds in \(m\), the number of types \(\tau\), \(s\), and the input bit length?
  • How does allowing fractional type compositions in teams and coalitions compare with finite instances that preserve integral team configurations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s team formation problem itself, with a large workforce described by masses of complete preference types. Imagine a large organization that repeatedly forms equal-sized project teams. Workers in one type have the same skills and the same utility for every partner type; the number of workers \(n=ms\) is large, while the number of such types \(\tau\) is moderate. A type must include every feature the problem uses, including preferences and relevant constraints. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that all team formation has one.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says model (SL) is a valid formulation of the TFP: assignment variables form equal-sized teams; pair variables identify which workers share a team; utility variables record each worker’s realized utility; and coalition constraints make \(r\) at least the uplift of every individually rational coalition of size \(s\). The objective maximizes \(\alpha\) times total utility minus \((1-\alpha)r\). The paper proves this formulation correct and computes with it in its branch-and-cut algorithm. This is a formulation result, not a P-versus-NP classification. The introduction’s NP-hardness statement for utility maximization is not a numbered result, so I would not present it as my anchor.

Here is the continuous problem I would ask a prover to study, Continuous Team Formation with Maximum Uplift (CTF-MU). Its input is a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=ms\), \(m\) teams of mass \(s\), rational nonnegative utilities \(u_{t,t'}\), and a rational weight \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). Equivalently, the normalized population shares are \(\mu_t/(ms)\). The population of each type is atomless, so a type’s mass can be divided among teams while each person is assigned to exactly one team.

A solution is a measurable partition \(A_1,\dots,A_m\) of the population with \(\nu(A_k)=s\) for every team. It can be represented by masses \(x_{t,k}\), where \(0\le x_{t,k}\), \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,k}=s\). A type-\(t\) person assigned to team \(k\) has utility
\[ v_{t,k}=\sum_{t'\in T}u_{t,t'}x_{t',k}, \]
and total efficiency is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,k}x_{t,k}v_{t,k}\).

A possible breakaway coalition is any measurable subset \(S\) of mass \(s\). Write \(y_{t,k}\) for the amount of type \(t\) selected from team \(k\), so \(0\le y_{t,k}\le x_{t,k}\) and \(\sum_{t,k}y_{t,k}=s\). Its type-\(t\) members receive \(v_t(y)=\sum_{t'}u_{t,t'}\sum_k y_{t',k}\). The coalition is individually rational when \(y_{t,k}>0\) implies \(v_t(y)\ge v_{t,k}\). Let \(R(x)\) be the maximum, over such coalitions, of
\[ \sum_{t,k}y_{t,k}\bigl(v_t(y)-v_{t,k}\bigr). \]
CTF-MU asks for a feasible \(x\) maximizing \(\alpha W(x)-(1-\alpha)R(x)\). The solution is the team allocation; a maximizing coalition can be supplied as a witness for its instability.

This is recognizably the authors’ question: it retains interpersonal utility, equal-sized teams, and the maximum individually rational coalition uplift. The type masses make the large population the continuous object. They also make the answer fractional at the type level while each person still belongs to one team. I would expect Class C for the unrestricted problem: the quadratic interactions and conditional coalition constraints remain after replacing named workers with type masses, so continuization does not by itself produce an LP. That is a forecast, not a result of this paper. A main follow-up is whether restrictions such as fixed \(s\), moderate \(\tau\), or structured type utilities make uplift separation or the full optimization tractable. Another is to prove a rounding or approximation relationship between this problem and its finite high-multiplicity version.

The weak point is that teams in the paper are small groups of indivisible workers, while CTF-MU permits splitting a type mass across teams. An author could reasonably ask whether this changes which coalitions can form. I think the mirror survives because the continuum assignment still gives every unit of population exactly one team and retains the same equal-mass coalition and uplift test. But the equivalence to discrete team formation, including how much fractional splitting changes the optimum, is an essential open question rather than something Theorem 1 settles.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only named anchor, Theorem 1, proves that model (SL) correctly represents the finite team formation problem. It proves no complexity bound or algorithmic guarantee. CTF-MU’s central question—how hard it is to optimize over a continuous society—is therefore not a continuous version of Theorem 1’s result. It is a new complexity question motivated by the paper’s model and algorithms.

There is a more basic issue with the proposed CTF-MU. The paper forms teams of exactly \(s\) discrete workers. CTF-MU gives each team mass \(s\) from an atomless population, so a team can contain fractional amounts of types, and its breakaway coalitions can do the same. That changes which teams and coalitions exist. With fixed \(s\), the more faithful high-multiplicity model would keep each team’s type counts integral—each configuration \(q\) has \(\sum_t q_t=s\)—and let a continuous mass of teams use each configuration. Its coalition test would also preserve integral type counts. That repairs the mismatch, but it is no longer the mirror the proponent specified, and its useful algorithmic properties are open questions rather than consequences of Theorem 1.

This objection has a limit. A large organization repeatedly forming small teams from workers in a moderate number of complete preference types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Keeping teams discrete while treating the supply of workers in each type as continuous could be a worthwhile mirror of the paper’s computational problem. The paper’s pairwise utilities make that regime restrictive: workers grouped into one type must be indistinguishable in every preference the model uses. But an alternative setting with type-level preferences could meet that condition, and the paper’s discussion of skill constraints gives some basis for it.

So the case against is strongest against the stated anchor and formulation: Theorem 1 does not supply the computational result the proposed question purports to mirror, and CTF-MU alters finite-team composition and deviation. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The repaired configuration-based version remains plausible, and the honest negative case is weak against that version.
