What the mirror covers

The population restatement covers Theorem 1's acyclic-majority guarantee for rankings compatible with a fixed tiling; it does not cover the paper's other structural results or its extremal size counterexample.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper has a natural continuous-population reading, but no qualifying computational anchor. None of its numbered results classifies a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or otherwise. For example, Theorem 1, proved here, says that for every rhombus tiling \(T\), the compatible orders \(\Sigma(T)\) form a complete Condorcet domain. That is a structural aggregation result, not a complexity result. The paper’s counterexample to Fishburn’s conjecture is about the sizes of domains, not computational complexity.

The nearest continuous analogue is precise. Fix a tiling \(T\); let voter types be the complete rankings \(\sigma\in\Sigma(T)\), and let \(\mu_\sigma\geq 0\) be their masses, summing to one. Ask whether the weighted pairwise-majority relation induced by \(\mu\) can contain a strict cycle. Theorem 1 implies it cannot: if real masses produced a strict cycle, a sufficiently close rational distribution would preserve its three strict majority inequalities, and scaling those rational masses would give an integer \(\Sigma(T)\)-opinion with a cycle, contradicting the theorem.

That is a sensible continuous reading of the paper’s result, and large electorates with few distinct rankings make the population interpretation plausible. But it does not yield the requested computational mirror: the paper studies which sets of rankings form large Condorcet domains, rather than a computational task over voter populations. I would present this as a scope-adjacent extension, not as an anchored positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continue. Theorem 1 and the other results characterize Condorcet domains, their structure, and their sizes. The counterexample to Fishburn’s conjecture is an extremal combinatorics result. None gives an algorithmic problem with an input, output, and complexity claim.

The proponent’s Theorem 1 mirror is well-posed and genuinely about a population: give each ranking in \(\Sigma(T)\) a mass and ask whether strict pairwise majorities can cycle. But its computational task is just to sum pairwise tallies and check the resulting graph for cycles, in polynomial time in the support size, number of alternatives, and bit length. The paper contributes a structural guarantee about those tallies, not a computational challenge. A real-mass version of the guarantee follows by approximating strict inequalities with rational masses; that is a natural mathematical extension, but still not a complexity result.

The strongest upgrade is to give a society distribution \(\mu\) and ask for a tiling CD capturing as much of it as possible:
\[ \max_T \sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(T)}\mu_\sigma. \]
This could be a worthwhile weighted domain-selection problem. With uniform mass over all rankings, it reduces to the paper’s maximum-size question after scaling by \(n!\). With nonuniform mass, it adds a new objective and an instance distribution the paper does not study. It may be a good follow-on question, but it is not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed anchor and any direct mirror: the population interpretation is sensible, but the paper’s results do not pose a computational problem for that population. The universal claim is weaker. Weighted domain selection is a plausible new project, and I cannot rule out that the authors would recognize it as part of their agenda. Its case for this paper, though, rests on extending the agenda rather than continuizing a computational result the paper actually establishes.
