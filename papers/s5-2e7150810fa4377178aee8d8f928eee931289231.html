The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(S\subseteq\mathfrak S(C)\) of ranking types, and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\rho\) summing to \(1\), compute a ranking \(\pi\in\mathfrak S(C)\) minimizing \(\sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho\,\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\rho)\), or decide whether that minimum is at most a supplied rational threshold.

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete ranking \(\rho\) with population mass \(\mu_\rho\). Choose one consensus ranking \(\pi\) to minimize population-average Kendall–Tau loss. For a common denominator \(Q\), the score parameter corresponds to the scaled total loss \(Q\sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho\,\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\rho)\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity Kemeny consensus problem. Imagine a large electorate ranking a fixed, small slate of candidates. A type is one complete ranking \(\rho\); its mass \(\mu_\rho\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. With \(n\) people but only \(\tau\) distinct rankings, \(n\) can be far larger than \(\tau\). The decision is still exactly the paper’s decision: choose one consensus ranking, and measure its loss by the population’s average Kendall–Tau distance. This is a natural population model of the paper’s own formal problem, not continuity in the outcome space.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this work. It states that the algorithm \(s\)-kconsens computes Kemeny Score in \(O(1.5079^k + m^2 n)\) time, where \(m\) is the number of candidates, \(n\) the number of input votes, and \(k\) the Kemeny score. The paper also says its algorithms compute an optimal score and a corresponding consensus list.

The corresponding problem is Scaled Continuous Kemeny Score. An instance gives a candidate set \(C\), a finite support \(S\) of complete rankings with \(|S|=\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_\rho>0\) summing to one, and an integer \(Q\) such that \(Q\mu_\rho\) is integral for each \(\rho\in S\). For a proposed consensus \(\pi\), define its population loss by
\[ \operatorname{loss}_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{\rho\in S}\mu_\rho\,\operatorname{KT}(\pi,\rho). \]
Given a budget \(b\), decide whether some \(\pi\in\mathfrak S(C)\) has \(Q\operatorname{loss}_\mu(\pi)\le b\), and return such a ranking if it exists. The optimization version returns a minimum-loss ranking and its score.

I expect the parameterized version to be tractable, with \(b\) as the parameter. The direct bridge is to give each ranking \(\rho\) exactly \(Q\mu_\rho\) copies: then \(Q\operatorname{loss}_\mu(\pi)\) is the ordinary Kemeny score. I expect the paper’s dirty-set branching proof to extend to these aggregated integer type counts, giving a bound of the form \(O(1.5079^b\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L))\), where \(L\) is the bit length of the masses. That compressed-input bound is a proposed mirror result, not a theorem proved in the paper; simply expanding the electorate gives the paper’s \(m^2 n\) term with \(n=Q\).

There is a real limitation: \(b\) scales with the common denominator \(Q\). If average disagreement stays constant as the population grows, this parameter can grow with the population too. So this mirror carries over the paper’s score-parameterized tractability, but does not establish an efficient algorithm in the normalized score alone. The paper also states in prose, citing earlier work, that Kemeny Score is NP-complete, even with four input rankings. Those are relevant expectations for the unrestricted problem, but they are not numbered results in this paper and so are not my formal anchors. The unrestricted mirror should be expected to retain hardness; its combinatorics lie in the candidate agenda \(m\), not just in the number of people.

A narrower secondary anchor is Theorem 2, cited from [BGKN09], not proved here: Kemeny Score without majority-dirty pairs is solvable in polynomial time. A pair is majority-dirty when neither orientation occurs in more than \(2/3\) of the votes. Its continuous mirror, Two-Thirds-Structured Continuous Kemeny, has the same distribution input and promises that for every candidate pair, one orientation has mass greater than \(2/3\). It asks for a minimum-loss consensus ranking and its score. The paper says every Kemeny consensus in this regime orders every pair according to that \(2/3\)-majority. I therefore expect the continuous problem to be polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\): compute the pairwise masses and return the ranking fixed by the promise. A natural follow-up is whether the general problem is fixed-parameter tractable in the number of pairs that fail this promise.

These mirrors cover the paper’s central Kemeny aggregation objective and one structurally tractable special case. They do not attempt to mirror the experiments or every parameterization. The weakest point is the scaling in the lead result: its score parameter is a total integer disagreement cost, not a per-capita quantity, so its algorithm may be of limited use at large population scales. The paper’s own sports and search-engine examples also are not themselves large electorates. The positive case rests instead on a different, plausible regime that preserves the authors’ problem exactly: many agents, a fixed candidate set, complete preference rankings, a single consensus ranking, and Kendall–Tau loss.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the lead mirror is a scale mismatch. For a distribution \(\mu\), let \(\ell(\pi)=\sum_\rho \mu_\rho\,\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\rho)\). If \(Q\) is a common denominator for the masses, the paper’s score parameter becomes \(k=Q\ell(\pi)\). At a fixed distribution, \(\ell\) stays constant as the represented population grows, while \(k\) grows with it. And \(Q\) can be exponential in the bit length of the rational masses. So Theorem 1’s bound transfers as \(O(1.5079^{Qb}+\operatorname{poly}(m,Q))\) for a normalized threshold \(b\); it does not yield the proposed efficient algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\). Its branching argument also relies on each disagreement costing at least one integer unit, a guarantee lost under fractional masses.

A more careful mirror would take rational type masses directly and ask for an exact Kemeny ranking, perhaps with complexity measured in \(m,\tau,L\). That is a coherent computational question. Its objective, however, depends only on pairwise masses \(w_{ab}=\sum_{\rho:\,a\succ_\rho b}\mu_\rho\). The resulting problem is weighted linear ordering over those pairwise coefficients. This formulation avoids expanding the electorate, but it does not inherit the paper’s score-parameterized result. The case against is that the proposed payoff would require a separate weighted-Kemeny theory, rather than a useful continuization of the theorem’s algorithm. That weakens the claimed mirror; it does not show the weighted problem is unworthy.

Theorem 2 offers even less support for a negative verdict. It is attributed to [BGKN09] and used here as a preprocessing result, so it is not an independent contribution of this manuscript. Its continuous version is still well-posed: if every pair has an orientation with mass above \(2/3\), that relation must be transitive, and the ranking follows from the pairwise masses. I can question its value as a separate anchor, but its simplicity is not a sound reason to dismiss the question.

I can’t honestly make the universal case the prompt asks for. A large electorate with a small set of recurring rankings is a plausible instance regime, and weighted Kemeny over its distribution is an exact continuous-population problem. The strongest objection is that the paper’s particular FPT guarantee loses its useful score scale under normalization; that does not rule out a worthwhile mirror or a new result for the weighted problem. The negative case is therefore weak at the point that matters most.
