The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\), a finite support of \(\tau\) stakeholder utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to one, and access to each type's representative utility \(u_t:S^{d-1}_+\to\{0,1\}\), what communication or query complexity is required to return an exact maximizer of \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t(x)\) over \(x\in S^{d-1}_+\)?

The model it lives in

A stakeholder population is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete utility-function types. Each type's representative answers queries about \(u_t\); the decision variable is a normalized outcome profile \(x\in S^{d-1}_+\), and the objective is mass-weighted welfare \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t(x)\).

The objection that survived

The paper's 22-person study does not establish that a large stakeholder community has few exact utility types; the proponent flags this gap but offers only a hypothetical constituency regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1's arbitrary-monotone-utility welfare lower bound and the per-type extensions of Proposition 3 and Corollary 2. It leaves the empirical elicitation comparisons, user-study results, and transplant-policy simulation results without a population-complexity mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the exact communication or query complexity depend on \(\tau\) and nonuniform rational masses?
  • What approximation guarantees remain possible for arbitrary monotone utilities, and how do they change under the paper's linear-utility restriction?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a real, bounded mirror here. The population should be the stakeholder community whose values the allocation policy is meant to serve. A type is a complete utility function over system-level outcome profiles; its mass is the fraction of that community sharing the function. For example, a national transplant-policy consultation might represent large patient-and-caregiver and clinical constituencies as two shared preference types. The number of people could be very large while the number of types stays small.

My lead is the mirror of Proposition 1, “Exponential lower bound for arbitrary monotone utilities” (proved in this paper; its proof uses a set-disjointness lower bound cited from prior work). The proposition says that, for every even \(d\), any protocol that receives two coordinate-wise monotone utilities \(u_1,u_2:\mathbb R_{\ge0}^d\to\{0,1\}\) and outputs an exact maximizer of \(u_1(x)+u_2(x)\) over \(S^{d-1}_+\), with probability at least \(2/3\), needs \(2^{\Omega(d)}\) bits of communication in the worst case. The paper also derives an exponential lower bound for ternary comparison-based elicitation.

The continuous problem I would ask is Mass-Monotone Stakeholder Welfare Maximization. An instance gives \(d\), a finite support of \(\tau\) stakeholder utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and access to each type’s utility function \(u_t:S^{d-1}_+\to\{0,1\}\), which is coordinate-wise monotone. In the communication version, each type’s utility is held by its representative; in the elicitation version, the representative answers pairwise queries. The question is to return an exact maximizer of
\[ U_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t u_t(x),\qquad x\in S^{d-1}_+. \]
A solution is such a maximizing profile, with communication or query cost measured in \(d,\tau\), and the mass encoding length \(L\).

The proposition applies already when \(\tau=2\) and \(\mu_1=\mu_2=\tfrac12\): scaling the sum by one half does not change its maximizers. Repeating each type across a large constituency leaves the same objective. I therefore expect the exact problem to retain the paper’s exponential communication barrier; this is a hardness-transfer style boundary, not a claim of NP-hardness. The hardness is driven by the number of objectives \(d\), not by population size. Further questions include whether additive approximation or restrictions on the feasible policy frontier evade the lower bound, and how the bound changes for more than two utility types. This is a direct mirror of the paper’s utilitarian objective, though its lower bound is about eliciting arbitrary preferences, not the transplant optimizer itself.

A second, tractable mirror comes from the paper’s hybrid elicitation algorithm. Proposition 3, “Weight approximation for Algorithm 1” (proved here) says that for a true linear preference direction \(u^*\in S^{d-1}_+\), Algorithm 1 returns \(\hat u\) with \(\|\hat u-u^*\|_2\le\epsilon\). Corollary 2, “Query complexity of Algorithm 1” (also proved here) says its expected number of queries is at most
\[ O\!\left(d^2\log^2(1/\epsilon)\log\!\bigl(d\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr)\right)+T, \]
where \(T\) is the ACCPM query budget.

The corresponding problem is Finite-Support Linear Community Elicitation. The instance gives \(d\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) on \(\tau\) types, and for each type an unknown linear preference direction \(u_t\in S^{d-1}_+\), accessible through the paper’s ternary comparison oracle. The population’s utility is \(\bar U(x)=\langle x,\bar u\rangle\), where \(\bar u=\sum_t\mu_tu_t\). The task is to return \(\hat{\bar u}\) whose utility error is at most \(\epsilon\) for every \(x\in S^{d-1}_+\). Run Algorithm 1 once per type and return \(\sum_t\mu_t\hat u_t\). Proposition 3 gives the guarantee, since the weighted aggregate error is at most \(\sum_t\mu_t\epsilon=\epsilon\); Corollary 2 gives expected query cost \(O(\tau[d^2\log^2(1/\epsilon)\log(d\log(1/\epsilon))+T])\). This is tractable in the comparison-oracle model when \(\tau\) is moderate. It is the more direct mirror of the paper’s practical contribution: its user-level elicitation and arithmetic-mean aggregation extend naturally to a mass distribution over repeated preference types.

The regime for both questions is a large stakeholder electorate with a small number of shared, formally stated preference types—not a claim that patients themselves are interchangeable in every respect. The continuous population is the stakeholder population; the paper’s continuous outcome/query domain \(S^{d-1}_+\) is not itself the continuization. Nor does the paper already model this population continuously: its study has 22 respondents. Its use of a mean utility is strong evidence that a mass-weighted version is a sensible extension, not a novelty collision.

My weakest point is the regime assumption. The paper does not show that a large transplant-policy community can be represented by a small number of exact utility types; its study is small, and people’s linear weights may vary. The algorithmic mirror also requires access to a representative query oracle for each type. The hardness mirror is firmer as a formal embedding, but its lower bound survives even with two types and therefore does not show that high multiplicity itself causes hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the first anchor is already the same optimization problem in mass notation. Proposition 1 optimizes \(u_1(x)+u_2(x)\) over \(S^{d-1}_+\). For any two positive masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2\) summing to one, the proposed society maximizes \(\mu_1u_1(x)+\mu_2u_2(x)\). The lower-bound construction still works: if the utility sets intersect, some profile has welfare \(1\); if they do not, every profile has welfare at most \(\max\{\mu_1,\mu_2\}<1\). An exact optimizer therefore lets the two holders of the utilities detect intersection. The paper’s communication lower bound already resolves this two-type population case, and duplicating either constituency does not change the objective. Approximation with sufficiently small error inherits the same gap. Restricting the feasible profiles could change the problem, but that would no longer be the paper’s frontier-agnostic query domain. The submitted hard mirror adds no open result at its core.

The linear-elicitation mirror is sound as a formal extension, but its algorithm is a direct sum. Give each of \(\tau\) known types its own comparison oracle, run Algorithm 1 on each, and combine the estimates using the supplied masses. The mass-weighted error bound follows immediately from the single-user guarantee; mass does not change which queries are asked or how the algorithm works. A mass-aware query budget could avoid spending much effort on rare types, but it is still a separable allocation of per-type elicitation effort, not a new population-level optimization problem.

That direct-sum account also depends on having the type groups and a representative oracle for each group already available. In a real stakeholder community, a type is a complete preference vector. With continuously varying linear weights, exact repeats need not be common, so the support can grow with the number of people. If groups are stipulated to share exactly the same utility, one representative can answer for each group—but then the scenario is a small set of known constituencies, not evidence that the paper’s elicitation task has a useful high-multiplicity regime. Treating nearby weights as one type might be useful, but it changes the guarantee and needs a separate approximation model.

A better proposal would sample stakeholders from an unknown distribution and estimate the population’s aggregate utility. That could be a worthwhile research question. It requires new assumptions and analysis about sampling, heterogeneous preferences, and possibly noisy responses; Proposition 3 and Corollary 2 do not supply them. I would assess that as a distinct distributional preference-learning problem, rather than crediting it as a surviving mirror of the paper’s results.

So I would reject both submitted anchors as reasons to spend ChoCo effort here: one is already covered by the paper’s lower bound, and the other reduces to running its algorithm independently for each known type. I cannot honestly make the universal claim airtight. The unknown-distribution version is plausible, but its value would need to be argued on its own rather than inherited from these anchors.
