The manuscript provides no named computational result to mirror. It defines a score rule and gives a recipe for constructing its matrix from participatory-budgeting data, but states no formal computational problem with an algorithm or complexity result. The references to Cohen et al. and other work do not supply results proved by this paper. That leaves no anchor for a continuous mirror.

The strongest candidate is the paper’s stated aim of strategyproof voting among cloud tenants. A plausible population model gives each tenant type \(t\) a score ballot \(b_t\) and mass \(\mu_t\). With a fixed score matrix \(M\), the tally is \(q=\sum_t\mu_t b_t\), and the rule selects a maximizer of \(Mq\). Computing that winner is just aggregation and matrix multiplication; the paper offers no harder computational result about it. For strategyproofness, an individual tenant has zero mass, so changing their report leaves \(q\) and the winner unchanged. That makes individual incentives vacuous. Treating a positive-mass group’s deviation as the strategic act would require a new coalition-manipulation problem, with a utility model and permitted reports the manuscript never specifies. Preserving pivotal individuals instead retains finite agents and changes the population model.

The cloud and participatory-budgeting settings can plausibly have many tenants or voters per ballot type. That supports the possibility of useful future high-multiplicity work. But a richer cloud-allocation model would need to specify how resource constraints, workloads, and preferences enter the mechanism; this paper does not do so. The honest negative case is therefore strong for this manuscript: it gives no computational result to continuize, and its most natural strategic candidate degenerates or requires a newly defined problem. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise in any scenario is stronger than the paper supports.
