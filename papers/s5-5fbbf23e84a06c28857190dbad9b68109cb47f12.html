What the mirror covers

The proposed search concerns core-state existence related to Theorem 2; it provides no computational treatment of the equilibrium implications in Theorems 1 and 3 or the utility bound in Section 4, and no result is computationally anchored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper studies an unusually credible high-multiplicity regime. In its \(r\)th replica, there are \(rT\) agents but only \(T\) types, with each type’s population share fixed at \(1/T\). Agents of a type share their relevant characteristics, and cliques may overlap and grow to the size of the whole population. A continuous population with mass \(\mu_t\) on each type is therefore a natural limit to investigate. The paper itself emphasizes fixed type proportions and points toward relaxing its replication assumptions. That makes the existing high-multiplicity work evidence for the regime, not a novelty collision.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Section 2’s Theorem 1 says every \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium state is in the \(c(\varepsilon)\)-core. Section 4’s Theorem 1 bounds equal-treatment utilities across replications under desirability of wealth. Theorem 2 says that, under that assumption, for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is an \(r(\varepsilon)\) such that every replication with \(r>r(\varepsilon)\) has a nonempty \(c(\varepsilon)\)-core; its proof invokes results of Wooders. Theorem 3 says that a state in the \(c(\varepsilon)\)-core for every replication is an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium. These are equilibrium and existence results, not complexity classifications or algorithms. Theorem 2 is the closest candidate, but it does not qualify as an anchor.

A plausible follow-up mirror would be Mass-Network \(\varepsilon\)-Core Search. Its instance would specify a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), private-good endowments and utilities by type, a clique-production rule, and a communication-cost vector. Agents would form an atomless population \(I\), partitioned into measurable type sets of masses \(\mu_t\). A solution would be a feasible private-good allocation \(x(i)\) and a measurable network of overlapping cliques such that no measurable coalition can form its own feasible network and allocation, pay communication cost \(\varepsilon\,\lambda(B)\bar z\), and make all its members strictly better off. Feasibility would account for aggregate private-good use and the inputs required by the chosen cliques.

The paper gives reason to expect a corresponding nonemptiness result under suitable extensions of its assumptions. It gives no basis for predicting whether finding such a state is tractable, inherits discrete hardness, or is hard for continuum-specific reasons. That computational question also needs a finite representation for utilities and clique technology; the paper does not supply one. Further questions would include whether finite replica cores converge to the continuum core in both directions, and whether blocking coalitions or equilibrium prices can be computed efficiently.

The weakest point is that the paper’s agents care about their particular clique memberships, while cliques can overlap and scale with the population. Defining a faithful continuum network and encoding it for computation is substantial work. So the positive case is convincing that a continuous population is a sensible *economic* mirror of this model. It is not yet an anchor-led computational social-choice case: the paper offers no qualifying computational result, and the proposed search problem remains an extension rather than a restatement of one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that there is no computational result here to continuize. The paper proves economic existence and equivalence statements: Theorem 1 puts an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium in the \(c(\varepsilon)\)-core; Theorem 2 establishes eventual nonemptiness of that core across replications; Theorem 3 derives an equilibrium from a state that remains in the core under every replication. None specifies a computational problem, an input representation, or an algorithmic bound. In ChoCo’s terms, these are analytic results, not complexity anchors.

Theorem 2 is the best candidate for a mirror, but “search for a core state” does not yet define one. In the proposed continuum model, a solution includes an allocation over an atomless population and a network of overlapping cliques. The paper allows cliques of arbitrary size and utilities that depend on agents’ particular memberships. To ask how hard it is to find such a state, one needs a finite representation of those utilities, clique technologies, and networks. An unrestricted representation gives no ordinary computational input; a compact one must impose additional structure that may rule out the very overlapping, population-scale cliques the paper is designed to allow. Choosing that structure could yield a worthwhile new problem, but its complexity would be driven by the added representation and assumptions, not by a computational question posed or answered in this paper.

The other theorem candidates do not fare better: the equilibrium-to-core and core-to-equilibrium implications are characterizations, not search or decision procedures. Their continuous counterparts would extend the economic theory. A later algorithmic study could build on that extension, but that would be a new research direction rather than a mirror of a named computational result.

There is no sound negative case based on lack of multiplicity. The replica economies have fixed type proportions, repeated agents, and cliques that may grow with the population; the paper even motivates global organizations as an application. That makes a continuous population economically plausible. It is also not a novelty objection that related continuum or high-multiplicity work exists. The narrower point is that a plausible continuous economic model does not supply the missing computational problem.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope, though not as a universal claim about whether anyone could formulate useful computation around this model. The paper provides an unusually credible population regime, but no result for a continuous computational mirror to classify.
