The strongest positive candidate I see is a continuous version of the paper’s formal fair influence maximization problem in Section IV—not a result proved by the paper. Consider large public-health or information campaigns on a platform where many people share a small set \(T\) of complete contact-profile types. A type records a person’s protected group and their interaction rates with every other type. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), and let \(\lambda_{tt'}\) specify the expected number of contacts from a person of type \(t\) to people of type \(t'\). Model the network as a sparse stochastic block model: among \(N\) people, each ordered pair of types \(t,t'\) has an edge independently with probability \(\lambda_{tt'}/N\). Each edge transmits influence with probability \(p\) under the Independent Cascade process.

The regime has \(N\) very large and \(|T|=\tau\) relatively small: there are many people per contact profile. The decision is an allocation of seed mass \(x_t\) to types, with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\beta\), where \(\beta\) is the seed budget as a fraction of the population. Let \(\rho_i(x)\) be the limiting expected fraction influenced in protected group \(i\), and let \(\rho(x)\) be the limiting expected fraction influenced overall.

Call the lead candidate Continuous Fair-IC Seed Selection. Given rational \((T,\mu,\lambda,p,\beta)\), a group assignment, and rational thresholds \(q,r\), ask whether there is a seed allocation \(x\) with budget \(\beta\) such that
\[ \min_i \rho_i(x)\ge q \quad\text{and}\quad \rho(x)\ge r. \]
This threshold form captures the paper’s stated objective: maximize the least-influenced group’s outreach, then maximize total outreach among allocations attaining that fairness optimum. Solutions are real mass allocations, not lists of individually named seed nodes.

I think the authors would recognize this as their fair seed-selection question in a plausible large-population network regime: it keeps the seed budget, IC diffusion, protected groups, and maximin outreach criterion. The network model is a substantive co-modification needed to make a continuum population’s contacts meaningful. The mirror covers the formal fair-IM objective; it does not cover the particular DQ-learning architecture or its generalization experiments.

I would expect tractable behavior to be possible when \(\tau\) is fixed and the limiting diffusion can be described by finite-type equations. With \(\tau\) as input, the interaction structure may still carry substantial combinatorics. The ordinary IM hardness cited as [23] motivates investigating transfer, but does not prove hardness for this fair, stochastic-block-model problem. The main follow-up questions are whether the limit outreach values are efficiently computable, and whether optimizing the type-level seed allocation is tractable for fixed \(\tau\) or hard when \(\tau\) grows.

The weakest point is the anchor gap: this candidate is grounded in the paper’s problem formulation, not in a named computational result of the paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the anchor gap: the paper states a fair influence-maximization problem, but proves no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about its computational complexity. The NP-hardness and \(\#\)P-hardness claims are background results cited from other work. The paper’s own contributions are an RL method, experiments, and a training-time estimate. So the proposed mirror is not anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The proponent’s stochastic-block-model question is coherent, but it changes the task in two important ways. The paper selects \(k\) particular vertices from an observed graph; the proposed model allocates a fraction \(\beta\) of seeds to each type before seeing a random graph. A type’s expected contact rates do not capture the realized neighborhoods that determine which vertices are valuable seeds. If types encode enough of those neighborhoods to preserve vertex-level choices, their number will generally grow with the graph. If they do not, the model asks about ex-ante group quotas rather than optimizing seeds on the input graph.

The best repair is to embrace that change: study fair seeding in a population of network roles, with a per-capita seed budget and expected maximin outreach over a specified random-graph model. That is a plausible computational question. But it is a neighboring problem, whose outcome depends on the ensemble and the restricted seed policy. Keeping a fixed number of seeds as the population grows also makes seed mass vanish; scaling the budget with population avoids that, but changes the intervention regime.

So the negative case is strongest as a critique of the paper’s fit to the requested anchor-based exercise, and weaker as a universal claim. A researcher in fair influence maximization could plausibly recognize the population-level ensemble question as worthwhile.
