The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed number of DAOs and true member values supported on at most \(\tau\) values in \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), with rational masses \(\mu_j(v)\) satisfying \(\sum_{j,v}\mu_j(v)=1\), determine the best worst-case welfare approximation factor \(\alpha^*(\tau)\) among deterministic two-level mechanisms. Each mechanism awards one indivisible item to at most one DAO, assigns each value class access \(x_{jv}\in\{0,1\}\) and per-member payment \(p_{jv}\), and is truthful, budget-balanced, individually rational, and equal-treatment on every finite refinement of the masses, where one member's report changes mass by \(1/N\). Welfare is \(\sum_{j,v}\mu_j(v)v x_{jv}\), compared with the optimum \(\max_j\sum_v\mu_j(v)v\).

The model it lives in

Represent each DAO by value types \(v\) carrying mass \(\mu_j(v)\). A DAO reports one scalar bid; the upper mechanism awards the item to at most one DAO and sets its normalized payment \(P_j\). The winning DAO assigns binary access \(x_{jv}\) and per-member payments \(p_{jv}\), with \(P_j=\sum_v\mu_j(v)p_{jv}\). A natural lift of the paper's mechanism sorts value classes, uses \(B_j=\max_r Q_{jr}v_{jr}\) for cumulative mass \(Q_{jr}\), and gives access to the largest qualifying prefix.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result about running time or a standard complexity class such as P or NP-hardness. Its strongest relevant computational results are approximation theorems about truthful mechanisms. The lead anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in the paper: no truthful, budget-balanced, individually rational two-level mechanism satisfying equal treatment can guarantee a welfare approximation better than \(H_{\ell}\), where \(\ell\) is the largest group size. Its proof uses a characterization from Shapley’s work, cited through Lemma 3.5, then gives the paper’s own lower-bound construction. The companion anchor is Theorem 3.2, also proved here: the paper’s maximum equal-split willingness-to-pay mechanism, followed by a Vickrey auction between groups, achieves an \(H_{\ell}\)-approximation. The lower bound is the stronger anchor; the companion matters because it supplies a concrete mechanism whose mass version can be studied.

A plausible regime is a very large crowdfunding DAO with anonymous members whose single-item valuations fall into a modest number of shared buckets—for example, standardized membership tiers or pledge levels. The type is a member’s value for access to the item. Let \(V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_s\}\) be the finite set of possible values, with \(s\leq\tau\). For DAO \(j\), let \(\mu_j(v)\) be the fraction of the overall population in that DAO with value \(v\), where \(\sum_{j,v}\mu_j(v)=1\). Here \(N\), the number of members, is large compared with \(\tau\). Members with the same value are indistinguishable for this single-item problem.

The continuous mirror I would put to a prover is DAO-Mass-OptimalApprox. Its instance is the rational measures \((\mu_j)_{j=1}^k\). A mechanism maps each DAO’s reports to a scalar group bid; the upper mechanism awards the item to at most one DAO and sets its total payment \(P_j\). The winning DAO’s lower mechanism gives each reported value class access or no access, \(x_{jv}\in\{0,1\}\), and sets a per-member payment \(p_{jv}\geq0\). Equal treatment requires one allocation and payment for everyone in the same class; budget balance requires \(\sum_v\mu_j(v)p_{jv}=P_j\); individual rationality requires truthful members not to pay more than their value. Welfare is \(\sum_{j,v}\mu_j(v)v x_{jv}\), and the optimum is \(\max_j\sum_v\mu_j(v)v\), since the item could be given to every member of one DAO.

The question is to determine the best worst-case approximation factor \(\alpha^*(\tau)\) over such mechanisms, uniformly as \(N\) grows while the value support stays fixed. To make truthfulness meaningful in that limit, require it on every exact finite refinement of the mass profile: in an \(N\)-member refinement, one member’s deviation moves mass \(1/N\) from their true value to their report. A solution is a two-level mechanism family satisfying these constraints on every refinement, together with a matching approximation guarantee and impossibility bound. This keeps the authors’ individual-incentive question in view instead of silently replacing it with price-taking behavior.

For Theorem 3.2, the corresponding mass question is whether its concrete mechanism remains truthful under those refinements and what guarantee depends on \(\tau\), rather than on \(N\). Sort a DAO’s values as \(v_{j1}>\cdots>v_{js_j}\), with class masses \(w_{jr}\), and let \(Q_{jr}=\sum_{h\le r}w_{jh}\). Its mass willingness to pay is \(B_j=\max_r Q_{jr}v_{jr}\). A natural lift bids \(B_j\), runs the second-price auction, and, at price \(P\), selects the largest feasible high-value prefix with \(Q_{jr}v_{jr}\ge P\), charging each selected member \(P/Q_{jr}\). The candidate’s welfare bound is support-dependent: if \(W_j=\sum_r w_{jr}v_{jr}\), then \(v_{jr}\le B_j/Q_{jr}\), so \(W_j\le B_j\sum_r w_{jr}/Q_{jr}\le s_jB_j\). This suggests a factor at most \(\tau\), independent of headcount. I expect this constructive side to be tractable, with the refinement truthfulness and sharp support bound made explicit.

For Theorem 3.4, the lead question is whether the optimal factor \(\alpha^*(\tau)\) is governed by the number of distinct types rather than by population size. I expect the \(H_{\ell}\) barrier not to transfer unchanged when \(N\) grows but \(\tau\) stays fixed: the paper’s lower-bound profile uses a long sequence of distinct valuations, so its \(\ell\)-dependence does not by itself establish an \(N\)-growing lower bound in a fixed-support regime. I would expect a finite support-dependent factor, with no constant uniform as the support itself grows. The open work is to prove the best lower bound for fixed \(\tau\), and see whether the mass mechanism’s \(\tau\) guarantee can be improved. This is a population-multiplicity question, not a claim that the paper proves NP-hardness.

The soft spot is substantial: in an exactly atomless model, one member’s report has zero effect on the group’s aggregate bid. The finite-refinement truthfulness requirement is a proposed bridge back to the paper’s DSIC notion, and the authors might regard it as a sequence of discrete problems rather than a standalone continuum mechanism. The lower-bound theorem’s witness also has almost as many distinct valuation ranks as members, so it is weak evidence for the fixed-\(\tau\) regime itself. I would keep the case to the single-item DAO result: Theorem 4.2’s multi-item unit-demand bound depends on instances with many effectively singleton groups, for which a high-multiplicity population story is much less convincing.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about fit with ChoCo’s computational programme, not about whether these populations can sensibly be made continuous. Large DAOs with repeated pledge or membership tiers are a plausible high-multiplicity regime: members with the same value are indistinguishable for the problem. I would concede that premise.

The trouble is what the paper’s results ask. Theorem 3.4 is an impossibility from truthfulness, budget balance, and equal treatment; Theorem 4.2 is an impossibility from truthfulness and the upper mechanism’s lack of information about group structure. Both hold regardless of computational resources. Theorem 3.2 gives an explicit mechanism whose work is sorting bids and computing maxima, taking \(O(n\log n)\) time in the single-item case. These results do not expose a pricing or separation problem, or a computational bottleneck that continuization might resolve. That is a substantial mismatch with ChoCo’s focus on complexity and algorithms.

That objection does not erase the single-item mirrors. A mass version of Theorem 3.2 is coherent: for each group, sort its value classes, let \(Q_r\) be the cumulative mass through class \(r\), and use \(B=\max_r Q_rv_r\) as its per-capita willingness to pay. A winning group can give access to the largest qualifying prefix and split the normalized payment across its mass. With support size \(\tau\), the computation is finite-dimensional, and the proposed \(\tau\)-dependent welfare bound is plausible. But that makes this a tractable continuous mechanism-design question, not a new computational-complexity result. Optimizing the best truthful approximation factor \(\alpha^*(\tau)\) could be worthwhile work; its value would be a new minimax theorem about incentive and budget constraints.

Theorem 3.4’s harmonic witness also needs care. Its long sequence of distinct valuations does not establish a growing lower bound when support size stays fixed. Allowing richer or growing support may preserve a meaningful continuous welfare-loss question, but the issue remains an incentive-compatibility bound, not algorithmic hardness. The proponent’s finite-refinement formulation has a further modeling cost: a mass profile does not identify the refinement size \(N\), even though the size of a unilateral deviation is \(1/N\). Requiring truthfulness over all refinements is a useful bridge, but it is a family of finite problems; a mass-only model needs its own explicit incentive definition.

Theorem 4.2 is the weaker anchor. Its lower-bound construction grows through many singleton groups. A mirror over a continuum of groups with a fixed finite supply of indivisible items risks degeneracy: an item assigned to one zero-mass group contributes zero aggregate welfare. Keeping positive-mass groups or scaling the supply can avoid that, but changes the regime behind the theorem. Continuizing members inside a fixed set of DAOs does not recover the original construction’s growth in the number of singleton groups.

So I can make a strong case that these results do not open the kind of computational landscape ChoCo is charting. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the fixed-support single-item version of Theorems 3.2 and 3.4 is a sensible population model. If the reader is judging whether any meaningful mass question survives, the proponent’s single-item anchor survives this negative case.
