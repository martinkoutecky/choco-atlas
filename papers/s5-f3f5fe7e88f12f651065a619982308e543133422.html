What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.6 alone; it leaves the paper's other cost-sharing bounds and group-strategyproof mechanism results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a two type continuum mirror of Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper. The paper contains no named result classifying the complexity of computing a scheme or deciding whether one exists; its named results concern budget balance and mechanism properties. Theorem 3.6 is therefore a structural guarantee barrier, not a P/NP-style complexity anchor.

Theorem 3.6 says that in the maximum-flow game, where agents are edges and a coalition’s value is the maximum \(s\)-\(t\) flow using its edges, there is no cross-monotonic profit-sharing scheme with a budget-balance factor \(o(n)\), where \(n\) is the number of agents served. The paper proves this here using a graph with vertices \(s,u,t\) and \(n-1\) parallel edges on each of \(s\to u\) and \(u\to t\). Its selected coalitions have value \(1\), while cross-monotonicity forces their total payouts to grow linearly with \(n\).

Call the mirror Two-Type Continuum Max-Flow Profit Sharing. The agents are capacity suppliers on those two network segments. Type \(A\) supplies one unit of capacity on \(s\to u\); type \(B\) supplies one unit on \(u\to t\). For population scale \(2q\), the society has mass \(q\) of each type, so its normalized distribution is \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), with support size \(\tau=2\) regardless of \(q\). A serviced coalition is a pair of masses \((a,b)\in[0,q]^2\), and its value is \(v(a,b)=\min(a,b)\).

A solution is a pair of nonnegative payout rates \(r_A(a,b),r_B(a,b)\), one per unit mass of each type, defined for every serviced mass profile. The scheme must be cross-monotonic: increasing either coalition mass cannot lower the rate for agents of a type who remain in the coalition. For a factor \(\beta\), it must also satisfy
\[ \min(a,b)\le a r_A(a,b)+b r_B(a,b)\le \beta\min(a,b) \]
for every \((a,b)\). The problem is to determine the best possible \(\beta\), or establish that no finite one exists. The network interpretation is plausible at high multiplicity: many operators can own interchangeable capacity units on either of two backbone segments, while the network value depends on the aggregate capacity on each segment. The mirror covers Theorem 3.6 alone.

In this fully divisible model, the obstruction is even stronger than the paper’s discrete bound. Take \(P=(\epsilon,\epsilon)\), where \(\epsilon>0\). Since \(v(P)=\epsilon\), budget balance requires \(r_A(P)+r_B(P)\ge1\). Now expand to \(X_A=(\epsilon,q)\) and \(X_B=(q,\epsilon)\); each still has value \(\epsilon\). Cross-monotonicity implies that the average of their total payouts is at least
\[ \frac{q+\epsilon}{2}\bigl(r_A(P)+r_B(P)\bigr)\ge \frac{q+\epsilon}{2}. \]
Thus a valid factor must satisfy \(\beta\ge(q+\epsilon)/(2\epsilon)\) for every positive \(\epsilon\), so no finite \(\beta\) works. The argument can also be made for individualized measurable payouts by partitioning each type’s mass into equal blocks and averaging over pairs of blocks.

I expect a continuum-specific obstruction: the continuous model admits arbitrarily small positive coalitions, and cross-monotonicity carries their required payouts into much larger coalitions. At \(\epsilon=1\), the construction recovers the paper’s linear-in-population lower bound; allowing \(\epsilon\) to approach zero makes the factor unbounded. This is a mechanism-feasibility result, not a claim of computational hardness.

The main vulnerability is precisely that divisibility assumption. If the authors would only recognize coalitions made of whole unit contracts, the stronger no-finite-factor conclusion would not be their mirror; the remaining case would be the discrete \(o(n)\) barrier. There are also natural follow-up questions: what factor is possible if every nonempty coalition must have mass at least \(\delta>0\), and how does the obstruction change on longer networks?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is the paper’s strongest candidate, and the two-type mirror is mathematically sound. With masses \(a,b\) and value \(\min(a,b)\), the argument using \((\epsilon,\epsilon)\), \((\epsilon,q)\), and \((q,\epsilon)\) does force the required factor to grow without bound as \(\epsilon\) shrinks. Partitioning the types into equal blocks also gives a route beyond uniform per-type payout rates. I would not reject this mirror as a bad model.

My case against it is that it mirrors a different kind of result from the one ChoCo is meant to study. Theorem 3.6 proves an existence barrier for cross-monotonic profit-sharing schemes. The paper gives no algorithmic problem for finding such a scheme, deciding whether one exists, or computing the best factor: it specifies no computational input/output task or running-time claim. Recasting “is a finite factor possible?” as a computational question would add a new objective; it would not continue a computational result in this paper.

For this two-type example, that added task also appears to collapse to a short structural argument. If one imposes a minimum positive coalition mass \(\delta\), the rates \(r_A=r_B=1/2\) on coalitions containing both types achieve the factor \((q+\delta)/(2\delta)\) when each type has maximum mass \(q\) and coalition masses lie on the \(\delta\)-grid. As \(\delta\) vanishes, the factor diverges. That is a clean description of the granularity effect, but it does not open a complexity landscape. Extending the task to arbitrary networks might be interesting, though it would be a new optimization programme rather than a mirror supplied by Theorem 3.6.

So the negative case is narrow: this is a worthwhile structural continuization, but not a computational mirror in ChoCo’s stated sense. If the programme is willing to count structural feasibility results as anchors, the proponent’s case survives, and my argument against it is weak.
