The continuous mirror question

Given finite left and right type sets \(I\) and \(J\), rational type masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and strict rankings of the opposite-side types with being unmatched last, find a rational matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\). For each pair \((i,j)\), define \(w^L_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}\) and \(w^R_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_{i':\,i'\succeq_j i}x_{i'j}\); require \(\min\{w^L_{ij}(x),w^R_{ij}(x)\}=0\). Among such stable matrices, find one \(x\) such that for every stable matrix \(y\), type \(i\), and cutoff \(j\), \(\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}\ge\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}y_{ij'}\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided placement market with interchangeable agents within each preference type, represented by rational type masses. The decision variable is the mass \(x_{ij}\) matched between each pair of types; the goal is a stable matrix maximal for side 1 under rank-cutoff dominance.

The objection that survived

The paper orders named-contract sets through choice functions, while the proposed mirror uses rank-cutoff dominance over mass matrices; the case does not derive that mass-level order from the paper’s order.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s stable-agreement existence and side-1 extremality in the one-to-one strict-preference case. It leaves the general coherent-choice framework, the lattice results, and the money-economy results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational instance have a rational stable matrix that is maximal for side 1 under rank-cutoff dominance, and can one be computed directly from the type representation?
  • How does the mass-level order relate to the paper’s choice-function order, and what running time is achievable in the number of positive-mass types and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly applying your anchor rule, this paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Section 7.3 says in prose that the algorithm takes at most \(|X|\) steps, but its cost also depends on how the choice functions are represented and evaluated.

The closest named result is Theorem 3, proved here. Its full content is: for the agreement problem on a finite contract set \(X\) with two coherent choice functions \(f_1,f_2\), the algorithm’s output \(S=f_1(Z_f)\), where \(Z_f\) is the sequence’s fixed point, is a stable agreement; moreover, every stable agreement \(A\) satisfies \(A\le_1 S\) and \(S\le_2 A\). Here \(B\le_i A\) means \(f_i(A\cup B)=f_i(A)\). Thus \(S\) is extremal for side 1 and, in the inverse ordering, for side 2. The theorem establishes correctness and extremality, not a complexity bound. I would use it as an algorithmic anchor-adjacent result, with §7.3 supplying the unnumbered runtime claim, rather than present it as a numbered “in P” theorem.

The strongest mirror I can defend is a one-to-one subcase of that agreement problem: a type-continuous stable matching. Consider a large two-sided labor or placement market in which each side has a relatively small set of preference types. A type completely specifies its members’ preferences and constraints; members of a type are interchangeable, including with respect to the terms of any contract. Let \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) be the rational masses of types \(i\) and \(j\), each side’s masses summing to one. The regime is many individuals but relatively few positive-mass types, \(\tau\ll n\). This is plausible for repeated bulk placement into standardized roles; it is not a claim that every matching market has this structure.

For this mirror, each type ranks the types on the other side, with being unmatched last. The decision variable is a rational matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of distinct type-\(i\) individuals matched to distinct type-\(j\) individuals. Require \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\): the mass is divisible, but no individual is split across matches. Write \(j'\succeq_i j\) when type \(i\) ranks \(j'\) at least as highly as \(j\), and define
\[ w^L_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}, \qquad w^R_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_{i':\,i'\succeq_j i}x_{i'j}. \]
These are the masses on each side who would prefer the proposed partner type to their current one or to being unmatched. Stability means that for every pair \((i,j)\), at least one of \(w^L_{ij}(x)\) and \(w^R_{ij}(x)\) is zero. The problem is to find a stable \(x\) that is best for side 1 among stable matrices, in the sense that for every stable \(y\), every type \(i\), and every cutoff \(j\),
\[ \sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}\ge \sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}y_{ij'}. \]
This is a precise mass version of the one-to-one stable-agreement result the paper explicitly identifies with Gale–Shapley matching.

I would expect this restricted problem to be tractable (Class A), plausibly with running time polynomial in the support size and rational bit length. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 3. Natural follow-up questions are whether a rational proposer-optimal stable matrix always exists, how it relates to finite high-multiplicity instances, and whether the result extends to the paper’s many-to-many contracts and general coherent choice functions. The mirror covers only the one-to-one subcase, not the paper’s full agreement framework; the paper supplies no hardness anchor whose complexity might disappear under continuization.

The weak point is that Theorem 3 reasons over subsets of named contracts. A mass matrix and its blocking condition are a genuine extension, not a fractional version defined by the paper. The authors would likely recognize it as a continuous form of their marriage/agreement problem, but the fractional stability model and its algorithm still need to be justified.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is a source problem: the paper gives no numbered complexity result to mirror. Theorem 3 proves that the finite algorithm returns a stable agreement with an extremal preference property. It does not give a runtime bound. The paper’s runtime discussion is in §7.3: at most \(|X|\) iterations, each applying the choice functions. For arbitrary \(f_i:2^X\to2^X\), that is a count of choice-function evaluations, not a bit-complexity bound for a specified compact input representation. The paper does claim polynomial time, but the claim is not a numbered complexity result and does not analyze the compressed type input that the proposed mirror needs. Theorem 1, Theorem 4, and Theorem 5 establish structural properties, not complexity classifications.

The proposed one-to-one mass matching is plausible: standardized placement markets can have many interchangeable participants and few preference types, and rational masses can be normalized to finite cohorts. But plausibility does not supply the missing anchor. The mass matrix, its weak-blocking condition, and the requirement that one side’s rank-cutoff totals dominate those of every other stable matrix make a continuous allocation problem. Theorem 3’s order is defined through choice functions on sets of named contracts; the paper does not prove that this order becomes the proposed rank-dominance relation on mass matrices.

The strongest repair would be to clear denominators, expand the cohorts into named individuals, and apply the paper’s finite algorithm. That gives a faithful high-multiplicity instance, but its iteration bound is in the explicit contract set \(|X|\); it says nothing about running time in support size \(\tau\) and binary input length \(L\). Alternatively, one could define mass-level choice functions and prove that the resulting stable allocations preserve the paper’s stability and extremality claims. That would be a legitimate extension, but it needs new definitions and results—and still would not turn this paper’s unnumbered runtime discussion into a named complexity anchor.

So I would reject this paper as an anchored ChoCo complexity mirror. I would not claim that no continuous matching question inspired by it could be worthwhile: the proposed type-mass problem is a reasonable extension. The honest negative case is narrower, and strongest there: this paper does not provide the required computational result for that extension to mirror.
