The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a compact rational polytope \(P\), and positive rational piecewise-linear concave utilities \(u_t\), compute a shared predictor maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u_t(\theta)\). Is the resulting predictor core-stable against every measurable coalition of mass \(\alpha>0\) that prefers an alternative \(\theta'\) by the factor \(1/\alpha\), with strict improvement on positive mass?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of clients has finite support over complete expected-utility types, with mass \(\mu_t\) per type. Each type receives utility \(u_t(\theta)\) from a shared predictor \(\theta\); the decision maximizes mass-weighted Nash welfare, and blocking is tested against measurable coalitions whose mass sets the proportionality factor.

The objection that survived

Exact expected-utility profiles may be unique or numerous in some deployments, making the support size \(\tau\) grow with the population; the stated repeated-cohort regime makes the mirror plausible, but its reach is limited when only approximate profiles recur.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s concave-utility computation, including the linear and logistic regression cases. It leaves aside the existence-only result, the local nonconvex DNN guarantee, and the empirical comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a bit-complexity bound, in model dimension, support size, input length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), for computing an \(\epsilon\)-optimal weighted Nash-welfare predictor.
  • Bound the coalition-blocking violation of an \(\epsilon\)-optimal solution.
  • Characterize when finite-client weighted-core instances approximate the nonatomic guarantee as type counts grow.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a population version of the paper’s exact convex-utility computation. Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that if every agent’s utility \(u_i\) is concave, then any predictor \(\theta^*\) maximizing \(\sum_{i=1}^n \log u_i(\theta)\) over \(\theta\in P\)—equivalently, maximizing the product of utilities—is core-stable. The paper derives this from convex optimization and proves the stability claim; it does not cite the result from elsewhere. [Theorem 2 in the paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/25e92e33ac8c35fd49f394c37f21b6da-Paper-Conference.pdf)

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study. Consider a nonatomic population \(\Omega\) of clients, with total mass \(1\). Each client has a type \(t\) that fully specifies the client’s expected loss function, utility scaling, and model task. There are \(\tau\) types, with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); many clients share each type. A shared predictor \(\theta\in P\) gives every client of type \(t\) utility \(u_t(\theta)=M_t-\ell_t(\theta)\), where \(P\) is compact and convex and each \(u_t\) is positive and concave.

Call a predictor core-stable if there is no measurable coalition \(A\subseteq\Omega\), of mass \(\alpha>0\), and alternative predictor \(\theta'\in P\) such that \(\alpha u_t(\theta')\geq u_t(\theta)\) for almost every member of \(A\), with strict improvement on a positive-mass subset. Continuous CoreFed asks: given \(P\), the type utilities, and \(\mu\), find a core-stable shared predictor. Its natural objective is the mass-weighted Nash welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(\theta)\). For a concrete computational version, take rational polyhedral \(P\), positive rational piecewise-linear concave utilities, and ask for an \(\epsilon\)-optimal predictor.

The regime is cross-device federated learning: millions of devices may share a prediction task while falling into a moderate number of cohorts with the same expected local risk profile and utility scaling. A type represents that entire profile, rather than a named device. Thus \(N\) is large relative to \(\tau\), and the mass is the fraction of participating clients. The shared decision remains the predictor \(\theta\); the object being continuized is the client population.

The connection to Theorem 2 is direct. Its proof gives \(\sum_i u_i(\theta')/u_i(\theta^*)\leq n\) at an optimum. In the mirror, the corresponding inequality is \(\sum_t\mu_t u_t(\theta')/u_t(\theta^*)\leq 1\). A blocking coalition of mass \(\alpha\) would contribute more than \(1\) to that sum: its members’ utility ratios are at least \(1/\alpha\), with strict improvement on positive mass. So the weighted-log optimum rules out a block. The paper’s weighted-core section, which allows coalition size to be measured by total weight, gives further reason the authors would recognize this extension.

I expect Class A for the stated concave-utility problem: it is a convex-optimization target, with complexity naturally measured in model dimension \(d\), support size \(\tau\), encoding length \(L\), and approximation precision. That is an expected tractability classification under a standard rational convex-program representation, not a bit-complexity theorem established by the paper. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s exact concave-utility computation, including the paper’s linear and logistic regression cases. It does not claim to cover the local, nonconvex DNN result.

The main weakness is that the nonatomic limit loses the paper’s finite-population guarantee for a singleton: a zero-mass individual cannot form a blocking coalition. The mirror studies positive-mass coalitions, which preserves the paper’s central mass-proportional notion of blocking but narrows what the guarantee says about individual clients. Also, this paper supplies no hardness anchor, so the mirror is a credible tractable population problem, not a case where continuization makes a known hard problem easy. Further questions are whether finite-client approximations preserve the stability guarantee, how optimization error translates into approximate core-stability, and whether the DNN neighborhood theorem extends to mass-weighted coalitions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that the paper’s computational result is about choosing a predictor from client utility functions; it does not study a combinatorial problem over the clients. With \(\tau\) utility types, the proposed continuous version changes the Nash objective to \(\sum_t \mu_t\log u_t(\theta)\). For rational piecewise-linear utilities, that is a finite-dimensional convex-optimization problem. The complexity then comes from how \(P\) and the utility functions are represented, not from the continuous population. The paper gives no finite-input complexity theorem for its discrete clients that this formulation would extend or explain.

There is also a modeling cost to the proposed type compression. A type must specify the client’s entire expected loss function and utility scaling. Sharing a broad task label or data cohort does not necessarily make clients the same type. If exact risk profiles vary substantially across clients, the support may grow with the population; if nearby profiles are merged, the core guarantee needs an approximation analysis. That makes moderate \(\tau\) a substantive assumption for federated learning, though it is plausible for standardized tasks or genuinely repeated data-generating profiles.

The theorem anchor itself survives the mathematical objections: under the stated concavity and positivity assumptions, the mass-weighted log objective gives the ratio bound needed to exclude positive-mass blocking coalitions. The paper’s weighted-core extension already uses the same weighted log objective, so the proposed version may be a direct application of an existing result, with an additional argument for coalitions that split a type’s mass. That makes it hard to present this as a new complexity landscape. It does not, however, defeat the mirror under the programme’s rules: a known or immediate solution to a well-posed population problem is not by itself a reason to reject the question, and the finite weighted-client model is not plainly identical to a nonatomic population with divisible coalitions.

So the negative case is weak. It raises a real question about whether exact, moderate-support risk types describe enough federated settings, and whether the optimization representation yields a distinct complexity contribution. But the finite-type scenario is sensible, the population interpretation is recognizable, and the mirror is computationally well-posed. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
