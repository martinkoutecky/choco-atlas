What the mirror covers

The proposed evaluation covers applying BallotRank to a distribution over rankings; it does not mirror a numbered computational result. The paper’s axiomatic propositions and empirical findings remain unanchored. The self-loop scale also means the mass version need not preserve the ranking from the corresponding finite electorate.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive candidate is Mass-BallotRank evaluation. In a large ranked-choice election or poll, let each voter type be a complete ranking \(r\) of the candidates, and let \(\mu_r\) be that ranking’s share of the electorate. There may be millions of voters but relatively few distinct rankings. For a fixed rational damping parameter \(d\in(0,1)\), compute pairwise margins
\[ q_\mu(a,b)=\sum_r \mu_r\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_r b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_r a]\bigr). \]
Use these margins, with the paper’s self-loop rule, to form the transition matrix, then return the exact weak ranking given by its unique damped stationary vector. The input is the candidate set, the supported rankings and their rational masses, and \(d\); the solution is the resulting ranking. This is a ranking computation, not an optimization problem: there is no action variable or objective. For fixed \(d<1\), the computation appears tractable by rational linear algebra in the number of candidates and the input bit length.

That is a plausible continuous-population version of the method: the population is the continuous object, and pairwise margins are calculated from shares rather than ballot counts. It also suggests further questions about the smallest mass transfer that changes the winner, or the damping values at which the ranking changes. Those would be new computational questions, though; the paper does not establish their complexity.

I cannot honestly give this candidate a qualifying anchor. The numbered propositions—Propositions 6.2 through 6.5—assert social-choice properties such as anonymity, neutrality, Pareto, and Smith. They do not classify a computational problem as tractable or hard. Definition 2.1 and the stationary-vector equations specify how to compute BallotRank, but are not numbered computational results; the empirical findings are not such results either. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. So Mass-BallotRank evaluation is a candidate mirror, not an anchor-backed case.

The main weakness is the bridge to the discrete method. In the paper, off-diagonal margins are raw vote counts, while the self-loop is a ratio of total margins. Replacing counts with population shares changes their relative scale and can therefore change the ranking. A continuous formulation is natural, but it need not reproduce the paper’s output on the corresponding finite election. That scale sensitivity makes author recognition uncertain—and, together with the lack of a named computational anchor, keeps the positive case limited.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 6.2–6.5 prove social-choice properties; the later examples show failures of axioms; and the empirical sections report how the rule behaves on election data. The fixed-point equations define BallotRank, but do not establish the complexity of a computational problem. By the programme’s criteria, that is the strongest objection: there is no complexity claim here whose population-continuous counterpart would extend the paper’s results.

The proponent’s Mass-BallotRank evaluation is a plausible way to apply the rule to a continuous society, but it is a weak computational target. From \(\tau\) supported rankings, one can compute all pairwise margins in \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations, form an \(m\times m\) transition matrix, and, for rational \(d<1\), solve the resulting linear system and compare the exact scores. The computation factors through only \(O(m^2)\) pairwise margins; it does not require search over the ranking types or expose a pricing problem. It is a straightforward evaluation procedure, not a complexity landscape of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart.

There is also a scale problem with claiming this is the direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s rule. For a society with proportions \(\mu\), pairwise margins from \(n\) voters are \(n\) times the margins from shares, while the paper’s self-loop \(TW(a)/TW\) is unchanged by duplicating the electorate. Thus the self-loop’s normalized weight changes with \(n\) and tends to zero as the electorate is replicated. The same \(\mu\) does not determine the paper’s transition matrix. A scale-invariant mass version is easy to define, but it is a recalibration of the rule; evaluating that version remains the same routine matrix computation.

The stronger candidates—minimum mass transfer to change the winner, or finding damping values at which the ranking changes—are new computational questions, not mirrors of results in this paper. They require choices the authors did not make, such as intervention costs and permitted type conversions. The paper’s future-work discussion of sensitivity makes those questions plausible, but does not supply a computational result to continue. They might merit separate study; their existence does not rescue the proposed evaluation anchor.

So the negative case is strong against the only concrete anchor raised, but it cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population problem could be built around BallotRank. Ranked elections are a natural high-multiplicity setting, and a carefully specified mass-robustness problem could be interesting. The narrower conclusion is that this paper itself offers no qualifying computational result, and the proposed evaluation mirror is unlikely to justify a place in the continuization programme.
