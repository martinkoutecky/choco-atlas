The continuous mirror question

Given a connected undirected role graph \(H=(T,E)\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), choose seed masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\). Starting from \(y_t^0=x_t\), all remaining mass of type \(t\) activates once every neighboring type is fully active; require eventual activation of total mass \(1\).

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\in T\) has mass \(\mu_t\), and each individual of type \(t\) influences every individual in each adjacent type. The decision is the seeded mass \(x_t\); unseeded mass activates when all neighboring types are fully active, and the objective is to minimize \(\sum_t x_t\) subject to full activation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 13’s NP-hard Length result for maximum influence and maximum spread. It leaves that theorem’s polynomial-time Width result and the paper’s other influence-game settings unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which graph classes \(H\) does minimum seed mass admit polynomial-time algorithms or useful approximation guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the Length result in Theorem 13, proved in this paper. The theorem says that, for unweighted influence games with maximum influence and maximum spread on a connected graph \(G\), computing Length is NP-hard; for the same game \(\Gamma(G)\), computing Width is polynomial-time solvable even when \(G\) is disconnected. Here maximum influence means each node’s threshold is its degree, and maximum spread sets the quota to the entire graph. The paper also shows that a team succeeds in \(\Gamma(G)\) exactly when it is a vertex cover. I mirror the Length result: it asks for the smallest team whose influence activates everyone.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large organization with recurring roles and broadcast-like channels between roles. People in a role share the same influence relationships and threshold; there may be many people but relatively few roles. The role graph records which cohorts influence one another. Mass is the fraction of the population in each role, and the action is to seed some mass of eligible people. This is a specific block-structured network regime, rather than a claim that social networks generally have few types.

Here is the continuous problem, Typed Maximum-Threshold Length. An instance consists of a connected, undirected role graph \(H=(T,E)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a rational budget \(b\). Every person of type \(t\) can influence every person in an adjacent type, and no one else. A seed is a mass vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). Set \(y_t^0=x_t\); at each round, the unseeded mass of type \(t\) activates if all its neighboring types are fully active:
\[ y_t^{k+1} = y_t^k+(\mu_t-y_t^k)\, \mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E} y_u^k = \sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}\mu_u\right]. \]
The instance is yes if some seed vector with \(\sum_t x_t\le b\) eventually activates total mass \(1\). A solution is such a vector \(x\) and its resulting activation process; the optimization version minimizes \(\sum_t x_t\).

The authors should recognize this as their Length problem over a role-based population: it keeps the seed team, the linear-threshold spread process, the full-population quota, and the objective of minimizing the team’s size, with mass replacing cardinality. It also has an exact high-multiplicity connection. Replace each vertex \(t\) of \(H\) by \(r\) indistinguishable agents and connect adjacent role groups completely. With \(\mu_t=1/|T|\), the discrete Length divided by the population size equals the continuous optimum.

In this maximum-threshold, full-spread setting, every successful seed vector must fully seed a vertex cover of \(H\): if neither endpoint of an edge is fully seeded, neither endpoint can be the first to become fully active. Conversely, fully seeding a vertex cover activates every remaining type. Thus the optimum is the minimum vertex-cover mass. With equal masses, deciding whether the optimum is at most \(k/|T|\) is exactly Vertex Cover on \(H\). I expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the combinatorics live in the role graph, and increasing the population within each role does not remove them. For fixed support size \(\tau=|T|\), enumeration is possible, so the hardness is in growing \(\tau\), not in population size alone.

This mirror covers Theorem 13’s NP-hard Length clause. It does not cover the theorem’s polynomial Width result; allowing arbitrarily small unseeded mass can make the supremum of losing seed mass uninformative, so that clause needs a separate definition and analysis. The main open questions are whether less extreme thresholds admit an LP or convex formulation, and which structural restrictions on the role graph preserve tractability or an exact discrete-to-continuous identity.

My weakest point is the block-network assumption: complete influence between adjacent role cohorts is a narrow network model, and the quota requires everyone to activate. Those restrictions are also the source paper’s exact maximum-influence, maximum-spread case. The mirror therefore makes a strong case for that named result, while supporting a narrower claim about the paper’s other settings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is its narrow setting: members of each role need identical, complete connections to neighboring roles; everyone requires unanimity from those neighbors; and success requires activating the whole population. In sparse networks, neighborhood structure may make nearly every person a separate type, so the high multiplicity disappears. That limits how broadly the result applies, but it does not defeat this scenario: a large organization with recurring roles and dense cross-role ties is plausible, and the paper itself studies the maximum-threshold, full-spread case.

The best version of the mirror holds up. For rational role masses \(\mu_t\), choose a common denominator \(n\) and make \(n\mu_t\) agents of each type. Replace each edge of the role graph with a complete bipartite connection between its two groups. Every agent of type \(t\) then has the same threshold: the total number of agents in neighboring roles. This is exactly the paper’s maximum-threshold model.

In this model, the roles that are fully seeded must cover every edge: if both endpoints of an edge retain unseeded mass, neither endpoint can activate across that edge. Conversely, fully seeding a vertex cover activates every remaining role. The continuous optimum is therefore
\[ \min\left\{\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t : S\text{ is a vertex cover of }H\right\}. \]
With equal masses, this is precisely the discrete Length problem scaled by population size. Partial seeding within a role cannot improve the optimum, but the mass objective remains meaningful and represents a genuine high-multiplicity regime.

That leaves a valid Class B mirror: hardness transfers through the role graph. Its close connection to Vertex Cover may make the result quick to establish, and its narrow network assumptions may limit its reach. Those are modest novelty and scope concerns; they do not show that the continuous question lacks value. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor, so the case against a worthwhile mirror is weak here.
