The continuous mirror question

For a fixed λ ∈ [0,1), let μ be a finite-support probability distribution over resident locations \(x \in [0,1]\), and let the predicted site be \(p ∈ {0,1}\). Does the endpoint rule that chooses the predicted endpoint when its side's mass plus λ is at least the other side's mass, and otherwise chooses the opposite endpoint, remain group-strategyproof against every coalition of positive mass and satisfy robustness \((3+λ)/(1-λ)\) and consistency \((3-λ)/(1+λ)\), where \(W_μ(y)=∫ |y-x|\,dμ(x)\)?

The model it lives in

Types are resident locations \(x ∈ [0,1]\); the society is a finite-support distribution μ with rational masses, and \(p ∈ {0,1}\) is the predicted endpoint. The mechanism outputs an endpoint by comparing the masses on either side of \(1/2\), with λ added to the predicted side and ties favoring the prediction. Welfare is per-capita distance, \(W_μ(y)=∫ |y-x|\,dμ(x)\); robustness compares its value at the mechanism's output with \(\max_y W_μ(y)\) for arbitrary predictions, and consistency does so when \(p\) is optimal. Group strategyproofness rules out a positive-mass coalition whose members all strictly gain by jointly changing their reported locations.

The objection that survived

The mass rule and its guarantees follow by replacing counts with weights, so the extension may add little complexity theory; the proponent acknowledges the narrow regime but does not answer this concern about research yield.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the one-dimensional segment case of Theorem 1: the endpoint mechanism, its robustness and consistency guarantees, and group strategyproofness. It leaves the square, circle, tree, dual-preference, and frontier results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a real, narrow mirror here. The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or FPT. Its strongest clean anchor is an explicit mechanism guarantee; that makes this an algorithmic mechanism-design mirror, not a complexity-class result.

Take a corridor with only two permitted sites for an obnoxious facility, the endpoints \(0\) and \(1\). Many residents live in the corridor, and residents at the same location have identical distance-based utility. An instance is a finite list of distinct rational locations \(x_i\) and population fractions \(\mu_i>0\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), plus a predicted site \(p\in\{0,1\}\). The type is a resident’s location \(x_i\); its mass is the fraction of the population there. This is plausible in a large residential area with many residents in the same buildings: the number of people can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct locations, while the type fully determines their preferences in this model.

For a site \(y\), per-capita welfare is \(W_\mu(y)=\sum_i\mu_i|y-x_i|\). The mechanism chooses an endpoint, and its approximation is measured against \(\max_{y\in[0,1]}W_\mu(y)\), which is attained at an endpoint. The input society is thus continuous in the programme’s sense: it is a distribution over resident types. The paper’s spatially continuous facility space is already part of the original model; that alone would not make it a population mirror.

Lead question — Continuous Robust-Majority Design for Obnoxious Siting. For a fixed \(\lambda\in[0,1)\), does there exist a deterministic endpoint mechanism on these distributions and predictions that is mass-group-strategyproof, \((3+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)\)-robust, and \((3-\lambda)/(1+\lambda)\)-consistent? Here mass-group-strategyproof means no coalition of positive population mass can jointly misreport so that all its members strictly gain.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper’s Section 5.1. For a segment, it says Algorithm 1’s robust-majority mechanism is group-strategyproof, has robustness \((3+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)\), and has consistency \((3-\lambda)/(1+\lambda)\). The paper also gives the same ratios on the square, but that mechanism is only strategyproof there, so I do not extend this mirror to the square. In the continuous version, the one-dimensional rule replaces counts by masses: if \(p=1\), choose \(1\) when \(\mu([0,\tfrac12])+\lambda\geq\mu((\tfrac12,1])\), with ties favoring \(p\); if \(p=0\), use the symmetric threshold. This rule can be evaluated with \(O(\tau)\) rational additions and comparisons. I expect this question to be Class A: tractable. The paper’s proof already reasons through the two sides of the midpoint and their counts, so replacing normalized counts by masses is a natural extension.

A useful secondary question is the continuous endpoint robustness–consistency frontier: among deterministic mass-group-strategyproof mechanisms that output endpoints, what is the least robustness achievable at a given consistency bound? Theorem 4, proved in Section 6 using a characterization cited from earlier work, claims that for every \(0<c\leq2\) and \(\delta>0\), a mechanism that is \((1+c)\)-consistent cannot be \((1+4/c-\delta)\)-robust; it says Algorithm 1 attains the best tradeoff using \(\lambda=(2-c)/(2+c)\). The corresponding continuous question is whether the same frontier holds for mass distributions, with a proof of the lower bound and a matching mechanism. I expect a Class A structural argument: these instances reduce to the mass on either side of one threshold, rather than combinatorics in population size.

There is an important qualification to Theorem 4: its printed statement omits strategyproofness, but its proof uses the characterization of strategyproof mechanisms. Without that premise, the claimed lower bound cannot hold as stated: a mechanism that simply chooses an optimal endpoint is both robust and consistent with ratio \(1\). So the continuous frontier question must explicitly include mass-group-strategyproofness; the paper does not establish that mass version literally. Theorem 1 remains the sound lead anchor even if Theorem 4 is set aside.

The authors should recognize the lead mirror: the decision, utility, prediction, and incentive concern are unchanged, while sums over residents become expectations under a population distribution. The weakest point is also clear: in a nonatomic society, unilateral incentive constraints can become vacuous. Positive-mass group strategyproofness preserves a meaningful strategic question and is supported by Theorem 1, but it is not identical to every finite-coalition guarantee. The case is therefore strongest for this one-dimensional, two-site regime; it does not justify a mirror of all the paper’s topologies or a claim that the paper settles a broader complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about fit with ChoCo’s computational gap. The paper’s Theorems 1–5 give mechanism guarantees: approximation ratios, incentive properties, and a robustness–consistency bound. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem such as bribery, control, or winner robustness. The proposed mirror of Theorem 1 asks whether a mechanism with specified guarantees exists. That is a legitimate algorithmic mechanism-design question, but it does not by itself open the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo is set up to chart.

For Theorem 1, that concern is especially sharp because the natural population model has almost no computational structure. Let \(a=\mu([0,\tfrac12])\) and \(b=\mu((\tfrac12,1])\). The proposed rule chooses the predicted endpoint \(1\) when \(a+\lambda\ge b\), and the symmetric rule applies when the prediction is \(0\). Its incentive and approximation proofs translate by replacing counts with masses; its running time is just the time to sum those masses. Residents at the same address or building are a plausible repeated type, so this is not a convincing case that high multiplicity is meaningless. But the resulting mirror asks for a weighted restatement of a threshold rule, not a new optimization or complexity problem. That limits its value for this programme; it does not show that the continuous question is ill-posed.

Theorem 4 offers little more computational substance. Its printed statement omits strategyproofness, though its proof relies on it. The proponent’s repaired version includes mass-group-strategyproofness. For the endpoint setting, any such continuous mechanism can be restricted to societies with \(n\) equally weighted atoms. Its restriction is a finite group-strategyproof mechanism, so Theorem 4’s lower bound applies; the weighted threshold rule supplies the matching upper bound for arbitrary masses. Thus the repaired frontier follows through the finite case and a direct mass extension, without a new algorithmic obstacle. This is a low-value argument against the mirror, though: a discrete-to-continuous correspondence can itself be a worthwhile result under the programme’s criteria.

The broader objections do not hold up. Co-located residents with identical distance utilities make a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the objective aggregates their utilities rather than tracking individual identities. Positive-mass coalitions also preserve a meaningful incentive question. Extending the question to arbitrary facility outputs or to other spaces might produce a richer mechanism-design problem, but that would be a new formulation; I cannot use the paper’s endpoint lower bound to rule it out.

So the best case against is that these anchors offer little to ChoCo’s specific complexity agenda: Theorem 1 yields a near-immediate weighted rule, and Theorem 4’s corrected frontier largely inherits the finite result. But I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The one-dimensional population regime is plausible, and the proposed mirrors are well-posed. The negative case is weak precisely where it needs to be strongest: it argues for low research yield, not for the absence of a worthwhile mirror.
