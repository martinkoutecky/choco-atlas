The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), a laminar family \(\mathcal E\subseteq 2^T\) of coalition templates, strict rankings \(\succ_t\) over each type's incident templates, and rational weights \(w_e\), choose coalition masses \(x_e\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\le \mu_t\). Define \(q_{t,e}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{f\ni t:\,f\succeq_t e}x_f\); stability requires every template \(e\) to have some \(t\in e\) with \(q_{t,e}(x)=0\). Find a stable allocation maximizing \(\sum_{e\in\mathcal E}w_ex_e\); for the one-positive-weight case, decide whether a distinguished singleton template can receive its type's full mass.

The model it lives in

A population with mass \(\mu_t\) of each complete agent type \(t\), and laminar coalition templates that use one agent of each listed type. The decision variables \(x_e\) are masses of coalitions formed under each template; each infinitesimal agent joins at most one coalition. Feasibility is \(\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\le\mu_t\), stability rules out templates with positive willing mass at every required type, and the objective is maximum total template weight.

The objection that survived

The SAT construction uses clause templates spanning every type, which is engineered and weakens the nested-workforce illustration; this does not undermine the abstract type-template problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I find most convincing is the paper’s maximum-weight stable matching on laminar hypergraphs, viewed as matching in a large organization with repeated worker profiles. A type is a complete worker profile: the same role, the same incident project or contract templates, and the same strict ranking of those templates. Mass is the fraction of the workforce with that profile. The regime is one where many workers share a relatively small set of profiles, so the number of workers \(N\) is much larger than the number of types \(\tau\). A project template may require workers from several profiles, and the allowed profile sets form a laminar hierarchy. Nested teams in a large firm are one plausible setting.

My anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper by a reduction from CNF-SAT. It says that finding a maximum-weight stable hypergraph matching is NP-complete even on laminar hypergraphs when every edge has weight \(0\) except one edge of weight \(1\). In the reduction, a stable matching containing that distinguished edge exists exactly when the formula is satisfiable. I would anchor on this hardness result because it asks whether the paper’s discrete optimization difficulty survives when the population itself becomes divisible.

I would call the mirror Laminar Continuous Maximum-Weight Stable Hypergraph Matching. Its input is a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a finite set of coalition templates \(E\). A template \(e\) is a multiset of types, with \(a_{te}\) members of type \(t\); the supports \(\{t:a_{te}>0\}\) form a laminar family. Each type has a strict ranking over its incident templates, and each template has a weight \(w_e\). A solution is a rational mass \(x_e\geq 0\) of coalitions formed using each template, satisfying
\[ \sum_{e\in E} a_{te}x_e\leq \mu_t \qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
Thus each infinitesimal worker participates in at most one coalition, while the aggregate mass assigned to a template can be fractional.

Stability means that no template can form a positive mass of blocking coalitions. More precisely, define
\[ q_{t,e}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{f\succeq_t e}a_{tf}x_f, \]
the mass of type \(t\) that is unmatched or assigned to a less-preferred template than \(e\). A template \(e\) blocks at positive mass exactly when \(q_{t,e}(x)>0\) for every type it requires: atomless pools then supply a common positive mass of willing participants for all its roles. So a solution is stable if, for every \(e\), some required type \(t\) has \(q_{t,e}(x)=0\). The task is to find a stable allocation maximizing \(\sum_e w_ex_e\). The anchor’s one-weighted-edge case becomes maximizing the mass assigned to that distinguished template; its decision version asks whether that mass can reach a specified rational threshold.

I would first expect hardness to transfer: the SAT construction puts its combinatorics in the types, template incidences, and preference orders, rather than in the sheer number of copies of each type. But Theorem 4.2 does not establish that transfer. A continuous pool can split among templates, and that may break the reduction’s all-or-nothing choices. The central question is whether the stability constraints still force enough integral structure to encode the formula, or whether fractional allocation makes the problem tractable. Natural follow-up questions are the complexity of the threshold version, whether hardness persists with bounded \(\tau\) or bounded template size, and when the optimal continuous value agrees with the normalized high-multiplicity discrete optimum.

This mirror covers the paper’s laminar maximum-weight stable matching hardness result; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s capacity algorithms or its other hypergraph classes. The paper’s authors should recognize the underlying object: it remains their stable matching problem, with indivisible participation for each worker and blocking defined by the same preferences. The change is that a project template can serve a divisible mass of repeated workers.

My weakest point is that the theorem’s reduction uses finely differentiated vertices, and grouping them into repeated types may change both laminarity and the stability behavior. A plausible large-workforce regime supports the mirror, but the paper’s hardness proof is not itself a proof that the continuous mirror is hard. That gap is precisely what makes this a worthwhile continuization question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong case against the proponent’s anchor. Theorem 4.2 is a named computational hardness result, and its SAT reduction appears to survive a natural type-mass version of the problem.

The best objection is that the reduction gives every vertex a clause-sensitive preference list and incidence pattern. Its complete types are therefore distinct roles, with their number growing with the formula. In many real matching settings, workers or students also have individual preferences, and projects may involve specific people rather than interchangeable profiles. Turning those edges into templates changes which coalitions can form.

That objection does not rule out the proposed mirror. Take types \(z,v_i,\bar v_i\), each with mass \(\mu=1/(2n+1)\), and copy each type \(K\) times. Keep the reduction’s templates and preference orders: \(e_z\) uses \(z\), \(e_i\) and \(\bar e_i\) use the corresponding variable pair, and each clause template uses all types. Their supports remain laminar. If a stable solution assigns the full mass \(\mu\) to \(e_z\), no clause template can use \(z\). Each clause then needs a literal type whose entire mass is assigned to its satisfying variable template; otherwise that clause has willing mass at every required type and blocks. The paired variable templates cannot both receive full mass, so these choices give a consistent satisfying assignment. Conversely, a satisfying assignment gives a stable allocation with \(x_{e_z}=\mu\). Thus the threshold question “can a stable allocation achieve \(x_{e_z}\geq\mu\)?” appears to encode SAT even when each profile has arbitrarily many indistinguishable agents.

The remaining negative case is about application quality: a workforce with repeated roles and interchangeable project participants is plausible, while the reduction’s global clause templates are engineered. That limits how persuasive this particular illustration may be. It does not defeat the abstract mirror, and I see no more fundamental obstruction. Under the stated burden, the anchor survives; the honest negative case is weak.
