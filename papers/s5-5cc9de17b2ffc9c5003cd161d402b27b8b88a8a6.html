The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Algorithm 1 and Tables 2–4 report simulations and pivotality estimates; Appendix 7.1 gives an asymptotic calculation, but it is not a numbered complexity result. I can’t honestly attach a mirror to an anchor the paper does not have.

The strongest positive candidate I see is a continuous version of the paper’s May-model pivotality calculation. Take \(K\) states with electoral weights \(w_k\) and population shares \(\alpha_k>0\), where \(\sum_k\alpha_k=1\). The types are \((k,D)\) and \((k,R)\): a voter’s state and preference. Let \(X_k\) be independent uniform random variables on \([0,1]\), and let the society have masses \(\mu_{k,D}=\alpha_kX_k\) and \(\mu_{k,R}=\alpha_k(1-X_k)\). Each state’s majority determines its electoral-college vote; the states then use the paper’s weighted-majority rule.

Call this May continuum influence computation. Given \(K\), the rational shares \(\alpha_k\), and the weights \(w_k\), compute for every state \(k\) the marginal change in the probability that \(D\) wins when an amount \(\varepsilon\) of the whole population’s mass moves from type \((k,R)\) to \((k,D)\). More precisely, if \(\rho(\mu)\) is the probability that \(D\) wins under the paper’s tie rule, the output is \(\Lambda_k=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}(\mathbb E[\rho(\mu^{k,\varepsilon})]-\mathbb E[\rho(\mu)])/ \varepsilon\), where \(\mu^{k,\varepsilon}\) is the society after that transfer. A solution is the exact vector \((\Lambda_1,\ldots,\Lambda_K)\), or an approximation with a stated error.

This is a plausible mirror because the random mass proportions are the continuum limit of the paper’s May model: each state has its own uniform latent support for \(D\). Let \(\beta_k\) be the expected change in the top-tier probability that \(D\) wins when state \(k\)’s representative switches from \(R\) to \(D\), averaging over the other states’ independently fair outcomes. The transfer changes state \(k\)’s local outcome with probability \(\varepsilon/\alpha_k\), so \(\Lambda_k=\beta_k/\alpha_k\). The paper’s Section 2.2 gives the matching finite-population factorization \(M(i,n)=B(k)/n_k\) for a voter in state \(k\); when \(n_k/N\to\alpha_k\), this yields \(N M(i,N)\to B(k)/\alpha_k\). That is a strong bridge to its own model, though it is not a numbered anchor.

I would expect exact computation to retain the top-tier swing-count difficulty as \(K\) grows: the continuous limit removes the population size but leaves the weighted state game’s pivotality calculation. For the paper’s fixed \(K=51\), or for approximation variants, the algorithmic picture could differ. This candidate covers the May-model reversal in Section 3.4 and the asymptotic discussion in Appendix 7.1; it does not mirror the paper’s Banzhaf and Shapley–Shubik simulation results.

The soft spot is real: a voter in an atomless society cannot literally be pivotal. The derivative is a mass-scaled marginal influence, and the finite-population correspondence is what makes it recognizable as the paper’s question. Also, this remains an exploratory mirror rather than an anchor-based case: the paper’s central conclusions are simulation results, not named computational-complexity results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper offers no qualifying anchor. Its central results are simulated comparisons of voter influence under three probability models. It gives no named computational problem with a theorem about complexity or algorithms. Algorithm 1 describes a simulation, and Appendix 7.1 studies asymptotics; neither states a complexity result to mirror. Under the brief’s anchor rule, that is decisive.

The proposed May mirror is mathematically coherent. In the continuum limit, a state’s \(D\)-share is uniform on \([0,1]\); transferring \(\varepsilon\) mass changes its winner with probability about \(\varepsilon/\alpha_k\). The resulting derivative \(\Lambda_k=\beta_k/\alpha_k\) matches the paper’s finite-population scaling. So I cannot honestly dismiss it as a bad model or a mistaken limit.

Its weakness as a mirror is that the paper does not pose an algorithmic problem about computing that derivative. Once the May distribution is fixed, the proposed quantity reduces to the upper-tier swing probability \(\beta_k\), scaled by the population share. That is a new computational question suggested by the paper’s numerical analysis, rather than a continuous version of a named computational result in it. More elaborate questions about changing the electoral rule or making voters robust would add a new problem of their own.

That makes the negative case strong at the anchor boundary and weak as a universal claim. If the programme accepts computational questions newly prompted by simulation results, the May construction survives as a plausible mirror; the paper itself gives no basis for ruling out every such extension.
