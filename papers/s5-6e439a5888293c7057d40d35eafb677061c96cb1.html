The continuous mirror question

Given a finite list of societies \(h\), each specified by rational masses \(\mu^h_{\theta,r}\) over bidder classes \(\theta\) and signals \(r\), and explicit positive values or costs \(v^h_{\theta,r}\) or \(c^h_{\theta,r}\), compute a truthful selection policy minimizing the worst-case approximation ratio across societies. The policy chooses intensities \(q^h_{\theta,r}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{\theta,r}\mu^h_{\theta,r}q^h_{\theta,r}=1\); truthfulness is defined by incentive constraints on per-capita utility rates, holding the aggregate society fixed.

The model it lives in

A type \(\theta\) is a complete shared bidder description, while \(r\) is the bidder’s signal. The population has mass \(\mu^h_{\theta,r}\), and \(q^h_{\theta,r}\) is the per-capita selection intensity, so the winner is drawn from the resulting unit selection measure. Values or costs may depend on the society \(h\); payments and utility rates implement truthful reporting. Optimize the worst-case value or cost ratio over the listed societies.

The objection that survived

In a literal atomless model each individual has zero probability of selection, so the proposal needs normalized utility rates or a finite-replica interpretation for its truthfulness constraints to remain meaningful.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s randomized Val and Cst optimization questions in a finite-scenario, type-based market. It leaves the deterministic hardness result of Theorem 4 and the oracle query lower bound of Theorem 5 outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what regularity conditions does this normalized incentive model arise as a limit of finite populations with repeated types?
  • For succinctly described values or costs over the full simplex of aggregate signal distributions, can the resulting optimization problem be solved efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is for the paper’s randomized mechanisms. Call it Interdependent Mass Val/Cst: replace the finite list of bidders with a population distribution over bidder types, while keeping the object indivisible and selecting exactly one person. The distribution describes the society; a selection measure describes who may win.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that, for arbitrary value and cost functions, one can solve Val and Cst in \(N^{O(1)}\) time, where \(N=nk^n b\) is the explicit input size, \(n\) is the number of bidders, \(k\) the number of signals, and \(b\) the maximum bit length of a value or cost. Val computes a truthful randomized allocation rule minimizing its worst-case value approximation ratio; Cst does the same for the cost ratio. The paper’s proof expresses both problems as linear programs.

Here is a continuous population version. Let \(\Theta\) be a finite set of bidder classes, with rational masses \(p_\theta\) summing to one. A class \(\theta\) specifies the bidder’s complete problem-relevant description: its value or cost function, signal semantics, and any other relevant parameters. Members of a class share that description; their private signals \(r\in[k]\) may differ. An admissible society \(h\) is a distribution \(\mu^h_{\theta,r}\) over class and signal, with \(\sum_r\mu^h_{\theta,r}=p_\theta\). The input lists \(H\) possible societies and rational values \(v^h_{\theta,r}\), or costs \(c^h_{\theta,r}\), for each class, signal, and society. These values may depend on the aggregate signal distribution, so the interdependence remains.

The decision variable is a nonnegative selection intensity \(q^h_{\theta,r}\) for each class and reported signal in each society. The mass \(\mu^h_{\theta,r}q^h_{\theta,r}\) is the probability measure from which the single winner is drawn, so feasibility is \(\sum_{\theta,r}\mu^h_{\theta,r}q^h_{\theta,r}=1\). This is a way to select one person from a large population, not to divide the object. Truthfulness requires that, for each class and society, intensity is nondecreasing in the agent’s value across signals; for costs, it is nonincreasing in the agent’s cost. These are the paper’s monotonicity conditions applied with the aggregate society held fixed. They allow payments to implement truthful reporting.

The two objectives are the worst-case ratios across the listed societies: for values, \(\max_h \max_{(\theta,r):\mu^h_{\theta,r}>0}v^h_{\theta,r}/\sum_{\theta,r}\mu^h_{\theta,r}q^h_{\theta,r}v^h_{\theta,r}\); for costs, \(\max_h \sum_{\theta,r}\mu^h_{\theta,r}q^h_{\theta,r}c^h_{\theta,r}/\min_{(\theta,r):\mu^h_{\theta,r}>0}c^h_{\theta,r}\). The required solution is an optimal truthful intensity policy and corresponding payments. For this finite-scenario version, the feasibility, truthfulness, and ratio constraints give an explicit linear program, with size polynomial in \(H,\tau,k\), and the input bit length \(L\). I expect this stated problem to be tractable, in Class A.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large market of contractors with many repeated capability and cost profiles, where each profile has a signal about project conditions and the pool’s signal composition affects contractors’ costs. The agent count can be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct complete descriptions. The mirror retains the paper’s central question—how to choose a truthful winner with a good worst-case approximation—while making society itself a distribution.

The finite list of admissible societies is a deliberate boundary of the stated problem. A natural next question is whether the list can be replaced by the full simplex of aggregate signal distributions when values and costs have succinct rational descriptions. That becomes a semi-infinite optimization problem; its tractability depends on whether the relevant separation over the simplex is efficient. Another question is how quickly finite populations with repeated types converge to the mass model.

I would not use the paper’s deterministic hardness result as an anchor for this mirror. It proves NP-hardness for four named bidders, but deterministic selection has no direct type-mass limit: in an atomless population, selecting one individual is a zero-mass event, whereas the selection measure above represents randomized selection. The paper’s hardness construction also puts its combinatorics in the four bidders’ signal coordinates, not in population multiplicity. My weakest point is therefore that this is a meaningful continuous version of randomized Val/Cst, but it does not continue arbitrary labeled-bidder dependencies or explain what happens to the deterministic hardness result. The broader full-simplex problem remains open; Theorem 3 motivates the LP route but does not establish its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a real computational result, so there is no decisive “nothing to mirror” objection. But it is a result about a finite set of named bidders: the input gives values at every signal profile \(s\in[k]^n\), and the allocation rule has a coordinate for each bidder and profile. Its polynomial bound is in the size of that explicit table, \(N=nk^n b\). It does not establish a bound in the number of bidder types or in the support of a population distribution.

The proposed mirror gets that smaller description by assuming the valuation model is anonymous within each class and depends on the aggregate signal distribution. That is a substantial restriction on the paper’s arbitrary functions \(v_i:[k]^n\to\mathbb R_{>0}\): it works only when relabeling members of a class leaves the relevant values and incentives unchanged. The listed-society version also gives a separate allocation policy for each \(h\). Its LP may be perfectly valid, but Theorem 3 does not supply its tractability; the LP follows from the newly chosen finite-scenario formulation. Extending it to a policy on the full simplex would require a succinct function class and an efficient way to handle the resulting continuum of constraints. Without those choices, there is no single complexity problem to mirror.

There is also a gap in the atomless interpretation. The variable \(q^h_{\theta,r}\) is a selection intensity, not an individual’s probability of winning. In a finite population of size \(M\), the corresponding probability would be \(q^h_{\theta,r}/M\); expected utility and payments per person then shrink with \(M\). In the literal atomless model, an individual has zero probability of being selected, so ordinary individual utility and incentive compatibility can collapse. One can repair this by defining incentive constraints on normalized utility rates, or by taking a finite-replica limit. Either repair may be reasonable, but it is extra structure, and the paper’s theorem does not show that its finite-agent truthfulness notion passes to that limit.

These points defeat Theorem 3 as direct support for the proposed anchor: the proposed LP describes a restricted anonymous model, and its nondegenerate incentive interpretation needs to be specified. They do not defeat every improved mirror. A large market of genuinely interchangeable contractors, with values depending on market composition, is plausible; a normalized finite-replica model could make the question precise. So the honest negative case is limited: the paper does not yet furnish a clean, general population mirror, and the particular formulation risks being a small LP exercise after strong modeling choices. I cannot make the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The plausible replica repair is where this case against is weakest.
