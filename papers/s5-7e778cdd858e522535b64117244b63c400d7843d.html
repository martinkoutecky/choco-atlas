The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an eligible named computational result to anchor a case for a continuous mirror. The paper presents the greedy and bubble algorithms and evaluates them by simulation, but it does not state a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity result or a computational formulation.

The closest candidate is Theorem 5 (Theorem 9, [30]), which the paper cites from earlier work. It says that projecting an additive pairwise comparison matrix onto the subspace where alternatives \(i\) and \(j\) tie leaves every other alternative’s row sum unchanged, and sets the two tied alternatives’ row sums to the average of their original row sums. That is a useful property of the projection used by the algorithms, but it is not a named complexity result of this paper. Theorem 1 (Theorem 5, [30]) is likewise a cited basis result.

A population model of assessors whose types are complete pairwise comparison matrices is conceivable, but the paper does not define that aggregate problem or analyze its computation. Presenting it as an anchored mirror would go beyond the evidence the requested standard allows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no surviving continuous anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 5 are results about the geometry of a single additive pairwise-comparison matrix, and both are imported from [30]. Neither establishes a computational problem or its complexity.

The strongest possible continuization of Theorem 5 would take a distribution of assessors’ matrices and project its average onto the subspace where two alternatives tie. But projection and averaging are linear: projecting each matrix and then averaging gives the same result as averaging first and projecting once. The row-sum identities then carry over directly. That produces no new population-level computational question. Theorem 1’s basis construction has the same limitation: it describes the tie subspace regardless of whether the matrix represents one assessor or an average.

The best candidate beyond those results is a mass version of the paper’s greedy or bubble manipulation. One could imagine a large assessment exercise in which many people submit additive comparison matrices, and ask for the least costly mass of assessors to persuade to change their judgments so a chosen alternative leads. That is plausible, especially given the paper’s discussion of group decision-making. But it requires an aggregation rule for multiple matrices and a cost for changing an assessor’s full matrix; the paper specifies neither. Its algorithms alter entries of one matrix, and its ARSI measures gaps within that matrix. Applying those procedures to an aggregate would carry over the matrix calculation, while the question of changing a population of assessors would be a newly formulated intervention problem. The paper gives no result that connects the two.

So the strongest negative case is about the absence of a computational result to mirror, not about the likely answer to some newly defined mass-manipulation problem. The limitation is real: a large-group assessor model is credible enough that I cannot rule out a worthwhile adjacent project, and the paper’s group-decision context gives that proposal some footing. What the text does not support is treating it as a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s results.
