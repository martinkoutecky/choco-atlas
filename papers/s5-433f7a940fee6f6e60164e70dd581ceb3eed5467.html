The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that a very natural continuous-population problem exists here—but this paper does not contain a qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s strict rule.

The mirror would take a finite set of utility types \(T\). Type \(t\) has a complete utility vector \(u^t\in[0,1]^m\), and mass \(\mu_t\ge 0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type-\(t\) voter generates a ranking through the Plackett–Luce model with parameter \(\beta\). Thus

\[ p_{j,j'}=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\sigma_\beta(u^t_j-u^t_{j'}) \]

is the population probability that \(j\) beats \(j'\), while

\[ U_j=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t u^t_j \]

is candidate \(j\)’s social utility. The chosen candidate is produced by a voting rule applied to the population statistics; the objective is the utilitarian distortion \(\max_j U_j/U_W\).

A plausible regime is a very large electorate of annotators, voters, or preference-feedback providers with only a small number of recurring latent utility profiles: \(n\gg |T|\), perhaps even \(|T|=3\). Agents sharing a type have the same utilities and noise parameter, while their rankings remain independent Plackett–Luce samples. This is a sensible high-multiplicity model for the paper’s RLHF motivation. Rational masses \(\mu_t=a_t/N\) correspond directly to \(a_t\) voters of type \(t\), and empirical pairwise frequencies converge to the population quantities as \(N\) grows.

My lead conditional anchor would be Theorem 8.2, proved in this paper. It states:

For every fixed precision \(\rho>0\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is \(\beta_0(\rho,\varepsilon)\) such that, for every \(\beta\ge\beta_0\) and every deterministic \(\rho\)-finite-precision tournament-based voting rule \(f\), some three-candidate election with utilities in \([0,1]\) has distortion at least \((5/8-\varepsilon)\beta\).

The proof uses three voter types and constructs a symmetric coarsened tournament cycle. Cyclic relabelling makes the rule choose the low-welfare role in one of the instances. In the population formulation, the same construction uses masses \(p\), \(q\), and \(1-p-q\), with utility vectors \((1-\eta,1,0)\), \((1,0,\delta/\beta)\), and \((0,0,\delta/\beta)\). Hence the theorem is unusually well suited to a high-multiplicity mirror: the witness already has only three types.

The corresponding continuous problem could be called Continuous Finite-Precision PL Distortion Witness.

Given \(\beta\), \(\rho\), a deterministic \(\rho\)-finite-precision rule \(f\), and a threshold \(D\), ask whether there exists a finite-support society \((T,\mu,u)\) such that, with

\[ W^\infty_\rho(j,j') = \left\lfloor \frac{p_{j,j'}-\frac12}{\rho} \right\rfloor, \]

the rule selects \(W=f(W^\infty_\rho)\), some candidate \(B\) maximizes \(U_j\), and

\[ \frac{U_B}{U_W}\ge D. \]

A solution is the type set, rational masses, and utility vectors certifying these conditions. Equivalently, one can ask to compute

\[ \operatorname{OPT}_{\rho,\beta}(f) = \sup_{(T,\mu,u)} \frac{\max_j U_j}{U_{f(W^\infty_\rho)}}. \]

Theorem 8.2 says that this witness problem has value at least \((5/8-\varepsilon)\beta\) for every such rule when \(\beta\) is sufficiently large. The natural further questions are whether the supremum has a bounded-support optimum, whether it can be computed by a convex-hull or separation formulation, and whether exact optimization is tractable or becomes hard because of the nonlinear Plackett–Luce statistics and finite-precision cells. Any hardness here would be continuum-specific: the theorem uses only three candidates, so there is no obvious agenda-hardness source to transfer from a discrete problem.

The mirror is therefore faithful and mathematically plausible. The authors would recognize it immediately, because their own Sections 2 and 8 already work with a distribution over utility vectors, population pairwise probabilities, and the \(n\to\infty\) limit.

That is also its decisive weakness. This paper has already made the population continuous. The proposed “mirror” mostly makes its implicit distributional model explicit and asks computational questions the paper did not ask. Moreover, Theorem 8.2 is a distortion lower bound, not an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or approximation-algorithm theorem. Theorem 6.3 and Theorem 7.1 are likewise analytic distortion bounds; Lemma 2.11 is cited from prior work. None is a qualifying computational anchor under the programme’s gate.

So the best positive verdict is: a coherent, author-recognizable continuous-population computational extension exists, plausibly probing a Class A/Class C boundary. But it is not a compliant paper-anchored ChoCo mirror, because the paper contains no named computational-complexity result and its central model is already a continuous population model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has found a legitimate population model, but not a qualifying ChoCo mirror. The decisive problem is that this paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 8.2 is a lower bound on distortion for a class of rules, not a complexity, algorithmic, approximation, or parameterized result. Theorems 6.3 and 7.1 are likewise analytic distortion bounds. There is therefore no paper-level computational problem whose high-multiplicity version the proposed mirror could be.

Theorem 8.2 is also already formulated at the population level. Definition 2.9 takes a supremum over distributions \(\mathcal V\); Section 2.4 replaces empirical margins and utilities by population quantities; and the proof of Theorem 8.2 uses three utility types with masses \(p\), \(q\), and \(1-p-q\). The \(n\)-voter election is only a sampling device whose statistics converge to those quantities. Writing those masses as \(\mu_t\) and replacing sums by expectations does not continuize the society: it merely rewrites the theorem’s existing limit model.

The proposed optimization,

\[ \sup_{(T,\mu,u)} \frac{\max_j U_j}{U_{f(W^\infty_\rho)}}, \]

is therefore not a mirror of Theorem 8.2. It is essentially the paper’s own worst-case distortion definition with an explicit witness-search interpretation. Asking whether a witness reaches a threshold does not repair this: the paper supplies an analytic witness, while no discrete computational task is being relaxed. If \(f\) is arbitrary, the proposed problem also lacks a meaningful finite input encoding; if \(f\) is fixed and explicitly represented, the problem becomes a new distributionally robust optimization problem, not a continuization of a result in the paper.

The strongest possible rescue would be to insist that optimizing over arbitrary utility distributions creates a genuinely difficult continuous problem. But even that difficulty would not be population multiplicity. For fixed \(m\), the society affects the rule only through finitely many moments such as

\[ \mathbb E_\mu[u_j] \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbb E_\mu[\sigma_\beta(u_j-u_{j'})]. \]

Finite-precision cells and winner conditions impose linear constraints on these moments, while the welfare objective is linear-fractional. Thus arbitrary distributions can be replaced by bounded-support distributions in the corresponding finite-dimensional convex hull. The paper’s three-type construction is already exactly such a bounded-support witness. Any residual hardness would come from optimizing over the nonlinear utility-to-moment map or from encoding \(f\), not from replacing many named voters by mass.

Changing the type space does not help. Ranking types alone lose the cardinal utilities needed to evaluate distortion; utility-vector types return precisely to \(\mathcal V\). Adding identities, individual prices, or idiosyncratic utility vectors would produce a different problem, but then the repeated-type regime disappears. Adding mass-control, perturbation, or bribery would be a potentially interesting new ChoCo project, yet it would be an invention inspired by the paper, not a mirror of any result it proves.

So the negative case is strong under the programme’s actual gate: the paper already contains the continuous population object, and it contains no computational theorem to mirror. I would not claim that no future computational work based on this model could ever be worthwhile; the distributionally robust optimization problem may itself be research-worthy. But that concession undermines the proponent’s claim only slightly: it confirms that the proposed anchor is a new problem layered onto an already-continuous analytic paper, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
