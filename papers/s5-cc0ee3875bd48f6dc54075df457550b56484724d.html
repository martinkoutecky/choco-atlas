The continuous mirror question

Given finite male and female type sets \(M,W\), rational masses \(\mu^M,\mu^W\) with total masses \(1+\kappa\) and \(1\), strict ranked compatibility lists, and mass-weighted mean list length \(d\), compute the men-optimal stable mass matching \(x\) under aggregate deferred acceptance and the statistics \(R_M,R_W,\Delta_M,\Delta_W\); for a specified high-multiplicity random profile family, determine whether \(\lvert R_M-\sqrt d\rvert,\lvert R_W-\sqrt d\rvert\le d^{0.3}\) and \(\lvert\log\Delta_M+\sqrt d\rvert,\lvert\log\Delta_W+\sqrt d\rvert\le d^{0.3}\) hold when \(\Delta_M,\Delta_W>0\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided continuum matching market whose complete types encode side, compatibility list, ranking, and priority information. The decision variables are matched masses \(x_{ab}\ge0\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu^M_a\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\mu^W_b\), and compatibility constraints; the selected solution is the men-optimal stable mass matching, not a lottery over outcomes.

The objection that survived

The paper's random local profiles are almost surely unique, so a finite-type mass model will not automatically reproduce the rejection-chain mechanism or the \(\log^2 n\) threshold without an explicit stochastic profile family and possibly an atom-resolution parameter.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a single-anchor Class A mirror of the paper’s stable-matching computation. It is not a claim that this paper already contains a continuous-computational-social-choice result: none of its numbered results is an NP-hardness, \(P\), FPT, or similar complexity classification. The anchor below is therefore a named structural-computational theorem, not a complexity theorem.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1 (Moderately Connected Markets), proved in this paper. It considers random markets with \(n+k\) men and \(n\) women, \(|k|\le n^{1-\epsilon}\), \(d=\omega(1)\), and \(d=o(\log^2 n)\). For the men-optimal stable matching, with high probability,

\[ \left|R_{\mathrm{MEN}}-\sqrt d\right|\le d^{0.3}, \qquad \left|R_{\mathrm{WOMEN}}-\sqrt d\right|\le d^{0.3}, \]

and

\[ \left|\log\delta_m-\log\left(ne^{-\sqrt d}\right)\right|\le d^{0.3}, \qquad \left|\log\delta_w-\log\left(ne^{-\sqrt d}\right)\right|\le d^{0.3}. \]

Thus both sides obtain approximately their \(\sqrt d\)-th choice, and both leave approximately \(ne^{-\sqrt d}\) agents unmatched. The paper states the success probability as at least \(1-O(e^{-d^{1/4}})\).

My continuous problem would be:

Continuum Moderately Connected Stable Matching. An instance consists of finite male and female type sets \(\mathcal M\) and \(\mathcal W\), rational mass vectors \(\mu^M,\mu^W\), and strict preference lists over compatible types. Normalize female mass to \(1\) and male mass to \(1+\kappa\), where \(\kappa\) is the mass imbalance. A type is a complete local market profile: its side, its compatibility list, its ranking of compatible counterpart types, and any priority information used by the matching mechanism.

The decision variable is a mass matching \(x=(x_{ab})\), where \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of male type \(a\) matched to female type \(b\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_b x_{ab}\le \mu^M_a, \qquad \sum_a x_{ab}\le \mu^W_b, \]

with \(x_{ab}=0\) for incompatible pairs. The selected solution is the men-optimal stable mass matching produced by continuum deferred acceptance: unmatched mass of each male type proposes down its list, and each female type retains its most-preferred incoming mass up to its capacity.

For this matching define \(R_M\) and \(R_W\) as average partner ranks, assigning an unmatched agent rank one greater than the length of its list. Let \(\Delta_M\) and \(\Delta_W\) be the unmatched male and female masses. The problem asks for the exact stable mass matching and these four statistics, and asks whether the instance satisfies the weak-competition bounds

\[ |R_M-\sqrt d|\le d^{0.3}, \qquad |R_W-\sqrt d|\le d^{0.3}, \]

\[ |\log\Delta_M+\sqrt d|\le d^{0.3}, \qquad |\log\Delta_W+\sqrt d|\le d^{0.3}. \]

This is a genuine population continuization. The variables \(x_{ab}\) describe how much mass is matched across type pairs; they do not represent lotteries over outcomes. Every infinitesimal agent receives one integral partner.

The natural high-multiplicity regime is a large recurring matching market: for example, cohorts of graduates and vacancies, or applicants and school seats, where many agents share the same eligibility set, preference template, location, credential profile, and priority class. There may be millions of agents but only hundreds or thousands of complete types, with each type carrying substantial mass. The continuous analogue of the paper’s random graph is a distribution over repeated local profiles: each type has a random-looking but explicitly recorded compatibility list and ranking. In a finite approximation with \(N\) agents, \(\kappa=k/N\), so the paper’s sublinear-imbalance regime becomes a small mass imbalance. The paper’s \(d=o(\log^2 N)\) regime is then the moderate-connectivity regime of the mirror.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The objects remain the same: a two-sided compatibility structure, ordinal preferences, stable matching, deferred acceptance, partner ranks, and unmatched agents. What changes is only the population representation: named agents are replaced by repeated types and mass. This is not a collision with the paper’s model, because the paper still has a finite random graph and identity-level agents. Its large-\(n\) asymptotics are evidence for the mirror, not already a continuous-population formulation.

I expect the finite-type continuous problem itself to be tractable, hence Class A. Continuum deferred acceptance processes each type’s list and performs mass comparisons and capacity truncations using rational arithmetic. The main research question is not whether one can compute a particular finite type realization, but whether the paper’s weak-competition characterization survives in the high-multiplicity limit: does a random type-mass society still produce \(R_M,R_W\approx\sqrt d\) and unmatched mass \(e^{-\sqrt d}\)? That would turn Theorem 1 into a genuine continuous computational result and provide a bridge back to finite elections or matching markets by rounding rational masses.

I would not anchor on Theorem 2. Its dense-regime conclusion is that the short side has rank \(O(\log n)\) while the long side has rank \(\Omega(d/\log n)\). The absolute \(n\) dependence is not determined by normalized masses alone; preserving it would require carrying an additional atom-size or resolution parameter. That is an interesting follow-up question, but a less clean continuous mirror.

The weakest point is that the paper’s randomness is genuinely agent-level. If almost every agent has a unique neighbor set and ranking, then the exact type space may be enormous, and replacing individual neighbors by counterpart cohorts may alter the coupon-collector mechanism that drives Theorem 1. This is not something to conceal. The positive case survives because the proposed mirror keeps the underlying stable-matching question intact and makes that alteration explicit: the central question is precisely whether the weak-competition phenomenon is a property of the matching structure and mass distribution, or an artifact of finite identity-level randomness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor fails at the programme’s first gate: Theorem 1 is not a computational result in the relevant sense. It is a probabilistic asymptotic characterization of the output of deferred acceptance on a random finite market. The paper gives no complexity classification, optimization problem, approximation problem, or nontrivial algorithmic obstruction to mirror. Computing a stable matching for a finite collection of homogeneous types is simply aggregate deferred acceptance. That is a legitimate problem, but it is not the computational content of Theorem 1; the theorem’s content lies in proving concentration for a random process.

More seriously, a faithful high-multiplicity version cannot preserve the paper’s random-market mechanism. A type must include the compatibility list and ranking information that affects matching. In the paper, a man’s random profile consists of a \(d\)-subset of women together with a permutation of that subset, giving roughly
\[ \binom{n}{d}d! \]
possible profiles. For \(d=\omega(1)\), almost every agent has a distinct complete profile with high probability. The same applies on the other side. Thus the faithful type representation has \(\tau=\Theta(n)\) types, each of mass about \(1/n\). That is an empirical relabelling of the finite market, not a high-multiplicity society.

Coarsening types to “agents with the same degree,” “same rank distribution,” or “same latent quality” does not solve this. The identities of the neighbors and the incidence pattern of the compatibility graph are exactly what drive the rejection chains, coupon-collector estimates, and the threshold at \(\log^2 n\). Once many women are collapsed into one positive-mass female type, an edge to that type gives access to a mass of women rather than to \(d\) individual candidates. The sparse individual graph has become a different cohort-level matching model.

One can propose that different version: finitely many recurring cohorts, each with a common compatibility list and common rankings. But then deferred acceptance is a finite mass-flow calculation, and scaling the number of agents within each cohort does not reproduce the paper’s \(n\)-dependent random occupancy process. Alternatively, let the number of types grow with \(n\) so that individual-level scarcity survives. That restores the paper’s mechanism only by reintroducing an atom-size or resolution parameter; the purported continuum is no longer doing the explanatory work.

The normalization also removes the theorem’s most important phenomenon. The paper proves
\[ \delta_m,\delta_w \approx n e^{-\sqrt d}. \]
After passing to mass, this becomes
\[ \Delta_m,\Delta_w \approx e^{-\sqrt d}. \]
In the theorem’s regime \(d=\omega(1)\), the unmatched mass tends to zero, even though the absolute number of unmatched agents diverges because \(d=o(\log^2 n)\). The distinction between “many unmatched agents” and “almost no unmatched mass” is therefore erased by the continuum limit. Holding \(d\) fixed preserves positive unmatched mass, but then one is no longer mirroring Theorem 1’s asymptotic regime.

The proposed extensions do not rescue the anchor. A continuum version of the platform’s choice of \(d\) is a scalar design exercise unless one adds a new type-level network-design problem. If one adds individual edge restrictions to make that problem nontrivial, one has restored the identity-level structure the mirror was meant to eliminate. Likewise, a mass version of Principle 1 would be a diagnostic statement about a stochastic matching model, not a computational problem.

The negative case is not airtight: school admissions and recurring labour-market cohorts are genuinely plausible high-multiplicity settings, and a fluid deferred-acceptance model could be useful analytically. Existing high-multiplicity matching work would support that modelling choice, not undermine it. But the strongest honest conclusion is that such a model would be a new continuum matching theory, while the proposed mirror of Theorem 1 is either a routine aggregate implementation, an analytic random-limit question, or a disguised return to individual agents. I would not green this paper’s anchor as a worthwhile continuous-computational mirror.
