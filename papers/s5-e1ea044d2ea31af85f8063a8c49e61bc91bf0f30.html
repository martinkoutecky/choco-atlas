The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support society with rational masses \(\mu_t\), each type’s vote between status quo \(r\) and proposal \(p\), an accepted-threshold set \(A_t\) encoded as a finite union of rational intervals, and an accepted-outcome set \(B_t\subseteq\{r,p\}\), find a threshold \(q\in[1/2,1)\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[q\in A_t\ \text{or}\ y_q\in B_t]\), where \(y_q=p\) exactly when the proposal-vote mass exceeds \(q\), and return \((q,y_q)\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of members represented by complete preference and acceptance types with rational masses; choose a supermajority threshold, determine its outcome from the types’ aggregate votes, and maximize accepted mass.

The objection that survived

The interval representation narrows the paper’s generic feasible-rule model to structured supermajority amendments.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers exact acceptance maximization for absolute disjunctivists in two-outcome supermajority amendments, with a related universal-acceptance analogue for Theorem 3; leaves the generic arbitrary-rule model, other preference structures, the acceptance-rate table, and the elicitation and strategy questions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact bit complexity of finding a maximizing threshold from binary rational masses and interval endpoints?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is credible, though bounded. The paper has no numbered theorem establishing a standard complexity classification such as P or NP-hardness. Its strongest computational anchors are mechanism-correctness and acceptance guarantees; I would use those without presenting them as complexity results.

For the population regime, picture a large association choosing whether to amend its voting constitution. Many members share the same complete rule preferences and the same conditions for accepting a decision, while the number of such types \(\tau\) stays small as membership \(n\) grows. A type includes everything the problem uses about a member: their ballot behavior, preference over supermajority thresholds, and accepted rules and outcomes. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), the fraction of members of that type. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s own rule-selection problem.

Write \(\mathcal Q=[1/2,1)\) for normalized supermajority thresholds. The finite paper uses a grid with spacing \(1/n\); letting that grid become continuous is a natural co-modification as the population grows. For status quo \(r\), proposal \(p\), and proposal-vote share \(a\), threshold \(q\) selects \(p\) if \(a>q\), and otherwise selects \(r\). The society is the continuous object: the vote share and acceptance objective are computed from type masses.

One anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says Mechanism 1 maximizes acceptance among feasible decisions for absolute disjunctivists: agents who accept a decision when either its rule is in their accepted-rule set \(R_i\) or its outcome is in their accepted-outcome set \(Y_i\). For each feasible outcome, the mechanism chooses a feasible rule producing it that satisfies the most agents who do not already accept that outcome, then returns the rule–outcome pair with the greatest total acceptance.

Its mirror is Continuous Absolute Amendment Max-Acceptance. Fix \(r,p\), a finite-support mass distribution \(\mu\), and each type’s vote between \(r\) and \(p\). A type has an accepted-threshold set \(A_t\subseteq\mathcal Q\) and accepted-outcome set \(B_t\subseteq\{r,p\}\); its mass accepts threshold \(q\) exactly when \(q\in A_t\) or the outcome selected by \(q\) lies in \(B_t\). The task is to find \(q\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[q\in A_t\ \text{or}\ F_q(V)\in B_t]\), and return \(q\) with its selected outcome. With finite unions of rational intervals as the encoding of the \(A_t\), this is expected to be Class A: the objective changes only at interval endpoints and at the proposal-vote share, so a sweep over those breakpoints finds an optimum. Theorem 1 supplies the exact optimization target; it does not itself give a polynomial-time bound.

My lead is Theorem 3, also proved here. In the paper, \(h\) is the largest feasible supermajority threshold \(z\) for which at least \(nz\) agents have ideal threshold \(\delta_i\ge z\). The theorem says that if the status quo \(r<h\), the amendment \((V_{r h},R_r,h)\) is accepted by every II-disjunctivist agent, assuming single-peaked preferences over thresholds, \(v_i\in Y_i\), and \(\delta_i\in R_i\). Here an II-disjunctivist accepts if the outcome is in \(Y_i\), or if some rule in \(R_i\) would have selected that same outcome.

The continuous question is Continuous II-Amendment Max-Acceptance. Its input is a finite-support society \(\mu\), a status quo \(r\), and complete types with rational, single-peaked threshold preferences, accepted-outcome sets \(O_t\), and accepted-procedure sets \(P_t\), represented by finite unions of rational intervals. Given \(r,p\), each type votes for its preferred rule. For a chosen proposal \(p\) and procedure threshold \(q\), type \(t\) accepts if the selected outcome is in \(O_t\), or if some \(q'\in P_t\) would select that same outcome on the vote profile. The task is to choose \(p,q\in\mathcal Q\) maximizing accepted mass and return the pair and resulting outcome.

For this mirror, define \(h(\mu)=\max\{z\in\mathcal Q:\sum_{\theta_t\ge z}\mu_t\ge z\}\), where \(\theta_t\) is type \(t\)’s ideal threshold. On instances with \(r<h(\mu)\), the continuous counterpart of Theorem 3 says the optimizer can return proposal \(p=h(\mu)\) and procedure threshold \(q=r\), attaining acceptance \(1\). The proof follows the paper’s logic: at least \(h(\mu)\) mass has peaks at or above \(h(\mu)\), so single-peakedness makes those types prefer the proposal and their ballot mass makes \(R_r\) select it. Types preferring the status quo have peaks below \(h(\mu)\); their ideal thresholds are in their accepted-procedure sets and would also select the proposal. With finite support, sorting the peaks and accumulating tail masses computes \(h(\mu)\) in \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) arithmetic operations. I expect the broader optimization problem to be Class A under the stated finite encoding too: ballots and accepted outcomes change at finitely many proposal breakpoints, after which threshold optimization is a sweep. That broader classification would need its own proof.

This case covers exact acceptance maximization for absolute disjunctivists and the universal-acceptance result for II-disjunctivist supermajority amendments. It does not claim a mirror for every preference structure or for the paper’s open elicitation and strategy questions. Further questions include whether \(r<h(\mu)\) is necessary for universal acceptance, how the guarantee changes when types violate the vote and peak conditions, and how rounding behaves when a continuous instance is represented on the finite \(1/n\) threshold grid.

The weak point is the strength of the type assumption. Grouping members requires matching their full relevant preferences and acceptance sets, not merely their votes; in some real populations that could make \(\tau\) large. Also, the paper’s theorems do not establish a complexity landscape, and the natural continuous mirrors here may be easy one-dimensional optimization problems. That limits the novelty of the complexity claim, but the mirror remains the same constitutional amendment and acceptance-maximization problem the authors study, with mass replacing counts in a sensible large-population regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that neither anchor identifies the kind of computational question ChoCo is meant to chart. The paper proves that certain mechanisms maximize acceptance and that a particular amendment can be universally accepted. It does not give a complexity classification, and its general model does not specify compact encodings for feasible rules or agents’ satisfying sets. That leaves no defined input size or pricing problem for the continuous version to investigate.

For Theorem 1, the mass version is immediate once a society is given. For a decision \((V,R,y)\), its acceptance is simply
\[ W(R,y)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[(V,R,y)\in D_t]. \]
The proof partitions agents by whether they accept the outcome or the rule; replacing counts with type masses preserves that proof. This is a coherent weighted approval problem, and repeated complete preference types are a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But the theorem’s mechanism still ranges over the feasible rules and the rule sets each type accepts. The paper gives no representation or oracle for those objects. If the continuous mirror retains that generality, its computational content remains unspecified. If it instead uses the proponent’s interval encoding over thresholds, it becomes a new one-dimensional optimization problem whose breakpoints come from that added encoding. That may be a useful exercise, but it does not expose a computational gap in Theorem 1.

Theorem 3 is the stronger anchor. A finite-support society gives a natural candidate,
\[ h(\mu)=\max\left\{z:\sum_{\theta_t\ge z}\mu_t\ge z\right\}, \]
and the paper’s acceptance argument carries over when \(r<h(\mu)\). Sorting the ideal thresholds and accumulating their masses computes the candidate. A large association with repeated constitutional preference types is plausible, so dismissing this mirror on the ground that members are individuated would be a mistake.

The harder objection is about what this extension contributes. The theorem is a sufficient guarantee for a constructed amendment, not a complexity result for a specified computational problem. Its continuous proof substitutes mass for counts in the same inequalities. To turn it into the broader max-acceptance problem in the proponent’s case, one must additionally encode how every type’s accepted-procedure set varies over a continuous threshold space. Finite unions of rational intervals make that optimization manageable, but they are an extra modeling choice, and the paper does not identify a difficult search problem that the choice resolves. A distribution over general peaks or acceptance sets would need its own finite representation before the question even has a defined runtime.

So the negative case is not that continuous populations are implausible, that high-multiplicity work is prior art, or that the continuous answer would be unchanged. It is that Theorem 1’s continuous counterpart is a weighted restatement without an encoded computational core, while Theorem 3’s is a measure-valued extension of a sufficient acceptance guarantee whose general optimization version requires extra structure. Neither currently demonstrates the complexity landscape ChoCo is designed to chart.

This case is weakest against Theorem 3: the finite-support amendment scenario is coherent, and its mass-weighted guarantee may be worth recording. If an exact continuous acceptance theorem of that kind is enough to count as a worthwhile mirror, the negative case cannot honestly rule it out.
