What the mirror covers

The proposed scope covers only large-population mean estimation; it leaves the metric and disagreement mechanisms, the minimal-revelation impossibility result, and the deferred linear-regression results aside. None is established here as a qualifying computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive seed is the large-population mean-estimation result, but it falls short of ChoCo’s required kind of anchor.

A plausible setting is a very large cohort answering the same statistical question: each person has the same signal-generating model and costs, but receives a private signal \(s\in\mathbb{R}^d\). Let the latent mean be \(\Theta\sim\mathcal{N}(m_0,\Sigma)\), with \(S\mid\Theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\Theta,V)\). In an atomless version, type \(s\) has mass \(\nu_\Theta(B)=\Pr[S\in B\mid\Theta]\) for any set \(B\). Agents report either \(s\) or their posterior mean \(\eta(s)=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid S=s]\); the mechanism uses peer reports and aggregate moments to estimate \(\Theta\) and set payments.

The candidate problem, Atomless Proxy-BTS Mean Estimation, is: given rational Gaussian parameters \((m_0,\Sigma,V)\), find a finite description of a report, payment, and estimator rule for this population such that truthful reporting is a strict Bayes–Nash equilibrium, yields maximum expected total payments and strictly more welfare than any oblivious equilibrium, and makes the aggregate reports sufficient for \(\Theta\). A solution is the rule and a proof of those properties. This covers only the paper’s large-population mean-estimation result, not its metric or disagreement mechanisms or its deferred linear-regression results.

The tempting anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It states that as \(n\to\infty\), under a Gaussian common prior on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the Proxy-BTS mechanism in Mechanism 2 is an optimal estimator and is informed truthful: truthful reporting is a strict equilibrium, achieves maximum welfare, and has strictly greater welfare than any oblivious equilibrium. The paper also says in Remark 4.2 that splitting agents between signal reports and posterior-mean reports gives a minimal version that remains informed truthful.

I would expect an explicit Gaussian atomless mechanism to be tractable by replacing sample moments with population integrals. But that is only a plausible extension; it is not a complexity classification established by the theorem. The paper contains no named result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its named theorems establish mechanism properties and impossibility, so none is a qualifying computational anchor under your criteria.

The weakest point is the population regime. Exact Gaussian signals are almost surely distinct, so if type includes the private signal—as it must when that signal affects incentives—the support grows with the number of agents. Treating a shared task or signal-generating model as the type would omit a strategically relevant difference. Binning signals could produce a small type support, but would change the paper’s Gaussian signal problem. Also, Theorem 4.1 already takes \(n\to\infty\), making the proposed mirror close to a population-limit reformulation of the paper’s own setting. Thus it is a concrete research question, but not a strong, compliant ChoCo mirror anchored in a computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 is the strongest anchor, but it is not a computational result of the kind ChoCo is charting. It proves equilibrium, welfare, and estimation properties for a mechanism. The other named results have the same character: they establish mechanism properties or impossibility, not the complexity of an algorithmic problem. There is no stated search, optimization, or decision problem whose complexity the paper classifies.

The best version of the proposed mirror is an atomless society whose signal distribution is Gaussian, with a mechanism that elicits reports and estimates the latent mean. That is a coherent model, but “find a finite description of a mechanism with these properties” does not yet define a complexity question: it gives no bounded mechanism class, input and output encoding, or running-time measure. For the stated Gaussian family, the paper already supplies the large-population mechanism and its strategic guarantees. Replacing sample moments with population integrals would make the aggregate signal mean exact, so it changes the estimation problem while leaving the central work as mechanism and equilibrium analysis.

There is also a mismatch with ChoCo’s high-multiplicity input. A signal affects an agent’s posterior and hence their incentives, so it belongs in the agent’s type. Under the paper’s continuous Gaussian signal model, exact signal types have zero mass in the atomless population; in finite samples, exact repeats occur with probability zero. Grouping agents by their shared prior or signal-generating model would discard the private signal that drives the mechanism. Binning signals could yield finite support, but then the proposed mirror concerns a discretized model, with different incentives and no direct complexity result inherited from Theorem 4.1.

One could formulate a new algorithmic project—for example, optimizing over a restricted class of peer-payment mechanisms given a rational finite-support signal distribution. That might be worthwhile in its own right, but it would be a new mechanism-synthesis problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. So the case against is strong as a case against this paper as a ChoCo anchor. The broader claim that no related scenario could ever be worthwhile is harder to defend: it is a judgment about programme fit, not something the paper establishes.
