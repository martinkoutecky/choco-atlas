The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no numbered computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. It states no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about complexity or algorithms. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite impossibility result is cited as background, not proved here; the paper’s claim that truth-telling is dominant under the Clarke tax points to a separate technical report, not a numbered result in this paper.

The strongest adjacent follow-up would ask whether a Clarke-tax mechanism still elicits truthful reports when agents are modeled by types and population masses. But it has a serious obstacle: in an atomless population, one agent has zero influence on the chosen state, so that agent’s pivotal tax vanishes. Treating a whole type’s mass as one strategic bloc would change who the agents are. That makes this a possible new research question, not a defensible mirror anchored in this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result to continuize. Its central claim is about incentives: the Clarke tax makes truthful reporting dominant. The paper gives no complexity theorem or formal algorithmic problem for candidate generation, planning costs, or group formation. With a finite set of alternatives and reported utilities, choosing the maximum-sum alternative is just a scan. The cited Gibbard–Satterthwaite result is background, and the paper points elsewhere for the truthfulness proof. Turning an implementation difficulty into an optimization problem could be useful work, but it would supply a new formal problem rather than mirror a result here.

A large population of agents with repeated goals or utility profiles is plausible, so lack of multiplicity is not the objection. The difficulty is that the mechanism charges an agent for changing the outcome. In a population represented by a distribution, one individual has zero mass. At a society with a strict aggregate winner, that individual’s report cannot change the winner or impose a Clarke externality. Truthful reporting then ceases to distinguish itself from other reports: the mechanism cannot reveal that individual’s preference. The paper itself notes that an individual’s chance of affecting the decision shrinks as the group grows.

The strongest repair is to let an entire type mass report as one strategic bloc. Removing or changing that positive mass can alter the winner and yield a nonzero tax. But this makes a type a coordinated player; the incentive question is about bloc deviations, precisely where the paper flags coalition manipulation as unresolved. With finitely many types and alternatives, the weighted choice is also a direct computation over those types and alternatives. That is a coherent new mechanism-design question, but it is neither the individual truthfulness claim in the paper nor a computational result the paper establishes.

Candidate generation and future-cost evaluation might conceal hard planning problems. Their complexity would depend on a separately specified language for states, predicates, and actions; this paper gives no computational classification to carry into a continuous model. So the negative case is strongest at the boundary between the paper’s subject and ChoCo’s: a continuous population either erases individual pivotality or turns each type into a strategic bloc, while the paper supplies no computational result to anchor that new question.
