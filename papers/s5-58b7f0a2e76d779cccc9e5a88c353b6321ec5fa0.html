The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered computational result of the required kind. Its numbered results concern economic properties—Propositions 1 and 2 establish Bayesian incentive compatibility and Pareto efficiency with money; Proposition 3 establishes Bayesian incentive compatibility and ex-post Pareto efficiency for the feasible EEM without money; Lemma 1 characterizes when that mechanism is feasible. Here “efficient” means Pareto efficient, not polynomial-time solvable. None of these results classifies the computational complexity of a problem.

The paper’s model is also a poor fit for a population mirror: it allocates goods between two strategic agents. Treating many agents with shared valuation types as continuous mass would change the mechanism’s underlying problem, rather than continuize its population. Since there is no qualifying anchor, I can’t state the requested continuous problem per anchor without inventing a result the paper does not prove.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor, so there is no numbered computational result to defeat. That is the strongest case against a paper-specific mirror: Propositions 1–3 establish incentive compatibility and Pareto efficiency; Lemma 1 characterizes when the numeraire transfers are feasible; Propositions 4–5 give a welfare guarantee and a uniqueness result. Here “efficient” means Pareto efficient, not computationally tractable. The paper proves no runtime, hardness, approximation, or parameterized-complexity result.

A better candidate would be to study the computation needed to evaluate EEM transfers or find a feasible numeraire. But that would add a computational problem the paper does not analyze. Its distributions describe uncertainty about each of two agents’ private valuations; they are not a continuous population of interchangeable agents. Treating them as population mass would continuize the wrong object. And in a nonatomic population, an individual report may have no effect on allocations, potentially erasing the incentive role the EEM is designed to create. Grouping many people into strategic cohorts could preserve that role, but it would require a new multi-agent model. Section 6 sketches a finite-agent extension; it gives no computational analysis of one.

So I would reject a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. The universal claim is less secure: a computational study of a large-cohort EEM, especially with a carefully specified representation of valuation distributions, could be worthwhile. But that would be a new computational contribution inspired by this paper, not a continuization of a computational result it contains.
