The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchored computational case for a continuous mirror: the paper contains no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest anchors are Proposition 1, proved in the paper, which says a coalitionally strategyproof social choice function on \(B\)-measurable profiles gives the same outcome on top-equivalent profiles; Lemma 1, also proved there, which says its winning coalitions form an ultrafilter; Proposition 2, which proves an extension to admissible profiles; and Proposition 4, which characterizes the function using that ultrafilter. These are social-choice and structural results, not computational results under the criteria for this task.

There is a plausible population regime behind a follow-on question: many people share one of the finitely many strict rankings of \(X\), with \(\tau\) ranking types and rational masses \(\mu_t\). Proposition 1’s proof groups people with matching preferences into finitely many classes, so type aggregation is close to its reasoning. But the paper represents society through individuals and observable coalitions in a Boolean algebra; it assigns no population masses. Replacing coalitions with fractional mass could change which deviations matter, including whether a coalition of zero mass can manipulate.

So I would not supply a continuous problem or predict its complexity as a mirror of one of these anchors. That would invent a computational question rather than continuize a computational result from the paper. My weakest point is that the paper’s infinite-society setting and finite preference classes offer genuine support for a future mass-based model; they still do not meet the required computational anchor condition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is unusually direct: none of the paper’s results is a computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes coalitionally strategyproof functions; Lemma 1 and Proposition 4 describe them through ultrafilters; Proposition 2 proves an extension exists. None gives an algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or optimization task. Under ChoCo’s stated remit, a continuous axiomatic analogue would not fill the computational gap.

The best mass-based version is easy to state: let a rule \(F\) map a distribution over complete rankings to an alternative, define coalitional manipulation by mass transfers, and ask whether strategyproofness forces \(F\) to depend only on the masses of top-ranked alternatives. That could be a legitimate axiomatic question. But it still studies a property of rules, not the complexity of computing an outcome or finding a manipulation. To turn it into a computational problem, one would have to choose a representation for \(F\)—a circuit, formula, oracle, or something else—and define a new decision or search task. The paper supplies no reason to privilege one such choice, so that would be a new project attached to the theorem rather than its continuous mirror.

The proposed high-multiplicity connection is real but does not change that conclusion. Finite rankings make it natural to describe a society by ranking types and their masses. Yet Proposition 1 compares exact coalitions of individuals with each top alternative; equal masses do not preserve those coalitions or the Boolean algebra \(B\) of observable sets. The proof also refines two profiles by *complete rankings* before applying Gibbard–Satterthwaite. A distribution over rankings can support a sensible new model, but it does not itself encode the paper’s input or turn its theorem into a computational result.

There is a further modeling choice to settle in that new model: whether coalitions of zero mass may manipulate. Including them preserves an individual-level notion that a mass distribution cannot generally record; excluding them replaces the paper’s unmodified coalitional strategyproofness with a positive-mass variant. Either choice may be worth studying axiomatically, but neither supplies the missing computational anchor. The extension theorem has the same issue: it proves existence of an extension from one profile domain to another, with no finite representation or procedure whose complexity could be analyzed.

So the other analyst is right to treat Proposition 1, Lemma 1, Proposition 2, and Proposition 4 as the nearest anchors, but each fails the computational gate. The paper’s infinite society and finite set of rankings make a mass model plausible; they do not make a continuous computational mirror worthwhile for this programme.
