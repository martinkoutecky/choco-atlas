The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is real but narrow. This paper has no numbered result saying that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in a parameterized complexity class. Its Section 4 proposes a classification algorithm and proves some classifications, but it gives no complexity bound for that algorithm. The best anchor I can offer is therefore a formulation-style result, under your exception for a formulation that the paper computes with.

My lead anchor is Lemma 1, proved in this paper. It says: for a complete simple game, take any matrix \(\tilde S\) of winning coalition vectors and any matrix \(\tilde L\) of losing coalition vectors. If there are nonnegative real row vectors \(x,y\) with equal, strictly positive \(\ell_1\)-norms and \(x\tilde S\le y\tilde L\) coordinatewise, then the game is not weighted. The paper applies this certificate to constructed games; the converse is described as known from the literature, not proved as this lemma.

A concrete continuous mirror is Continuous Mass-Weightedness Recognition. Its instance is an ordered list of \(t\) agent types, rational masses \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a complete binary voting rule given by its rational polyhedral winning region \(W\) in
\[ \mathcal X_\alpha=\prod_{i=1}^t[0,\alpha_i]. \]
A point \(z\in\mathcal X_\alpha\) records the fraction of society from each type supporting a proposal. Types are classes of agents identical in the voting features the rule uses. Taking type \(1\) as most desirable, require \(W\) to be upward closed under
\[ x\preceq y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i=1}^k x_i\le \sum_{i=1}^k y_i \quad\text{for every }k. \]
The question is whether there are weights \(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_t\ge0\) and a quota \(q>0\) such that, for every mass coalition \(z\), the rule accepts exactly when \(\sum_i w_i z_i\ge q\). A solution is such weights and quota, or a proof that no such threshold represents \(W\). Lemma 1 suggests a finite negative certificate: equal-total mixtures of winning and losing mass profiles whose mean winning profile is coordinatewise no greater than the mean losing profile. Establishing how complete that certificate system is for continuous polyhedral rules would itself be a follow-on result.

The regime is a large membership organization or federation with many members in each of a fixed number of formal voting classes. Members within a class have the same voting role; \(\alpha_i\) is the fraction of the membership in that class, and coalition mass is the fraction of that class supporting the proposal. The number of people can grow while the number of distinct types stays small. This is a high-multiplicity model of group voting: the outcome remains a binary decision, while the society and its supporting coalitions become continuous.

The mirror preserves the paper’s central question: whether a complete voting rule over interchangeable types can be represented by additive type weights and a quota. It does not replace the question with fractional outcomes or lotteries. For an explicitly given polyhedral winning region, I would expect weightedness recognition to be tractable through finite-dimensional optimization and separation, though that is a research expectation, not a result of this paper. With a more succinct representation of the rule, finding the relevant separation constraints could become the hard part.

The weakest point is the anchor itself. Lemma 1 is a certificate of non-weightedness, not a complexity theorem or a complete decision algorithm. The authors may also regard recognition for a specified continuous rule as narrower than their classification of which *type compositions* make every complete simple game weighted. So this is a plausible mirror of the paper’s weightedness question, but it does not support a claim that the paper has already identified a continuous complexity problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper supplies no computational complexity result to continuize. Lemma 1 is a certificate of non-weightedness for a finite game: its winning and losing vectors are points on a finite coalition lattice. The paper uses such certificates in constructions, but proves no complexity bound for weightedness recognition. Its later classification procedure is a sketch, not an algorithm with a running-time result. So the proposed mirror has to supply a computational problem that the paper itself did not formulate.

There is also no unique continuous version of a finite game. For example, the game \([2;2,1,1]\) has type composition \((1,2)\). In count-scaled coordinates, its coalition grid is \(a\in\{0,1\}\), \(b\in\{0,1,2\}\), and it accepts exactly when \(2a+b\ge2\). One continuous extension is \(W_0=\{(a,b):2a+b\ge2\}\). Another, on the same box, is
\[ W_1=\{(a,b): b\ge f(a)\},\qquad f(a)=\max\{2-3a,1-a\}. \]
Both agree with the finite game at every grid point. \(W_1\) is a rational polyhedral complete rule: under a fixed-total shift from type \(2\) to the more desirable type \(1\), its acceptance condition is \(a+b\ge\max\{2-2a,1\}\), which remains satisfied as \(a\) increases. But \(W_1\) is not a single weighted threshold: its boundary has two different slopes. Thus the same finite weighted game can have either a weighted or non-weighted continuous extension. The discrete instance does not determine which question to ask.

The stronger version of the proponent’s mirror is to take a polyhedral region \(W\) itself as the input. That is a coherent continuous voting problem, but \(W\) supplies new decisions at fractional coalitions that the paper’s lattice data never specifies. Lemma 1’s continuous analogue then becomes a convex-separation question about that supplied region; its computational content comes from the added representation, not from the paper’s result. This weakens the claim that the paper motivates a particular continuous mirror, though it does not show that the new problem is uninteresting.

Nor can I make a convincing universal case that no scenario would support one. A large federation with many members in each formal voting class is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and a continuum game defined directly over such classes could be worthwhile to study. The negative case is therefore limited: it defeats the claim that this paper provides a clear, determinate computational anchor for a continuous mirror, but it cannot rule out a valuable new continuous weightedness problem.
