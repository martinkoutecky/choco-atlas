The continuous mirror question

Given two complete utility types \(i\in\{1,2\}\) with masses \(\mu_i\), indivisible good categories \(M\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and rational endpoint states \(x^0,x^1\), does a finite sequence of rational-mass exchanges connect them? Each state \(x_{i,B}\) assigns mass of type \(i\) to integral bundle \(B\subseteq M\), respects \(\sum_B x_{i,B}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_{i,B:g\in B}x_{i,B}=q_g\), and is EF1 across every pair of supported type-bundle pairs. A move exchanges one good between equal positive masses of two supported bundles and must leave the state EF1.

The model it lives in

Use two cohorts with the utility functions from Theorem 4.1, each of mass \(1/2\), and integral bundles over the theorem's good categories. In the cloned endpoint instance, each category has per-capita supply \(q_g=1/2\); states record the mass \(x_{i,B}\) of each type receiving each bundle. A move exchanges one good between equal rational masses, with EF1 required for every supported bundle pair throughout.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1's exchange-only EF1 reachability decision problem. It leaves Theorem 4.2, Theorem 4.4, and the connectivity results in Sections 3 and 5 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Theorem 4.1's Partition reduction lift to this mass-state model, or can splitting cohorts create routes around its \(\beta=t\) bottleneck?
  • How does mass-state reachability relate to finite populations of \(N\) sites, and do reachable instances have polynomial-size rational path certificates?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is HM-EF1 Reconfiguration, the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s exchange-only EF1 Reconfiguration problem.

My anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: for every fixed number of agents \(n\ge 2\), deciding whether two given EF1 allocations are connected by an EF1 exchange path is NP-hard, even when both endpoint allocations are exactly balanced. The proof reduces from Partition. In its two-agent construction, EF1 confines the path to a narrow utility corridor. Before the exchange that moves \(z_1\), the goods \(G\) held by agent 1 must have total utility \(\beta\ge t\); after it, they must have \(\beta\le t\). That exchange leaves \(\beta\) unchanged, so it can happen only at \(\beta=t\). Reaching that point requires a subset of the Partition items summing to \(t\).

For the mirror, let there be two complete agent types, \(\theta_1,\theta_2\), with the paper’s additive utility functions \(u_1,u_2\), and masses \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\). Let \(M\) be a set of good categories, with rational per-capita supply \(q_g\) for each \(g\in M\). A bundle is still an integral subset \(B\subseteq M\). The state variable \(x_{i,B}\) is the mass of type \(\theta_i\) receiving bundle \(B\). Thus \(\sum_B x_{i,B}=\mu_i\), and \(\sum_{i,B:g\in B}x_{i,B}=q_g\). For the cloned version of the theorem’s two-agent instance, every category has supply \(q_g=1/2\): each of the two large cohorts receives the same bundle as its corresponding agent in the original construction.

The input includes rational initial and target states \(x^0,x^1\), supported on bundles of the same cardinality \(k\), and both must be EF1. A state is EF1 when every supported bundle is EF1 toward every other supported bundle: for all types \(i,j\) and bundles \(B,C\) with \(x_{i,B}>0\) and \(x_{j,C}>0\), \(u_i(B)\ge u_i(C\setminus D)\) for some \(D\subseteq C\) with \(|D|\le 1\).

The question is whether there is a finite sequence of EF1 states from \(x^0\) to \(x^1\). A legal move chooses positive mass \(\epsilon\) of type-\(i\) agents with bundle \(B\) and the same mass of type-\(j\) agents with bundle \(C\), then exchanges one good \(g\in B\) for one \(h\in C\), producing \(B'=(B\setminus\{g\})\cup\{h\}\) and \(C'=(C\setminus\{h\})\cup\{g\}\). It subtracts \(\epsilon\) from each source state and adds it to each corresponding destination state; the resulting state must remain EF1. A solution is the finite list of mass moves and their amounts. The objective is reachability, with complexity measured in \(m\), the number \(\tau\) of supported types, the input bit length \(L\), and the endpoint supports.

This has a credible large-population setting: thousands of local service sites with two shared priority profiles, receiving bundles of standardized supply categories and permitted to trade one unit at a time. The sites within a profile have the same utilities and other relevant parameters; the number of sites grows while \(\tau=2\). Mass records how much of each cohort has each integral bundle. The continuity is in the population and its allocation across bundles.

My expectation is Class B: hardness transfers, though that transfer is not proved by Theorem 4.1. Its reduction’s key constraint comes from sums of indivisible good values, rather than from having many distinct agents. Splitting the population into fractions does not let an individual in a moving mass hold a fractional bundle, so the critical \(\beta=t\) gate may still require a Partition witness. The concrete question is whether the reduction can be lifted to the mass-state model with only two types.

The weakest point is that a large cohort creates comparisons absent in the two-agent proof: an agent must satisfy EF1 toward other bundles held by members of their own type, as well as toward the other type. The paper’s proof does not establish that those extra comparisons preserve the reduction, or rule out new mass-based routes. That is the main prover task. Further questions are whether the lift holds with \(\tau=2\), whether every reachable instance has a polynomial-size rational path certificate, and how reachability compares between the mass model and finite populations with \(N\) sites. My scope here is Theorem 4.1’s exchange-only EF1 reconfiguration result; the paper’s other connectivity results are not needed for this case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this anchor is that Theorem 4.1’s reduction is built around a *pair* of agents, not a population whose multiplicity drives the problem. Its Partition witness is one agent’s bundle of goods \(G\): the value \(\beta\) must equal \(t\) at the exchange of \(z_1\). Cloning the two agents into types of mass \(1/2\) changes the state from that pair of bundles to a distribution over bundles. A submass can move while the rest stays put, and the proof’s single \(\beta=t\) bottleneck no longer describes the state. The proposed mirror therefore needs a new argument showing that support-wise EF1 prevents subcohorts from staging a route around the bottleneck. The paper supplies no such argument, and its reduction does not establish hardness for that model.

That weakens the claim that this particular theorem already gives a strong continuous mirror. But it does not defeat the better version of the mirror. A large population of identical service sites or households receiving standardized categories of indivisible goods is plausible; per-capita supplies and distributions over integral bundles make a coherent model. Theorem 4.1 is a genuine computational result, and its population version would still ask a computational question. Whether mass splitting makes it easier is a question about the answer, which is not grounds for dismissing the mirror.

So my negative case is weak at its central point. The proponent has not shown that the NP-hardness lifts, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists. The mirror survives as a sensible new reachability problem; what remains unsupported is the suggested connection between that problem and Theorem 4.1’s hardness proof.
