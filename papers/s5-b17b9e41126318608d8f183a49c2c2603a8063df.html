The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its strongest relevant result, Theorem 2 (proved here), says that when \(m\ge 2\), \(\operatorname{int}(A)\) is nonempty and connected, and \(A\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{int}(A))\), every quasi-transitive welfare function on \(R_p^N\) satisfying weak Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives is oligarchic: there is a nonempty coalition \(S\) such that society strictly prefers \(a\) to \(b\) exactly when every member of \(S\) strictly prefers \(a\) to \(b\). Theorem 1 (also proved here) says that under the same geometric conditions, transitive welfare functions cannot satisfy weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship together. Both are axiomatic characterizations, not results about the complexity of deciding or solving a problem. The integer-programming work mentioned in the introduction is cited prior work, not a computation carried out in this paper.

There is a plausible population regime behind a possible extension: many residents choosing among bounded, multidimensional policies, with preferences determined by their \(L_p\)-bliss points and relatively few distinct preference types. A natural extension would replace the finite profile by a distribution \(\mu\) over bliss-point types and ask whether the paper’s oligarchy characterization still holds for social orderings generated from \(\mu\). That is an axiomatic question, however, and specifying what a fixed oligarchy means in a type distribution requires additional modeling choices. It cannot serve as the requested computational mirror.

So the strongest honest positive case is that the paper has a plausible high-multiplicity population setting for a future extension. Its numbered theorems provide no computational anchor, and I cannot give a qualifying continuous computational problem per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate, but it gives no computational problem to continuize. It characterizes welfare functions \(f:R_p^N\to Q\): if a rule satisfies weak Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives on the whole domain, it must be oligarchic. Theorem 1 is another axiomatic characterization. Neither has an instance-level input, an algorithmic output, or a complexity claim. The integer-programming work mentioned in the introduction is prior work, not a computational result of this paper.

The best version of the proposed extension would study a large population distributed over bliss-point types and characterize social welfare functionals on those distributions. That could be a worthwhile axiomatic question, but it still would not ask for an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm. To make it computational, one would have to choose a particular rule or encode a family of rules, then invent a task such as evaluating or verifying it. The paper supplies neither choice, and those tasks would be new questions rather than mirrors of its theorems.

There is also a modeling wrinkle in carrying Theorem 2 over: its oligarchy is a coalition of named individuals. A mass version would need to decide what counts as a coalition—perhaps a positive-measure set of agents or a set of types—and how that set relates to the distribution. A sensible definition may be possible, but resolving it would not turn the characterization into computational social choice.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper may motivate a continuous-population axiomatic extension, but it offers no named computational result to anchor a continuous computational mirror. The universal claim cannot rule out a useful computational project someone might build around spatial aggregation; it can say that such a project would need a new computational question the paper itself does not provide.
