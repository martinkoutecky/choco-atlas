The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and room type sets \(T\) and \(S\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t,\nu_s\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), continuous monotone decreasing piecewise-linear utilities \(v_{t,s}\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), find nonnegative type-level prices \(p_s\) and flows \(x_{t,s}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}=\nu_s\), such that every \(x_{t,s}>0\) satisfies, for every \(q\in S\), \((1+\varepsilon)v_{t,s}(p_s)\geq v_{t,q}(p_q)\) when \(v_{t,s}(p_s)\geq 0\), and \(v_{t,s}(p_s)\geq(1+\varepsilon)v_{t,q}(p_q)\) otherwise?

The model it lives in

A large housing market with applicant types defined by their complete utility curves for every room class, and room types defined by their utility to each applicant type. The population and room supply are represented by rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\); decisions are a price per room type and a feasible assignment flow \(x\), with no welfare objective beyond finding an approximately envy-free market-clearing allocation.

The objection that survived

Exact repetition of complete applicant utility schedules may be uncommon in many housing markets, so the support may grow with the population outside standardized settings.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s approximate envy-freeness guarantee. Theorems 3 and 4 add a fixed or average rent constraint; Theorem 5 concerns optimal prices and a truthful mechanism; Theorem 6 gives a complexity-class containment. These yield related mirrors but are not needed for this verdict.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does this approximate rent-equilibrium problem admit an algorithm polynomial in the numbers of applicant and room types, the total number of utility pieces, the input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), or is it hard even with small supports?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is a large housing market with repeated applicant profiles and repeated room classes. Think of many applicants choosing among singles, doubles, and accessible rooms. An applicant type is the applicant’s *complete* utility function for every room class, including its price response; a room type consists of rooms with the same utility to every applicant type. A large population may have \(N\) applicants but only \(\tau\) applicant types and \(\rho\) room types, with \(\tau,\rho\ll N\). This is plausible for a university or municipal housing system, even though a small roommate group is not itself a high-multiplicity setting.

Formally, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of applicants of type \(t\), and \(\nu_s\) the fraction of rooms of type \(s\), with both distributions summing to \(1\). Each utility function \(v_{t,s}(p_s)\) is continuous, monotone decreasing, and piecewise linear. The decision is a price \(p_s\) for each room type and an assignment flow \(x_{t,s}\geq 0\), satisfying
\[ \sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,s}=\nu_s. \]
A positive \(x_{t,s}\) means that this mass of type-\(t\) applicants each gets one indivisible room of type \(s\). It does not give any applicant a fractional room. Exact envy-freeness requires that whenever \(x_{t,s}>0\), \(v_{t,s}(p_s)\geq v_{t,q}(p_q)\) for every room type \(q\). The search has no welfare objective, just as in the paper: find prices and an assignment that clear the market and meet the envy condition.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that, for any rent-division instance with continuous, monotone decreasing, piecewise-linear utilities, an \(\varepsilon\)-approximately envy-free solution can be computed in time polynomial in \(1/\varepsilon\) and the input size. The guarantee uses the paper’s sign-sensitive definition: for an agent with nonnegative assigned utility, \((1+\varepsilon)\) times that utility must be at least their utility for every other room; for an agent with negative assigned utility, the assigned utility must be at least \((1+\varepsilon)\) times their utility for every other room.

The continuous question is High-Multiplicity Approximate Rent Equilibrium: given \(\mu,\nu\), the type utilities, and \(\varepsilon>0\), find nonnegative prices and a feasible assignment flow such that every positive-mass assignment edge meets that same sign-sensitive approximation guarantee. The target is an FPTAS in the compressed input size, polynomial in \(\tau,\rho,K,L,1/\varepsilon\), where \(K\) counts utility pieces and \(L\) is the bit length of the rational input. I expect Class A. The paper’s slope-rounding and price-search approach looks like it could carry over, with a transportation or min-cost-flow computation over types replacing matching over named individuals. That is a research target, not a consequence of Theorem 2: its runtime is in the explicit finite input size, so it does not itself give a support-polynomial algorithm.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. It says that given a rent-division instance and total rent \(C\), an \(\varepsilon\)-approximately envy-free allocation and price vector with \(\sum_r p_r=C\) can be found in time polynomial in \(1/\varepsilon\) and the input size; negative rents and utilities may occur. Its continuous mirror, High-Multiplicity Fixed-Average-Rent Equilibrium, imposes
\[ \sum_s \nu_s p_s=c, \]
where \(c=C/N\) is the per-capita total rent in a finite market of \(N\) rooms. The assignment and approximation conditions are otherwise the same. I also expect Class A for this approximate problem, with the same compressed-input FPTAS target. Per-capita rent and room supply scaling with population are natural co-modifications of making the population continuous.

For an exact-search anchor, Theorem 6, proved here, says that exact envy-free rent division with continuous, monotone decreasing, piecewise-linear utilities—EF-PIECEWISELINEAR—is in \(\mathrm{PPAD}\cap\mathrm{PLS}\). Its mirror is High-Multiplicity Exact Rent Equilibrium: given the type distributions and utilities, find prices and a feasible flow satisfying exact envy-freeness on every positive-mass assignment edge. For bounded \(\tau,\rho,K\), I would target an exact algorithm polynomial in \(L\), or more ambitiously an FPT algorithm in \(\tau+\rho+K\). In a fixed type system, the assignment constraints are a transportation problem and the envy conditions are linear once the relevant price segments and demand edges are fixed. For unrestricted supports, I would not claim tractability: singleton types embed finite instances, so any discrete hardness result would transfer. Theorem 6 itself is a class containment, not a hardness result.

The paper contains no named hardness theorem for its cardinal, piecewise-linear rent-division problem. Its cited PPAD-completeness result is for an ordinal variant, which is a different model and not a valid hardness anchor here. The authors should recognize these mirrors: they explicitly formulate rent division as a unit-demand matching market with monetary transfers. That economic equilibrium connection is useful machinery and context, not a collision with the computational question of how complexity changes when the population is supplied as a distribution over types.

The weakest point is the compressed runtime claim. The paper’s algorithms process named applicants, and the fact that identical profiles can be grouped does not prove that its runtime depends only on \(\tau\) and \(\rho\). Also, the motivating roommate example is usually small. A large housing system with recurring applicant profiles and standardized room classes is more convincing, but it requires genuinely repeated complete utility schedules. The continuum mirror remains worth asking because it preserves the paper’s core problem—unit-demand assignment at prices—while making the population itself, rather than the rooms or utilities, the high-multiplicity object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about how narrow the plausible high-multiplicity regime is. An agent’s type here must encode the complete utility curve for every room, and a room type must preserve its utility to every agent type. In a real housing market, location, condition, accessibility, budgets, and price sensitivity can make those complete schedules distinct. Approximate clustering would not establish the exact type identity the model needs, and near-ties can make even small utility changes affect envy. So the paper’s natural instances may have support growing with the population, leaving little compression.

That weakens Theorem 2’s mirror most: the paper gives an FPTAS in the explicit instance size, while the proposed type-level flow model needs a new algorithm whose running time depends on support size. The matching-to-flow substitution is mathematically coherent, but the paper’s price-search analysis does not itself show that it works in the compressed model. Still, that is an open computational question, not a reason the question is unworthy.

Theorem 3 has the same issue, plus a more specific modeling choice: fixing total rent becomes fixing average rent as the number of rooms scales. That is a sensible co-modification, but it describes a housing system with an aggregate rent constraint. It is less obviously part of ordinary rent division, where the total rent may be determined externally. The mass-flow formulation remains well-defined if such a constraint matters.

Theorem 6 is the hardest anchor to dismiss. Its exact mirror asks for an envy-free equilibrium over type masses and room supplies. The paper’s \(\mathrm{PPAD}\cap\mathrm{PLS}\) result does not transfer automatically, but the continuous search problem is plainly formulable. Its practical reach again rests on exact repetition of complete utility schedules.

The best version of the positive case—standardized housing with repeated room classes and recurring applicant utility templates—survives these objections. In that setting, a type-level flow respects individual room indivisibility in the continuum model, and the rent constraints have a natural per-capita interpretation. I cannot honestly argue that no such scenario exists. The negative case is therefore weak: it questions how often the exact compression is realistic, but it does not defeat the proposed mirrors or satisfy the universal burden.
