The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent gives me no computational anchor to rebut. Theorem 1 proves existence of a strict Markovian core by a finite sequence of Pareto improvements. Theorem 2 proves almost-sure convergence under a condition on which blocking coalitions occur. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem, and neither specifies an input encoding or a running-time bound.

The constructive proof of Theorem 1 does not close that gap. It says the process terminates because the finite market has finitely many allocations; it does not bound the number of steps or explain how to find a blocking coalition. Theorem 2 likewise leaves the transition process abstract. A complexity question about finding a core allocation or simulating the process would need its own choices of preference representation, bundle limits, and transition rules. A continuous version would need further choices about mass allocations and blocking. Those could be worthwhile new questions, but they are not computational results in this paper for a continuous mirror to carry over.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is inherently implausible here. Cohorts with the same endowments and preferences in a standardized barter market are easy to imagine. But a continuum question about existence or convergence for such cohorts would be an analytic market-process question. Turning it into a complexity study requires formulating a new computational problem, rather than continuizing one the authors actually analyze.
