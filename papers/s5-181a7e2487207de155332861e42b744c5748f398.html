What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive thought is a nonatomic extension of the paper’s cooperative games. Imagine a large cooperative with members in a few interchangeable roles. A society is a unit mass of members; a coalition is a measurable subset, and its worth \(V(A)\) depends only on the mass of each role it contains. An allocation is a payoff density \(p\), with efficiency \(\int p=V(X)\). The paper’s Average coalitional desirability then has a natural counterpart: if adding coalition \(R\) to a disjoint coalition \(C\) gives more worth per unit mass than adding \(T\), the average payoff density on \(R\) should be at least that on \(T\). Cohorts in a large cooperative enterprise or public consortium make the many-members, few-types regime plausible.

But this is only a candidate *axiomatic extension*. The paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: no theorem about an algorithm, complexity class, or computational problem. Its numbered propositions are proved here, but they are axiomatic results:

  • Proposition 1 says that for \(n\ge3\), no value on \(V_N\) satisfies both Coalitional desirability and Efficiency.
  • Proposition 2 says that for \(n\ne2\), the Equal Division value is the unique value satisfying Efficiency, Additivity, and Average coalitional desirability.
  • Proposition 3 says that for \(n\notin\{2,3,4\}\), the Equal Division value is the unique value satisfying Efficiency, Additivity, and Uniform coalitional desirability.

None asserts computational tractability or hardness, so none qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard. There is consequently no honest per-anchor computational mirror to hand to a prover. Turning the extension into, say, a decision or optimization problem would introduce a new computational question absent from the paper, rather than mirror one of its results.

That limitation matters especially because the paper treats arbitrary TU games, where worth can depend on the identities and combinations of coalition members. The few-role model restricts this structure to make a continuous population sensible. It may be a worthwhile cooperative-game-theory extension, but it does not rescue a computational mirror of these results. The paper’s own contribution is axiomatic, so the positive case is weak at the programme’s central gate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–3 characterize values by axioms and show an incompatibility; none defines an algorithmic problem or proves a complexity bound. That leaves no qualifying anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme.

The proposed cohort model is a sensible way to make a continuous population plausible. Even granting finitely many interchangeable roles, a worth function over role masses, and payoff densities, the paper’s propositions remain axiomatic statements about which value rules satisfy conditions across a class of games. A computational question about finding such rules, checking axioms, or computing allocations would need a new input model and a new problem definition. It might lead to worthwhile research, but it would be a new computational project, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on programme fit, though weaker as a claim that no continuous cooperative-game computation could ever be worthwhile. The cohort scenario survives as a plausible model; it does not supply the missing computational result.
