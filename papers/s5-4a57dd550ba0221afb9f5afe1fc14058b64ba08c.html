The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that \(L_E\) preserves path independence over responsive choice functions if and only if \(E\) is threshold-linear with cardinal reuse. In the paper’s notation, for some threshold \(t\), \(K=E(\varnothing)\), and nested sets \(T^n\subseteq X\setminus K\), \(E(Z)=(Z\setminus T^{|Z|})\cup K\) when \(|Z|<t\); otherwise the gross exclusion \(G_E(Z)\) is \(X\). This is a substantial characterization, but it is not a complexity result: the paper gives no computational input representation for \(E\), no running-time claim, and no reductions.

Extending that operation to a continuum of divisions while preserving its order-dependent meaning would require a new model. Theorem 1 would not establish a result about that model, and presenting it as the paper’s continuous mirror would overclaim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the closest thing to an anchor, but it does not state a computational result. It characterizes which exclusion functions make lexicographic composition preserve path independence over responsive choice functions. The paper gives no finite input representation for an exclusion function, no encoded computational problem, and no algorithm or complexity bound. A continuous version of that characterization would still be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s stated scope.

The strongest rescue is the proposed large organization with many divisions: give each division a type consisting of its ranking and quota, and ask how composition behaves as the fractions of division types vary. But the composition depends on order, and the paper emphasizes that it is generally neither associative nor commutative. A distribution over division types forgets the sequence; adding position or the full composition tree to each type preserves that information only by making the order itself part of the input. That may motivate a new model, but it does not turn Theorem 1 into a computational question about a continuous society.

Other reinterpretations fare no better as mirrors. Treating the items in \(X\) as the population would continuize the alternatives, not the society. Treating divisions as agents requires extending the paper’s two-function operation to an ordered population, then choosing a finite representation and a computational task. The paper’s discussion suggests such extensions, but it does not analyze their complexity.

So the case against a worthwhile mirror is strong for this paper: none of its named results supplies the computational anchor ChoCo is meant to continuize, and the most plausible population scenario loses essential order information unless it becomes a new model.
