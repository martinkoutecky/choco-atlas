What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the general characterization in Theorem 33 and the rule-specific characterizations in Theorem 48; the paper proves no algorithmic or hardness results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is built around Theorem 33, proved in this paper: on a regular domain of finite preference profiles, every social choice correspondence satisfying neutrality, consistency, and cancellation is one of the net-outdegree rule \(O\), the net-indegree rule \(I\), or the rule that always selects all alternatives. The paper also proves related characterizations for Borda, Approval Voting, and other rules, including in Theorem 48.

A continuous version would take voter types to be complete preference relations on \(A\), and a society to be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support of such types. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the population fraction of type \(t\). Define each alternative’s net-outdegree score by summing its pairwise preference contributions over those masses; the corresponding rule selects the score maximizers. One could ask whether, on an appropriate domain of these distributions, neutrality, consistency under population mixtures, and cancellation characterize just the continuous versions of \(O\), \(I\), and the rule selecting all alternatives. This is a plausible population reinterpretation, but it is an axiomatic characterization question.

That distinction is decisive for this programme: the paper contains no named computational result asserting a complexity classification such as polynomial-time solvability or hardness. Theorem 33 and Theorem 48 are axiomatic results, so they cannot serve as the required anchors. Their continuous analogues also continuize an axiomatic question, which the programme explicitly places outside scope. Thus there is no qualifying continuous problem to hand to a prover on the basis of this paper.

The weak point is therefore the central one: the plausible mirror is not computational. The paper offers a natural continuous-population interpretation, but not an eligible computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest population model is sensible: let types be complete preference relations and let \(\mu_t\) be their population masses. Neutrality, consistency under mixtures, and cancellation can all be restated for those societies. But that gives a continuous axiomatic question, not the computational question ChoCo is set up to study.

That defeats the Theorem 33 anchor even under this better model. The theorem classifies social choice correspondences by properties they satisfy across a domain of profiles; it makes no claim about the complexity of an algorithm on an encoded society. Theorem 48 does the same for Borda, Approval Voting, and other rules. Its many characterizations are not computational results in disguise. The paper’s network-level characterizations have the same limitation.

One could add a computational task involving these rules, such as manipulation or winner robustness. That might be worthwhile research, but it would be a new question attached to the paper’s rules, not a continuous mirror of any result this paper proves. Rational capacities in the network formalism and weighted preference types make a population interpretation plausible; they do not supply the missing computational anchor.

So the honest negative case is narrow but strong: neither named anchor survives ChoCo’s computational-scope gate, even though both admit natural continuous restatements. The limit of this case is that the paper could inspire adjacent computational work. It provides no basis for claiming that such work is impossible—only that it would not be a mirror of this paper’s results.
