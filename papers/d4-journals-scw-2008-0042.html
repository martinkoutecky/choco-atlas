The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid anchored case for a continuous computational mirror of this paper. Its numbered results concern stability properties of screening rules: for example, whether a rule always selects a weak Condorcet set. The paper reports that the six procedures used in practice can violate stability and gives results explaining their use; it does not establish named hardness or algorithmic-complexity results for a problem. [The paper’s abstract](https://bse.eu/research/working-papers/how-choose-non-controversial-list-k-names) describes those stability questions as its focus.

A large organization choosing a shortlist could plausibly have many members and relatively few distinct rankings, so a high-multiplicity population model is imaginable. But that would not supply the required computational anchor. Turning the paper’s stability claims themselves into the proposed question would continuize an axiomatic property, which the programme’s scope explicitly excludes. I would not present that as a mirror of this paper’s computational result: there is no named computational result here to anchor one to.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The supplied “case for” offers no affirmative anchor to defeat; it argues that the paper lacks one. That is the strongest objection here. The paper’s results concern whether screening rules satisfy stability, and which rules can fail it. They do not give complexity results for a computational problem.

A large organization could plausibly have many members drawn from a small set of ranking types. Representing it by masses over those types is sensible. But that changes the input to the paper’s analysis, not its question: scores and pairwise majority comparisons become weighted sums, and stability remains an axiom about the rule. Computing those sums for a listed support does not turn the paper’s stability results into computational results.

The strongest nearby proposal is to ask how much mass must change preferences before a rule’s shortlist becomes stable, or unstable. That could be a worthwhile robustness problem. But it adds a distance measure, intervention costs, and an optimization objective that the paper does not study. It is an extension inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of its results. My case is strong on that distinction; its weak point is that the proposed robustness problem may still be worth pursuing as a separate ChoCo question.
