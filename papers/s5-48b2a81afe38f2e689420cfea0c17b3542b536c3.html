The continuous mirror question

Given finite candidates \(C\), an explicitly listed finite type set \(\Theta\) of complete fallback ballots \(\theta=(S_\theta,\prec_\theta)\), rational registered mass \(\mu\), available mass \(\nu\), target \(w\), and budget \(\lambda\), decide whether there exists \(x\) with \(0\le x_\theta\le\nu_\theta\) and \(\lVert x\rVert_1\le\lambda\) such that \(w\) is the unique fallback winner of \(\mu+x\). The deletion variant asks whether such an \(x\) exists with \(0\le x_\theta\le\mu_\theta\) and \(w\) the unique winner of \(\mu-x\), using \(s^\ell_\rho(a)=\sum_{\theta:\,a\in S_\theta,\ \operatorname{pos}_\theta(a)\le\ell}\rho_\theta\) and the paper's majority-level and approval-stage rule with masses.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity fallback electorate with complete ballot types \(\Theta\), registered and available mass vectors \(\mu,\nu\), and a continuous control vector \(x\). The objective is to minimize \(\lVert x\rVert_1\), subject to type-capacity constraints and the requirement that the target \(w\) uniquely wins under the mass-based fallback rule.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the two constructive voter-control hardness results in Theorem 3.11: adding voters and deleting voters. It leaves candidate control, voter partition control, and destructive voter-control results outside the proposed mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove exact polynomial-time solvability and bit-complexity bounds for the explicitly represented mass-control problems, including strict majority and unique-winner conditions.
  • Characterize the pricing or separation complexity when \(\Theta\) is implicitly represented as the set of all fallback ballots.
  • Establish rounding guarantees and a formal two-way dictionary between the continuous mass problems and finite high-multiplicity elections.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s voter-control results, with Theorem 3.11 as the lead anchor.

Theorem 3.11, proved in this paper, states that fallback voting is resistant to constructive control by adding voters and by deleting voters. “Resistant” means both susceptible and NP-hard. For adding voters, the input is an election \((C,V\cup V')\), a distinguished candidate \(w\), and a limit \(k\); the question is whether at most \(k\) voters from \(V'\) can be added so that \(w\) becomes the unique fallback-voting winner. For deleting voters, the input is \((C,V)\), \(w\), and \(k\); the question is whether at most \(k\) voters can be deleted so that \(w\) becomes the unique winner. Both hardness results are proved here by reductions from Exact Cover by Three-Sets.

My continuous problem is Continuous Constructive Voter-Mass Control for Fallback Voting. Fix a candidate set \(C\). A voter type is a fallback ballot \(\theta=(S_\theta,\prec_\theta)\), consisting of an approved subset \(S_\theta\subseteq C\) and a linear order of its approved candidates. A society is a rational mass vector \(\mu\) over these types. For type \(\theta\), let \(\operatorname{pos}_\theta(a)\) be the position of \(a\) among the approved candidates, and define

\[ \operatorname{score}^{\ell}_{\mu}(a) = \sum_{\theta:\,a\in S_\theta,\ \operatorname{pos}_\theta(a)\le \ell} \mu_\theta . \]

Fallback voting is applied exactly as in the paper, replacing voter counts by masses and the strict-majority threshold by \(\|\mu\|_1/2\).

In the addition version, an instance consists of a registered society \(\mu\), an available pool \(\nu\), a distinguished candidate \(w\), and a rational budget \(\lambda\). The decision question is whether there exists a vector \(x\) satisfying

\[ 0\le x_\theta\le \nu_\theta, \qquad \|x\|_1\le\lambda, \]

such that \(w\) is the unique fallback winner of \(\mu+x\). Equivalently, one can minimize \(\|x\|_1\). The deletion version replaces \(\mu+x\) by \(\mu-x\) and imposes \(0\le x_\theta\le\mu_\theta\).

This is a genuine mirror of the underlying problem. Candidates remain discrete, every unit of mass receives an ordinary integral fallback ballot, and the chair still performs exactly the paper’s action—adding or deleting voters. Only the electorate is continuized. The appropriate regime is a large election or platform consultation with \(N\gg\tau\) participants but relatively few distinct fallback ballots: many voters share the same approval set, approved ranking, and registered/unregistered status. For example, a national consultation using a standardized approval-and-ranking interface could have millions of voters but only a much smaller number of recurring ballot types.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. For each possible fallback-winning level \(\ell\), the conditions that \(w\) wins uniquely are linear in \(x\). Before level \(\ell\), every candidate must have mass at most half of the total; at level \(\ell\), \(w\) must exceed half the total and strictly exceed every rival’s level-\(\ell\) score. The approval-stage case is also linear. Thus one can solve one rational LP for each \(\ell\), with variables \(x_\theta\), capacity constraints, and an objective minimizing added or deleted mass. The resulting algorithm is polynomial in \(m\), the explicitly represented number of types, and the input bit length.

The interesting point is exactly what Theorem 3.11 makes visible: its discrete NP-hardness comes from choosing an integral subset of individual voters. In the continuous high-multiplicity version, those choices become fractional mass transfers. The Exact Cover construction therefore becomes a fractional-cover system handled by LP. This is not a claim that the paper’s hardness proof was mistaken; it is the computational question the continuization programme is designed to ask—whether population multiplicity, rather than the candidate structure, was carrying the obstruction.

The mirror covers Theorem 3.11’s constructive voter-addition and voter-deletion results. I would not claim to mirror the candidate-control theorems, since continuizing candidates would violate the programme’s scope. The main weakness is that a referee could regard arbitrary fractional selection from each ballot type as too permissive. My answer is that this is precisely the intended high-multiplicity regime: if a type represents a large interchangeable cohort, selecting \(37\%\) of that cohort is the honest population-level analogue of selecting voters. The remaining boundary question is whether an implicitly represented type space creates a nontrivial pricing problem; with explicitly supplied type masses, the continuous control problem has the LP formulation above.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 3.11’s hardness is a particularly thin object to continuize. Its reductions use one specially constructed voter per set in an Exact-Cover instance. Replacing those voters by divisible mass turns the control action into ordinary linear resource allocation; there is no identity-sensitive objective, no meaningful individual constraint, and no nontrivial type-level pricing problem. If the programme required every mirror to expose new geometry or a difficult separation problem, this would look like a routine high-multiplicity reformulation.

That objection does not survive the programme’s rules, however. Theorem 3.11 is explicitly a numbered computational result, and high multiplicity is precisely the intended regime. A national consultation or standardized platform can naturally contain many voters sharing the same approval set and approved ranking. Registered voters and an available pool can be represented by two mass measures over complete fallback-ballot types; no individual identity is needed.

The proponent’s formulation is also a genuine mirror, not merely a slogan. For each possible first winning level, the conditions for \(w\) to win uniquely are linear inequalities in the added or deleted masses. The approval fallback case is linear as well. A finite case split over winning levels and tie situations yields LPs, with rational strict inequalities handled in the usual way. Normalizing after addition or deletion changes nothing, since fallback winners are invariant under positive scaling.

The obvious repairs do not create a negative case. If the ballot support is explicitly listed, the LP is a valid high-multiplicity problem. If the support is implicit, one can ask a stronger pricing version—but that is an additional worthwhile question, not an obstruction. Requiring integral whole-voter actions would simply return to the discrete problem and would defeat the purpose of continuization.

Thus the only plausible criticism is that this anchor may be less ambitious than mirrors involving exponential type spaces and nontrivial separation. It is not that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. Under the programme’s stated standard, I cannot honestly defeat this anchor: the negative case is weak, and Theorem 3.11 should remain a green candidate for a continuous mirror.
