No result is mirrored. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4, along with the paper’s other numbered results on assignment-rule properties and Markov chains, remain outside the proposed computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the *model* has a credible high-multiplicity regime. Section 5.1.1 explicitly says the analysis can extend to groups of agents entering and leaving together. Think of a large school system or university assigning posts to recurring cohorts. With fixed tenure \(n\) and a fixed set \(K\) of productivity categories, agents of the same age, productivity, and current object are indistinguishable for the paper’s purposes. Cohort size and the supply of each object rank can scale together, while the number of these types stays bounded. A continuous version would treat those groups as masses and assignments as flows of mass to eligible objects, preserving the paper’s individual-rationality constraint and discounted-surplus objective. That is a plausible mirror of the authors’ assignment problem, rather than a mirror of continuity in the outcome alone.

The obstacle is the required anchor: this paper has no numbered computational-complexity result asserting that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its nearest substantive results are not such anchors. Theorem 3, proved here, says that when there are at least three productivity types, there are type distributions and/or discount factors for which no assignment rule can be both efficient and quasi-convergent. Theorem 4, also proved here, says that with exactly two types, the type-rank and type-seniority rules are both efficient and quasi-convergent. These are existence and characterization results about assignment rules, not complexity results.

So the population-continuum model is a reasonable research lead, and the authors’ group-entry extension supports its plausibility. But turning Theorems 3 or 4 into anchors would violate your criterion: the continuum question would concern an axiomatic existence property, while the programme asks for computational questions. I cannot honestly supply a qualifying continuous problem per anchor for this paper. The missing piece is a computational result on the underlying assignment problem; any mass-flow optimization question would be a new question, not a mirror anchored by the paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with the paper’s actual results. Theorem 3 gives an existence impossibility: for some type distributions or discount factors, no rule is both efficient and quasi-convergent. Theorem 4 exhibits efficient, quasi-convergent rules when there are two types. The other named results characterize axioms and Markov-chain behavior. None states a computational problem or classifies its complexity, so the proponent correctly supplies no qualifying anchor.

A plausible population regime does exist. Fix the lifetime \(n\) and productivity set \(K\), let large cohorts enter together, and scale the supply of each object rank with cohort size. Agents with the same age, productivity, and current object are then indistinguishable for the model’s purposes. The state can record the mass assigned to each age–productivity–object combination; those masses may be fractional even though each person holds an integral object. The paper’s group-entry extension supports this reading. I would not argue that identity, arrival time, or indivisibility makes a continuous population incoherent.

The strongest proposed mirror would ask whether an efficient dynamic assignment policy can be found—or whether it can also satisfy a fairness condition—in this cohort model. That could be worthwhile research. But it adds a computational task the paper does not pose: the authors give no finite encoding of the policies, algorithmic input, or complexity parameter for selecting one. Recasting Theorems 3 or 4 as algorithmic questions would therefore add a problem, rather than mirror a computational result. The resulting dynamic control problem might also need substantial new machinery; a continuous population does not by itself produce an LP with a tractable pricing problem.

So the defensible negative is specific: this paper is a poor *anchored mirror* for ChoCo’s computational programme, despite having a credible high-multiplicity regime. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous study is too strong. The group-entry version could motivate a new computational paper, but that would be a new contribution rather than a continuous mirror of this one.
