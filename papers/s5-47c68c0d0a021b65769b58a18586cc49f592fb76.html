The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(d\) and \(0<\varepsilon<1-\frac{1}{d+1}\), given \(m\) goods, \(\tau\) valuation types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), value oracles \(f_\theta:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) satisfying \(\mathrm{SMW}(f_\theta)\le d\), and binary-rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), does there exist a value-query algorithm using \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) queries that returns a feasible allocation with welfare at least \((\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\)? Here an allocation is \(x_{\theta,S}\ge0\), the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving whole bundle \(S\), with \(\sum_S x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S:j\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le b_j\).

The model it lives in

A configuration allocation model over integral bundles: each type \(\theta\) has mass \(\mu_\theta\) and valuation oracle \(f_\theta\); decision variables \(x_{\theta,S}\) distribute that mass across bundles, subject to per-capita item supplies \(b_j\), and the objective is \(\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}f_\theta(S)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns the welfare-maximization query lower bound in Theorem 11; it makes no claim about Theorem 9, the approximation guarantees, or the auction price-of-anarchy results.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(d\) and \(\varepsilon\), is the stated approximation achievable with \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) value queries, or does a query lower bound persist when mass can split across bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 11, proved in this paper. For every \(d\in\mathbb N\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), it constructs a family of \(n\) monotone valuation functions \(f_1,\ldots,f_n:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb R^+\), each with supermodular width exactly \(d\), such that any algorithm producing a \((1/(d+1)+\varepsilon)\)-approximation to \(n\)-agent welfare maximization with constant success probability needs \(\Omega((n/(2D))^{(m/n)^{0.99}})\) value queries, where \(D=d+1\). This is a proved information-theoretic oracle lower bound, rather than an NP-hardness or running-time result.

A plausible mirror is SMW-\(d\) continuous welfare maximization. Take \(m\) categories of goods and a finite set \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\) buyer types. A type \(\theta\) is its complete valuation function \(f_\theta\); the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types. Let \(b_j\) be the per-capita supply of good category \(j\), with \(\mu\) and \(b\) encoded in binary. A solution is a sparse allocation \(x_{\theta,S}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq[m]\), satisfying \(\sum_{S}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S:j\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le b_j\) for every good \(j\). Its welfare is \(\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}f_\theta(S)\). Given value oracles for the \(f_\theta\), the task is to return an allocation whose welfare is at least \((1/(d+1)+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}}\), under the promise that each type has supermodular width at most \(d\).

The scenario is a large market with many buyers drawn from a small number of stable demand profiles: for example, a population of customers with a handful of recurring preferences over standardized goods. The type records everything relevant to the allocation problem, including the valuation, and mass records the share of buyers with that profile. In a finite realization with \(N\) buyers, type \(\theta\) occurs \(N\mu_\theta\) times and category \(j\) has \(Nb_j\) units; each buyer still gets a whole bundle. Thus \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). Scaling category supplies with the population is part of making this welfare problem a sensible high-multiplicity market.

The question for a prover is whether, for fixed \(d,\varepsilon\), the continuous problem admits a polynomial-in-\((m,\tau,L)\) value-query algorithm at that approximation threshold, or whether a lower bound analogous to Theorem 11 holds even when types have large populations. A concrete hardness conjecture would ask whether there are uniform-mass instances with support \(\tau\) requiring \(\Omega((\tau/(2D))^{(m/\tau)^{0.99}})\) queries. My tentative expectation for this sharper threshold is Class C, from the difficulty of handling complementary bundle valuations through oracle access; a pricing problem of the form \(\max_{S\subseteq[m]}(f_\theta(S)-\lambda(S))\) is a natural place that difficulty might remain. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 11. If fixed-width structure makes pricing manageable, the answer could instead be Class A.

The mirror covers only the welfare-maximization lower bound in Theorem 11. It does not claim to mirror Theorem 9’s single-function constrained maximization or the auction price-of-anarchy results. The paper’s finite-agent welfare problem is evidence that welfare is a natural object for these authors; it is not a novelty collision with its high-multiplicity version.

The soft spot is that Theorem 11’s hard construction uses \(n\) valuation functions, while the proposed regime has \(N\gg\tau\) buyers sharing types. Its lower bound may track the number of distinct valuations rather than the population size, and allowing each type’s mass to split among bundles changes the allocation problem. So the theorem motivates this precise mirror question, but does not settle it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent starts with Theorem 11’s parameter \(n\). It counts distinct valuation oracles, and the hard instance gives each agent a different hidden set \(R_i\). The lower bound therefore establishes difficulty as the number of separately queried valuations grows. It does not establish difficulty caused by population multiplicity. Replicating each valuation many times leaves only \(\tau\) oracles, and the theorem’s proof does not show that its welfare gap survives when each type’s mass can be split among bundles.

That gap matters in the proposed mirror. Its variables \(x_{\theta,S}\) form a configuration LP: each type’s mass is distributed across whole bundles, subject to per-capita supply constraints. Fractional configuration variables and dual bundle-pricing queries are standard in combinatorial-auction welfare optimization.[Configuration-LP overview](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~feige/AGT2024/11CLP.pdf) So the LP itself supplies little novelty. The paper’s value-query lower bound also sits in a mature line of oracle-complexity results for welfare maximization.[Mirrokni, Vondrák, and Schapira](https://theory.stanford.edu/~jvondrak/data/alloc-bounds.pdf)

That is a serious objection to presenting the continuous question as a transfer of Theorem 11. It does not defeat the stronger mirror the proponent proposes: whether the SMW-\(d\) welfare problem over \(\tau\) valuation types has useful query algorithms or lower bounds as a function of \(m,\tau,L\). The configuration LP leaves the pricing problem open, and Theorem 11 does not answer it. A market with many buyers sharing a few demand profiles and proportionally scaled supplies is a coherent high-multiplicity regime.

I cannot honestly make the negative case prevail. Theorem 11 is a genuine computational anchor, and its best continuous-population mirror is well-posed. The defensible opposition is narrower: the theorem motivates that mirror but gives no evidence that its hardness survives aggregation and mass splitting. Since the programme treats a well-posed question as worthwhile even if its eventual answer is unsurprising, that objection does not defeat the anchor.
