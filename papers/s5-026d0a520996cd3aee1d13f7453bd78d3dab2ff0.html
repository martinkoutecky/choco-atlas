The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification. The closest anchors are both proved here, but neither qualifies:

  • Proposition 1 says that, for a solution \(F\) satisfying anonymity and scale invariance, efficiency plus BWARP1 and BWARP2 is equivalent to \(F\) selecting from the Nash solution and agreeing with it on simple problems.
  • Theorem 1 says that \(F\) satisfies efficiency, anonymity, scale invariance, BWARP1, BWARP2, weak contraction independence, and the equity principle if and only if \(F=F^{EN}\).

Both are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results. So there is no eligible anchor on which to base a continuous problem, and no honest way to give the requested expected complexity outcome.

The nearest imaginable mirror would replace the finite set of bargaining parties \(N\) with a population distributed across utility types, then define a bargaining solution over type-level utility profiles. But the paper’s feasible sets \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n_+\) describe outcomes for a fixed number of parties; their continuity is in the outcome space, not in the population. Replacing the Nash product and the equity criterion with population aggregates would introduce a new model, not a well-supported continuization of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 do not provide computational anchors. They characterize which bargaining solutions satisfy specified axioms; neither defines a computational problem, gives an algorithm, nor classifies complexity. The feasible set \(A\) can be any compact comprehensive subset of \(\mathbb{R}^n_+\), with no input encoding. If \(A\) is given as the comprehensive hull of finitely many points, the equitable Nash solution can be found by checking those generators: the Nash product is maximized at a generator, and the equity criterion then selects among the maximizers. If \(A\) is given implicitly, the complexity depends on that representation. Continuizing the parties does not resolve this missing computational model.

The strongest candidate mirror would be a large bargaining society with finitely many utility types and masses \(\mu_t\). If everyone of type \(t\) receives the same utility \(u_t\), a natural Nash objective is \(\sum_t \mu_t\log u_t\), followed by an equity tie-break such as maximizing \(\min_t u_t/m_t\). But that formulation adds choices the paper does not settle. If same-type people may receive different utilities, type averages lose information needed by the Nash product and the minimum-ratio criterion. If they must receive the same utility, that is an added restriction on feasible allocations. With a diffuse type space, an essential minimum ignores null sets, while other population-level equity criteria would yield different solutions. These are potentially sensible models, but they do not carry either characterization over as a computational result.

So I would argue against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: its results are axiomatic, and a complexity question would require both a new instance representation and substantive choices about population equity. The universal claim is less secure. Section 6 expressly invites collective-choice reinterpretations, so a separately developed continuum bargaining project could be worthwhile. That possibility does not rescue Proposition 1 or Theorem 1 as computational anchors in this paper.
