The case FOR (proponent)

Under the anchor rule, I can’t make a compliant positive case for this paper: it contains no named computational result. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1 and 2, proved here, are structural factorization results. In particular, Theorem 2 says that for a weakly superadditive game \(v\), its core is the core of the additive part \(v-\hat v\), plus the intersection of the cores of the zero-normalized component games \(\hat v_B\). That is a decomposition identity, not an algorithm, complexity classification, or formulation the paper computes with. The paper’s real-valued games and polyhedral payoff sets also do not make its population continuous: the player set remains finite.

A plausible follow-on model would take a large cooperative organization with finitely many interchangeable roles \(T\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of role \(t\), let \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) describe a coalition, and let \(V(q)\) give its worth. The core problem would ask for per-capita payoffs \(p_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=V(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t q_t p_t\ge V(q)\) for every feasible coalition \(q\). That is a genuine continuous-population version of core feasibility, and the authors could recognize the underlying solution concept. But it is an extension, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper; the paper establishes neither a continuum analogue of Theorem 2 nor the computational status of this problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1 and 2 prove factorization identities for solution maps. Theorem 2, for example, expresses a finite game’s core using the cores of games indexed by its coalitions. It gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or computational problem with an input encoding. The paper’s other results have the same structural focus.

The proposed role-type model is sensible: represent coalitions by mass vectors \(q\), specify worths \(V(q)\), and ask whether per-capita payoffs \(p_t\) satisfy \(\sum_t \mu_t p_t=V(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t q_t p_t\ge V(q)\) for all \(q\). But this introduces both a new worth-function representation and a new computational question. If \(V\) is unrestricted, the model does not specify a finite computational input; if it has a compact piecewise or oracle representation, that choice supplies essential structure absent from the paper. Extending Theorem 2 directly would give an intersection indexed by continuously many coalitions. That is a possible identity, not a computational result.

The other results do not provide a stronger anchor. Theorem 1 decomposes an additive solution over generators of a game cone, and Proposition 1 characterizes such solutions on a basis. The probabilistic-value, nucleolus, Weber-set, and selectope sections likewise factor solution definitions. Their organizing objects are games and solution geometry; none studies population multiplicity or computation. I would not argue that repeated player types are inherently implausible: symmetric role classes could make a mass model natural. The issue is that this paper supplies no computational question for that model to continue.

So I would oppose a green verdict for a *continuous mirror of this paper*. A continuous core computation for role-type populations could be a worthwhile independent project, but its value would depend on modeling and input choices the paper never makes. I cannot honestly rule out every valuable follow-on scenario; the negative case is strongest on the narrower point that none is anchored in a computational result here.
