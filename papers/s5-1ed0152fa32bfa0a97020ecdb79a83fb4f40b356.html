What the mirror covers

The proposed case covers only Theorems 3.7 and 3.8; it leaves the EM guarantees, majority-voting comparisons, and Theorem 5.1 without a proposed mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a high-multiplicity crowdsourcing cohort: many recruited workers label the same binary items, and workers with the same reliability \(p\) share a type. The society is the rational type measure \(\rho_p=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{p_i}\); its masses are worker proportions. Workers of one type have the same response law, while their individual noisy labels remain part of the observed data. This is plausible when a large labeling pool has a few stable skill levels and the requester knows the aggregate composition, but not which worker belongs to which level.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.7, proved in this paper. It says that when \(\bar\nu<1/2\), for every estimator \(\hat y\in[0,1]^m\),
\[ \sup_{y\in\{0,1\}^m,\;p\in\mathcal P_{\bar\nu}} \mathbb E_{p,y}\!\left[\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m|\hat y_j-y_j|\right] \ge \frac{1}{8(6e)^2}\exp(-6n\bar\nu), \]
for every \(n\ge4\) and \(m\ge1\), where \(\mathcal P_{\bar\nu}\) constrains \(\frac1n\sum_i(2p_i-1)^2=\bar\nu\). The measure version of that problem is: given \(n,m,\bar\nu\) (and optionally a support bound \(\tau\)), characterize the minimax average labeling risk when the unknown society is a rational worker-type measure with that second moment, the assignment of types to workers is hidden, and each worker independently labels each item according to the one-coin model. A solution is a minimax risk characterization and an estimator attaining the claimed upper bound, or a lower-bound certificate showing no estimator can do better. The theorem’s lower bound applies to the unrestricted type class; its least-favorable construction also has just expert and spammer types when the proportions align with \(n\).

A second useful anchor is Theorem 3.8, also proved here. When \(\bar\mu\ge3/4\), every estimator obeys
\[ \sup_{y\in\{0,1\}^m,\;p\in\mathcal P_{\bar\mu}} \mathbb E_{p,y}\!\left[\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m|\hat y_j-y_j|\right] \ge \frac18\exp\!\bigl(-8nD(\bar\mu\|1-\bar\mu)\bigr), \]
for \(n\ge6\) and \(m\ge1\), where \(\mathcal P_{\bar\mu}\) fixes the average effective ability \(\bar\mu\). Its type-measure counterpart asks for the minimax label risk over worker societies satisfying \(\int \eta(p)\,d\rho(p)=\bar\mu\), with \(\eta(p)=p\) for \(p\ge1/2\) and \(1-p\) otherwise. The same estimator and risk-certificate outputs count as solutions. This anchor includes the single-type society \(\rho=\delta_{\bar\mu}\), so it tests the finite-sample noise limit even when there is no heterogeneity.

Theorem 3.7 is the stronger mirror: its low-wisdom regime makes the unknown allocation of a small expert mass central, and the theorem quantifies the resulting limit. Theorem 3.8 is a useful separate benchmark for a homogeneous crowd. Both concern only minimax ground-truth labeling risk; I would not stretch this case to cover the paper’s worker-ability estimates, EM guarantees, or majority-voting comparison.

There is a real limitation for ChoCo’s computational agenda. The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind that says a problem is in P, NP-hard, or FPT. These anchors prove information-theoretic lower bounds, not computational hardness. The corresponding computational question would be whether a polynomial-time estimator in \(n,m,\tau,L\) attains the minimax rate; these theorems give a target, but do not answer that question. Their lower bounds persist for the finite-panel type-measure formulation, but that is not a Class B hardness transfer, and the trichotomy does not classify statistical impossibility.

The weakest point is that the positive mirror must retain a finite recruited panel of \(n\) workers. If “continuous population” instead means infinitely many independent workers whose responses are observed only as aggregate mass, the sampling fluctuations behind these lower bounds disappear; the resulting estimation problem can be fundamentally easier. The paper’s authors should recognize the finite-panel type-measure version as their Dawid–Skene problem expressed by worker shares. Whether they would accept that as a continuous-population computational problem is less certain: \(n\) and the individual response matrix remain essential, and the continuous measure chiefly reorganizes the model parameters.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Neither Theorem 3.7 nor Theorem 3.8 is a computational-complexity result. They bound statistical risk: for any estimator, some allowed instance forces a certain labeling error. The paper gives no theorem about worst-case runtime, hardness, or exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithms for a computational problem. Asking whether a polynomial-time estimator attains a minimax rate could be worthwhile statistics research, but these theorems do not establish a ChoCo complexity question.

For Theorem 3.7, the proposed type measure has two possible meanings, and neither makes the theorem a strong continuous mirror. If it is the empirical measure \(\rho_n=\frac1n\sum_i\delta_{p_i}\), it is an exact, permutation-invariant encoding of the finite ability vector. The theorem’s class and loss are already symmetric in worker order; recording them as masses changes notation, not the statistical experiment. If instead it is a genuinely continuous worker population, the finite-\(n\) sampling noise behind the bound is gone. With a continuum of workers, response frequencies within a type equal their probabilities; the theorem’s finite-panel risk bound no longer describes that setting. A formulation that keeps the finite panel to preserve the bound keeps the finite sampling problem, while a formulation that replaces it with mass must ask a different statistical question.

The paper’s least-favorable expert/spammer construction does not rescue the mirror. Its expert proportion is a finite-panel quantity, about \(\bar\nu\), and the hard part is uncertainty about the sampled workers and their realized labels. A continuous mass of experts removes that finite allocation and sampling granularity. One could retain a finite sample drawn from a continuous ability distribution, but then the distribution is a parameter or prior for a statistical experiment, not a mass-transfer input to an optimization problem. If the type assignments are hidden, the question becomes inference over latent types; if they are given, the original minimax uncertainty has changed.

Theorem 3.8 is an even less persuasive population mirror. Its class includes the single-type society \(\rho=\delta_{\bar\mu}\). For that case there is no heterogeneous population structure to aggregate: the result is ordinary finite-sample binary testing from \(n\) noisy responses per item. Keeping \(n\) retains that same finite testing problem; replacing the workers by a continuum removes the sampling error that produces the bound. The theorem’s lower bound may be a useful statistical benchmark, but the type measure adds no new computational object.

The proponents’ best scenario—a large crowdsourcing pool with a few stable ability classes—is plausible, and workers with the same ability are exchangeable under this model. That makes a type measure a sensible statistical description. It does not, by itself, create the kind of computational mirror ChoCo is charting: the target is the truth of \(m\) named items, the society distribution is unknown or merely a summary of finite workers, and there is no fractional intervention over the population. The paper’s EM analysis concerns estimating from random observations, not optimizing a social choice operation over a continuous society.

So the negative case is strongest on fit with the programme, rather than on whether one could invent a continuous statistical model. A finite-panel Dawid–Skene problem can be reparameterized by worker shares, and a continuous-population statistical extension could be studied. But the former is only a measure-valued encoding of the finite experiment; the latter changes the experiment and is not a computational result these theorems anchor. I do not see a worthwhile continuous *computational-social-choice* mirror here, though the statistical extension could belong in a different research programme.
