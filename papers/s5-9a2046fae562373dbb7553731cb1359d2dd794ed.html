The continuous mirror question

For each group \(i\), let \(\mu_i\) be a rational probability distribution on a finite support of additive valuation types \(a_{it}\in[0,1]^m\), with \(\tau_i=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu_i)|\). Goods remain indivisible. Given an ordering of each support and bounds \(\Delta^i_t\) such that every suffix has each column sum at most its corresponding \(\Delta^i_t\), with \(d\le\min_{i,t}\Delta^i_t\) and \(r\ge\max_{i,t,i',t'}\Delta^i_t/\Delta^{i'}_{t'}\), find a partition \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\in\{0,1\}^m\), \(\sum_i v_i=\mathbf 1\), for which every supported type satisfies \(\left|\langle a_{it},v_i-\frac1k\mathbf 1\rangle\right|\le C'\,\Xi(\tau_{\max}/d,r)\sqrt{\Delta^i_t}\), where \(\tau_{\max}=\max_i\tau_i\) and \(\Xi(s,r)=C\log_2(4s)\sqrt{\log_2(4r)}\).

The model it lives in

Each group's society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu_i\) over complete additive valuation types. The decision variables are integral bundle indicators \(v_i\); feasibility requires the structured per-type deviation bound. The exact masses determine which types are present, while the every-type guarantee makes their positive values otherwise immaterial.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 13's structured, two-sided allocation guarantee over supported valuation types. It leaves the unrestricted worst-case bounds in Theorems 2 and 4 and the lower-bound construction in Section 4 without a corresponding continuous result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for a structured, high-multiplicity group-division problem. The paper has no named NP-hardness or P-classification result for its main fair-division problem. Its most relevant in-paper result is a constructive allocation guarantee; the explicit polynomial-time algorithm it relies on is a cited discrepancy result. So this is a case for the paper’s rounding machinery, not a claim that its headline bounds settle a new complexity classification.

My lead anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper. It says that for valuation matrices \(A_i\in[0,1]^{n_i\times m}\) that are \((\Delta^i_j)\)-structured, with the ratio of the largest to smallest \(\Delta^i_j\) at most \(r\) and \(d\le n_1\) a lower bound on all the \(\Delta^i_j\), there is a partition \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\in\{0,1\}^m\) of the goods such that every row satisfies
\[ \left|\left\langle A^i_j,v_i-\frac1k\mathbf1\right\rangle\right| \le C'\,\Xi(n_1/d,r)\sqrt{\Delta^i_j}, \qquad \Xi(s,r)=C\log_2(4s)\sqrt{\log_2(4r)}. \]
Here “structured” means that, in the row order for group \(i\), every suffix has each column sum at most its corresponding \(\Delta^i_j\). This is a two-sided guarantee around each group’s \(1/k\) share.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Continuous-Type Structured Group Partition. There are \(m\) indivisible goods and \(k\) groups. Group \(i\) is a population measure \(\mu_i\) over a finite support of additive valuation types \(a_{it}\in[0,1]^m\), with positive rational type masses summing to one. A type fully specifies an agent’s valuation; everyone of that type receives the same group bundle. Let \(\tau_i=|\operatorname{supp}\mu_i|\). The input also gives an ordering of each group’s types and bounds \(\Delta^i_j\) satisfying the suffix column-sum condition, together with valid \(r\) and \(d\). Find a partition of the goods such that every positive-mass type \(t\) in group \(i\) satisfies
\[ \left|\langle a_{it},v_i\rangle-\frac1k\langle a_{it},\mathbf1\rangle\right| \le C'\,\Xi(\tau_{\max}/d,r)\sqrt{\Delta^i_t}, \qquad \tau_{\max}=\max_i\tau_i. \]
This is Theorem 13 with one row per distinct type. A plausible regime is large cohorts—perhaps residents of several districts sharing a common bundle—with millions of people but a modest number of standardized valuation types. The structural promise is most credible when valuations have sparse or bounded demand patterns; it is a real restriction, not a property of every group-division instance.

I expect Class A on that promised family: the proof constructs the partition through recursive rounding and gifting, using the paper’s rounding results. That expectation comes from the construction; Theorem 13 itself does not state a polynomial-time classification or a bit-complexity bound. The mirror covers this structured upper-bound construction, not the paper’s full worst-case \(\mathrm{PROP}(n_1,\ldots,n_k)\) theorem or its lower bound.

The algorithmic anchor behind that construction is Theorem 5, the Partial Coloring Lemma cited from Lovett and Meka (2012), not proved in this paper. For \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\in\mathbb R^m\), a starting point \(y\in[-1,1]^m\), and thresholds \(c_i\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_i e^{-c_i^2/16}\le m/16\), it gives an efficient randomized algorithm that, with probability at least \(0.1\), finds \(y'\in[-1,1]^m\) with
\[ |\langle y-y',v_i\rangle|\le c_i\|v_i\|_2 \]
for every \(i\), while at least \(m/2\) coordinates have \(|y'_j|\ge1-\delta\). Its stated runtime is \(O((m+n)^3\delta^{-2}\log(nm/\delta))\).

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous-Type Partial Split Rounding. Given two groups represented by finite-support measures over valuation vectors, a fractional split \(z\in[0,1]^m\), thresholds \(c_t\) with \(\sum_t e^{-c_t^2/16}\le m/16\), and an endpoint tolerance \(\eta>0\), find \(z'\in[0,1]^m\) so that every positive-mass type satisfies
\[ |\langle a_t,z-z'\rangle|\le \tfrac12 c_t\|a_t\|_2, \]
and at least half the goods have \(z'_g\le\eta\) or \(z'_g\ge1-\eta\). The latter goods are nearly assigned to one side. Substituting \(y=2z-\mathbf1\) in Theorem 5 gives this guarantee; the algorithm succeeds with probability at least \(0.1\) and has polynomial runtime in \(m\), the number of distinct types, and \(\eta^{-1}\), up to the stated logarithm. This is a randomized polynomial-time rounding subproblem, and the paper uses this kind of step in its larger allocation construction.

The weakest point is that type masses affect the population description but not these guarantees: every positive-mass type is protected, and duplicate agents collapse to one row. The structured partition result also needs the suffix bounds, while the partial-rounding algorithm is a cited tool rather than a new result of the paper. Still, the population model is doing legitimate work: it represents a large society by its distinct complete types, keeps the goods indivisible, and asks for the same per-person valuation guarantees. The continuous mirror is credible for this algorithmic slice; it does not turn the paper’s extremal bounds into a new hardness-versus-tractability result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the population measure disappears from the paper’s central guarantees. I would not argue that group fair division lacks a plausible high multiplicity setting: large cohorts sharing a bundle and drawn from a small set of valuation types are easy to imagine. The question is whether the distribution itself creates a worthwhile continuous computational problem.

For Theorem 13, write group \(i\)’s population as \(\mu_i=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_i}w_{it}\delta_{a_{it}}\), where \(a_{it}\) is a valuation vector. Its guarantee protects every positive mass type. The constraints, and the rounding construction, depend on the vectors and their structural bounds \(\Delta^i_t\); they do not depend on the weights \(w_{it}\). Two populations with identical support and different masses therefore give the same partition problem. The best exact mirror—one constraint per supported type—is coherent, but it is a row-compressed finite discrepancy instance. Repeated agents with identical valuations can already be collapsed in a discrete input.

There is a sharper difficulty in trying to make the masses matter while keeping the paper’s fairness notion. A type with mass \(\varepsilon>0\) imposes the same guarantee as a type with mass \(1/2\); at mass zero its constraint vanishes. Thus a vanishingly rare type can still determine feasibility, and the support-based problem jumps when that type disappears. An average or quantile guarantee could use the masses continuously, but it would replace the paper’s every-agent PROP guarantee with a different fairness objective.

Theorem 5 has the same problem as a population mirror. It is an efficient partial coloring result cited from Lovett and Meka, and its constraints are indexed by vectors, with no type masses. Applying it once per supported valuation is a valid algorithmic subproblem, but the fractional point being rounded is a temporary variable over goods. The population measure does no work in that formulation.

The headline bounds in Theorems 2 and 4 offer the strongest alternative mirror: take a worst case over \(\tau_i\) supported valuation types and ask for bounds in the support sizes. That could yield a useful guarantee for repeated types. But the bound would be about the number of distinct constraint rows; arbitrary weights on those rows would not affect it. This is a finite discrepancy question parameterized by support size, rather than a computational question over population mass distributions.

I would not base the negative case on the claim that the paper lacks an algorithmic result: Theorem 13 is constructively proved, and Theorem 5 supplies an explicit polynomial time rounding tool. The harder argument is that their algorithmic content acts on valuation constraints, while population multiplicity adds no mass-sensitive structure. This supports a low-priority verdict, not an airtight one. A high multiplicity group-division scenario is plausible, and a support-compressed bound could be useful; I cannot honestly claim that no author would recognize it as worth studying.
