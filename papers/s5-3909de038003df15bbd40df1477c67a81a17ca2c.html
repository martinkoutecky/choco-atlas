The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and employer type sets \(T_A,T_B\), acceptable type pairs, positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and two preference profiles ranking counterpart types, does there exist a feasible measurable matching \(M\) such that, for each profile \(\ell\in\{1,2\}\) and every feasible measurable matching \(N\), \(\Delta_\ell(M,N)\ge 0\)? Each side’s types occupy atomless populations of their specified masses; matching pairs equal masses along acceptable pairs, and unmatched mass is allowed. Each individual receives at most one integral partner, while individuals of the same type are indifferent between partners of the same counterpart type. The comparison \(\Delta_\ell\) sums each individual’s \(+1\), \(0\), or \(-1\) vote over both populations, comparing that individual’s assignment in \(M\) and \(N\) under profile \(\ell\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population partitioned into finitely many preference types with rational masses; feasible matchings pair measurable subsets across acceptable type pairs, and popularity is measured by mass-weighted votes of the same individuals across alternatives. The object is a matching of population mass, not a lottery.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 25’s two-sided popular-multilayer problem with two profiles and its popular-uncertain counterpart; leaves the paper’s one-sided, dominant, robust, and aggregated results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What finite representation captures measurable matchings and their head-to-head comparisons, and what is the decision complexity in the number of supported types \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\)?
  • Can cohort splitting undo the paper’s forced-edge gadgets, and does hardness persist through a different reduction or bounded support regime?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of the paper’s two-sided, two-layer popularity problem.

The anchor is Theorem 25, proved in this paper. It proves that deciding whether a two-sided market has a matching popular in every listed preference profile is NP-complete, even with exactly two profiles and preference variation on only one side. The paper also proves the analogous result for its independent-uncertainty model. The reduction starts from Theorem 1, cited from Faenza, Powers and Zhang [15]: the restricted forced-edge problem is NP-complete. Given an edge \((x,y)\), with \(x\) and \(y\) ranking each other first, \(x\)’s only neighbors being \(a\) and \(y\), and a specified vertex \(z\), it asks whether there is a popular matching that contains \((x,y)\) and covers \(z\).

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Certainly-Popular Two-Sided Matching. An instance has finite sets of applicant types \(T_A\) and employer types \(T_B\), an acceptable-pair graph between the types, and a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each type specifies its acceptable counterpart types and its rankings of those types in each of two profiles. Individuals within a type are interchangeable; they are indifferent between individual partners of the same counterpart type. This type-level indifference is the natural quotient when the type, rather than the named individual, carries the preference information.

Represent type \(t\)’s population by an interval of length \(\mu_t\). A feasible matching \(M\) is a measurable, measure-preserving pairing of subsets of applicant and employer intervals along acceptable type pairs; each unit of mass receives at most one partner, and unmatched mass is allowed. Thus each person still gets an integral outcome, while a cohort can be divided among different counterpart types.

For profile \(\ell\), let \(M(u)\) denote the type of \(u\)’s partner, or \(\bot\) if unmatched, and define

\[ \Delta_\ell(M,N)= \int_{\Omega_A\cup\Omega_B} \left( \mathbf{1}\{M(u)\succ^\ell_{\tau(u)}N(u)\} - \mathbf{1}\{N(u)\succ^\ell_{\tau(u)}M(u)\} \right)d\lambda(u). \]

The question is whether there is a feasible \(M\) such that \(\Delta_\ell(M,N)\ge 0\) for every feasible alternative \(N\) and both profiles \(\ell\in\{1,2\}\). A solution is the matching of population mass, not a lottery over matchings or a fractional partner for each individual. The finite input is measured by the number of supported types \(\tau\), the type-pair graph, and the bit length \(L\) of the masses and rankings.

A plausible regime is a large residency or labor clearing market: many applicants and positions, but relatively few recurring qualification, specialty, and job types. A type records the same acceptable counterpart categories and rankings in both scenarios. The number of agents can greatly exceed \(\tau\), while the two preference profiles remain exactly the paper’s two market-wide layers. The mass represents population share, not uncertainty over preferences.

This seems recognizable to the authors: it keeps their two-sided matching, pairwise popularity rule, and requirement that one matching work in both profiles. It does not make the outcome space continuous; every unit is matched to at most one partner. The paper studies finite agents and preference variation, not a continuous population distribution, so this is not a novelty collision. The mirror covers Theorem 25’s multilayer hardness result, not the paper’s whole set of results.

My provisional expectation is Class B, hardness transfers. The hard choices appear to lie in the compatibility and ranking structure over the supported types, with the number of profiles fixed at two, rather than solely in the number of individual agents. But that is a forecast, not a hardness claim: a proof must show that cohort splitting does not undo the reduction’s forced choices. Useful next questions are whether a candidate continuous matching can be verified in polynomial time, whether support-size restrictions restore tractability, and when a popular mass matching can be represented by an integral type-level matching.

The weakest point is that popularity in the paper compares assignments of named individuals. A type-flow table alone may not preserve which individual receives which type of partner across two compared matchings. The measurable-matching definition makes those comparisons well-defined on a shared population, but it also exposes the modeling choice: partners within a type are treated as indifferent. If the authors reject that type-level quotient, the proposed problem may no longer count as their analogue. The case survives only as a focused continuation for genuinely interchangeable cohorts, with fractional mass at the cohort level and integral partners at the individual level.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to the Theorem 25 mirror is that its reduction uses particular agents: it forces an edge and requires one specified vertex to be covered. The proposed type model replaces those identities with interchangeable cohorts and allows each cohort’s mass to split across partner types. So the paper’s reduction does not automatically establish hardness for the mirror.

That is a limit on the hardness claim, though, not a reason the question is unworthy. A stronger mirror would define popularity directly for measurable matchings of typed applicant and employer populations, with votes weighted by mass in each of the two profiles. The same individuals are compared across alternatives, and each still receives an integral partner. This is a coherent population version of the paper’s computational question. Splitting mass may undo the reduction’s gadgets, but whether it does is precisely something to investigate; it cannot count against asking the question.

The identity objection also fails against this version. If preferences depend on partner types and individuals within each type share all relevant features, grouping them is the high-multiplicity assumption itself. Labor or residency cohorts with recurring qualification and job types are plausible settings. The paper supplies a named computational result, and neither its finite-agent formulation nor the matching literature mentioned here answers this continuous-population question. High-multiplicity work would support the regime rather than create a novelty collision.

I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists. I can only say that the proposed mirror’s NP-hardness forecast is unproved and that cohort splitting may change the answer. Those are reasons to formulate and analyze the continuous problem carefully, not reasons to reject it.
