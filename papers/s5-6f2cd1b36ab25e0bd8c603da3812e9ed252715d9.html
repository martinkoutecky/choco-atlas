The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many valuation types θ with rational masses μ_θ summing to \(1\), finitely many good types, dates, rational per-date supplies, and a fixed rational α_0>0, decide whether goods can be assigned so that each person receives an integral bundle history and every prefix is α_0-TEFX. Equivalently, choose nonnegative masses \(x_{θ,h}\) over type-history pairs, meeting each type mass and good supply, such that for every two positive-mass pairs \((θ,h)\), \((θ',h')\), every prefix \(t\), and every good \(g\) in \(h'_{≤ t}\), \(v_θ(h_{≤ t}) ≥ α_0[v_θ(h'_{≤ t})-v_θ(g)]\). The optimization version asks for the largest feasible α.

The model it lives in

A population of unit mass partitioned into finitely many valuation types, with rational mass per type and rational supplies of indivisible good types at discrete dates. The decision variables \(x_{θ,h}\) give the mass of each type receiving each integral bundle history; they must match type masses and per-date supplies. The objective is to maximize the α for which all positive-mass histories satisfy prefix α-TEFX comparisons.

What the mirror covers

The selected mirror covers Theorem 9's impossibility of a uniform positive constant α-TEFX guarantee; Theorem 5 also has a separate surviving mirror. The other fairness, approximation, and scheduling results are not covered.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed rational α_0>0, what is the complexity of deciding feasibility as a function of the number of types, good types, dates, and input bit length?
  • Can the largest feasible α be computed or bounded in terms of valuation ratios, including when some agents assign zero value to goods?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 9. It is proved in this paper, and states that no algorithm can guarantee any fixed positive \(\alpha\)-TEFX approximation on all instances, even with two agents who have identical valuations. Its witness has three goods arriving in successive rounds, with values \(1,1,1+1/\alpha\). The paper gives no named NP-hardness theorem; this is its strongest negative approximation result.

Here is a continuous-population version of that problem. The population has mass \(1\) and one type: every person has the same additive valuation. There are three dates, with mass \(1/2\) of good type \(a\) arriving at the first, mass \(1/2\) of type \(b\) at the second, and mass \(1/2\) of type \(c\) at the third. Each copy remains indivisible for the person who receives it; fractional masses describe how many people receive each integral bundle history. Set \(v(a)=v(b)=1\) and \(v(c)=1+1/\alpha_0\), for a fixed proposed guarantee \(\alpha_0>0\).

Call this Continuous \(\alpha\)-TEFX Feasibility. More generally, its input is a finite list of agent types with rational masses summing to \(1\), rational valuations over a finite set of good types, and rational per-date supplies. A solution is a finite-support assignment of population mass to individual histories of integral bundles, meeting every supply exactly. For every two histories assigned positive mass, every prefix, and every good \(g\) in the second person’s cumulative bundle, it must satisfy
\[ v_\theta(B_{\le t})\geq \alpha_0\bigl(v_\theta(B'_{\le t})-v_\theta(g)\bigr), \]
where \(\theta\) is the first person’s type. The optimization version asks for the largest feasible \(\alpha\).

The witness gives a negative answer to any *uniform* positive guarantee in this continuous model too. After the first two dates, the total supply of value-\(1\) goods equals the population mass. A positive-\(\alpha_0\) guarantee cannot leave anyone empty while giving someone two such goods: removing one from the two-good bundle leaves positive value. Since the average number of those goods per person is \(1\), everyone must have exactly one. At the last date, the \(c\)-supply gives \(c\) to mass \(1/2\). A person who kept only a value-\(1\) good then compares with someone holding \(c\) and another good. Removing the other good leaves value \(1+1/\alpha_0\), so the required inequality would be \(1\geq\alpha_0(1+1/\alpha_0)=1+\alpha_0\), impossible. Thus the obstruction survives with infinitely many indistinguishable agents and just one type. I expect the no-constant-guarantee result to transfer. This is an approximation impossibility, not a proof of NP-hardness; the further question is what value-dependent guarantee is achievable, especially under bounded valuation ratios.

A second, more restricted anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper using an earlier two-round construction by Elkind et al. It says that a TEF1 allocation can be computed in polynomial time for \(T=3\) under identical days and house allocation: the same item set and valuations recur each day, and every agent receives exactly one item per round. Its continuous mirror is Continuous Three-Day House-Allocation TEF1. The input is a finite set \(G\) of good types, a finite set \(\Theta\) of valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), and a repeated per-day supply \(q_g\) of each good type, where \(\sum_g q_g=1\). Every person receives one indivisible good on each of the three days. A solution assigns mass \(x_{\theta,w}\) to each type \(\theta\) and each word \(w=(g_1,g_2,g_3)\), with \(\sum_w x_{\theta,w}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,w:w_d=g}x_{\theta,w}=q_g\) for each day \(d\) and good \(g\). For every pair of positive-mass histories \(w,w'\) and each prefix \(d\), require
\[ \sum_{s=1}^d v_\theta(g_s)\geq \sum_{s=1}^d v_\theta(g'_s)-\max_{s\leq d}v_\theta(g'_s). \]
The problem is to find such an assignment, or decide that none exists.

A plausible regime is a large recurring assignment of daily slots or shifts: each person receives one slot per day, the same menu recurs for three days, and people fall into a small number \(\tau\) of stable valuation types. The expected question is whether the allocation can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of good types, and the input bit length \(L\), independent of the population size. I would initially expect tractability: the paper’s proof has a bipartite selection step that may become a capacitated matching or flow over masses. A useful next question is whether that flow preserves all pairwise TEF1 constraints between the resulting cohorts.

These are population mirrors, not continuizations of the goods or of time. Mass records the fractions of people assigned each history; every person still receives integral goods over the same discrete rounds. For Theorem 9, the recurring-release scenario is a large homogeneous population facing scarce goods at different dates. For Theorem 5, it is a large cohort with a few stable slot-preference types. The weakest point is the tractability prediction for Theorem 5: a polynomial algorithm in named agents does not automatically yield one polynomial in the number of types, and universal pairwise fairness constrains which histories can coexist in the support. That gap is itself a precise question for the continuous mirror to ask. My case covers these two results only.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can make the mirror of Theorem 9 look modest: the paper proves a constant-factor impossibility using a three-date, two-agent witness, so its population version may simply reproduce that obstruction. But the stronger version survives the obvious objection. Give a unit-mass population one type, and supply mass \(1/2\) of each of three indivisible good types on successive dates, with values \(1,1,1+1/\alpha_0\). At the second date, temporal \(\alpha_0\)-TEFX forces the first two supplies to different cohorts. At the third, any cohort receiving the last good envies a cohort that did not receive it even after the latter’s earlier good is removed: the required comparison is \(1\geq\alpha_0(1+1/\alpha_0)\), which fails. This is a coherent high-multiplicity instance with integral bundles per person. Calling the result “only an impossibility” does not defeat the mirror: optimizing the guarantee or characterizing value-dependent bounds remains a computational question.

Theorem 5 is harder to oppose. A large workforce receiving one slot per day from a recurring menu, with a small number of stable preference types, is a plausible population regime. Representing the assignment as masses over three-day histories preserves individual indivisibility and makes prefix TEF1 comparisons well-defined between every pair of histories in the support. The proponent is right that the paper’s bipartite selection step does not by itself establish a polynomial algorithm in the number of types: TEF1 constraints couple the histories that can coexist. But that leaves a concrete feasibility and complexity question. It does not show the mirror is ill-posed or unimportant.

So the best negative case is that the tractability prediction for Theorem 5 is speculative, and Theorem 9’s simplest mirror may add little beyond a scaled witness. Neither point defeats the better versions of those mirrors. I cannot honestly make the requested universal case: both anchors admit sensible population models, and Theorem 5 in particular leaves a genuine computational question.
