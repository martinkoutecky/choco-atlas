The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population-continuum motivation here, but no eligible result to anchor the requested positive case. The paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized-complexity classification.

The closest results are about economics rather than computation: Lemma 1 gives a sufficient condition for a stable coalition profile; Propositions 1 and 2 describe how concentration affects welfare and coalition payoffs; Proposition 3 bounds seller revenue across stable profiles in the limited-value case; and Proposition 4 shows that, when \(n=1\) in the optimized-value case, the grand coalition is uniquely stable. Corollary 4 says a size ceiling makes a particular profile uniquely stable and attains the revenue bound. These are substantive equilibrium and welfare results, but none analyzes the computational complexity of finding a stable profile or maximizing revenue. Section 4’s revenue comparison is numerical simulation, not a named complexity result. The appendix’s Theorems A1–A3 are cited stochastic-order tools, not results about computing the paper’s problems.

The most plausible continuum setting would be a large industry of eligible firms that share the same ex-ante valuation distribution \(F\), adoption rights, and other relevant attributes. A type would be that full ex-ante description, and mass would represent the fraction of firms of that type. The difficulty is that the paper’s central decision is a partition into coalitions, with payoffs determined by order statistics of random sums. In an atomless population, a single firm’s unilateral deviation may have no effect on the aggregate, so the paper’s stability notion does not carry over automatically. A continuum version would need to justify how coalition formation and auction payoffs scale.

So the best honest positive claim is that the paper offers a plausible high-multiplicity *economic setting* for a new continuum model. It does not provide a named computational anchor for a continuous mirror in the ChoCo sense, and I cannot state a per-anchor prover question or predict its complexity class without inventing a connection the paper does not establish.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its Lemma 1 and Propositions 1–4 are equilibrium, welfare, and revenue claims; Corollary 4 is an auction-design result; Section 4 reports a numerical revenue comparison. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of finding an outcome. The proponent identifies this gap, but therefore supplies no continuous computational question for any anchor.

The most promising candidates would be “find a stable coalition profile” or “maximize seller revenue over stable profiles.” Those could be worthwhile new problems, but they are not computational questions posed by the paper’s results. The paper assumes ex-ante identical firms, and its analysis reduces coalition profiles to coalition sizes. A continuous population would therefore have one firm type; a distribution over coalition sizes would describe the endogenous grouping, not the society’s distribution over agent types. The central input structure of a ChoCo mirror is missing.

There is also a deeper modeling obstacle. The paper’s stability test allows one firm to move between coalitions or become a singleton. In an atomless population, one firm has zero mass, so such a deviation cannot change a coalition’s aggregate value or the auction outcome. Preserving meaningful deviations would require treating a positive mass of firms as a deviating bloc, changing the stability notion and the game. One could instead use finite atoms, but that gives up the population continuum.

Propositions 1 and 2 are the best candidates for a continuous analogue: a transfer of mass between coalition sizes could yield derivative or comparative-static claims. That would be an analytic study of coalition formation, not a complexity mirror. Proposition 3 and Corollary 4 look more algorithmic, but the paper already identifies a candidate profile and a size ceiling that implements its revenue bound. Turning those into a computational problem would require new inputs and a new output specification. Proposition 4 has a related scaling issue: keeping \(n=1\) as \(N\) grows makes per-capita adoption capacity vanish, while scaling \(n\) with \(N\) changes the regime being studied.

A more ambitious variant could treat realized valuations as a continuum of types and study coalition formation over that distribution. But the paper forms coalitions *before* values are realized; moving valuation types into the population changes its timing and strategic problem. Likewise, the distribution \(F\) is uncertainty about individual valuations, not a continuous society. Neither supplies the missing computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong for the question ChoCo asks: this paper offers no worthwhile continuous *computational mirror* among its results. It does not establish that a continuum model of coalition formation would be uninteresting; that could be a separate research project. The case is that such a project would need to create its computational problem, type space, and stability concept from scratch rather than continuize a computational problem this paper actually studies.
