The continuous mirror question

Given a road network, routing costs, fleet and service constraints, and a rational distribution μ over complete customer types (including exact location, demand, schedule, channel, and contract), form the routing-cost cooperative game \(v_n\) with \(nμ_t\) customers of each type, scaling delivery capacity with the repeated operation. For a target type \(t^\star\), let \(\bar{\phi}^{(n)}_{t^\star}=(nμ_{t^\star})^{-1}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t^\star}\mathrm{Sh}_i(v_n)\), for replication factors \(n\) such that \(nμ_t\) is integral. Does \(\bar{\phi}^{(n)}_{t^\star}\) converge as \(n\) grows, and when it does, can its limit be approximated within a fixed factor in time polynomial in the network description, τ, input bit length, and fleet parameters?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity routing-cost Shapley allocation: μ gives the masses of complete customer types; replicated finite instances retain the paper’s coalition routing costs, with delivery capacity scaled for repeated operation; the continuous quantity is the limiting per-customer Shapley share of a target type.

The objection that survived

The paper gives little evidence that its actual customer base contains many customers with identical complete types, including exact location and service profile; the shared-dock regime is plausible but narrow.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3’s hardness question for routing-cost Shapley allocations. It leaves Proposition 4’s proxy pathologies, empirical sampling results, and Propositions 1 and 2’s mechanism properties unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions do the per-customer Shapley shares converge when route activation costs and capacity constraints are present?
  • When the limit exists, what are its exact and approximation complexities as functions of the network, τ, input bit length, and fleet parameters?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Proposition 3. It states that, unless \(P=NP\), there is no polynomial-time \(\alpha\)-approximation to the Shapley value of a customer for any constant \(\alpha>1\). The paper reports this result from reference [17]; it does not prove it in the paper. This is the right anchor because it is a hardness result about the cost-allocation problem in the distribution network, rather than a result about an unrelated allocation setting.

A credible regime is a large, repeated delivery network with many customer accounts but relatively few exact service profiles. For example, many accounts might receive deliveries at a modest number of shared receiving docks, with the same order profile, delivery schedule, channel, and contract terms. A type records all the features the routing and allocation model uses, including its exact location and demand. Mass is the fraction of customer-equivalents of that type; \(N\) is the population scale, so type \(t\) represents \(N\mu_t\) customers. The case depends on \(N\gg\tau\), not on pretending that customers at different locations are identical.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Routing Aumann–Shapley Approximation. An instance gives a rational type distribution \(\mu\), \(N\), a road network, a fleet, per-customer demands, and a target type \(t^\star\). For a coalition described by a fractional type vector \(q\), define \(C(q)\) as the minimum delivery cost in a fractional route-frequency LP. Its columns are feasible daily vehicle routes, and a variable \(y_r\geq0\) says how often route \(r\) is used across the repeated operation. Route frequencies may be fractional because the network operates repeatedly. A route must serve the aggregate demands \(Nq_t\) of each type.

The requested output is a multiplicative \(\alpha\)-approximation to the target type’s per-customer allocation
\[ \phi_{t^\star}=\frac{1}{N}\int_0^1 \frac{\partial C(\lambda\mu)}{\partial q_{t^\star}}\,d\lambda . \]
The value function of this route LP is piecewise linear, so the derivative along the path exists except at finitely many breakpoints. The type shares add up to the total route cost: \(\sum_t N\mu_t\phi_t=C(\mu)-C(0)\). The underlying question is whether this target allocation can be approximated in time polynomial in the network description, \(\tau\), the rational input length, and the relevant fleet parameters, for fixed \(\alpha\).

I think the authors could recognize this as their problem: it still allocates delivery costs among customers, and it retains the route geometry, demands, fleet limits, and cost objective that make their Shapley calculation difficult. The fractional route frequencies also have an operational interpretation in their repeated-delivery setting. For unrestricted networks, I would expect hardness to persist because optimizing route columns can still encode difficult routing choices among the types. That is a prediction, not a consequence of Proposition 3: the proposition alone does not establish hardness for this continuous allocation. The useful research question is whether pricing is tractable on meaningful restricted networks, which would make this a possible Class A case, while unrestricted routing may remain Class B.

The weakest point is that the mirror changes two things together: it replaces the Shapley average over discrete predecessor coalitions with an Aumann–Shapley path allocation, and it uses fractional route frequencies. Fixed stop costs and indivisible route choices can make the continuous allocation differ from the original Shapley value. So I would not claim that this is a limit identity already established by the paper. I would ask whether such an identity holds under suitable conditions, and where it fails. Even with that caveat, this is a serious mirror of Proposition 3’s transport-cost allocation question, rather than merely a continuous-outcome variant.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is a real computational anchor, so the strongest objection is not that the paper lacks a result to mirror. It is that the proposed mirror changes the cost-allocation question. Proposition 3 concerns a customer’s Shapley value in a game whose coalition costs come from routing that coalition. The proposed Aumann–Shapley value instead uses a fractional route-frequency LP. Fractional route frequencies may be natural for repeated operations, but they need not preserve the coalition costs whose Shapley value the proposition studies.

A one-stop example shows the gap. Suppose serving any nonempty coalition costs a fixed \(F\). With \(N\) interchangeable customers, each customer’s Shapley share is \(F/N\), so the group receives \(F\). The corresponding continuous coalition cost \(C(q)=F\) for \(q>0\), with \(C(0)=0\), has derivative zero almost everywhere. Integrating that derivative misses the entire fixed cost. A fractional route-frequency LP can smooth this jump and allocate the cost, but then it models paying fractions of a route across repeated operations, not the original coalition-routing game. That is a different computational question.

The best repair is to keep the finite Shapley game intact: study large instances with complete, repeated customer types, then ask whether the aggregate Shapley shares by type have a meaningful continuous limit and what it costs to compute them. That is a coherent high-multiplicity mirror, and fixed route costs make its limit more subtle, not meaningless. It also seems less representative of the paper’s concrete distribution network: customers’ exact locations, demands, schedules, and delivery channels affect routing, so complete types may be mostly singletons. A convincing \(N\gg\tau\) regime would need many operationally interchangeable customers, perhaps sharing delivery points and service profiles.

That leaves a credible objection to the proposed Aumann–Shapley formulation and a weaker case against the mirror for the paper’s actual network. It does not defeat the repaired high-multiplicity Shapley question in every plausible scenario. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case airtight: the anchor survives in that form, though the paper itself gives limited evidence that its real routing setting has the needed multiplicity.
