The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: this paper has a credible continuous-population analogue, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under the programme’s rule.

Its named results are structural, not computational. Theorem 2.1, proved in the paper, states that the Nash social welfare function satisfies Pareto optimality, the paper’s modified independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives condition with neutrality, anonymity, and continuity. Theorem 3.6, also proved there, states that when there are at least three alternatives besides the origin \(x_0\), these four conditions characterize the Nash social welfare function uniquely: it ranks alternatives by

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\bigl(u_i(x)-u_i(x_0)\bigr). \]

Neither theorem asserts membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), hardness, approximation, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic formulation. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. Thus these cannot formally serve as ChoCo anchors.

The best candidate mirror would nevertheless be:

Continuous Nash-Welfare Optimization. An instance consists of a finite alternative set \(X\), an origin \(x_0\), a finite set \(T\) of complete vNM utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and normalized utilities \(u_t(x_0)=0\). A type represents all agents with the same preferences over lotteries, up to positive affine rescaling. For a lottery \(p\in\Delta(X\cup\{x_0\})\), let

\[ g_t(p)=\sum_{a\in X\cup\{x_0\}}p_a u_t(a). \]

Assuming there is a lottery with \(g_t(p)>0\) for every positive-mass type, find a lottery maximizing

\[ \Phi_\mu(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log g_t(p). \]

Equivalently, output an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal lottery \(p\) satisfying

\[ \Phi_\mu(p)\ge \sup_q\Phi_\mu(q)-\varepsilon. \]

The population interpretation is natural: a large society of households or citizens falls into a small number of repeated welfare types—identical preferences over a finite set of public policies or social states. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\); the action remains the paper’s lottery over alternatives. The lottery is inherited from the paper, while the population is the object being continuized.

This is an exact high-multiplicity version of the paper’s index. If \(n_t\) individuals have type \(t\) and \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), then

\[ \Phi_\mu(p) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log\bigl(u_i(p)-u_i(x_0)\bigr), \]

so maximizing \(\Phi_\mu\) gives exactly the same social ordering as the finite Nash index. This is not an objection: it is the desired discrete-to-continuous dictionary.

I would classify this candidate as likely Class A. For explicitly represented \(X\) and \(T\), it is concave maximization over a simplex, hence standard convex optimization gives polynomial-time \(\varepsilon\)-approximation under ordinary encoding and positivity assumptions. The natural follow-up questions are whether the result remains efficient with implicitly represented alternatives or types, whether exact optimization has a useful bit-complexity bound, and whether intervention or robustness variants—such as the minimum population mass that must change before a specified lottery becomes optimal—produce a nontrivial complexity landscape.

The weakest point is decisive: this would be a new computational problem extracted from an axiomatic welfare rule, not a continuization of a computational result proved by Kaneko and Nakamura. Moreover, the tractability comes almost immediately from concavity, so it may look like a routine convex-optimization reformulation rather than a substantive ChoCo contribution. The mirror is mathematically plausible and faithful to Theorem 3.6, but under the strict anchor requirement I would report: no eligible computational anchor; plausible low-impact Class A candidate only.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no computational anchor at all. Theorem 2.1 proves that a welfare rule satisfies four axioms; Theorem 3.6 proves that those axioms characterize the Nash rule. Neither theorem defines an algorithmic problem, an input encoding, a complexity claim, an approximation task, or even an optimization problem. Under ChoCo’s gate, that is already fatal.

Theorem 2.1 is especially unsuitable. Its “continuity” condition is continuity of preferences over lotteries: for \(aP cP b\), some mixture \(\alpha a+(1-\alpha)b\) is indifferent to \(c\). It is not continuity of the population. The other conditions are likewise axiomatic properties of a social preference relation. Replacing a finite profile by a measure over types merely changes anonymity into invariance under measure-preserving relabellings. There is no computational question to continuize. Testing whether the Nash rule satisfies the lifted conditions is simply re-proving the theorem with sums replaced by integrals.

Theorem 3.6 gives the strongest possible rescue, but it still does not produce a worthwhile mirror. For finite types, the proposed objective is

\[ \Phi_\mu(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log g_t(p), \qquad g_t(p)=\sum_a p_a u_t(a). \]

If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), then

\[ \Phi_\mu(p) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log\bigl(u_i(p)-u_i(x_0)\bigr), \]

so this is exactly the finite Nash index after aggregation. That high-multiplicity correspondence is mathematically sound, but it is only a re-encoding of the paper’s anonymous finite profile. The population contributes weighted coefficients; it contributes no mass-transfer decision, integral assignment, winner-robustness object, or compressed combinatorial structure.

With explicitly represented \(X\) and \(T\), the resulting optimization is ordinary concave maximization over a simplex: \(g_t\) is affine in \(p\), and \(\Phi_\mu\) is concave. Its approximate solution follows from standard convex optimization. This is not disqualifying merely because the answer is easy; the problem is that the tractability comes entirely from the paper’s pre-existing lottery domain, while the population continuization adds no computational phenomenon. Exact-optimum questions mainly introduce generic logarithmic/algebraic encoding issues.

The stronger repairs do not rescue the paper. An infinite type space leads to

\[ \int \log g_\theta(p)\,d\mu(\theta), \]

but then the central difficulty is representing and integrating an arbitrary distribution of utility functions. That is a new stochastic-optimization or oracle model, not a finite-type high-multiplicity version of the paper. Conversely, making \(X\) an implicitly represented family of allocations, matchings, or policies could create a substantial optimization problem, but all of its difficulty would come from the newly introduced outcome structure. The paper treats \(X\) abstractly and proves nothing computational about such families.

One could also invent Nash-welfare population manipulation—say, the minimum mass that must change before one lottery overtakes another. For fixed lotteries this reduces to linear inequalities in the new masses, and with transport costs it becomes a standard linear program. More elaborate versions may be interesting, but they add bribery, robustness, or intervention machinery absent from both named theorems. They are new ChoCo problems inspired by the Nash formula, not mirrors of this paper’s contribution.

Thus the proposed continuous model is sensible, and the proponent is right not to object to high multiplicity. But it is a routine weighted restatement of an axiomatic welfare rule, with no qualifying computational result and no population-specific computational object. I would reject it as a worthwhile ChoCo mirror. The only honest qualification is that a sufficiently rich implicit-outcome or intervention model could yield a good research problem—but then the research question would no longer be a continuous mirror of Kaneko and Nakamura’s results.
