The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), \(\tau\) strict ranking types \(t_i\), rational masses \(\mu_i\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac12)\), compute a lottery \(q\) over \(C\) with support size at most \(\lceil 100/\epsilon^2\rceil\) such that, for every \(a\in C\), \(\sum_{b\in C}q_b\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,\mathbf{1}[a\succ_{t_i}b]\le \tfrac12+\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

Represent the society by ranking types and their masses, and form the pairwise-payoff matrix \(P_{ab}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,\mathbf{1}[a\succ_{t_i}b]\). Compute a maximal lottery for this zero-sum game, sample \(O(1/\epsilon^2)\) candidates, and return their empirical lottery; the candidate lottery remains the decision variable while the population is the continuous object.

The objection that survived

The weighted society collapses to an \(m\times m\) pairwise-payoff matrix, so the mirror appears to add no algorithmic work beyond the paper’s construction; this bounds the new work required, but does not make the continuous-population question unrecognizable.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s constant-support approximate-domination guarantee and its maximal-lottery sampling construction over ranking types. It does not translate the appendix’s lower-bound construction or settle the sharp \(k_\alpha\) tradeoff.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can minimum support be optimized efficiently while preserving the pairwise-preference guarantee?
  • What is the sharp dependence of the required support size on \(\epsilon\) for continuous societies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper’s approximate maximal lottery construction extends naturally to a society represented by masses over ranking types. There is a qualification under your anchor rule, though. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition that classifies a computational problem as being in P, NP-hard, or similar. Its algorithmic claim appears in unnumbered Remark 2. So the main theorem is the closest useful anchor, but it is not formally a named complexity result.

The main result, Theorem 1, is proved here. It says that for every \(\epsilon>0\), every election has a \((\tfrac12-\epsilon)\)-dominating set \(S\) of at most \((1+o(1))\frac{\pi}{8\epsilon^2}\) candidates; moreover, there is a distribution \(D\) over \(S\) such that, for every candidate \(a\), the expected fraction of voters who prefer \(a\) to a candidate drawn from \(D\) is at most \(\tfrac12+\epsilon\). Remark 2 explains how to find such a set in expected polynomial time by computing a maximal lottery, sampling from it, and repeating until the sample meets the guarantee.

A precise continuous version is the Sparse Approximate-Maximal-Lottery Construction. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a list of \(\tau\) strict rankings \(t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_i\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac12)\). The required output is a rational lottery \(q\) over \(C\) with at most \(\lceil100/\epsilon^2\rceil\) supported candidates and, for every \(a\in C\),
\[ \sum_{b\in C}q_b\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,\mathbf{1}[a\succ_{t_i}b]\le \tfrac12+\epsilon. \]
The support is the committee; the lottery constraint says that each excluded candidate is beaten by the committee in expectation by at least a \((\tfrac12-\epsilon)\) fraction of the population. The paper’s proof gives the sharper asymptotic support bound above; the fixed constant here gives a concrete search task, and the displayed DKW estimate in the proof supports expected-polynomial construction for it.

A plausible regime is a very large pool of people ranking a stable set of AI models or other finalists. A type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of raters with that ranking. There may be millions of raters but relatively few distinct rankings. The input is then compact in \(\tau\) and the bit length of the masses, even when expanding it into individual voters would be costly. This keeps the paper’s pairwise-preference question intact while making the population itself the continuous object. The expected running time of the adapted construction is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\) and \(1/\epsilon\), where \(L\) is the mass encoding length, so I expect this mirror to be tractable.

The weakest point is that the paper’s main theorem is a combinatorial existence bound, and the computational construction is presented in a remark rather than a numbered complexity result. The mirror is therefore a credible high-multiplicity algorithmic extension, but it does not supply the kind of named computational anchor the requested standard calls for. The next questions would be whether minimum support can be optimized efficiently, whether a deterministic construction is available with comparable bounds, and how the best support size depends on \(\epsilon\) for continuous societies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is Theorem 1, which proves an existence bound for approximately dominating committees. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s algorithmic statement is in Remark 2: it gives an expected-polynomial construction, without a named complexity result. That leaves little computational landscape for ChoCo to chart.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is still mathematically sound. Given ranking types with masses \(\mu_i\), form the pairwise-payoff matrix
\[ P_{ab}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,\mathbf{1}[a\succ_{t_i}b]. \]
Then compute a maximal lottery and sample \(O(1/\epsilon^2)\) candidates, as in the paper. The same proof and construction use weighted sums in place of voter counts, with verification over the \(\tau\) types. For rational masses, clearing denominators also turns the input into an ordinary finite election. This is a useful high-multiplicity encoding, yet it appears to be a direct compact-input corollary of the paper’s construction: all relevant information is already in an \(m\times m\) payoff matrix, and the algorithm avoids enumerating the population.

Changing the scenario does not add much while preserving the result. A large pool of AI raters with repeated rankings is a sensible population, but its rankings still collapse to that same matrix. Adding identity-specific information would change the problem; studying minimum support or optimal \(\epsilon\)-dependence would be new work rather than a mirror of a result here.

I can’t make the universal negative airtight: the compact high-multiplicity input is legitimate, and a formally stated running-time result could be useful. My strongest case against is narrower: this paper offers a constructive existence theorem, while its natural continuous version seems already answered by the same algorithm and proof, with no substantial new computational question left to investigate.
