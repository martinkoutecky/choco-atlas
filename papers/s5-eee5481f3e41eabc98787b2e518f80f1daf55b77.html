The continuous mirror question

For an even \(\tau\ge4\), the input is \(\tau\) hungry preference types with closed preference sets, each of mass \(\mu_t=1/\tau\), accessed by the paper’s Next-indifference-point, Previous-indifference-point, and Evaluate queries. Does a finite protocol always find a cut \(y\in[0,1]\) and masses \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) assigned to the left interval, with \(\sum_t z_t=1/2\), such that every type assigned positive mass left prefers the left interval at \(y\), and every type assigned positive mass right prefers the right interval? A type may be split only if it prefers both intervals at \(y\).

The model it lives in

A society with \(\tau\) equally weighted preference types, where each type is a complete interval-preference correspondence and the total mass assigned to each group is \(1/2\). The decision variables are the cut \(y\) and left-assigned type masses \(z_t\); the objective is to find a feasible envy-free assignment using the paper’s type-oracle queries.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s two-group setting with both groups of positive size; it leaves Theorem 2’s singleton-group protocol and the chore-division and mixed-cake results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the finite-protocol obstruction persist when each type is given by a finite encoding, such as a rational piecewise-linear utility function?
  • Does a weighted protocol exist for monotone preferences when the input is given as type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest lead is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that for \(n\) hungry players with closed preference sets, two groups of sizes \(k_1,k_2\) with \(k_1+k_2=n\) and \(\min\{k_1,k_2\}\ge 2\) admit no finite protocol that always computes an envy-free assignment using the paper’s Next-indifference-point, Previous-indifference-point, and Evaluate queries.

A direct population mirror is Balanced Type-Mass Two-Group Cake Assignment. Fix an even number \(\tau\ge4\) of preference types, each with mass \(\mu_t=1/\tau\). A type is the complete preference correspondence \(h_t(y)\subseteq\{L,R\}\) over the two intervals formed by a cut \(y\in[0,1]\); each type is hungry and has closed preference sets. The input gives the type oracles and their rational masses. The target groups each receive half the population. A solution is a cut \(y\) and masses \(z_t\) assigned to the left interval, with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t z_t=1/2\), such that positive mass assigned left prefers \(L\), and positive mass assigned right prefers \(R\). A type can be split only at a cut where it prefers both intervals. The task is to find such a solution using the same three kinds of queries, asked of types.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large employer might allocate two contiguous holiday periods to two equally sized groups of employees, where employees with the same work calendar and constraints share the same complete interval preferences. With \(N=q\tau\) employees, \(q\) can be very large while the number of types stays fixed; the population is represented by its type masses. The mirror keeps the paper’s assignment question and envy-free criterion. It does not replace them with outcome-space continuity or a new welfare objective.

I expect the finite-query obstruction to survive. Run the paper’s adversary on the \(\tau\) representative type oracles. At the eventual cut, its construction can leave at least \(\tau-1\) types preferring only the same interval. That is at least \((\tau-1)/\tau\) of the population, leaving at most \(1/\tau<1/2\) able to fill the other group. Fractionally splitting the remaining type cannot fill that group either. This is an adaptation of the proof, not a result stated in the paper. The search problem remains total: the group-existence result cited in the paper gives an envy-free assignment for \(\tau\) representative players divided into groups of \(\tau/2\), which yields a valid mass assignment here. I expect a no-finite-protocol answer, a transfer of the oracle obstruction in a Class-B-like sense—not an NP-hardness claim.

The mirror covers only Theorem 3’s two-group setting with both groups having positive population shares. It does not cover Theorem 2’s singleton-group case, whose group has vanishing share as the population grows, or the chore and mixed-cake results. Useful next questions are whether an explicit encoding of piecewise-linear utilities permits finite or polynomial-time computation, and whether the monotone-preference case admits a weighted version of the paper’s mark-and-cut protocol.

The weak point is the oracle model. Theorem 3 concerns arbitrary preference functions accessed through queries, and my mirror preserves that feature; its impossibility may reflect unbounded preference complexity rather than the computational difficulty of a finite binary input. Also, the theorem is an oracle-protocol result, not a conventional bit-complexity classification. That limits the claim, but it is also why this is the most faithful mirror of the paper’s named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and the mass adaptation is plausible. With \(\tau=4\) equally weighted types, the paper’s adversary can leave three types strictly preferring the same interval at the output cut. Their \(3/4\) mass cannot be split to fill the other half, so the argument appears to survive fractional assignment. I see no sound objection based on indivisible players, identity, or the finite-protocol result being “already done”: the paper treats that result as computational, and the continuous question is still about assigning population mass.

The best case against is about what the result measures. Theorem 3 is an oracle lower bound for arbitrary preference functions. Those functions have no stated finite encoding or bit length, so the impossibility measures how much information a protocol must learn about an unrestricted preference—not a complexity barrier created by population mass. With type oracles, the proposed mirror largely repeats the finite-player result on \(\tau\) representatives; the rational weights add little to the obstruction.

A finite-input version could specify, for example, rational piecewise-linear utilities for each type and rational type masses, then ask for a cut and envy-free mass assignment. That is a more convincing high-multiplicity computational problem, and the paper’s discussion of additive utilities makes it recognizable. But it changes the input model that powers Theorem 3: its oracle adversary does not settle the complexity of that encoded problem. I cannot rule out that this is a worthwhile mirror; saying it might be easy would be an objection based on the answer, which is outside the brief.

So the negative case is weak. The exact oracle mirror has limited novelty as a population-specific complexity result, but that does not defeat the anchor, and a finite-encoding version remains plausible. I would not support the claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
