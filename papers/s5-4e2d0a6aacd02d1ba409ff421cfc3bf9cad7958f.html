What the mirror covers

The candidate covers \(2\)-PL identifiability and GMM recovery from ranking moments. It leaves the paper’s non-identifiability and generic-identifiability results for other \(k,m\), and its experiments, outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an eligible anchor for the required positive case. Theorem 1 proves non-identifiability in a stated range; Theorems 2 and 3 prove strict and generic identifiability under stated conditions; Theorem 4 proves consistency of the GMM algorithm when parameters are bounded below by \(\epsilon>0\). None classifies a problem by computational complexity. The paper’s \(O(n)\) runtime claim for GMM appears in the prose, not as a numbered complexity result.

The closest possible mirror would be exact-population 2-PL recovery: given a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over rankings of four alternatives, recover \((\alpha,\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)})\) whose 2-PL law matches \(\mu\), or minimize the population moment error. The types would be rankings and their masses the fractions of ranking submissions in a large, repeated crowdsourcing or survey population, with \(n\gg\tau\) and at most 24 ranking types. Theorem 2 says that for \(k=2\) and \(m\ge4\), the model is identifiable; Theorem 4 says the paper’s GMM estimator is consistent under its parameter-bound assumption. These are meaningful support for recovery, but neither supplies the required complexity anchor.

That candidate also has a serious collision risk: the paper already defines its generative model as a probability distribution over rankings and studies identifiability at that population-law level. Replacing finite empirical frequencies with exact masses looks like the population limit of its own estimation problem, rather than a new computational mirror. So the paper has named statistical and algorithmic results, but no named computational-complexity result of the kind the requested case must anchor on.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s central point holds: there is no named worst-case complexity result here for ChoCo to mirror. Theorems 1–3 establish identifiability properties of the map from mixture parameters to ranking distributions. Theorem 4 establishes statistical consistency. The paper’s \(O(n)\) runtime statement concerns its fixed-moment procedure and appears in the prose; it gives no complexity bound for exact optimization as \(m\), \(k\), or input bit length grows. A complexity study of that optimization could be worthwhile, but it would add a result the paper does not claim.

The strongest candidate is exact recovery from a population mass vector over rankings—for example, rational masses on the 24 rankings of four alternatives, with the goal of recovering a \(2\)-PL parameter or minimizing population moment error. A voting-bloc or repeated-survey population makes this a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Individual identity is not needed, and the masses do not degenerate. Those are not good objections.

The problem is that the paper already works at the population-law level. Its identifiability theorems ask whether two parameter settings induce the same distribution over rankings; Theorem 4’s empirical moments approach the corresponding population moments as the sample grows. Replacing sample frequencies with exact masses supplies the model’s existing law directly. That does not create a new continuous mirror of a computational result. Theorem 3’s generic claim likewise concerns almost all parameters, not the complexity of deciding or computing anything. Adding an exact inversion algorithm with a complexity analysis would be new algorithmic work on a model already formulated over distributions.

A voting application cannot supply the missing anchor on its own. If the new task is to alter masses to change a winner, or to campaign against latent mixture components, it introduces an intervention or social-choice objective absent from this paper. It may be a useful follow-on, but it is not a mirror of the paper’s identifiability or consistency results.

The negative case is strongest on that boundary, and it is not a proof that no related research could be worthwhile. A complexity analysis of parameter recovery for growing \(m\) and \(k\) could be interesting. My claim is narrower: it would be an adjacent project, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s named results.
