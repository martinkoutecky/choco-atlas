The continuous mirror question

For a fixed number \(m\) of alternatives and a Condorcet-consistent rule \(f\), let the hidden atomless society be a measurable ranking map ρ from \([0,1]\) to the complete rankings of the alternatives, with finite support. A deterministic online policy may query a measurable cohort \(S\) at rank \(k\) after ranks \(1,\ldots,k-1\) have been revealed on \(S\); it receives the exact mass partition by rank-\(k\) response, and the query costs λ(S). It must stop only when every ranking map consistent with the transcript has the same winner set. Does every such policy have competitive ratio Ω(m), measured against the minimum mass-query cost of a hindsight certificate for the hidden society?

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution over complete rankings, hidden from the policy behind an exact measurable cohort-query oracle. The decision is which cohort mass to query at each next rank; the objective is to certify the complete winner set online, measured against the minimum certificate cost available with hindsight.

The objection that survived

The exact measurable cohort-query oracle is a new access primitive, and the paper's theorem does not establish that its Ω(m) lower bound holds under that primitive.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Condorcet lower bound of Theorem 7, the Borda lower bound of Theorem 3, and the MultiScaleScore guarantee of Theorem 6. It leaves the other voting-rule bounds, the level-pruning results, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the Ω(m) competitive lower bound hold for every deterministic online policy under exact measurable cohort queries?
  • Which representations of cohorts and response partitions make this query oracle a usable model of large-scale preference elicitation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a mass version of next-best preference elicitation. It continues the population, not the alternatives or the winner set: each unit of population still has one complete, integral ranking, while aggregate scores and query costs become fractional.

Let \(\Omega=[0,1]\) be an atomless population with measure \(\lambda\), and let a hidden measurable map \(\rho:\Omega\to\mathcal R(A)\) assign each person a strict ranking of the \(m\) alternatives. A ranking \(\pi\) is a type, with mass \(\mu_\pi=\lambda(\{x:\rho(x)=\pi\})\); the actual profile has support size \(\tau\). A query chooses a measurable set \(S\subseteq\Omega\) and a rank \(k\), provided ranks \(1,\ldots,k-1\) have already been revealed on \(S\). It reveals rank \(k\) for that mass and costs \(\lambda(S)\). The response partitions the queried mass by the alternatives revealed. An online policy sees only the query transcript; it must stop when every profile consistent with that transcript has the same complete winner set. For a fixed hidden profile, \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty\) is the least total mass-query cost of a certificate chosen with hindsight. The quantity to characterize is
\[ \mathrm{CR}_\infty(\mathcal A) =\sup_{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{\mathcal A}(\rho)} {\mathrm{OPT}_\infty(\rho)}. \]
The mass distribution is the societal instance, but it remains hidden behind the elicitation oracle. Giving \(\mu\) to the policy would make winner determination immediate and would erase the paper’s question.

This model fits large deliberation platforms where many respondents share one of a small number of complete ranking patterns. For instance, a slate of thousands of proposals might draw responses from a few recurring preference blocs. Then \(n\gg\tau\), and querying a share of a bloc is a natural fluid limit of querying its members. A five-type construction can even have many members in its rarest type by taking \(n\) sufficiently large.

Lead: Theorem 7, proved in the paper. It says that for *any* Condorcet-consistent voting rule, *any* elicitation algorithm in the next-best query model has competitive ratio \(\Omega(m)\). Its mass mirror is Condorcet-Mass-CR: for a hidden finite-support ranking distribution and a Condorcet-consistent rule, find the best online policy for certifying the full winner set, measured against the hindsight minimum mass-query certificate. A solution is an online policy with a matching competitive guarantee, plus a lower bound against all such policies. The theorem is a lower-bound result, not an NP-hardness claim. [The paper’s full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.29087)

The paper’s five-type adversary gives a concrete reason to expect the linear lower bound to survive. Fix the orders within \(X\) and \(Y\), and use its five ranking types \(T_1,\ldots,T_5\), with masses
\[ \mu_{T_1}=\tfrac12-\tfrac q2,\qquad \mu_{T_2}=\mu_{T_3}=\mu_{T_5}=q,\qquad \mu_{T_4}=\tfrac12-\tfrac{5q}{2}, \]
where \(q=1/(8m^2)\). All five masses are positive. In the actual profile, \(w\) beats each of \(X,Y,c\) by majority; if the unresolved \(T_4,T_5\) pool is completed as \(T_4\), then \(c\) becomes a Condorcet winner. The hidden distinction appears at the bottom of the rankings. The online policy should therefore have to inspect a constant mass through rank \(m-1\), costing \(\Omega(m)\). A hindsight certificate can query the \(T_1\) mass at the top and the rare \(T_2,T_3,T_5\) masses more deeply, for \(O(1+qm)=O(1)\) cost. This is the programme’s Class B intuition: the pressure comes from the number of alternatives and the need to rule out a different Condorcet winner, not from having many distinct voter types. A formal fluid adversary proof remains part of the mirror’s work.

Borda-Mass-CR is a second, independent anchor. Theorem 3, also proved in the paper, says that *every* online Borda elicitation algorithm has competitive ratio at least \(3\). Its continuous question is to characterize the best competitive ratio for certifying the complete Borda winner set under the same mass-query oracle. I expect the constant lower bound to survive: replace the paper’s single exceptional ballot with an arbitrarily small but positive exceptional cohort, while the rest of the population has the two base rankings. The online policy must still find that cohort to distinguish a tie from a unique winner; a hindsight certificate can target it. The cohort’s mass can be tiny but still contain many people when \(n\) is large. The original one-voter construction does vanish in the literal limit, so this replacement needs to be proved rather than assumed.

Theorem 6, proved in the paper, anchors the algorithmic side of that Borda problem. It states that deterministic online algorithm \(\textsc{MultiScaleScore}\) determines the complete Borda winner set with competitive ratio at most \(4\) for \(2\le m\le4\), and at most \(20\sqrt m\) for \(m\ge5\). The mass question is whether a deterministic policy can achieve the same guarantee when it queries unresolved population cohorts and maintains score bounds by integrating over mass. I expect a Class A result: the algorithm’s score bounds, thresholds, and charging arguments are additive over voters and appear to pass to integrals. This would give the continuous Borda problem the interval between a lower bound of \(3\) and an \(O(\sqrt m)\) policy, while leaving open—as the paper does—whether a constant ratio is possible.

The mirror covers active next-best elicitation for Condorcet-consistent rules and Borda, including winner certification and the online-versus-hindsight objective. It does not claim to mirror every result or the experiments. Its weakest point is the mass-query interface: the paper queries named voters, while this mirror lets a policy query measurable cohorts and observe how their responses split. If cohort selection is not an acceptable elicitation primitive, the mirror fails the authors-recognition test. Even with that interface, fractional splitting might weaken a discrete lower bound. But the query model is a natural population-scale version of their task, and the five-type Condorcet construction makes the strongest case that the agenda-size lower bound survives with fixed support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is a mismatch between the paper’s problem and ChoCo’s distribution-input model. The paper studies what to ask when rankings are hidden. Its input to the elicitation algorithm is not a known society distribution: the algorithm learns by querying named voters, and its competitive ratio compares those queries with a certificate chosen in hindsight. If the continuous society is supplied as a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, its winners can be computed from \(\mu\); the elicitation problem has disappeared.

Keeping the rankings hidden preserves the paper’s question, but requires a different model: an atomless population with an unknown ranking map, plus an oracle that lets the policy select measurable subsets and obtain responses from fractional mass. That oracle is doing substantive work. It gives the policy and the hindsight comparator different access to the hidden population, and it raises questions the paper’s theorems do not settle: how subsets are represented, what a query returns exactly, and how the policy can use fractional responses. This is not simply replacing voter counts by masses in the paper’s model.

That weakens each anchor in a specific way. The Condorcet construction has a plausible mass analogue with positive masses for all five types, but its lower bound depends on what an online policy can learn by querying a subset of the unresolved mass. The original theorem does not establish that bound for the proposed oracle. In the Borda construction, the exceptional voter’s mass vanishes as the electorate grows. A positive exceptional cohort is a promising repair, but whether the lower bound survives depends on the same query semantics. The multiscale Borda algorithm is the least vulnerable: its score bounds and charging argument look likely to extend to mass queries. But that would establish a competitive query-cost guarantee for the new oracle, not a complexity result on a society supplied as a distribution.

This is a scope objection, not a claim that these are not computational results: the paper proves real algorithmic guarantees. If ChoCo includes hidden-population elicitation with exact fractional cohort queries, then the proponent’s Condorcet and Borda questions are worthwhile mirrors, and I cannot honestly defeat them. The negative case is strong only if ChoCo requires the society distribution itself to be the computational input.
