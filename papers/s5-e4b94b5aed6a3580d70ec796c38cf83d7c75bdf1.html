What the mirror covers

No named theorem provides a computational result to mirror; Theorems 4.5, 4.6, and 2.5 are structural results about manipulability or dictatorship. The proposed mass-coalition variant is an unproved extension.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror to. Its closest candidate is Theorem 4.5, proved in the paper: if a social choice function satisfies Strong Standard Domain and Transitive Explanations, then for any lifting with both Simple Dominance properties it is manipulable or has a weak dictator. Theorem 4.6 is the Barberà–Kelly result the authors use to derive it, and Theorem 2.5 is the cited Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. These are results about when manipulation exists, not complexity classifications or algorithmic results.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity question nearby. Let types be complete rankings and \(\mu_t\) their population shares; extend a choice rule to take \((\mu,V)\) as input. A mass-lifting manipulation problem could ask for the smallest transfer \(x_{t,t'}\) of population mass from true ranking types to reported types that makes the new outcome strictly better for the participating voters under a specified lifting. A society with many residents and relatively few ranking types is a credible regime for that model.

But this changes the paper’s central manipulation notion. An individual in an atomless population has zero mass, so a consequential deviation must involve a positive-mass group. Theorem 4.5 does not establish anything about such coalitional deviations, and the paper does not specify a continuous extension of its social choice functions. So this is a reasonable research question inspired by the paper, but it is not an anchored mirror of one of its computational results. I cannot honestly assign it a tractability or hardness expectation from the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper’s main result, Theorem 4.5, is an existence theorem about manipulation or weak dictatorship, not a computational result. Its social choice function \(f\) is arbitrary: the paper gives no finite encoding of \(f\), no input problem, and no algorithmic task whose complexity could be charted. Theorem 4.6 and the cited Gibbard–Satterthwaite result do not supply that missing computational object.

A direct continuous version of individual manipulation also degenerates. In an atomless society, one person has zero mass, so changing that person’s report leaves the distribution and outcome unchanged. The natural repair is to let a positive mass of voters with the same true ranking coordinate on a report. But that asks about coalition manipulation, whereas Theorem 4.5 concerns a unilateral deviation. Its conclusion does not settle the coalition question.

The best replacement is to extend the paper’s Borda example to a family of rules and ask for the least mass that must move from a true ranking type to a reported ranking type to obtain a strictly preferred outcome under a chosen lifting. That is a coherent high-multiplicity computational problem. But it is a new, rule-specific coalition problem: the paper only illustrates Borda with three candidates and three individuals, and gives no computational result to mirror.

So the negative case is strong against an *anchored computational mirror*: this paper supplies no computational anchor. It is weaker against the universal claim. The mass-coalition Borda question is plausible and may be worth studying, even though it changes the paper’s unilateral notion. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror.
