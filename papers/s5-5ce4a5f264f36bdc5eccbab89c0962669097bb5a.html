The continuous mirror question

Given τ roles with binary-rational masses μ_i summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational contact matrix \(A\), and fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1/8\), define \(q_i(x)=\frac{\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_jx_j}{\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_j}\) for roles with contacts. Decide whether there is an initial state \(x^0\in[0,1]^\tau\) with \(\sum_i\mu_ix_i^0\le 1/4-\varepsilon\) and a finite sequence of legal mass updates reaching a stable state \(x^*\) with \(\sum_i\mu_ix_i^*\ge 1/2\). A legal update increases \(x_i\) by a positive amount when \(q_i(x)>1/2\), or decreases it when \(q_i(x)<1/2\); roles with no contacts never update. Stability means no positive mass in any role is unhappy.

The model it lives in

A society consists of τ network roles with masses μ_i; agents in role \(i\) face the same role-to-role contact intensities \(a_{ij}\). The coordinate \(x_i\in[0,1]\) is the fraction of role \(i\)'s mass holding preference \(1\), and mass can move between binary opinions only when its role's neighbor share \(q_i(x)\) is strictly on the other side of \(1/2\). The decision asks whether some initial state with at most \(1/4-\varepsilon\) mass at preference \(1\) can reach a stable state with at least half the population at preference \(1\).

The objection that survived

The reduction’s variable and clause gadgets rely on formula-specific links, which a role-level contact matrix may average away; thus Theorem 17 does not establish hardness for the proposed block model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 17’s small-minority campaign problem. It leaves the forbidden-graph characterization, the polynomial-time construction of a campaign, and Theorem 20’s fixed-initial-profile algorithm outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can within-role fractional splitting be rounded or otherwise shown not to turn NO instances of the discrete problem into YES instances?
  • What is the complexity of reachability when the number of roles τ is part of the input, or when τ is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

I see a credible mirror for the paper’s campaign-design problem. Let a type be a network role: agents in role \(i\) have the same contact intensity \(a_{ij}\) with every role \(j\). The society has type masses \(\mu_i>0\), summing to \(1\). The matrix \(A=(a_{ij})\) describes the network’s mixing pattern. If \(x_i\in[0,1]\) is the fraction of role \(i\) currently holding preference \(1\), then that role’s fraction of neighbors holding \(1\) is \(q_i(x)=\frac{\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_jx_j}{\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_j}\); a role with no contacts never updates. Each person’s opinion remains binary: \(x_i\) records the mass split among people of that role.

This fits large networks with many people in recurring structural roles and a much smaller set of distinct contact profiles. A block model of a large platform or organization is one plausible setting. Agents can update individually, so a type’s mass may split: mass can move from \(0\) to \(1\) only while \(q_i>1/2\), and from \(1\) to \(0\) only while \(q_i<1/2\). At a tie, neither side is unhappy. A state is stable when no positive mass is unhappy. This preserves the paper’s binary opinions, strict-majority update rule, and adversarial choice of update order while representing the population by proportions.

My anchor is Theorem 17, proved in this paper. For every fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1/8\), given an \(n\)-node graph, it is NP-hard to decide whether there is an initial profile with at most \(n(1/4-\varepsilon)\) agents at preference \(1\) from which updates can reach a stable profile with at least \(n/2\) agents at preference \(1\). The proof reduces from 2P2N-3SAT; the paper cites [17] for that source problem’s NP-hardness.

The corresponding question is Block-Continuum Minority-to-Majority Reachability. Given \(\tau\) roles, binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), a symmetric rational contact matrix \(A\), and fixed \(\varepsilon\), decide whether there is an initial mass vector \(x^0\in[0,1]^\tau\) with \(\sum_i\mu_ix_i^0\le 1/4-\varepsilon\), and a legal update trajectory from \(x^0\) to a stable \(x^*\) with \(\sum_i\mu_ix_i^*\ge 1/2\). A YES solution is such an initial vector and trajectory; a NO instance has none. Complexity should be stated in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The question is whether a small initial share can orchestrate the same kind of majority reversal under majority dynamics.

I provisionally expect hardness to transfer when \(\tau\) is part of the input: Theorem 17’s reduction encodes combinatorics in the influence graph, rather than merely in the number of people. But the theorem does not establish that for this mirror. Continuous mass can split within a role, creating trajectories unavailable in the finite graph, and the reduction’s NO instances might become YES instances. Proving or refuting a rounding result is therefore a central follow-up; an algorithm for fixed \(\tau\) is another. My weakest point is that the paper studies arbitrary individual networks, while this mirror assumes repeated contact roles. The mirror seems plausible to the authors because it asks their same adversarial campaign question under the same update rule, but Theorem 17 alone does not tell us whether that question stays hard after mass becomes divisible. This case covers Theorem 17 only, not the paper’s graph characterization or its polynomial-time result for a fixed initial profile.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 17 is the paper’s strongest anchor, and its reduction gives a real reason to doubt a direct high-multiplicity translation. Its variable and clause gadgets encode a particular incidence graph: which literal belongs to which clause determines which update sequences can succeed. A type that preserves each agent’s complete contact pattern must retain that formula-specific wiring, so the number of types grows with the reduction. Grouping agents by broad “role” instead averages away those links. The reduction then says nothing about the proposed block model’s complexity.

The more faithful continuum candidates have their own cost. A dense graphon limit would discard the reduction’s bounded-degree gadget edges, which carry the logical constraints. A model that retains finite neighborhoods and their correlations would need richer network structure than a distribution over finitely many contact roles. That weakens the bridge from Theorem 17 to the continuous problem; it does not show that the continuous problem lacks value.

I can’t honestly defeat the block-model mirror itself. A large platform or organization with recurring contact roles is a plausible setting, and deciding whether a small initial share can drive a majority reversal is a computational question about a continuous population. Fractional mass updates change the dynamics, but that is a natural population-level version of the campaign problem. The paper’s reduction does not establish hardness for it, yet that is a question to investigate, not a reason to dismiss the mirror.

So the strongest negative case is narrow: Theorem 17 does not carry over cleanly, and the block model may be a different network regime rather than a continuization of the theorem’s hard instances. I do not see an honest basis for the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
