The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 10

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 10 — survived: The opponent engaged this anchor, and the proposed type-mass allocation retains the paper’s utility objective and entitlement constraints.

The continuous mirror question

Given applicant type masses α_a and resource type masses β_b, each summing to one, shared rankings, nonnegative utilities \(u_{ab}\), a fairness level φ, and per-type entitlement values \(E_{a,j}\), find a mass plan \(X=(x_{ab})\) maximizing \(\sum_{a,b}u_{ab}x_{ab}\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{ab}=α_a\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}=β_b\), \(x_{ab}\ge 0\), and \(\sum_{b\in \operatorname{Top}_j(a)}x_{ab}\ge φα_a E_{a,j}\) for every type \(a\) and preference prefix \(j\).

The model it lives in

An atomless market of exchangeable applicant and resource types with unit total mass on each side. The decision variable \(x_{ab}\) is assignment mass; each person still receives one whole resource, and the mass plan represents type-level assignment marginals.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the utility-maximizing φ-fair allocation LP underlying Sections 4.1–4.2; it does not establish Theorem 10’s sampling robustness for type masses, compute continuum entitlement values, or cover the paper’s axiomatization and experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the per-type entitlement values \(E_{a,j}\) be computed or approximated from type-level merit laws and an appropriate continuum stable-matching benchmark?
  • Can sampling and perturbation guarantees depend on the number of types rather than the finite population size \(n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is for the paper’s utility-maximizing fair matching problem, in a large market with a small number of exact applicant and resource types. The paper has no proved, numbered hardness result to anchor on. Its suggestion that computing the fairness parameters \(\ell_{x,k}\) may be \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard is explicitly speculation, so I would not present it as a hardness result.

My anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper. It assumes estimates \(\widetilde{\ell}_{x,k}\) are within \(\epsilon/2\) of the true \(\ell_{x,k}\), and sets
\[ \widehat{\ell}_{x,k}=\frac{\widetilde{\ell}_{x,k}+k\epsilon}{n\epsilon+1}. \]
The modified LP is feasible; its optimal solution \(\widehat P\) is \(\frac{\phi(1+\epsilon/2)}{n\epsilon+1}\)-fair with respect to the true parameters and achieves utility at least \(\frac{1}{\phi n\epsilon+1}U(P^*)\), where \(P^*\) is the optimum under the true fairness constraints. The theorem is proved by the authors, not cited from elsewhere. [Theorem 10 in the published paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/devic23a/devic23a.pdf)

Here is the mirror I think they could recognize. Consider a repeated school-placement or hiring market with \(N\) applicants and \(N\) positions, where \(N\) is very large but applicants fall into \(K\) exact types and positions into \(J\) exact types, with \(K,J\ll N\). An applicant type includes the same ranking over position types, the same merit-distribution law, and the same principal utility for each match; a position type includes its capacity and merit treatment. Thus people of a type are interchangeable for everything the problem uses, while each person still receives one whole position.

Normalize total applicant and position mass to \(1\). Let \(\alpha_a\) be the mass of applicant type \(a\), and \(\beta_b\) the supply mass of position type \(b\). Let \(E_{a,j}\) be the per-person probability that an applicant of type \(a\) gets one of their top \(j\) position types under the individual-proposing stable-matching benchmark induced by the merit model. For this optimization problem, the \(E_{a,j}\) are supplied as part of the instance; computing them from the merit law is a separate question.

Call the problem Entitlement-Constrained Mass Matching. Given \(\alpha,\beta\), the preference orders, nonnegative match utilities \(u_{ab}\), entitlement values \(E_{a,j}\), and fairness level \(\phi\), find a mass plan \(X=(x_{ab})\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{a,b}u_{ab}x_{ab} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_b x_{ab}=\alpha_a,\qquad \sum_a x_{ab}=\beta_b,\qquad x_{ab}\ge 0, \]
and, for every applicant type \(a\) and preference prefix \(j\),
\[ \sum_{b\in \operatorname{Top}_j(a)}x_{ab}\ge \phi\,\alpha_a E_{a,j}. \]
The output is an optimal mass plan, equivalently a symmetric lottery over whole-market matchings with those type-level marginals. The entries of \(X\) are fractions of a type’s people assigned to positions, not fractional bundles given to individuals.

I expect this exact-entitlement problem to be tractable (Class A): it is a linear program with \(KJ\) assignment variables and a polynomial number of constraints, so its running time is polynomial in \(K,J\) and the input bit length. The mass constraints are the natural scaling of one-to-one matching, while the entitlement constraints preserve the paper’s comparison to a fair stable-matching benchmark. This is my inference from the paper’s LP approach, not a result the authors prove for a continuum market.

The match is plausible because Theorem 10 optimizes utility subject to the same kind of rank-based fairness constraints, and treats uncertainty in those constraints as an algorithmic issue. The paper’s main problem remains intact: assign indivisible positions to people, maximize the principal’s utility, and protect each person’s entitlement relative to the stable benchmark. What changes is the instance regime: instead of naming every person, the input records the masses of the types they belong to.

The scope is Sections 4.1–4.2’s fair utility-maximizing allocation problem. This does not continuize the outcome: every person still gets one position. It also does not cover the paper’s axiomatization or imply a result about every two-sided matching market.

My weakest point is the gap between Theorem 10 and the continuum limit. Its guarantee deteriorates with \(n\); it does not establish that sampling entitlements in a large typed market gives useful bounds depending only on \(K\) and \(J\). Nor does the paper prove that the continuum stable benchmark or its entitlement values are efficiently computable from a general merit law. Those are the next questions: can the finite theorem’s robustness be made to depend on the number of types rather than the population, and when can the benchmark entitlements themselves be computed or approximated efficiently?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that the theorem’s robustness guarantee does not survive a large population at fixed accuracy. Its fairness factor is \(\frac{\phi(1+\epsilon/2)}{n\epsilon+1}\), and its utility factor is \(\frac{1}{\phi n\epsilon+1}\). For fixed \(\epsilon\), both deteriorate as \(n\) grows. Keeping them bounded away from zero requires \(\epsilon=O(1/n)\); the stated sampling bound then grows as \(O(n^2\log n)\). So the theorem itself gives no population-independent robustness result.

The proposed mass LP avoids that problem by taking the entitlements \(E_{a,j}\) as input. That leaves a legitimate allocation problem, but it sets aside the paper’s main technical issue: estimating those entitlements robustly. The optimization is a direct type-level version of the paper’s LP and its Appendix B flow formulation. With exact type symmetry, a type-level plan can preserve each person’s fairness guarantee, but the resulting algorithm appears to be a straightforward aggregation of machinery the paper already supplies.

That is a real reason to doubt the value of this particular mirror. It does not defeat a stronger one. In an exchangeable large market, one can ask whether the merit law induces computable per-type entitlements, and whether estimating them and optimizing the mass plan admits guarantees depending on the number of types rather than the population. That keeps uncertainty, the stable-matching benchmark, and fairness-constrained utility optimization in the question. The paper does not answer it, and the population-continuous version is a coherent computational problem.

So I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the task demands. The strongest objection is that the direct exact-entitlement mirror is algorithmically routine and the theorem’s stated robustness bound degenerates with \(n\). A type-level robustness question remains a plausible, worthwhile mirror, and the proponent’s anchor survives that challenge.
