The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over municipality population types \(p_t\), and a rational average house supply \(H\), form finite instances with \(N\mu_t\) municipalities of type \(t\) and \(NH\) seats, for admissible \(N\). Do the type-level seat masses \(x^{(N)}_{t,k}\) produced by \(Q\) converge as \(N\) grows, and can their limit be computed from \((\mu_t,p_t,H)\)? In particular, is the limit the quota-respecting allocation that assigns each type only \(\lfloor q_t\rfloor\) or \(\lceil q_t\rceil\) seats, where \(q_t=Hp_t/\sum_u\mu_up_u\), filling upper-quota seats in descending priority \(p_t/(\lfloor q_t\rfloor+1)\) and splitting mass at ties?

The model it lives in

Types are municipality populations \(p_t\) with rational mass \(\mu_t\). Each municipality receives an integer seat count \(k\), while \(x_{t,k}\) records the fraction of type \(t\) receiving \(k\); the total seat mass is \(H\). The candidate limit restricts \(k\) to \(\lfloor q_t\rfloor\) and \(\lceil q_t\rceil\), with \(q_t=Hp_t/\sum_u\mu_up_u\), and fills upper-quota mass by the paper's priority rule.

The objection that survived

The municipality regime scales the total house with the number of units, extending the paper's motivating fixed-state Congress; its general formulation for arbitrary \(s\) and \(h\) makes that extension plausible but does not make it the paper's original setting.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's quota method and its recursive apportionment rule. It leaves Theorem 1's impossibility result and Theorem 3's unbiased minimum-requirement extension outside the stated mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational \(\mu_t\), \(p_t\), and \(H\), does the finite \(Q\) output converge along admissible multiples \(N\), and does it equal the proposed quota-and-priority allocation?
  • Can the limit be computed in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length, including cases with tied priorities?
  • As \(H\) varies, which of quota, house monotonicity, and consistency persist in the type-mass limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible case around the quota method, but the paper has no anchor that meets your computational-result requirement. Theorem 1 says no Huntington method satisfies quota, and among the five modern workable methods only smallest divisors satisfies upper quota and only greatest divisors satisfies lower quota. Theorem 2 says the quota method \(Q\) is the unique consistent, house-monotone method satisfying quota, and gives its recursive priority rule. Theorem 3 extends that characterization to unbiased minimum requirements. These are axiomatic results; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity.

The nearest positive case is a *candidate computational mirror* of Theorem 2, not a qualifying anchor. Imagine a very large federation allocating seats among many municipalities, with \(N\) municipalities but only \(\tau\) distinct population sizes. A type \(t\) records a municipality’s population \(p_t\), and \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of all municipalities. Given a house size of \(H\) seats per municipality on average, let \(\bar p=\sum_t\mu_t p_t\) and \(q_t=Hp_t/\bar p\).

Ask for masses \(x_{t,k}\geq0\), where \(x_{t,k}\) is the fraction of municipalities of type \(t\) receiving exactly \(k\) seats. Require \(\sum_kx_{t,k}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,k}kx_{t,k}=H\), and allow positive \(x_{t,k}\) only for \(k\in\{\lfloor q_t\rfloor,\lceil q_t\rceil\}\). Among these quota-respecting allocations, maximize the mass assigned an upper-quota seat according to \(p_t/(\lfloor q_t\rfloor+1)\), the paper’s next-seat priority. A solution is the resulting type-level allocation, allowing a tied priority to split mass. Each municipality still receives an integer number of seats; only the type-level totals can be fractional.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many municipalities share the same complete seat-relevant type, and \(\tau\) is small relative to \(N\). The computational question is to produce the aggregate allocation from the support and binary-rational masses. The candidate looks tractable: it has one decision variable per type, with quota bounds and a total-seat constraint, and the priorities can be sorted. The question for a prover would be whether this fluid priority rule is the limit of the paper’s finite \(Q\) method, including its consistency and house monotonicity. Extending it to Theorem 3’s unbiased requirements is another natural question.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s central results characterize a method; they do not classify a computational problem. Also, splitting identical types into mass can make the quota allocation easy while losing the finite method’s distinctive uniqueness and monotonicity claims. So this is a credible lead for a mirror of the *problem setting*, but it does not furnish a valid computational anchor under your rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–3 concern axiomatic properties of apportionment methods: impossibility, uniqueness, consistency, house monotonicity, and quota. They do not classify the complexity of a search or decision problem. Theorem 2 gives a recursive construction of \(Q\), but its claim is that \(Q\) is the unique method with specified properties across population vectors and house sizes. That is a characterization of a rule, not a complexity result about an instance.

The municipality example is the best candidate for a computational question, but it changes the object under study. If the population is the residents and the types are their states, there are at most \(s\) types. Scaling the resident counts by any positive factor leaves the apportionment unchanged: the quotas and priority comparisons depend on relative populations. This is a natural continuous description of population shares, but it adds no growing population dimension to the computation.

If instead the mass represents municipalities receiving seats, the model has a more genuine high multiplicity. Yet with a fixed house size \(h\), the average seats per municipality tend to zero as the number \(N\) of municipalities grows. Keeping a nonzero average requires scaling the house size with \(N\), which describes a growing family of legislatures rather than the paper’s usual fixed set of states and house. That could be a sensible new apportionment regime, but it is not supplied by the paper’s results.

There is also a gap between the proposed one-house optimization and Theorem 2. A type-level allocation at one house size cannot by itself express the theorem’s claims about a method across *all* house sizes and population vectors. One could ask whether a mass-level priority process preserves quota, house monotonicity, and consistency. But that is either an axiomatic characterization again, or a newly formulated computational problem about evaluating the process. The same issue applies to Theorem 1’s impossibility claim and Theorem 3’s extension to minimum requirements.

So the negative case is strongest as a case about fit: a many-municipality version might be worth studying on its own, and its plausibility cannot be dismissed just because the paper lacks complexity theorems. But making that version computational requires adding a new instance regime and task. Keeping the paper’s actual results leaves axiomatic questions outside ChoCo’s computational scope; changing them into finite-instance tasks no longer mirrors those results.
