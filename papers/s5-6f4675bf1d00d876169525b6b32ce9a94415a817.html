What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. Its one displayed THEOREM is unnumbered and axiomatic: an aggregation rule is the \(E\)-based scoring rule exactly when it satisfies Dominance and Cancellation Independence. The paper says the proof is in Gaertner and Xu [10] (2012). It establishes no complexity classification or algorithm for an underlying computational problem.

A population of engineers who share types of scoring functions could be a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. But the theorem concerns one evaluator aggregating a finite set of criteria, while the paper’s discussion of multiple engineers gives no corresponding named computational result. Turning either into a continuous computational question would therefore lack the required anchor. Under the stated criteria, there is no honest prover-ready mirror to propose for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no computational problem to continuize. Its displayed theorem characterizes the \(E\)-based scoring rule through Dominance and Cancellation Independence. It gives no complexity result, algorithmic task, or discrete problem whose high-multiplicity version could be compared with it.

The best candidate for a population is the committee in the opening example, or a large range-voting electorate evaluating designs. An evaluator’s complete type could be their score vector \(s\in E^X\); a society distribution \(\mu\) would give each design \(x\) the aggregate score \(\sum_s \mu_s s(x)\). That is a credible high-multiplicity setting, but it applies the paper’s rule to a continuous electorate. It does not turn the paper’s axiomatic characterization into a computational result.

A stronger proposal would ask for the least-cost mass of evaluators whose score vectors must change to make a target design win. That is a well-formed continuous bribery or robustness problem. But it needs an intervention model and costs \(\gamma(s,s')\) that the paper never supplies, and it changes the question from characterizing an aggregation rule to manipulating it. It could be worthwhile research on range voting; calling it a mirror of this paper would stretch the connection past the programme’s anchor requirement.

So the negative case is strong for “no direct computational mirror of this paper,” but weak as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise in any related setting. A large range-voting population is plausible. What is missing is a computational question from the paper itself; a nontrivial one has to be added.
