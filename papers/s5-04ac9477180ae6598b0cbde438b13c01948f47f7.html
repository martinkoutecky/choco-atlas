The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of programs \(P\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) for single-resident types \(t\in T\), rational masses \(\nu_u\) for couple-household types \(u\in U\), type rankings, program priorities, and rational quota rates \(q_p\), decide whether a stable assignment exists and, if so, return one with rational assigned masses \(x_{t,p}\) and \(y_{u,p,r}\). Each type's mass must be fully assigned to acceptable outcomes, quotas must hold, and stability requires that no positive mass of a single type can move to a preferred program, or of a couple type can move jointly to a preferred ordered program pair, where the programs would accept the movers after applying priority choice to the updated mass assignment.

The model it lives in

Model an atomless cohort of single-resident types and couple-household types, each type including all preferences and priority-relevant attributes used by the problem. A household receives a whole program or program pair, while type mass may split across assignments; programs have priority orders over resident types and quota rates \(q_p\) per unit of population. The decision variables are \(x_{t,p}\) and \(y_{u,p,r}\), and the task is to decide existence and return a stable assignment.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2 certifies SAT-E only for named finite agents, and the paper gives no representation result for the generally infinite set of stable mass assignments; the proposed full-set output is therefore unsupported, though existence and one witness remain well-posed.

  • Does every instance in a natural class of priority and quota models admit a stable mass assignment?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s problem of finding the complete set of stable matchings when couples are present. It has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large, centralized residency market with many applicants sharing the same preferences and the same program-relevant qualifications. A type must include everything the model uses: for a single resident, their acceptable-program ranking and relevant priority attributes; for a couple, both members’ attributes and their joint ranking over program pairs. Mass is the fraction of the cohort of each type. The regime is credible only when the number of residents is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of these distinct types. Program capacity should be expressed as a quota per unit of population.

The paper’s best named anchor is Theorem 2, which states that a matching \(\mu\) is stable if and only if its corresponding assignment \(\pi\) satisfies SAT-E. The paper cites the proof from Drummond et al. (2015), rather than proving it here. The authors then use the encoding to enumerate stable matchings. The paper does not prove a numbered complexity-class result of its own: it cites Ronn’s NP-completeness result for SMP-C, but that citation is not a numbered theorem in this paper. Theorem 2 is therefore the eligible anchor because it gives a formulation the paper computes with.

I would hand a prover this continuous problem:

Exact Stable Mass Assignments with Couples (ESMA-C). An instance gives a finite set \(P\) of programs, rational quota rates \(q_p\), single-resident types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and couple types \(u\) with rational masses \(\nu_u\). Each single type has a complete, possibly truncated ranking over programs and being unmatched. Each couple type has a complete, possibly truncated joint ranking over ordered program pairs, including unmatched options. Each program has a priority ranking over resident types, derived from the qualifications that affect its choices. A population normalization such as
\[ \sum_t\mu_t+2\sum_u\nu_u=1 \]
sets the unit in which quota rates are measured.

A proposed assignment consists of nonnegative masses \(x_{t,p}\) and \(y_{u,p,r}\): \(x_{t,p}\) is the mass of single type \(t\) assigned to \(p\), and \(y_{u,p,r}\) is the mass of couple type \(u\) assigned jointly to \(p,r\). Each type’s allocated mass must sum to its input mass; allocations must be individually rational; and no program may exceed its quota. A program’s choice from a mass of applicants is its highest-priority acceptable mass up to its quota, allowing fractional selection at a cutoff. Stability means that no positive mass of a single type can move to a preferred program that would accept it, and no positive mass of a couple type can move as a unit to a preferred pair of programs that would jointly accept both members, after the movers release their current places.

The task is to decide whether any stable mass assignment exists and, if so, return an exact finite rational description of the entire set of stable assignments. That last requirement is the continuous counterpart of the paper’s enumeration: a finite instance may have a list of matchings, while a mass instance can have infinitely many assignments, so the output must describe the set rather than list its points.

I expect this problem to be continuum-specifically hard, though that is a research hypothesis, not a claim established by the paper. Fractional mass may dissolve the indivisibilities behind discrete hardness, so Ronn’s result does not automatically transfer. But couples retain the central complementarities: a household chooses a pair, and each program’s willingness to take one member depends on what happens with the other. The resulting stability conditions may still have substantial combinatorial structure. The problem also opens natural follow-ups: existence alone; computing one stable assignment; characterizing resident-Pareto-optimal stable assignments; and whether these tasks become tractable when \(\tau\), the number of couple types, or \(m=|P|\) is small.

This mirror covers the paper’s exact stable-matching search and enumeration. It does not continuize the SAT encoding itself: SAT-E is how the authors compute with the discrete problem, while the mirror asks for stable assignments of a continuous population. The residency setting makes the authors’ underlying problem recognizable, and repeated cohorts with standardized preferences and program-relevant qualifications give a plausible instance regime. As in the paper, the couple units remain households whose members must be assigned together.

The weakest point is that the paper’s main contributions beyond Theorem 2 are an incentive result and empirical findings, not a proved complexity classification. Also, fractional assignment of identical couples may change the existence and structure of stable solutions substantially. That makes ESMA-C a serious question suggested by the paper, but it leaves room for the opposing case that the continuous problem is no longer the authors’ problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result in the sense the programme is trying to continuize. Theorem 2 says that a discrete matching is stable exactly when its SAT encoding is satisfied; its proof is attributed to earlier work. The paper cites NP-completeness and uses SAT to enumerate matchings, but does not prove a numbered complexity result of its own. So ESMA-C is a new problem suggested by the paper’s computational machinery, not a continuous counterpart of a result the paper establishes.

There is also a real gap between Theorem 2 and ESMA-C. The theorem concerns a one-to-one correspondence between Boolean assignments and matchings of named, indivisible residents. In ESMA-C, the variables are masses, programs may split a type at a quota cutoff, and stability must be redefined for mass deviations. Describing the entire solution set by a finite rational formula is a reasonable idea, but it adds an output problem that the theorem does not address. Asking only whether a stable mass assignment exists, or asking for one, would be a cleaner computational question—but that is a further step away from the stated anchor.

That objection does not dispose of the best version of the mirror. Couples can remain indivisible households while many identical households form a type; quotas can scale with population; and repeated cohorts with common preferences and program-relevant attributes are plausible in a large centralized market. The resulting stable-mass problem preserves the couples’ complementarities. I cannot establish that it would be trivial, or that matching researchers would fail to recognize it as their problem. The paper’s random-instance model may have too many distinct rankings for a useful small-support regime, but a more standardized cohort could supply one.

The paper’s strategy-proofness theorem is a weaker alternative anchor. For a truly nonatomic population, one resident’s report has zero effect on the aggregate distribution, so individual strategy-proofness risks becoming vacuous. Making a positive mass of residents report together would ask about coalition manipulation, a different question. The paper’s remaining evidence is chiefly empirical exploration of finite instances, not a computational classification.

So the negative case is strongest on the paper’s fit: its named theorem is an encoding-correctness statement, and its central computational work has no continuous complexity result to carry over. It is weak as a universal claim. ESMA-C, or the simpler existence or search version, remains a coherent high-multiplicity problem that this paper motivates. The material supports calling the mirror less directly anchored than the proponent suggests; it does not honestly support saying no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
