The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no computational anchor for a continuous mirror. Its formal contribution is to derive equilibrium payoffs in three specified, finite-player bargaining games; its experiments test whether earnings move as those predictions suggest. Neither is a result about the complexity or algorithms for a computational problem. The closest candidate—the comparisons in Section 2.6 and their experimental tests in Section 4.2—supports a question about strategic bargaining, but does not itself supply the computational target this programme is meant to continuize.

The strongest version of that candidate would compare incumbent role types’ payoffs before and after adding a positive mass of new members, with the budget scaled per capita. But the paper’s mechanism depends on named players with finite voting weights, each selected as proposer with probability \(1/n\), and each potentially pivotal. In an atomless population, an individual vote has zero mass and cannot change whether a proposal passes. A mass-positive type could be offered a share, but treating that type as a bargaining bloc gives it collective agency the paper’s individual players do not have. Alternatively, keeping people as individual agents leaves a proposer whose identity has zero mass. Either construction needs a new strategic model and equilibrium interpretation before there is a computational question to classify.

The experimental sample does not supply the missing population regime: participants were repeatedly assigned to bargaining groups of three or four, so the games themselves never had a large population of interchangeable members. One could study a different model of bargaining among large cohorts or blocs; that might be worthwhile in its own right. But the paper does not give a computational result for such a model to mirror. This is a strong case against calling the paper a ChoCo mirror, though not proof that no related continuous bargaining question could be valuable.
