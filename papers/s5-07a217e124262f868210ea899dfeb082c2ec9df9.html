The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of destinations \(H\), a listed set of strict ranking types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational per-capita capacities \(q_h\), let \(f(t)\) be type \(t\)'s first choice, \(F=\{f(t):t\in T\}\), and \(d_h=\sum_{t:f(t)=h}\mu_t\). In the regime \(q_h\le d_h\) for every \(h\in F\), \(H\setminus F\ne\varnothing\), and \(\sum_{h\in H}q_h\ge1\), find rational flows \(x_{t,h}\ge0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le q_h\), \(\sum_{t:f(t)=h}x_{t,h}=q_h\) for \(h\in F\), and \(x_{t,h}=0\) whenever \(h\in F\) and \(f(t)\ne h\). Among feasible flows, maximize \(\sum_t x_{t,s(t)}\), where \(s(t)\) is the most preferred destination in \(H\setminus F\); among ties, maximize \(\sum_{t,h}x_{t,h}(m+1-\operatorname{rank}_t(h))\). Each unit of mass receives one whole destination; the flow allows a type's population to split across destinations.

The model it lives in

A capacitated object-allocation model with a continuum of agents represented by rational mass over a finite support of strict preference types. Variables \(x_{t,h}\) allocate type mass to indivisible destination classes with per-capita capacities; the lexicographic objective minimizes mass left below its first and second choices, then selects a mass-level Pareto-efficient allocation.

The objection that survived

Theorem 5 concerns distinct unit-capacity houses with strict rankings, and neither it nor the proponent's case establishes that its characterization of minimal envy carries over unchanged to interchangeable destination capacities.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5's minimal-envy allocation objective and a population-level Pareto tie-break; it leaves the popularity characterization, local exchanges, convergence results, and most-popular extension aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does this capacity-scaled first- and second-choice characterization exactly minimize envy mass in the corresponding reduced allocation problem?
  • Can the finite replication correspondence and Pareto guarantee be stated precisely, with efficiency interpreted up to null sets?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Minimal-Envy Allocation (CMEA), anchored on Theorem 5, proved in this paper. The theorem says that the output of the MEM algorithm (i) is a minimal envy matching, (ii) is Pareto efficient, and (iii) is obtained in cubic time in \(|I|\). Here “minimal envy” means first minimizing the number of envious agents, then, among those matchings, minimizing envy in the reduced problem formed from the envious agents and the houses not assigned to the others. This is a clear computational anchor; the paper does not give a numbered hardness result for its allocation problems. Its footnote about Young’s rule cites hardness from elsewhere and does not supply such an anchor.

A plausible regime is a large city assigning daycare places from a commonly owned pool, with no school priorities. Let \(H\) be a finite set of destination types, such as centers, and \(T\) a listed set of strict preference rankings over them. The population is a rational distribution \(\mu\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of children of type \(t\). Each destination \(h\) has a per-capita supply \(q_h\), with \(\sum_h q_h\ge 1\). A city might have hundreds of thousands of children but only a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of exact ranking types over \(m=|H|\) centers. A type can be split across destinations, but each unit of its mass gets one whole place. Thus the allocation variables describe how many residents receive each destination type, not fractional places given to individuals.

Here is the continuous problem. For each type \(t\), let \(f(t)\) be its first choice, and let \(F=\{f(t):t\in T\}\). Write \(d_h=\sum_{t:f(t)=h}\mu_t\). Restrict to the regime \(q_h\le d_h\) for every \(h\in F\), so the supply of each first-choice destination can be assigned to residents who rank it first; assume also that \(H\setminus F\) is nonempty. The instance consists of these rankings, masses, and supplies, all with rational encodings. Find rational masses \(x_{t,h}\ge0\) such that
\[ \sum_{h\in H}x_{t,h}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,h}\le q_h, \]
and all supply of each \(h\in F\) goes to types that rank \(h\) first:
\[ \sum_{t:f(t)=h}x_{t,h}=q_h,\qquad x_{t,h}=0\quad\text{if }h\in F\text{ and }f(t)\ne h. \]
Among these allocations, maximize the mass assigned its second choice, where \(s(t)\) is type \(t\)’s most preferred destination in \(H\setminus F\); that is, maximize \(\sum_t x_{t,s(t)}\). Among ties, maximize \(\sum_{t,h}x_{t,h}(m+1-\operatorname{rank}_t(h))\). The first constraints express the paper’s first-stage minimum-envy condition in this regime; the second objective minimizes envy in the corresponding reduced problem; and the final rank-sum tie-break gives a Pareto-efficient allocation. The output is a mass allocation \(x\), not a lottery for any individual.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. Its feasible set has only \(\tau m\) allocation variables and rational supply constraints; the objectives are linear. Exact rational optimization is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. Clearing denominators gives finite cohorts and destination supplies; the resulting transportation problem has integral vertices, so the mass assignment can be realized by assigning each member of a sufficiently large replicated population one whole place. This makes the model author-recognizable: it retains the one-place-per-person allocation, the preference types, and the first-then-second-choice fairness criterion. Existing high-multiplicity allocation work supports the plausibility of the regime rather than colliding with the question.

I would limit the claim to Theorem 5. The local-majority and popularity results, including Theorem 3, depend on comparing named agents across two matchings and exchanging among groups of up to three agents. Such exchanges have zero mass in a population continuum, and a positive-mass version would need its own definition. I am not claiming a mirror for those results.

The weakest point is the treatment of a destination type as a supply of interchangeable places. The paper ranks distinct houses strictly, while this mirror scales supplies per capita and treats copies of a center as equivalent. That is a meaningful extension, not a theorem already proved by the authors. The restricted regime \(q_h\le d_h\) also avoids cases where first-choice supply exceeds first-choice demand. What makes the proposed mirror worth pursuing is that its fairness objective still counts the mass of residents who envy, and the second stage still asks how many of those residents can receive their best remaining choice.

The next questions are whether the finite-to-continuous correspondence extends beyond the scarce-first-choice regime, and whether the same complexity result survives when agents have priorities or eligibility constraints.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the only anchor, and it is a real computational result: the MEM algorithm finds a minimal-envy matching in cubic time and returns a Pareto-efficient outcome. So the case against cannot dismiss it as merely axiomatic. Nor can it rely on a lack of plausible multiplicity: large cohorts with repeated preference rankings are credible, and minimal envy can be counted by type mass without tracking who each person is. Existing work on mixed matchings is not a collision.

The strongest objection is that the proposed daycare model is not the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s stated model. The paper matches agents to distinct, strictly ranked houses. Keeping that model while enlarging the population requires enlarging the set of houses too. If copies of a center are strictly ranked, only a bounded number of them are literally first houses; the extensive first-choice supply \(q_h\) in the proposal requires treating copies as interchangeable and ranking destination classes instead. That is a sensible capacitated, weak-preference extension, but it changes the object of Theorem 5. The authors may well recognize that extension, so this objection alone does not defeat the better mirror.

In that better model, however, the computational question looks immediate. The proposal fixes first-choice assignments, then maximizes second-choice mass and a rank-sum tie-break over \(\tau m\) flows. Those are standard transportation constraints and linear objectives. The resulting problem is a min-cost-flow or LP formulation; with finite replicated cohorts and integral capacities, the flow formulation has integral solutions. Thus the continuous version appears to add no difficult pricing or structural question, and its finite high-multiplicity counterpart has the same compressed flow formulation. The paper already solves its original problem in polynomial time, so the proposed gain is chiefly a routine change from individual inputs to type-count inputs.

There is also a mismatch in the Pareto-efficiency claim. A rank-sum optimum rules out improvements affecting positive mass, but in a nonatomic population a single agent has measure zero. Pointwise Pareto efficiency therefore needs a separate definition or gives a degenerate guarantee; efficiency up to null sets is a weaker statement than Theorem 5’s finite matching guarantee.

That is the best negative case: the exact model does not scale cleanly, while the natural scaled model seems to reduce to ordinary flow optimization with little new complexity to chart. It makes the mirror a low-priority ChoCo target, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The capacitated extension remains coherent and author-recognizable, so an honest case against this anchor is weaker than the proponent’s case for it.
