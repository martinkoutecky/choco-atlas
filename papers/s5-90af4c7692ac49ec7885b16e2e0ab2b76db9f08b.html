The proposed type-flow existence problem changes both the model and the target statistic, so it cannot supply the missing computational anchor.

What the mirror covers

No result in the paper is covered by a continuous computational mirror: Theorem 1.1 and its supporting lemmas give threshold asymptotics for expected matching counts, while the proposed flow-existence question is distinct.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most plausible population regime is a large labor market with a small number of recurring worker and firm types, such as qualification and role cohorts. A type would include its complete utility vector over types on the other side. Let \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\) be the masses of firm type \(i\) and worker type \(j\), and let \(u_{ij}\) and \(v_{ij}\) be their utilities for each other. A candidate continuous problem is \(\varepsilon\)-Stable Type-Flow Existence: given those masses, utilities, and \(\varepsilon\), decide whether there is a matching flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\), such that no matched type pair gains more than \(\varepsilon\) on both sides by switching, and no unmatched worker mass can be joined by a firm type that gains more than \(\varepsilon\). The solution is such a flow. This preserves the paper’s pairwise utility-gain notion in a plausible high-multiplicity market, though it uses type-level average utilities when a type is matched across several types.

The required anchor is missing. Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper, says that the expected total number \(S^\varepsilon(n,n+k)\) of \(\varepsilon\)-stable matchings is polynomially large when \(\varepsilon\lambda=O(n^{-1}\log n)\), and super-polynomially large when \(\varepsilon\lambda=\omega(n^{-1}\log n)\), uniformly for \(k\ge0\). That is an asymptotic result about a random finite market, not a classification of an algorithmic problem. The lemmas prove the estimates behind it; they do not supply a named complexity result either.

The type-flow question is therefore only a plausible model-level direction, not a valid mirror of a qualifying result in this paper. It asks about existence of a stable flow, while Theorem 1.1 concerns the expected cardinality of a set of individual matchings; there is no canonical count of continuum flows to carry over that result. The paper’s continuous random utilities and large-market setting make the proposed cohort scenario imaginable, but they do not close that gap. The honest conclusion is that this paper contains no named computational result to anchor the requested continuous-complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 1.1 is an asymptotic statement about the expected number of \(\varepsilon\)-stable matchings in a finite random market. The paper gives no algorithm, reduction, or complexity classification for a computational problem. Its matrices have continuously distributed entries, but that makes utilities random; it does not make the population continuous.

The proposed type-flow existence problem is a plausible question about a large labor market, but it changes what the paper asks. The theorem concerns the expected *count* of individual matchings. In a continuum model, a matching flow may range over a continuum of feasible flows, so there is no direct count to take. One could instead ask whether a stable flow exists, count its extreme points, or measure the volume of stable flows. Each is a possible new question, but the paper gives no reason to prefer one, and the proposed existence question does not mirror the theorem’s statistic or its threshold in \(n\) and \(\varepsilon\).

There is a sharper obstacle to deriving that model from the paper. Here, each agent’s utility is a vector over named agents on the other side. With continuously sampled utilities, these vectors are almost surely distinct, and their dimension grows with market size. Grouping people by role or qualification therefore loses the pair-specific utility information used to define blocking. A cohort version could resolve that by giving a type pair a shared utility. That is a reasonable model choice, but it replaces the paper’s independent individual utility matrices with a block-structured random market. Computing stable flows there might be worthwhile on its own; it would not be a continuous counterpart of a computational result established here.

So the case against this paper as a ChoCo anchor is strong: it studies random-market asymptotics, not computational complexity, and its main statistic has no natural flow analogue. The broader claim that no related continuous stable-matching problem is worth studying is weaker. A fixed-type labor-market flow problem could be a sensible ChoCo project, but its motivation would need to come from that model and its computational questions, not from this paper’s theorem.
