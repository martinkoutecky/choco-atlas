The continuous mirror question

Input candidates \(C\), a committee \(W\), a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), approval sets \(A_t\subseteq C\), and rational payments \(p_{t,d}\geq 0\) and residuals \(r_t\geq 0\), where \(p_{t,d}=0\) if \(d\notin A_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_{t,d}=1\) for each \(d\in W\). Decide whether for every \(c\in C\setminus W\), \(S\subseteq W\), and nonnegative mass portions \(x_t,y_t\) satisfying \(x_t+y_t\leq\mu_t\), \(x_t=y_t=0\) when \(c\notin A_t\), \(x_t=0\) when \(|A_t\cap S|>1\), and \(y_t=0\) when \(A_t\cap S\neq\varnothing\), the deviation value \(\sum_{t\in T}(x_t\max_{d\in S}p_{t,d}+y_t r_t)\) is at most \(1\), with \(\max\varnothing=0\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support distribution over complete approval-and-price types. Payments and residuals are per-unit-mass amounts; \(x_t,y_t\) are disjoint portions of type \(t\)'s mass. For each unselected candidate and eligible selected set, the deviation objective is \(\sum_t(x_t\max_{d\in S}p_{t,d}+y_t r_t)\); decide whether any such deviation exceeds \(1\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Proposition A.5's weak-stability verification hardness; it leaves the Continuous Phragmén rule, the other axiomatic results, and the empirical analyses untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a narrow mirror of Proposition A.5, proved in this paper’s Appendix A. It says that, given an approval profile \(A\), a committee \(W\), and a price system \(ps\), deciding whether \(ps\) is weakly stable is coNP-complete, even when \(W\) is Pareto optimal. The paper proves this with a reduction from X3C; this is not a result merely cited from elsewhere.

Weak-Stability Verification\(_\infty\) is the direct population-continuum version. An instance has candidates \(C\), a committee \(W\), and a finite support \(T\) of voter types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has an approval set \(A_t\), payments \(p_{t,d}\) to selected candidates \(d\in W\), and residual \(r_t\). The supplied price system must satisfy \(p_{t,d}\geq 0\), \(r_t\geq 0\), \(p_{t,d}=0\) when \(d\notin A_t\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{t,d}=1\) for each \(d\in W\). Payments and residuals are amounts per unit of population mass.

The question is whether this price system is weakly stable. For each unselected candidate \(c\), consider a selected set \(S\subseteq W\) and two disjoint portions \(x_t,y_t\) of each type’s mass, with \(x_t,y_t\geq 0\) and \(x_t+y_t\leq\mu_t\). Both portions must support \(c\). A portion \(x_t\) may contribute its payment toward at most one candidate in \(S\), so it is allowed only if \(|A_t\cap S|\leq 1\); a portion \(y_t\) may use residual only if \(A_t\cap S=\varnothing\). Ask whether there are \(c,S,x,y\) for which

\[ \sum_t x_t\max_{d\in S}p_{t,d}+\sum_t y_t r_t>1. \]

Use \(\max\varnothing=0\). A YES instance is a weakly stable price system: no such mass deviation can afford an unselected candidate. The displayed quantity is the deviation’s objective, and \(x,y\) are its decision variables.

This preserves the paper’s audit question. The paper asks whether supporters of an unselected candidate can combine eligible payments and residuals to afford it; the mirror asks the same of portions of approval types. For a finite profile of \(N\) voters, set \(\mu_t\) to the fraction with type \(t\), and scale each individual payment and residual by \(N\) to obtain the per-unit-mass amounts. Then the mass-weighted funding and deviation sums match the original sums. Types should be complete for the audit: if a supplied price system distinguishes otherwise identical approval ballots, its differing price row is part of the type. The hardness construction below assigns identical rows to voters with identical approvals, so it needs no such refinement.

A plausible regime is a large participatory-budgeting election or public consultation: many citizens approve projects from a finite agenda, and repeated cohorts share both ballots and price-system rows. One can take arbitrarily many copies of each cohort while keeping the mass distribution and support fixed. In the reduction, for example, the population can have \(R(3q+2)\) citizens while using at most \(3q+2\) complete types. The hard structure lies in the incidence between approval types and projects, not in the ability to target a single named voter.

The paper’s X3C reduction shows why fractional mass does not dissolve this particular hardness. For a universe \(U\) of size \(3q\) and a family \(F\) of three-element sets, it creates voters \(U\cup\{i^\*,i_\varepsilon\}\), candidates \(c_F\) for each set and an unselected candidate \(c^\*\). Candidate \(c^\*\) is approved by \(U\cup\{i_\varepsilon\}\); candidate \(c_F\) is approved by the three elements of \(F\) and by \(i^\*\). The committee consists of all \(c_F\), and it is Pareto optimal because \(i^\*\) approves exactly those candidates. Voter \(i^\*\) pays \((q-1)/q\) toward each selected candidate; each element pays \(1/(3q)\) toward every selected candidate it approves; only \(i_\varepsilon\) has residual, \(\varepsilon\), where \(0<\varepsilon<1/(3q)\). An exact cover gives a violating deviation to \(c^\*\): choose the corresponding selected candidates, all elements as the payment-contributing group, and \(i_\varepsilon\) as the residual-contributing group. The total is \(1+\varepsilon\). Conversely, a violation requires all \(3q\) elements to contribute and each to approve exactly one candidate in the chosen set, which makes that set an exact cover.

For the continuum instance, give each original voter mass \(1/(3q+2)\) and scale each price and residual by \(3q+2\). The funding equations and the violation value are preserved. Although the continuous deviation may choose fractional portions, the elements can contribute at most \(1\) altogether; exceeding \(1\) requires every element’s full contribution, along with \(i_\varepsilon\)’s residual. The selected sets must therefore cover every element exactly once. This carries the X3C reduction through and gives coNP-hardness. Membership in coNP also carries over: a violating certificate specifies \(c\), \(S\), and rational type masses \(x_t,y_t\); for fixed \(c,S\), finding those masses is a bounded linear program with polynomial-size rational witnesses. Thus the mirror is coNP-complete, including on Pareto-optimal committees under the corresponding supported-type notion of Pareto optimality.

I expect hardness transfers (Class B). The combinatorics live in the candidate agenda and the approval-incidence pattern, and the reduction survives the move from voters to masses. This is a useful boundary result for continuization: it identifies a computational audit in the paper’s own framework that remains hard in the continuum.

The weakest point is the anchor’s scope. Proposition A.5 concerns checking a supplied explanation, not computing one, and this mirror does not cover Continuous Phragmén’s rule or the paper’s other axioms and experiments. A referee could regard fractional portions of a type as changing the coalition semantics. Still, the paper’s weak-stability test is already expressed through aggregate budgets and payments, and the lifted reduction shows precisely how that fractional interpretation behaves. The paper’s continuous-time Phragmén procedure is not itself a continuous-population model; this mirror is about the population distribution and the complexity of its price-system audit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is about its payoff, not its modeling. Proposition A.5 classifies verification of a supplied price system for weak stability. Weak stability is an auxiliary notion the authors introduce and then set aside: such systems may not exist, and checking them is coNP-complete. The mirror therefore says little about computing the paper’s main explanation rule or about its empirical audits. That makes it a narrower target than the paper’s central contribution.

But narrow is not the same as unworthy, and I cannot find a sound objection that defeats this anchor. The proposed population model is well formed: types can include both approval sets and price rows, and the finite-to-continuous scaling preserves the funding equations and deviation sums. With \(N\) voters, set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and scale each individual price and residual by \(N\); then the mass-weighted continuous sums equal the original discrete sums. Repeating each voter \(R\) times preserves the same type support and mass distribution, so the reduction also has an arbitrarily large population per type.

Fractional portions do not break the X3C reduction. In the lifted construction, the universe types can contribute at most \(1\) in total. A deviation strictly above \(1\) must use every universe type at full mass and the residual contribution; the selected candidates must then encode an exact cover. The positive-mass type corresponding to \(i^\*\), which approves exactly the selected committee, also preserves Pareto optimality. The reverse implication and the coNP membership argument carry over as the proponent describes.

So the honest negative case is weak at its core. I can argue that this is a peripheral audit problem and a lower-priority mirror than a result about computing explanations. I cannot argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists here: the proposed verification mirror is coherent, and the paper’s hardness result survives it.
