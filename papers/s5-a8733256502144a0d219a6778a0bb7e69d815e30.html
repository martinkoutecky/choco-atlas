Why no mirror

The sole anchor, Theorem 9, is an axiomatic impossibility about mechanisms, and the rubric expressly excludes such impossibility theorems from the computational-result gate. The two-cohort construction is a plausible high-multiplicity extension, but it cannot turn the paper's theorem into a complexity, algorithmic, or computational-feasibility result. Thus bit (a) fails even though a continuous version of the incompatibility is meaningful.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only the joint EFA–PEA impossibility in Theorem 9; it leaves the SP, OSP, EF, SEF, PEA, and PEP mechanism characterizations and the individual mechanism results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a single mirror anchored in Theorem 9, proved in this paper:

Theorem 9. With general cardinal utilities, no mechanism for online fair division is envy-free ex ante and Pareto efficient ex ante.

The theorem is not an NP-hardness or polynomial-time result; the paper contains no named theorem establishing NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), FPT, or a comparable complexity classification. Thus this is a qualified ChoCo case. It is nevertheless a genuine computational mechanism-existence impossibility, not merely an axiomatic statement about a profile simplex.

I would call the mirror Continuous Online EFA–PEA Feasibility. A society consists of finitely many complete agent types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type includes its entire additive utility vector over the arriving item classes. At round \(j\), \(q_j\) indivisible copies of item class \(o_j\) arrive per unit population. The \(q_j\)'s are rational, so a realization with population size \(N\) contains \(Nq_j\) physical copies. An aggregate allocation records, for each type, a distribution over integral bundles; only the mass of agents receiving each bundle is fractional. Thus the continuum represents many interchangeable agents, while goods and individual bundles remain indivisible.

An online mechanism sees only the current item and the allocation history, and chooses how the arriving copies are assigned among the type masses. Its expected allocation must be envy-free ex ante: every type must weakly prefer its own expected bundle to the expected bundle of every other type. It must also be Pareto efficient ex ante: no other feasible aggregate allocation may weakly improve every type's expected utility and strictly improve one positive-mass type. The problem is:

\[ \text{Given }(T,\mu,(q_j,u_{\cdot j})_{j=1}^r),\text{ determine whether an online rule satisfying both EFA and PEA exists.} \]

For the universal domain of finite-support rational societies and general cardinal utilities, the answer is no.

The paper's two-agent counterexample lifts exactly. Take two types of masses \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\), two item classes with \(q_1=q_2=1/2\), and utilities

\[ u_1(o_1)=1,\qquad u_1(o_2)=2, \]
\[ u_2(o_1)=2,\qquad u_2(o_2)=1. \]

EFA on the first prefix forces the first item mass to be divided equally between the two types. EFA on the second prefix then forces the second item mass to be divided equally as well. Each type consequently receives expected utility

\[ \frac12\cdot 1+\frac12\cdot 2=\frac32. \]

But assigning all \(o_1\)-mass to type \(2\) and all \(o_2\)-mass to type \(1\) gives both types utility \(2\). Hence the EFA allocation is Pareto dominated, so no online mechanism can satisfy both properties.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: \(N=2K\) agents form two large cohorts of \(K\) agents with identical utility vectors, while \(K\) copies of each item class arrive. The number of types stays \(2\) as \(K\) grows. A food bank serving two large classes of charities, or a cloud allocator serving two repeated service profiles, would make the type interpretation natural. Scaling the item supply with the population is necessary, but it is a permitted co-modification: the population is the continuous object, while each realized unit still receives an integral bundle.

The result should be classified as an impossibility transfer rather than Class A, B, or C in the strict complexity trichotomy. If forced into that vocabulary, it is B-like: the obstruction is already present with two types and survives arbitrary multiplicity. It is not continuum-specific, and continuization does not dissolve it.

The mirror covers only Theorem 9. I would not stretch it to the strategy-proofness characterizations, since unilateral deviations by zero-mass individuals become vacuous in an atomless model, nor to the \(\mypar\) characterization without a more delicate treatment of type splitting. Natural follow-up questions are whether EFA and PEA become compatible for binary utilities, under approximate fairness, with restricted arrival orders, or with different per-capita supply ratios.

The weakest point is formal eligibility: Theorem 9 is an impossibility theorem about mechanisms, not a named complexity classification. If ChoCo requires an anchor specifically of the form NP-hard, \(P\), FPT, or approximation complexity, then this paper has no qualifying anchor and cannot support a fully compliant positive case. Under the broader reading that computational mechanism impossibilities count, however, this is a faithful continuous-population mirror with an exact two-way finite/high-multiplicity correspondence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor does not meet ChoCo’s stated gate. Theorem 9 is an axiomatic mechanism-existence impossibility, not a computational result: it specifies no finite input, decision or optimization problem, complexity measure, or algorithm. None of the paper’s theorems establishes NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, approximation guarantees, or parameterized complexity. A continuous restatement of an axiomatic impossibility therefore remains outside the programme’s computational brief.

The proposed lift is formally imaginable, but it also changes the model in a way the case glosses over. The paper has one indivisible item arriving at each round. Replacing it by \(q_jN\) identical copies either introduces a new within-round arrival process or makes copies arrive simultaneously. The first choice requires specifying the order and prefix guarantees; the second is no longer the paper’s online model.

More importantly, Theorem 9 is quantified over one mechanism that must work for every possible finite instance. Its proof uses the fact that the mechanism’s first-round behaviour must satisfy envy-freeness even when the process stops after the first item, while the same behaviour is then used in a longer instance. A continuous input \((T,\mu,\ldots)\) does not by itself specify the possible future continuations against which an online rule must be judged. If the full future is known when the rule is designed, the problem is no longer the paper’s online mechanism problem. If the rule must handle the universal domain of continuations, the answer is immediately “no” because of the same constant-size two-type counterexample; the population distribution is not an input driving a computational problem.

The best repair would be to introduce an explicit uncertainty model for future item classes and ask for a feasible policy, or for the optimal tradeoff between EFA and PEA, over finite-support types. That could become an interesting new continuous online-allocation problem. But all of its computational content—the policy representation, uncertainty structure, approximation objective, and complexity question—would have to be added. It is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The two-cohort construction is a plausible high-multiplicity encoding, so I would not object that mass is inherently meaningless here. It shows that the paper’s counterexample can be replicated with many interchangeable agents. It does not, however, turn the paper into a computational-population paper: the impossibility is driven by online information and incompatible axioms, and the paper never studies the algorithmic problem of finding or approximating such mechanisms.

Thus the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated scope: there is no qualifying named computational anchor. Its honest limitation is that, if the programme broadens “computational” to include newly formulated mechanism-feasibility or tradeoff problems, the proponent has a legitimate starting point. That would be a new research direction built around the paper, not a worthwhile continuous mirror supplied by the paper itself.
