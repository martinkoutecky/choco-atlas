The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with distribution \(\mu\), item classes \(O\) with per-capita supplies \(q_o\), additive utilities \(u_t(o)\), and normalized type weights \(\beta_t\), find a measurable assignment of integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{O}\) with mass \(\lambda_{t,B}\), satisfying \(\sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}\lambda_{t,B}B_o=q_o\), such that almost every type-\(t\) agent satisfies weighted PROP1 relative to \(P_t=\beta_t\sum_o q_o u_t(o)\), and the assignment is fPO against fractional reallocations preserving the aggregate supplies.

The model it lives in

A replicated mixed-item allocation economy with interchangeable agent types, population mass \(\mu\), per-capita indivisible-item supplies \(q\), configuration variables \(\lambda_{t,B}\), weighted-PROP1 feasibility, and pointwise fractional Pareto efficiency.

The objection that survived

The proposed configuration measure ranges over potentially unbounded integral bundles, and the paper's finite acyclic-rounding argument does not by itself establish finite support, compressed rounding, or an efficient pricing formulation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its mixed-utility weighted-PROP1/fPO algorithmic guarantee. It does not directly cover the corollaries, the rounding flexibility remark, the PROPX impossibility example, or the unnumbered utilitarian-maximization hardness statement.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every feasible instance admit a finite-support configuration measure \(\lambda\) whose supported integral bundles satisfy PROP1?
  • Can the type-compressed acyclic rounding argument preserve individual-level PROP1 and fPO without expanding the population?
  • Does the resulting configuration formulation have polynomial-time separation or pricing in \(\tau\), \(m\), and the input encoding length, or is it continuum-specifically hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a genuine high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s main result, not its internal use of divisible allocations.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper, although Step 2 of its algorithm relies on the earlier algorithm of Sandomirskiy and Segal-Halevi [14]. The theorem says that, for any number of agents and indivisible items, with additive utilities that may be positive, negative, or zero and with arbitrary positive normalized agent weights, there always exists an integral allocation satisfying both weighted PROP1 and fPO. Moreover, Algorithm 1 computes such an allocation in strongly polynomial time in \(n+m\). Weighted PROP1 means that agent \(i\) either receives utility at least \(b_i u_i(O)\), or reaches that threshold after adding one item outside her bundle, or after removing one item from her bundle. fPO means that no fractional allocation Pareto-improves the output. This is a result proved here, not merely cited.

A natural high-multiplicity regime is a large public-allocation system with \(N\) households and a fixed catalogue of \(m\) recurring indivisible goods and chores. There are only \(\tau\) household profiles, where \(\tau\ll N\). A type \(t\) completely specifies the household’s additive utility \(u_t(o)\) for every item class \(o\), together with its relative fairness weight. For example, a mass of households may have the same valuation of public benefits, undesirable assignments, administrative burdens, and service slots. The item catalogue is repeated at per-capita supply: item class \(o\) has \(Nq_o\) indivisible copies. Thus the population becomes large without making individual bundles divisible.

Formally, the continuous instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), a distribution \(\mu\) over types, utilities \(u_t(o)\in\mathbb{Q}\), relative weights \(\beta_t>0\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t=1\), and per-capita item supplies \(q_o\). The finite lift with population \(N\) has \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), \(Nq_o\) copies of item \(o\), and gives each type-\(t\) agent weight \(\beta_t/N\). Its weighted proportionality threshold is exactly \( \beta_t\sum_o q_o u_t(o) \), independent of \(N\). This is the precise high-multiplicity dictionary.

I would call the continuous problem \(\mathrm{RM\text{-}fPO\text{-}wPROP1}_\infty\), for replicated mixed-item allocation. Its decision variable is a mass assignment \(\lambda_{t,B}\), where \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{O}\) is an integral bundle, possibly containing several copies of an item class. The constraints are \( \sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t \) for every type and \( \sum_{t,B}\lambda_{t,B}B_o=q_o \) for every item class. Thus \(\lambda_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\). The objective is a constructive feasibility objective: find any such assignment satisfying the two fairness/efficiency requirements.

Write \(v_t(B)=\sum_o B_o u_t(o)\) and \(P_t=\beta_t\sum_o q_o u_t(o)\). Every bundle in the support of \(\lambda_{t,\cdot}\) must satisfy the exact PROP1 condition from the paper: \(v_t(B)\ge P_t\), or \(v_t(B)+u_t(o)\ge P_t\) for some available outside copy of \(o\), or \(v_t(B)-u_t(o)\ge P_t\) for some copy of \(o\) contained in \(B\).

The fPO requirement is also retained at the individual level. Realize \(\lambda\) as a measurable assignment \(B(p)\) to population points \(p\). The assignment is fPO if there is no fractional reassignment \(y_o(p)\ge0\) with the same aggregate item supplies, \( \sum_t\int y_o(p)\,dp=q_o \), such that every agent weakly improves, \( \sum_o u_{t(p)}(o)y_o(p)\ge v_{t(p)}(B(p)) \), with strict improvement on a positive-mass set. This prevents the mirror from collapsing into merely checking average utility by type.

This is a question the authors should recognize as theirs. It has the same agents, additive mixed utilities, indivisible bundles, weighted proportionality, and Pareto efficiency. The only structural change is that named agents are replaced by masses of interchangeable agents, with item supplies scaled per capita so that the limit does not degenerate. The fractional quantities that appear are type averages and mass distributions; every unit of population still receives an integral bundle. The paper’s own divisible allocation is therefore not a novelty collision: it is an outcome-space device inside a finite-agent proof, whereas this mirror makes the population itself continuous.

I would provisionally expect \(\mathrm{RM\text{-}fPO\text{-}wPROP1}_\infty\) to be Class A. The paper’s proof suggests a compressed route: solve the fractional welfare problem over type-item masses, eliminate cycles in the type-item support, and then realize the resulting forest by a mass-rounding procedure. The acyclicity argument is exactly what limits each agent’s loss to one item and preserves fPO through a common welfare certificate. A successful version would run in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(m\), and the encoding length of \(\mu,\beta,q,u\), without expanding the population to \(N\).

The main further questions are whether the finite acyclic-rounding argument survives after identical agents are compressed into masses; whether a finite-support \(\lambda\) always exists with every supported bundle satisfying PROP1; and whether the resulting configuration LP has an efficient pricing oracle. If pricing over admissible integral bundles becomes knapsack-like, this could instead become a continuum-specific Class C problem. That boundary would itself be valuable.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 1, not every result in the paper. I am not using the paper’s final unnumbered NP-hardness statement about utilitarian maximization as an anchor, since it is not a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition.

The weakest point is the individual-level PROP1 requirement. A type-average formulation would be easier but would be too weak and would no longer faithfully mirror the paper. The exact formulation retains integral bundles and almost-everywhere Pareto efficiency, but proving a compressed algorithm for it is nontrivial. The other vulnerability is the per-capita scaling of item supplies; without it, fixing finitely many items while sending the population to a continuum produces a degenerate problem. That scaling is nevertheless the natural high-multiplicity co-modification, and the finite-lift identity above makes the mirror mathematically explicit rather than ad hoc.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 1, and the strongest negative case is that its guarantees do not possess a canonical population-only limit.

With the paper’s original item model, this is decisive. There are finitely many indivisible items, each assigned to one agent. In an atomless population, every item would belong to a measure-zero set of agents. Almost every agent would therefore receive nothing, while her proportionality threshold \(b_i u_i(O)\) could remain positive. Thus the literal continuum has no nondegenerate PROP1 allocation.

The proponent’s repair—replicating every item class at per-capita supply \(q_o\)—is mathematically coherent, but it changes more than the population scale. The paper concerns a finite set of individually scarce items; the repaired model concerns an extensive economy with \(Nq_o\) copies of each resource. “One item” in PROP1 now means one copy of a recurring resource, and \(fPO\) is taken over a growing resource system. That may be a sensible new fair-division model, but it is not a uniquely determined high-multiplicity form of the paper’s problem. Different choices of resource replication produce different mirrors.

The proponent’s stronger formulation does preserve integral bundles, but that exposes the deeper difficulty. The continuous state is not the type distribution \(\mu\). It is the whole configuration measure \(\lambda_{t,B}\) over integral bundles. Two assignments can have the same type masses and the same aggregate type-item consumption while differing on whether every individual bundle satisfies PROP1. Likewise, \(fPO\) is not a property of aggregate type utilities: it quantifies over pointwise improvements of the bundle assignment. If one replaces this by type-average PROP1 or fractional bundles, the formulation becomes easier, but it no longer mirrors Theorem 1; it becomes either an outcome-space relaxation or a different fairness notion.

The bundle formulation also lacks a finite, population-independent configuration domain. Because the paper allows mixed utilities, an agent may receive arbitrarily many positive and negative items whose utilities cancel. A valid continuum formulation therefore needs additional normalization or a nontrivial compactness argument before it even becomes a finite-input computational problem. The paper’s acyclic rounding proof does not supply that argument: its forest is on named agents and named items, whereas the proposed limit has mass distributed over potentially unbounded integral bundles.

This is the best negative case, but it does not honestly establish the universal claim. Per-capita recurring resources are a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, and the authors would likely recognize it as a fair-allocation problem. The configuration formulation is also a legitimate computational question, even if its eventual pricing problem proves difficult. Thus the objections show that the mirror is not canonical and requires substantial new modelling, but they do not defeat the repaired mirror under the programme’s rules. A categorical “no worthwhile continuous mirror exists” is therefore not supportable for this paper.
