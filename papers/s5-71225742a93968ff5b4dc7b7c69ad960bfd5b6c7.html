What the mirror covers

The proposed mass-and-capacity formulation covers a possible extension of the paper's envy-free assignment question; none of Theorems 4.1, 4.3, or 5.1 provides a computational result for that formulation.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give a valid computational anchor for a continuous mirror. The paper’s numbered results concern topological existence and obstructions, not whether an algorithmic problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest positive result is Theorem 4.1, proved here: when \(n=p^\alpha\), every pseudo-equivariant continuous map \(f:\Delta^{n-1}\to\Delta^{n-1}\) hits the simplex center. Via the paper’s reduction, this gives envy-free segment-partition existence when empty parts may be chosen but parts cannot be dropped. The closest negative result is Theorem 4.3, also proved here: when \(n\) is not a prime power, there exists a pseudo-equivariant map from \(\partial\Delta^{n-1}\) to \(\Delta^{n-1}\) with zero linking number, yielding a counterexample to universal existence. Theorem 5.1, proved here using a cited lemma, says that for odd, non-prime-power \(n\), every Hausdorff compactum with a free \(\mathfrak S_n\)-action admits an equivariant map to \(\mathbb R^n\) that avoids the diagonal. None of these is a computational-complexity result.

There is a conceivable population-scale setting with many claimants drawn from a few complete preference types over segment partitions. But the paper couples \(n\) claimants to \(n\) pieces. Turning repeated claimants into type masses requires new rules for how pieces serve mass and how assignments respect capacity. The paper does not formulate or analyze those rules. Its continuous maps and simplices describe the division configurations, not a continuous society. Treating that continuity as the ChoCo population mirror would confuse the outcome/configuration space with the population.

So the honest positive case is only that repeated preference types could motivate a *new* continuous-population fair-division problem. The paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it, and I would not present Theorems 4.1 or 4.3 as if they did.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that none of the three anchors is a computational result. Theorem 4.1 gives an existence guarantee for prime-power \(n\); Theorem 4.3 shows that universal existence fails for other \(n\); Theorem 5.1 rules out a topological guarantee for certain group actions. None studies an algorithm, a complexity class, or an encoded input. Their basic objects are arbitrary continuous maps and closed preference sets, with no finite representation from which to define running time. Asking for an efficient procedure after choosing an encoding could be worthwhile, but it would add a computational problem the paper neither states nor analyzes.

The best population-scale version is coherent: let type \(j\) have mass \(\mu_j\), and let \(x_{ij}\) be the mass of that type assigned to piece \(i\). Require \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\mu_j\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}=1/n\), and \(x_{ij}=0\) unless type \(j\) accepts piece \(i\). When \(\mu_j=q_j/n\), this is the high-multiplicity form of the discrete matching problem; flow integrality recovers an assignment of individual agents to pieces. That is a fair attempt to make the society continuous, and the paper’s one-to-one assignment is not itself an objection to it. But the preference sets in the paper are arbitrary closed subsets of a simplex. To turn this into a computational problem, one must supply a finite encoding—say, a restricted class of utility functions—and define how to find a partition and assignment from it. The paper provides neither, and its topological results do not give complexity bounds for that new input model.

That leaves Theorems 4.1 and 4.3 as existence statements, even in the strongest mass-based reinterpretation. A computational mirror would need to ask how hard it is to find a witness for a particular encoded instance, or to classify instances when universal existence fails. The theorems do not answer either question: for non-prime-power \(n\), Theorem 4.3 constructs a counterexample, but does not classify individual instances. Theorem 5.1 is further from an algorithmic anchor: its configuration space \(X\) can be any suitable compactum, and the result supplies an equivariant map avoiding the diagonal without representing a population or an encoded computational instance.

So I would reject all three as anchors for a computational continuous mirror. The negative case is weaker if the claim is that no worthwhile continuous-population fair-division problem could come from this paper: the mass-and-capacity formulation is plausible and may merit study. But that would be a new computational project built around a topological existence paper, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in it.
