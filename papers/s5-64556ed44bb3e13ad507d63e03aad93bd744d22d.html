What the mirror covers

The proposed direction targets Theorem 1.1’s feasibility question and leaves Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 and the later results on burning, randomness, and unconﬁrmed transactions untouched; it does not yield a mirror of a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is a continuous version of transaction-fee mechanism feasibility. Let each type \(t\) specify a user’s true transaction value and resource demand, \((v_t,g_t)\), with identical users grouped into a rational mass distribution \(\mu\). A mechanism maps every reported mass distribution \(\hat\mu\) to each type’s confirmation probability \(q_t\), expected payment \(\pi_t\), and miner revenue. With capacity \(\beta\) per unit of society, feasibility requires \(\sum_t \hat\mu_t g_tq_t\leq\beta\). The question would be: does there exist a nontrivial mechanism satisfying continuum versions of UIC and 1-SCP, and, if so, what is the maximum confirmed mass? A solution is such a mechanism, or an impossibility proof.

A plausible regime is a high-volume batch market where many users have the same value and resource-demand class, so the number of users is large relative to the number of types. The authors would likely recognize this as a population-level version of their TFM design problem: the object being made continuous is the society of users, while the decision remains confirmation and payment. This would cover the paper’s “dream mechanism” question, not its separate results about burning, randomness, or unconﬁrmed transactions.

There is, however, no qualifying computational-complexity anchor in this paper. The nearest candidate is Theorem 1.1, proved here: with finite block size, no nontrivial single-parameter TFM, deterministic or randomized, satisfies both UIC and 1-SCP; equivalently, the only such mechanism always confirms nothing and pays the miner nothing. That is a mechanism-feasibility impossibility, not a result classifying an algorithmic problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar. Theorem 1.2 constructs an incentive-compatible mechanism and Theorem 1.3 proves a randomness necessity result, but neither supplies the required complexity anchor. So the proposed continuous feasibility question is a credible research direction, but I cannot honestly present it as a mirror anchored in the paper’s named computational results.

The weakest point is the limit itself. If a block holds a fixed number \(B\) of transactions while the population grows, its capacity as a fraction of society tends to zero. Keeping \(\beta>0\) instead scales capacity with population, a regime Theorem 1.1 does not establish. Also, a single user becomes infinitesimal in the continuum, potentially changing the miner–user collusion that drives the theorem. Whether the mirror is tractable, inherits an impossibility, or becomes hard for continuum-specific reasons is therefore open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest proposed anchor is Theorem 1.1, but it is an impossibility about whether a mechanism exists, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1.2 constructs a mechanism, and Theorem 1.3 establishes when randomization is necessary; neither gives an algorithmic complexity classification either. By ChoCo’s stated criterion, this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Asking for a continuous-population analogue of its feasibility theorem could be worthwhile mechanism-design research, but it is not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The suggested market scenario is plausible: in a high-volume batch, many users could share a type such as \((v,g)\), where \(v\) is value and \(g\) is resource demand. So the objection is not that users cannot be grouped. The difficulty is preserving the theorem’s central incentive question. With fixed block size \(B\) and population \(N\), the confirmed fraction is at most \(B/N\), which tends to zero. Scaling capacity with population, so \(B/N\) tends to some \(\beta>0\), avoids that degeneracy, but then one user’s report has vanishing effect on the aggregate allocation and miner revenue. The single-user miner coalition in 1-SCP no longer has the same role in the limit. Replacing it with a coalition of positive mass studies resistance to a macroscopic bloc; retaining a pivotal individual requires information beyond the population distribution. Either change leaves the theorem’s defining question behind.

The proposed “maximum confirmed mass” question also adds an optimization objective the paper does not study. To turn it into a computational problem, one would need to specify how mechanisms are represented and what finite input the algorithm receives. The paper’s results quantify over mechanisms generally; taking the population limit supplies no such representation.

So the case against this as a ChoCo mirror is strong, but the universal claim that no continuous TFM question could be worthwhile would go too far. A continuum study of batch markets or macroscopic coalition resistance might be valuable on its own. The point is that the proposed question neither mirrors a named computational result here nor preserves the single-user incentive tension that drives the paper’s main theorem.
