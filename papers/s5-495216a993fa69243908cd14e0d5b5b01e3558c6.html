The continuous mirror question

Given finite resident types \(R\) and hospital types \(H\), complete weak-order preferences, rational resident masses \(\rho_r\) summing to \(1\), and hospital-type supplies \(\eta_h\) with integer capacity \(q_h\) per facility, choose assignment masses \(x_{rh}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{r,h}x_{rh}\). Require \(\sum_h x_{rh}\le \rho_r\), \(\sum_r x_{rh}\le q_h\eta_h\), and zero assignment on unacceptable pairs. For each acceptable pair \((r,h)\), let \(U_{rh}=\rho_r-\sum_{g\succeq_r h}x_{rg}\) and \(V_{rh}=q_h\eta_h-\sum_{s\succeq_h r}x_{sh}\); impose \(U_{rh}V_{rh}=0\), so no positive mass of residents and facility capacity can mutually form a blocking pair. Each individual receives at most one whole placement; \(x_{rh}\) records the mass receiving that placement.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete resident and hospital preference types, with resident masses \(\rho\) and per-capita supplies \(\eta\) of facilities of each type. The decision variables \(x_{rh}\) are assignment masses, the objective is total matched mass, and weak stability is enforced by the pairwise complementarity conditions \(U_{rh}V_{rh}=0\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s core problem: maximizing the size of a weakly stable hospital–resident matching when many agents share complete preference types.

The named anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that in model1, every feasible binary assignment of acceptable resident–hospital pairs produces a weakly stable matching, and every weakly stable matching produces a feasible assignment. The model enforces at most one hospital per resident, hospital capacities, and, for each acceptable pair \((r_i,h_j)\), the constraint
\[ c_j\left(1-\sum_{h_q\in S_{i,j}}x_{i,q}\right) -\sum_{r_p\in T_{i,j}}x_{p,j}\le 0, \]
where \(S_{i,j}\) contains the hospitals that \(r_i\) ranks at least as high as \(h_j\), and \(T_{i,j}\) contains the residents that \(h_j\) ranks at least as high as \(r_i\). With the objective of maximizing the number of assigned pairs, this characterization lets the authors compute a maximum-cardinality weakly stable matching. The paper’s introduction also says MAX HRT is NP-hard, citing [9], but that claim is not a numbered result proved here, so I treat it as background rather than an anchor.

My mirror is Mass-MAX-HRT. An instance gives finite sets of resident types \(R\) and hospital types \(H\). Each type has a complete preference description, including acceptability and ties. Resident type \(r\) has rational mass \(\rho_r\), with \(\sum_r\rho_r=1\). Hospital type \(h\) has rational mass \(\eta_h\) of facilities per unit resident mass, each with integer capacity \(q_h\). A type includes every feature the problem uses: preferences, capacity, and any relevant eligibility or ranking information.

Choose a mass \(x_{rh}\ge 0\) of residents of type \(r\) assigned to facilities of type \(h\), subject to
\[ \sum_h x_{rh}\le \rho_r,\qquad \sum_r x_{rh}\le q_h\eta_h, \]
with \(x_{rh}=0\) for unacceptable pairs. For each acceptable pair, let
\[ U_{rh}=\rho_r-\sum_{g\succeq_r h}x_{rg}, \qquad V_{rh}=q_h\eta_h-\sum_{s\succeq_h r}x_{sh}. \]
Here \(U_{rh}\) is the mass of type-\(r\) residents who are unmatched or strictly prefer \(h\) to their assignment. \(V_{rh}\) is the mass of capacity at type-\(h\) facilities that is vacant or occupied by residents that \(h\) strictly ranks below \(r\). Require \(U_{rh}V_{rh}=0\) for every acceptable pair: there cannot be positive mass on both sides of a blocking pair. The question is to maximize \(\sum_{r,h}x_{rh}\). Each resident still receives either one whole placement or none; fractional values describe how much of a type receives each outcome.

This is the same matching question under repeated, interchangeable profiles, not a move to fractional outcomes or a different stability notion. A plausible regime is a large centralized placement market—such as a national training or graduate-placement cohort—with tens of thousands of applicants but a much smaller set of exact preference and ranking profiles, generated by standardized menus, eligibility rules, and credential bands. The mirror is credible only where that compression is real: if nearly every applicant has a unique list or every facility ranks individuals idiosyncratically, the support size can approach the population and the high-multiplicity advantage largely disappears. The paper’s HRT setting helps: ties already represent genuine indifference, so shared profiles and ranking bands fit its semantics.

I would tentatively expect this problem to be Class C, continuum-specifically hard, though with low confidence. The aggregate stability condition still has a disjunction for each type pair, \(U_{rh}=0\) or \(V_{rh}=0\); mass variables alone do not turn the maximum-stability problem into an LP. But the cited discrete NP-hardness does not establish hardness here: divisible mass may admit stable assignments unavailable in the finite instance. A reduction or a tractable flow/LP characterization is still needed.

The weakest point is the regime assumption: the mirror is only useful when exact complete types remain far fewer than agents. That is the price of an honest high-multiplicity model, not a defect in the definition, but it limits how broadly the result would apply. The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s underlying maximum-cardinality weak-stability problem; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s implementation or empirical runtime results.

It also raises three natural questions: whether stable mass allocations always exist; whether the disjunction can be handled in time polynomial in the number of supported types and input bit length; and how the optimum compares with maximum stable matchings in large replicated discrete instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent’s anchor is that Theorem 3.1 is not a complexity result. It proves that the paper’s binary IP encodes weakly stable matchings correctly. Replacing counts by masses and stating a no-blocking condition would give a reasonable continuous model, but it would not mirror a computational guarantee made by that theorem.

That leaves Mass-MAX-HRT as a new question about the paper’s underlying problem. The paper gives a real motivation for asking it—MAX HRT is NP-hard, and the authors solve instances with an IP—but neither fact defeats the mass version. The proponent’s strongest model is coherent: retain finite hospitals, scale their capacities per capita, and group residents only when they share complete preferences and the same priority class at every hospital. If those groups are small in number, flows between types can be expanded into individual assignments without losing the meaning of stability.

The practical objection is that the paper supplies little evidence for that low-support regime. A resident type must include their preference list *and* their rank at every hospital. Grouping by preference list or broad credential band alone could change who blocks whom; refining groups until those details match could make the number of types approach the number of residents. That limits how directly the paper’s SFAS instances support this mirror. But it does not rule out a different placement market with standardized menus and coarse priority bands.

Existing work also makes a blanket negative difficult to sustain. Continuum matching literature studies stability in large markets, while parameterized work on groups of similar agents treats the number of types as a meaningful parameter for maximum stable matching. The former is largely analytic, and the latter is supporting high-multiplicity evidence, not a continuous computational solution to Mass-MAX-HRT. ([Stability in Large Markets](https://www.restud.com/stability-in-large-markets/); [Solving Hard Stable Matching Problems Involving Groups of Similar Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109))

So I can argue that Theorem 3.1 is a weak anchor and that this paper does not establish a realistic low-support market. I cannot honestly argue that the improved mass-MAX-HRT question fails in every plausible scenario. The negative case is weak at exactly that point.
