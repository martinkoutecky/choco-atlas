The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of roommate types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), an undirected compatibility graph, and a ranking with ties of compatible types for each type, does a strongly stable mass matching exist? Choose \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge 0\) for compatible unordered pairs, where \(x_{tt}\) is within-type pair mass; let \(\ell_{tu}=x_{tu}\) for \(t\ne u\), \(\ell_{tt}=2x_{tt}\), and \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_u\ell_{tu}\ge0\). Define \(S_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:u\succ_t v}\ell_{tv}\) and \(W_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:u\succeq_t v}\ell_{tv}\). Ask whether there is such an \(x\) for which \(S_{t,u}W_{u,t}=S_{u,t}W_{t,u}=0\) for every compatible pair \(t,u\), or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic roommate population with a finite support of complete preference types, rational type masses, and symmetric compatibility. Decision variables are pair masses \(x_{tu}\), including within-type pair masses, plus the implied unmatched masses. Strong stability means no compatible pair of types has positive mass on both sides of either strict-weak blocking orientation.

The objection that survived

A finite clone can be stable despite one agent being eligible on both sides of a same-type blocking condition, because that agent cannot pair with themself; the mass product condition rules this out, so the proponent's claimed converse from any stable clone to stable mass flows is not exact.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 34's task of finding one strongly stable roommate matching or reporting nonexistence. It leaves Theorem 35's representation of all solutions and the related rotation structure unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can feasibility of the rational mass-matching constraints, including their zero-product conditions, be decided in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\), or is it hard?
  • How do stable mass matchings correspond to stable matchings in cloned finite populations across different scaling factors, especially when a finite clone has only one eligible agent of a type?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s core task: deciding whether a roommate population admits a strongly stable matching, and producing one. This has a natural high-multiplicity setting: a large university assigning students to two-person dorm rooms, where many students share the same roommate acceptability and the same ranking of roommate categories. The population might have \(n\) students but only \(\tau\) relevant preference types, with \(\tau\ll n\). This is a restriction on the instance regime, not a change to the question: rooms still contain pairs of individuals, and stability still rules out a pair who would deviate under the paper’s strict-and-weak preference condition.

My anchor is Theorem 34, proved in this paper: for an instance of \(\mathrm{srti}\) with \(n=|V|\) and \(m=|E|\), there is an \(O(nm)\)-time algorithm that finds a single strongly stable matching or reports that none exists. I would mirror that result with the following problem.

Continuous Strongly Stable Roommates. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of roommate types, a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\) for each type with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and an undirected compatibility graph on the types. Each type ranks its compatible roommate types in ordered ties. A compatible self-pair is allowed when members of that type may room together. The masses are given in binary, and \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size.

A solution is a nonnegative rational mass \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) for every compatible unordered pair of types. For \(t\ne u\), \(x_{tu}\) is the mass of \(t\)-agents paired with \(u\)-agents; \(x_{tt}\) is the mass of pairs within type \(t\), and uses \(2x_{tt}\) units of type-\(t\) population. Write \(\ell_{t,u}=x_{tu}\) for \(t\ne u\), \(\ell_{t,t}=2x_{tt}\), and \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_u\ell_{t,u}\) for unmatched mass. Feasibility requires \(r_t\ge0\).

For a compatible pair \(t,u\), define the masses of type-\(t\) agents who would qualify for the strict and weak sides of a deviation by
\[ S_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}\ell_{t,v}, \qquad W_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:\,u\succeq_t v}\ell_{t,v}. \]
The matching is strongly stable when, for every compatible pair, neither orientation can form a blocking pair: \(S_{t,u}W_{u,t}=0\) and \(S_{u,t}W_{t,u}=0\). In a non-atomic population, any two positive-mass qualifying groups can supply a positive mass of deviating pairs, including within one type. The problem asks for such an \(x\), or a report that none exists. There is no cost objective in the paper’s anchored task.

This keeps the paper’s central object: an undirected roommate matching with incomplete lists, ties, and exactly its strong-stability requirement. A type is complete for this model because its members share the same compatibility and ranking data; the type mass can be split across different roommate categories while each individual still receives one integral roommate. That is a plausible dormitory regime, not merely an outcome-space relaxation.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A, with an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of compatible type pairs, and the mass bit length \(L\). The paper’s reduction and rotation structure give a reason to look for such an algorithm. But Theorem 34 does not prove one: aggregate stability introduces zero-product conditions, hence disjunctions, so this is not automatically an LP. A prover would need to establish tractability for those aggregate constraints or find a counterexample to it. Natural next questions include how the continuous solutions relate to discrete high-multiplicity instances with the same proportions, and whether the paper’s rotation representation can be extended to describe all stable mass matchings.

The paper’s named result is an algorithmic anchor rather than a hardness result. Its cited NP-completeness result concerns weak stability, a different problem, so I would not use it to strengthen this mirror. My weakest point is that the aggregate blocking conditions may be substantially harder than the individual matching algorithm suggests. The mirror remains convincing because it preserves the paper’s actual roommate-stability question in a credible large-population regime; the uncertainty is whether its continuous version is tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to Theorem 34 as an anchor is that its input is a graph of named people: each person is both a preference holder and a potential partner. Repeated preference lists alone do not create exchangeable types, since compatibility and rank can still depend on who the prospective partner is. A small-\(\tau\) model must replace preferences over people with preferences over categories and impose uniform compatibility between categories. That is a meaningful restriction, and the resulting algorithm would not describe arbitrary SRTI instances. In real roommate assignments, a single pair’s ability to block can also matter even when that pair has negligible population mass.

That objection limits the mirror’s reach, but it does not defeat the proposed dormitory regime. If students’ roommate preferences genuinely depend on categories, the mass conditions \(S_{t,u}W_{u,t}=0\) express the absence of positive-mass blocking groups. They are a natural stability question for that population. Moreover, with rational masses, each choice of which side of each zero-product constraint vanishes gives a rational polyhedron. A feasible mass matching therefore has a rational realization, which can be scaled to a sufficiently large finite population of cloned types; a stable matching in such a clone instance conversely gives mass flows. That makes the model more than a fractional relaxation of the assignment outcome.

So the negative case establishes a boundary: this is a mirror for exchangeable, category-based roommate markets, not a general limit of named-person roommate instances. The proponent’s dormitory scenario can satisfy that boundary, and I cannot honestly defeat its anchor. The expectation that the mirror is tractable remains unproved, but that uncertainty leaves a computational question to study.
