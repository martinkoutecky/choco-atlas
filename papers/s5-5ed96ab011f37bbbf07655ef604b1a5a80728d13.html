The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying anchor in this paper. Its named results—Theorems 1–4, Corollaries 1–2, and Propositions 1–4—compare immunity or manipulability across mechanisms, characterize when profiles are vulnerable, or refine those comparisons in equilibrium. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem: the paper gives no result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Proposition 1 is explicitly cited from elsewhere, and it too compares manipulability rather than computational complexity.

A large admissions system with many students sharing preference patterns could make population mass a sensible modeling choice. But turning the paper’s individual, profitable-misreport question into a mass-level question would require extra modeling: a cohort’s collective report is not the unilateral manipulation studied here, while an infinitesimal student’s effect on aggregate mass may vanish. That could motivate new research, but it is not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

So there is no lead anchor, no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover, and no class prediction I can responsibly attach. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s main point holds: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–4 and Corollaries 1–2 compare mechanisms by inclusion of the schools each student can reach through profitable manipulation. Proposition 2 characterizes vulnerable profiles; Proposition 3 identifies students protected under constrained serial dictatorship; Proposition 4 compares equilibrium immunity. These are structural claims about mechanisms and individuals, not complexity or algorithm results. The paper’s closing suggestion that strategy-finding complexity may matter is a research motivation, not a theorem.

The closest candidate is Proposition 3. In a common-priority setting, one could normalize capacities and ask what fraction of students has strategy-proof admissions. Its stated guarantee becomes a protected share of \(\hat q/n\), with \(\hat q\) the total capacity of the \(k\) smallest schools. That is a coherent population-level quantity, but for this result it is just a normalization of the existing formula; it supplies no computational question for the programme to classify.

A stronger computational variant would ask how much population can profitably manipulate, or what report maximizes a student’s outcome. But the paper’s notion is unilateral and identity-specific, while a mass-level deviation changes a cohort’s report. Preserving individual priority and unilateral deviations requires carrying priority positions into the model; treating a cohort as the deviator changes the problem. Either could support new work, but neither mirrors a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against a ChoCo-style mirror anchored in this paper. The broader claim that no continuous version could ever be worthwhile is weaker: a continuous priority-percentile model might yield useful population-level measures. It would be a new question, though, rather than a continuization of one of the paper’s computational results.
