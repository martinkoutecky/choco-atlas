The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over binary-rational locations on the line, with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), apply Mechanism 1 to the occupied locations. Does the resulting facility rule achieve a maximum-cost approximation ratio at most \(2\) relative to \(\min_y \max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)} \min\{|x|,|x-y|\}\), while remaining strategyproof against moving any rational mass \(0<\varepsilon\leq\mu_x\) from a true location \(x\) to a reported location \(r\)?

The model it lives in

Types are residents’ locations \(x\in\mathbb{Q}\), each carrying rational mass \(\mu_x\); the decision is a facility location \(y\in\mathbb{R}\). A resident of type \(x\) incurs cost \(\min\{|x|,|x-y|\}\), and the objective is the maximum cost over occupied types. Mechanism 1 uses the extreme occupied locations, and a mass deviation transfers \(\varepsilon\) from \(x\) to a reported location \(r\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Mechanism 1’s strategyproofness and \(2\)-approximation guarantee for maximum cost on the general line, corresponding to Theorems 3 and 4. It leaves the social-cost, randomized, one-sided, circle, and improvement-ratio results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s deterministic maximum-cost problem on the line in the general setting. It changes the population representation while keeping the facility, preferences, and objective recognizable to the authors.

Let a location type be a resident’s coordinate \(x\), which fully determines that resident’s cost. Represent a large population by a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{x_j}\), where \(\mu_j\) is the population share at \(x_j\). For a facility at \(y\), a resident at \(x\) pays \(d(x,y)=\min\{|x|,|x-y|\}\), and the maximum cost is \(MC_\mu(y)=\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}d(x,y)\). The decision is a new facility location; the benchmark is \(\min_y MC_\mu(y)\).

A plausible regime is a city with many residents but relatively few occupied residential sites—large apartment buildings or residential blocks, for example. Residents at the same site have identical preferences and costs; their mass is their share of the population. Each resident still receives service from one nearest facility. Only the population is represented continuously.

For incentives, require mass partial-group strategyproofness: for any occupied location \(x\), alternative report \(r\), and rational \(0<\varepsilon\leq\mu_x\), moving mass \(\varepsilon\) from \(x\) to \(r\) must not lower those residents’ cost. Formally, \(d(x,F(\mu))\leq d(x,F(\mu-\varepsilon\delta_x+\varepsilon\delta_r))\). This is a natural continuum counterpart to coordinated misreports by agents with identical locations. The paper itself invokes the result that, in this facility-location setting, strategyproofness is equivalent to partial group strategyproofness; it cites that equivalence from Lu et al. at Theorem 9. Since rational masses can be represented by finite replications, that provides a concrete bridge for this incentive notion.

The paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result. Its useful anchors here are named mechanism and approximation results, so the case is about a tractable mechanism and a sharp incentive-constrained approximation threshold.

Theorem 3, proved in this paper, is the truthfulness anchor. It states that Mechanism 1 is strategyproof. Its continuous question is: for every rational-mass society \(\mu\), location \(x\) in its support, report \(r\), and permitted mass \(\varepsilon\), does the mass transfer above leave the true-\(x\) residents no better off under the mechanism? The candidate solution is Mechanism 1 applied to the support locations. The paper’s cited partial-group equivalence, followed by replication, supports extending its truthfulness guarantee to this mass-deviation question. I expect this incentive property to carry over.

Theorem 4, proved in this paper, is the constructive lead. It states that Mechanism 1 is a \(2\)-approximation for maximum cost. The continuous mechanism-synthesis question is: over all finite-support rational distributions on the line, find a deterministic mass-truthful rule \(F\) minimizing its worst-case ratio \(\sup_\mu MC_\mu(F(\mu))/\min_y MC_\mu(y)\). The proposed solution is Mechanism 1 with the support endpoints substituted for the profile endpoints. It depends only on the extreme locations, so it can be evaluated in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\), without expanding the population into its individual agents. I expect this problem to be tractable, with ratio at most \(2\).

Theorem 2, also proved in this paper, is the matching lower-bound anchor. It states that every deterministic strategyproof mechanism has maximum-cost approximation ratio at least \(2\). The continuous question is whether every deterministic mass-truthful rule on the distribution domain must also have worst-case ratio at least \(2\). The rational-mass domain contains the finite profiles used by the paper’s lower-bound argument, and mass strategyproofness includes the corresponding individual deviations. Thus the lower bound transfers to this mirror. Together, Theorems 2 and 4 give the same optimal threshold, \(2\). This is an approximation impossibility, rather than a computational-hardness classification, so the programme’s A/B/C trichotomy applies directly to the mechanism computation, while the lower bound supplies its sharpness certificate.

The mirror covers only the deterministic, maximum-cost, general-line results of Theorems 2–4. It makes no claim about social cost, randomized mechanisms, the one-sided setting, the circle, or the improvement ratio.

The weakest point is also what makes this a modest ChoCo case: for maximum cost, population masses have no effect once the support is fixed. Every rational distribution is a finite replicated profile, and Mechanism 1 already ignores multiplicities. The continuous question is still a sensible high-multiplicity formulation—the mechanism runs on the compressed support—but its answer is largely inherited from the paper’s finite-population results, rather than exposing a new mass-sensitive complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational-complexity result for ChoCo to chart. Its theorems concern strategyproofness and approximation guarantees. Mechanisms such as Mechanism 1 are explicit geometric rules; the paper neither studies their running time nor poses a hard computational problem whose complexity might change when the population is represented by masses. That leaves a mismatch with the programme’s focus, even if the population model itself makes sense.

The site-based mirror is sensible: let each occupied location be a type with rational mass. But it gives the three proposed anchors little computational content. For Theorem 3, the mass analogue of truthfulness is a natural partial-group incentive condition. The paper itself cites the equivalence between strategyproofness and partial group strategyproofness for agents at the same location, so Mechanism 1’s truthfulness has a direct route to the rational-mass setting. That is a valid extension of an incentive result, but it does not create an algorithmic question.

Theorem 4 carries over especially directly. Maximum cost depends on the occupied locations, not their masses; the optimum and Mechanism 1 also use support locations. The rule can be evaluated by scanning the \(\tau\) locations. This is real high-multiplicity compression, but it produces no pricing problem, optimization over exponentially many choices, or complexity landscape. Its value is a reformulation of an already explicit mechanism, not a new computational result.

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and its lower bound can indeed be mirrored. For example, the two profiles in its argument can be represented by \(\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_2\) and \(\tfrac12\delta_1+\tfrac12\delta_{1+\varepsilon}\). The relevant deviation moves the entire mass at one location, so a mass partial-group incentive condition reproduces the contradiction. That makes a \(2\)-approximation lower bound on the distribution domain coherent. It remains a distortion impossibility, though, rather than a complexity classification or algorithmic task.

A more ambitious mirror could ask for optimal mass-truthful mechanisms under social cost, where weights do affect the objective. That may be a worthwhile mechanism-design question: the paper’s \(n\)-dependent bounds would need a new formulation in terms of masses, and its discrete lower bounds would not settle the whole problem. But it asks for a new theory of truthful mechanism design over distributions, not for the computational analysis that defines this programme. Computing the objective or an optimal facility location from a finite support is straightforward; optimizing over all mechanisms is not a finite input-output computation supplied by the paper.

So I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here, or that these mirrors are mathematically invalid. The negative case is strong on programme fit and weak as a universal claim that no useful continuous mechanism-design question exists. The proposed maximum-cost anchors all have coherent mass versions; the case against them is that they do not open a computational problem for ChoCo.
