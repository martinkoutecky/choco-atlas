The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper has a plausible *population-scale extension*: a planner could observe the behavior of many consumers, grouped by complete behavioral-preference type, and use the paper’s identification result to infer each type’s cognitive preferences before assessing Pareto improvements. Mass would be the share of consumers of each type.

But that is a new extension, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results. There are no named computational results here: Theorems 1–7 give analytic characterizations and representation results. The nearest foothold, Theorem 2 (proved here), characterizes when a behavioral preference relation is compatible with a unique complete, upper semi-continuous, strongly upper nonsatiated cognitive preference relation. It says nothing about computational complexity or a population of agents. The paper’s topological continuity concerns preferences over alternatives; it does not continuize society.

So I cannot state a continuous problem per eligible anchor without misrepresenting the paper. The population-scale planner problem might be worth studying, but it would be a new computational question, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational problem to continuize. Theorems 1–7 characterize preferences and representations; none gives a complexity result or an algorithmic task. The nearest anchor, Theorem 2, takes one agent’s behavioral relation and characterizes when it determines a unique cognitive relation. It is a pointwise result: for a finite-support population of behavioral types, apply it separately to each type. The theorem never uses the type masses, and it defines no operation that combines agents’ preferences.

The planner example is the best route to a population mirror, but it needs substantial additions. One could distribute consumers across types, infer each type’s cognitive preferences, then ask whether a status quo has a Pareto improvement. For an atomless population, that requires choosing what counts as a strict improvement—typically improvement for a positive measure of agents. With finite support, the resulting Pareto test depends on which types are present, not on their mass shares. A weighted welfare objective could use those shares, but the paper supplies neither that objective nor the representation of preferences and observations needed to compute it.

That missing representation is fundamental to a computational mirror of Theorem 2. Its domain is an arbitrary topological space, and its input is an entire binary relation. The paper gives no finite encoding for either. Restricting relations to, say, a chosen family of formulas might yield a worthwhile algorithmic project, but the complexity would then depend on those added encodings and restrictions. It would not be a computational version of a result this paper proves. The paper’s continuity concerns preference over alternatives; it does not make society continuous.

So the paper gives a strong case against a *mirror*: its results are analytic and individual, and the most plausible population extension needs a new computational question. The universal claim that no scenario could make such an extension worthwhile is weaker. A population-level welfare project might be valuable on its own; the point is that this paper does not furnish the computational result for it to mirror.
