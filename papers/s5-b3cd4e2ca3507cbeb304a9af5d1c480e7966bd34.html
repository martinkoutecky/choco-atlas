The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that MVE has a sensible high-multiplicity version, but the paper has no named computational result to anchor it to. Its named results are analytic. Lemma 1 characterizes voting behavior after MVE is accepted; Proposition 1 gives strict utility thresholds for when each group prefers MVE; Lemma 2 reports how those thresholds behave in two scenarios for committees with \(7\leq N\leq61\). None classifies a problem by complexity, gives an algorithmic result, or presents a computational formulation that the paper computes with.

The closest result is Proposition 1. Given \(N\), \(|W|\), the conditional probabilities \(\phi_W,\phi_L\) of having positive second-project payoffs, and the conditional mean payoffs \(z_E^+,z_E^-\), it says that an \(L\)-member strictly prefers MVE exactly when
\[ z_L>\phi_L(P^{SM}_{L+}-P^{MVE}_{L+})z_E^+ +(1-\phi_L)(P^{MVE}_{L-}-P^{SM}_{L-})z_E^-, \]
and a \(W\)-member strictly prefers MVE exactly when
\[ z_W>\phi_W(P^{SM}_{W+}-P^{MVE}_{W+})z_E^+ +(1-\phi_W)(P^{MVE}_{W-}-P^{SM}_{W-})z_E^-. \]
Here \(P^x_{G+}\) and \(P^x_{G-}\) are the probabilities that a member of group \(G\in\{W,L\}\) wins the second ballot under rule \(x\), conditional on having a positive or negative second-project payoff. These are genuine named results, but they are not computational anchors under the requested standard.

A plausible *prospective*, unanchored mirror would be Continuum MVE Consent. Consider a large membership assembly deciding on the same two projects. Its society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete payoff-law types \(t\), with rational masses summing to one. Each type records whether it belongs to \(W\) or \(L\), its first-project payoff \(a_t\), its probability \(q_t\) of a positive second-project payoff, and its conditional mean payoffs \(\bar z_t^+\) and \(\bar z_t^-\). Members of a type share that payoff law; their second-project draws are independent. Let \(\beta=\sum_{t\in W}\mu_t<1/2\). Under MVE, the \(W\)-mass supports project 1 at threshold \(\beta\), and only the \(L\)-mass votes on project 2. Under simple majority, project 1 fails and everyone votes on project 2.

In the continuum, the realized supporting mass for project 2 is determined by the type masses and their \(q_t\). Define \(y_{SM}=1\) exactly when \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t>1/2\), and \(y_{MVE}=1\) exactly when \(\sum_{t\in L}\mu_tq_t>\frac12\sum_{t\in L}\mu_t\); ties reject the project. If \(e_t=q_t\bar z_t^+ +(1-q_t)\bar z_t^-\), the expected utility change for type \(t\) from adopting MVE is
\[ \Delta_t=a_t+(y_{MVE}-y_{SM})e_t. \]
The question is whether \(\Delta_t>0\) for every supported type. A solution is the yes/no answer, together with the type-by-type utility changes (or a type witnessing rejection). This decision problem is tractable by exact sums and comparisons, in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length.

A credible regime would be a very large cooperative or civic membership assembly with recurring votes, stable blocs, and relatively few distinct payoff and belief types compared with the number of members. That preserves the paper’s two-ballot rule, minority threshold, loss of voting rights, and unanimous-consent question. It does not describe the paper’s motivating German councils, whose sizes are explicitly small.

The main weakness is that the continuum changes the role of pivotality. Proposition 1’s finite-\(N\) binomial winning probabilities become deterministic majority outcomes in the limit, so its thresholds do not carry over unchanged. The candidate problem is therefore a plausible high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s question, but it is not a computational result the paper established, and it may be too elementary to offer much of ChoCo’s intended complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Lemma 1 characterizes voting behavior, Proposition 1 gives expected-utility thresholds, and Lemma 2 reports how those thresholds behave in a few finite-committee scenarios. None asks for or classifies an algorithmic problem. Proposition 1 is the closest anchor, but its binomial winning probabilities are ingredients in an analytical comparison, not a computational result.

The proposed “Continuum MVE Consent” is a coherent possible extension, but it is not a continuization of a computational result in the paper. It adds a distribution over payoff-law types and asks whether each supported type gains from adopting MVE. Given those inputs, it computes the aggregate support for project 2 and checks the resulting utility differences. That is a decision procedure, but the paper did not define that computational task or study its complexity. The authors’ question is when MVE is acceptable; turning the comparison into an algorithm does not by itself supply the missing complexity problem.

The extension also changes the role of the finite-committee probabilities that drive Proposition 1. In the continuum, each individual’s vote has zero weight, and the second-ballot outcome follows from aggregate type masses. So the finite-\(N\) pivotality terms disappear. That is not automatically a defect: the change may be exactly what a useful limit should do. But it means the candidate’s deterministic consent check is a new mean-field formulation of the adoption question, rather than a direct mirror of the paper’s threshold result.

A stronger version might let the society choose \(\beta\), or ask which constitutional rule maximizes welfare subject to unanimous consent. The paper itself points toward that possibility in Section 6, but also says that free choice of \(\beta\) creates competing proposals and requires additional constitutional rules. A continuous optimization problem could be built once those rules and an objective are supplied. That would be a plausible follow-on project; the paper does not specify that problem or provide a computational result to continue.

The paper’s own application also offers little support for a high-multiplicity regime: it concerns councils of 8 to 60 delegates, and the council’s size and quorum rules are part of the institutional setting. Large cooperative or civic assemblies are possible alternative scenarios, but their plausibility would depend on whether the same two-ballot procedure, unanimous constitutional consent, and recurring blocs fit the institution. I cannot rule them out, and the paper’s general committee model leaves room for them.

So I can defeat Proposition 1 as a computational anchor, and the proponent’s candidate as an established mirror of a paper result. I cannot honestly establish the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous-population question exists in any scenario. A large assembly with a small support of payoff-law types could make the candidate recognizable to the authors, even though it would be a new computational formulation and its limit removes finite-voter pivotality. The negative case is strong about what this paper contributes to the programme; it is weak as a universal case against all possible mirrors.
