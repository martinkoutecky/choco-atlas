The continuous mirror question

Given two alternatives \(a_1,a_2\) with uniform \(\mathcal D\), a hidden society over the two ranking types \(a_1\succ a_2\) and \(a_2\succ a_1\), with masses either \((1/2+2\epsilon,1/2-2\epsilon)\) or the reverse, and oracle access that samples a type, how many preference queries are necessary to return an element of the \(\epsilon\)-PVC with probability at least \(1-\delta\)? Prove an \(\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^2)\) lower bound for valid small \(\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over two complete ranking types with unknown masses; the alternative distribution is uniform on \(\{a_1,a_2\}\). Each query reveals the ranking of a freshly sampled type, and the task is to identify an \(\epsilon\)-PVC alternative with error probability at most \(\delta\).

The objection that survived

The lower bound closely restates the paper’s finite two-ranking-type Bernoulli construction, so it may add little beyond allowing an explicit mass-vector interpretation.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is about finding a proportional veto core when the population is a distribution over preference types. A type is a complete ranking of the alternatives; its mass is the fraction of participants with that ranking. For example, a large public consultation might have millions of participants ranking a fixed slate of policy statements, but only hundreds of recurring ranking types. The coalition’s size is then the sum of its types’ masses. Each participant still receives an integral statement; only the society is represented continuously.

I would keep the paper’s alternative distribution \(\mathcal D\) fixed as part of its model. The proposed mirror continuizes the population, not the alternatives or outcomes.

My lead is Type-Compressed Critical-\(\epsilon\) Computation, mirroring Theorem 3.4. That theorem, proved in this paper, says that when \(\mathcal M\) is finite, Algorithm 1 computes the critical \(\epsilon\) of a given alternative in polynomial time; the surrounding section uses the uniform distribution over alternatives. The continuous instance gives the ranking types \(T\), their binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a target \(a\). It asks for the smallest \(\epsilon\) for which \(a\) is in the \(\epsilon\)-PVC.

Precisely, define \(\mu(Q)=\sum_{t\in Q}\mu_t\). The answer is

\[ \epsilon^\star_\mu(a) = \max\!\left( 0,\; \max_{\substack{Q\subseteq T,\;S\subseteq\mathcal M\setminus\{a\}\\ \forall t\in Q,\ \forall b\in S:\ b\succ_t a}} \left(\mu(Q)+\frac{|S|}{|\mathcal M|}-1\right) \right). \]

A solution is this exact value, computed from the type rankings and masses. Build a bipartite graph on types and alternatives other than \(a\), joining a type to an alternative when that type does not rank the alternative above \(a\). Then the compatible coalition-and-set pairs are weighted independent sets, with weights \(\mu_t\) on types and \(1/|\mathcal M|\) on alternatives. A weighted min-cut computes the maximum in time polynomial in \(|\mathcal M|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the mass bit length \(L\). I expect this mirror to be tractable, in Class A. It is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own critical-\(\epsilon\) computation: a profile with a huge common-denominator electorate can be processed through its small list of types instead of its individual voters.

A second, oracle-based question mirrors Theorem 4.1, also proved here. The theorem says that Algorithm 1 uses \(O(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/(\epsilon\delta)))\) generative queries and min-queries and returns an element of the \(\epsilon\)-PVC with probability at least \(1-\delta\). In Continuous-Type \(\epsilon\)-PVC Identification, the instance has the paper’s alternative generator for \(\mathcal D\), plus a population oracle that samples a fresh type \(t\sim\mu\); a min-query asks that type for its least-liked alternative in a finite queried set. The task is to return \(a\) such that there are no \(Q\subseteq T\) and measurable \(S\subseteq\mathcal M\) with every type in \(Q\) preferring every \(b\in S\) to \(a\) and \(\mathcal D(S)>1-\mu(Q)+\epsilon\). The solution is an algorithm with that guarantee and a bound on its oracle calls. Replacing the paper’s random draws of named voters with independent draws from \(\mu\) gives the natural analogue of its result, with the same query bound. This is tractable in the paper’s query-complexity sense; the bound need not depend on the population size or the number of supported types.

A useful lower-bound companion is Binary Population-Shift Identification, mirroring Theorem 5.2, proved here. The theorem claims that for any \(\epsilon,\delta>0\), no algorithm can use fewer than \(\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^2)\) discriminative queries and identify an \(\epsilon\)-PVC element with probability greater than \(1-\delta\), uniformly over the relevant instances. The continuous version fixes two alternatives with uniform \(\mathcal D\), and gives the population two ranking types, \(a_1\succ a_2\) and \(a_2\succ a_1\), with unknown masses \(1/2+2\epsilon\) and \(1/2-2\epsilon\), in either order. The \(\epsilon\)-PVC is a singleton that flips with the sign of the bias. Each sampled-voter query is a Bernoulli observation, so distinguishing the two populations takes \(\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^2)\) queries. A solution is a proof of that lower bound for this continuous instance class. I expect this statistical lower bound to persist; together with the general upper bound, it makes the query problem near-matched in its dependence on \(\epsilon\), though a logarithmic gap remains.

There is a proof detail I would not gloss over: Theorem 5.2’s printed construction uses masses \(1/2\pm\epsilon\). Under the paper’s strict blocking inequality, the claimed block lands at equality, so that construction does not establish the asserted singleton core as written. Using \(1/2\pm2\epsilon\) gives strict blocking and preserves the stated asymptotic lower bound. The lower-bound mirror is worth asking, but I would base it on that corrected construction rather than repeat the printed one uncritically.

These questions cover the paper’s PVC computation and query-complexity results. They do not claim to mirror its experiments, LLM generation methods, or all of its common-ground model. The paper gives no named NP-hardness result; the relevant anchors here are its polynomial algorithm and query bounds.

My weakest point is that the paper already expresses coalition size as a population fraction and samples voters, so the oracle-based mirror may look close to a reformulation of its existing finite model. The sharper continuization case is the explicit type-mass input: it makes support size \(\tau\) and binary encoding length \(L\) the relevant scale, rather than expanding a very large electorate. The authors would likely recognize both questions as theirs, but I would frame the contribution as a high-multiplicity complexity dictionary and a compressed-input algorithm, not as a newly discovered core concept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirrors is that they mostly repackage results the paper already proves. The case is strongest for the two query-complexity anchors; the type-compressed computation is harder to dismiss.

For Type-Compressed Critical-\(\epsilon\) Computation, a large public consultation on a fixed, short slate is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Repeated rankings can be represented by types and masses, and the paper’s finite-alternative theorem is a natural anchor. The skeptical point is the likely size of the contribution: in Theorem 3.4’s flow graph, voters with the same ranking have identical neighborhoods. Merging those vertices and adding their capacities gives the weighted min-cut version almost directly. This could yield a useful running time in \(m,\tau,L\), but it looks like a compact-input corollary to the paper’s existing algorithm rather than a new continuous-optimization problem. For the paper’s larger generated-text setting, complete rankings over all alternatives also provide no obvious small support of recurring types. That weakens the practical case, though it does not rule out the fixed-slate scenario.

The Continuous-Type \(\epsilon\)-PVC Identification question is closer still to Theorem 4.1. In the finite model, sampling a voter uniformly with replacement induces a distribution over ranking types, with each type’s probability equal to its empirical mass. Sampling \(t\sim\mu\) produces the same query transcripts for any such empirical distribution. The algorithm already uses random-voter queries, and its guarantee does not depend on population size. For a fixed finite type space, the same concentration argument applies directly to an arbitrary mass vector. So the stronger oracle formulation seems to ask for the same result under a more explicit description of the population, rather than a distinct computational question.

The proposed Binary Population-Shift Identification lower bound also largely repeats the paper’s existing statistical construction. Its correction from masses \(1/2\pm\epsilon\) to \(1/2\pm2\epsilon\) matters: under the strict blocking inequality, the printed construction reaches equality and does not establish the claimed singleton core. With the corrected masses, the lower bound is sound, but it remains the same two-ranking-type Bernoulli distinction used in Theorem 5.2, now written as a distribution over types. That is a repair and clarification of the paper’s finite-population lower bound, not a new continuous lower-bound phenomenon.

Those points make a respectable case that the query anchors do not merit separate mirror projects and that the computation anchor may be technically routine. They do not establish the requested universal negative. A fixed-slate, large-population consultation is plausible; type masses give a clean input regime; and the weighted min-cut question is well-posed and could be useful. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, I cannot honestly claim that every anchor fails. The strongest defensible negative is that this paper’s mirrors appear to offer limited new conceptual or algorithmic territory, with the type-compressed min-cut result the clearest point where that judgment could be wrong.
