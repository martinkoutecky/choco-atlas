The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named complexity-class result here: the paper proves no \(P\), NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity claim. My strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that, for fixed \(I,S,q,C\), a stable matching exists for every priority profile \(\pi\in\Pi\) if and only if every individual’s choice function is weakly acyclic and acceptable-consistent. Weak acyclicity rules out a strict cycle across binary choices among acceptable institutions; acceptable-consistency rules out choosing an unacceptable institution over an acceptable one. The proof constructs a deferred-acceptance-style Non-Block algorithm: unassigned individuals propose to a \(C\)-maximal remaining acceptable institution, and institutions keep their highest-priority applicants up to capacity. This is a constructive existence result, though the paper does not give a running-time bound.

My lead mirror is Stable Mass Admissions. An instance has a finite set \(S\) of institutions and a finite support \(T\) of applicant types. Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a complete choice function \(C_t\) over the institutions. A type includes everything this matching problem uses: its singleton and pairwise choices, and its priority tier at each institution. Each institution \(s\) has a rational per-capita capacity \(q_s\) and a priority order over type tiers; agents within a type share a tier. The task is to decide whether a stable assignment exists and, if so, output one.

Represent the assignment by rational masses \(x_{t,s}\), including \(x_{t,\varnothing}\) for unassigned applicants. Require \(\sum_{s\in S\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{t,s}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\). Positive mass may be assigned to \(s\) only if the type accepts \(s\) from the singleton menu and the institution accepts that type. Stability uses the paper’s pairwise test: if positive mass of type \(t\) is assigned to \(r\ne s\) and would choose \(s\) over \(r\), then \(s\) must be full and have no assigned mass from a lower-priority tier. For \(r=\varnothing\), use the singleton choice \(C_t(\{s\})\). Each person still receives one whole institution or remains unmatched; \(x_{t,s}\) records how much of a cohort receives each assignment. This makes the population continuous rather than making the outcome divisible.

A plausible regime is a large school or university admissions system with many applicants but relatively few recurring choice patterns and priority categories—for example, applicants grouped by choice behavior and by exam band or admissions category. The number \(N\) of applicants could greatly exceed the number \(\tau=|T|\) of supported combinations. The paper itself motivates school admissions and explicitly discusses groups whose members aggregate preferences, so the setting is close to its subject. The expected result is tractability in \(m=|S|\), \(\tau\), and the rational input bit length \(L\): a mass version of the paper’s proposal algorithm could move cohorts rather than named people, splitting mass when capacity requires it. That polynomial-time claim would be new work, not a result of this paper.

The main theorem question is whether, for rational masses and capacities, weak acyclicity and acceptable-consistency still guarantee a stable mass assignment for every priority profile over types—and whether failures of either condition still yield a profile with no stable assignment. A further algorithmic question is whether a stable assignment can be computed with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), using the singleton and pairwise choice data. I expect the constructive side to be tractable. This is not a hardness-transfer case: the paper supplies no hardness anchor, and its finite-person algorithm is already constructive.

The priority tiers are the serious qualification. The paper assumes strict orders over named individuals, whereas a high-multiplicity type requires its members to be indistinguishable in priority as well as choice. Tiered priorities are a plausible admissions-market extension, and the programme explicitly allows co-modifications forced by population continuity; still, the authors or a referee might see the tie structure as more than a routine translation. That leaves the mirror credible but weaker than one that keeps the paper’s strict-priority model intact. Also, this paper’s matching algorithm is not a case where continuization dissolves discrete hardness; the prospective gain is dependence on the number of types rather than the number of applicants.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s stable-matching existence and constructive algorithm. I would not add Theorem 3 as another anchor: its incentive-compatibility result is important, but in an atomless population an individual’s deviation may have no effect on the outcome, so a meaningful continuous incentive question needs additional modeling.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only serious anchor, and it is a weak computational one. It characterizes which choice functions guarantee a stable matching for every priority profile; the proof also gives a deferred-acceptance-style construction. But the paper states no complexity result for computing that matching. A continuous computational question would therefore have to be added, rather than mirror an algorithmic bottleneck the paper identifies.

The proposed mass model also faces a structural problem under the paper’s exact assumptions. A type must include an applicant’s priority at every institution. Since each institution strictly orders named individuals, two applicants cannot have identical priority relations: one must outrank the other. Thus, in the paper’s model, exact types do not repeat and \(\tau\) grows with the applicant count. A distribution over continuous priority ranks could preserve the strict ordering, but it would no longer have the finite support that gives the proposed algorithm its compression. The finite-support repair is to group applicants into priority tiers and allow a tier’s mass to be split across assignments. That may be a sensible admissions model, but it replaces the paper’s strict priorities with tied priorities, which affect blocking and assignment. The authors’ willingness to treat that as their problem is the key unresolved assumption.

Even granting priority tiers, the proposed mass algorithm looks like a direct cohort version of the paper’s existing proposal procedure. That makes it a less compelling computational target than a paper with a stated hardness barrier or an algorithmic question the authors themselves leave open. Theorem 3 is a tempting alternative, but an atomless individual has zero mass, so their misreport cannot affect the allocation; giving a deviation positive mass changes the question to a group-deviation problem.

That is the strongest case against treating this as a high-priority ChoCo target: its computational anchor is indirect, and finite-support compression does not survive the paper’s strict-priority model without a substantive change. But I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Admissions markets with genuinely coarse priority bands are plausible, and a stable mass-assignment question for them could be one the authors recognize. The negative case is strongest as a reason to make the mirror conditional, not as a reason to rule it out.
