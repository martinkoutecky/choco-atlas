The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), a deterministic scoring rule with tie-breaking, a finite set \(U\) of voter preferences over outcomes, and an initial rational distribution \(\mu_0\) over types \((u,\pi)\in U\times\mathfrak{L}(C)\), decide whether there is a finite sequence from \(\mu_0\) to a sink. Each step transfers a nonzero amount of mass between ballots while preserving each preference type, moves no more mass from a source type than it contains, and requires every voter preference type with positive moved mass to strictly prefer the new winning outcome to the old one. A sink is a distribution admitting no such transfer; determine the decision problem’s complexity in the input parameters, including the support size \(\tau\) and encoding length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over voter preference and ballot types \((u,\pi)\). The decision variables at each step are mass transfers \(x_{u,\pi,\pi'}\); the objective is to determine whether a finite sequence of strictly improving transfers reaches a distribution with no allowed move.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers sink reachability in voting improvement dynamics, corresponding to the paper’s sink and reachability formulas. It leaves aside the complexity of constructing or representing the improvement graph from a compact game description, as well as the paper’s game and allocation applications.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is finite sink reachability decidable, and what is its complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?
  • Does this transition system admit a finite-state abstraction or a bound on the length of a witnessing path?
  • How does reachability from a supplied \(\mu_0\) compare in complexity with the property that every distribution can reach a sink?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s voting improvement dynamics, with coalitional deviations extended to divisible voter mass. That extension is doing real work: in an atomless population, one voter has zero mass and cannot change the result by switching ballots. A positive-mass coalition can.

The regime is a large election or platform vote with many users but relatively few combinations of ballot and outcome preference. For example, millions of participants might be drawn from a small set of common rankings over the possible winning slates and current ballot types. A type is the pair \((u,\pi)\): \(u\) is the voter’s strict preference ordering over election outcomes, and \(\pi\) is their currently reported ranking of candidates. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types. The preferences \(u\) stay fixed; the ballot component may change. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper itself makes voting improvement dynamics part of its framework.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, given a finite relational structure \(\mathfrak A\) and an MLFPC formula \(\phi\), Algorithm 1 decides whether \(\mathfrak A\models\phi\) in polynomial time. The paper gives the more specific bound \(O(|A|^{v(\phi)}|\phi|^2)\), where \(v(\phi)\) is the maximum number of first-order free variables in a subformula. For the formulas it gives for sinks, finite improvement property, and weak acyclicity, the stated bound is \(O(|A|^2|\phi|^2)\). In particular, the paper defines weak acyclicity as every node having a finite improvement path to a sink. The theorem concerns model checking on an explicitly given finite structure; it does not give a polynomial algorithm in the size of a compact game description.

A precise continuous question anchored to the theorem is Atomless Coalitional Voting Sink-Reachability. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a deterministic scoring rule with a specified tie-breaking rule, the voter preference types \(u\) present in the electorate, and an initial distribution \(\mu_0\) over pairs \((u,\pi)\). The initial support has size \(\tau\), its masses are binary rationals summing to \(1\), and the rule and preferences are part of the input. The rule computes scores from the mass of each reported ballot; its outcome \(W(\mu)\) is the winning set.

A move from \(\mu\) to \(\nu\) is a nonzero mass transfer \(x_{u,\pi,\pi'}\ge 0\) between ballots that preserves each voter’s preference type. Outgoing mass cannot exceed the mass available at its source. A transfer is allowed only if every positive-mass group that moves strictly prefers the new outcome \(W(\nu)\) to the old outcome \(W(\mu)\). A sink is a distribution with no such move. The question is whether there is a finite sequence of allowed transfers from \(\mu_0\) to a sink. A yes-solution is such a sequence, including its transfer amounts; for a finite sequence, the transfer constraints are rational linear constraints once its outcomes are fixed, so rational transfer amounts suffice as witnesses.

This is the paper’s sink-reachability property over the voting improvement graph, with the population represented by masses. The output space remains the paper’s finite set of election outcomes. The continuous object is the society, and the mass transfer is the natural co-modification needed to make coalition improvement meaningful when voters are atomless. The authors have a credible reason to recognize this as their problem: they explicitly extend improvement dynamics to coalitions and describe coalitional improvement paths in voting systems.

My provisional expectation is Class C: continuum-specific hardness, though that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result of this paper. Theorem 1’s polynomial bound is in the size of the explicit graph. Here the state space is a simplex of distributions, and least-fixed-point reachability ranges over infinitely many states; fractional transfers can also create new residual mass configurations. The theorem therefore motivates the question but does not settle its complexity. Nor can I claim hardness transfers from the paper’s cited finite-game result: allowing fractional coalition mass may create paths unavailable to a finite electorate. The first questions for a prover are whether finite-path reachability is decidable, whether there is a finite-state abstraction or path-length bound, and how the complexity depends on \(m\), \(\tau\), and the bit length \(L\). A related question is whether continuous weak acyclicity—every distribution in the simplex can reach a sink—has a different complexity from reachability from one supplied society.

The weakest point is exactly that gap between Theorem 1 and the proposed problem. Its algorithm operates on a finite, explicitly represented improvement graph; the mirror replaces that graph with an uncountable one and changes unilateral voter moves to positive-mass coalitions. Without the coalition change, atomless voters cannot affect the outcome; with it, the transition system is genuinely different. The case survives because the paper already treats coalitional voting dynamics, and because the continuous question preserves the same computational subject—whether improvement dynamics can reach equilibrium—while making the population itself continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 does not give a complexity result for a voting problem in terms of the electorate or its types. It gives a polynomial-time model-checking algorithm for an explicit finite relational structure. In the voting application, the domain is the set of complete ballot profiles, which can have size \(m!^n\); the paper itself notes that polynomial time in the graph size can still be exponential in a compact game description. The theorem says nothing about constructing or searching that graph from a society represented by type masses. So its polynomial bound has no high-multiplicity content to carry over.

The literal limit of the paper’s single-voter moves also degenerates. A voter of zero mass cannot change a score-based outcome by changing their ballot. If moves remain unilateral, every mass distribution is therefore a sink, and sink reachability becomes trivial.

The proponent’s mass-coalition move is the best escape from that problem. It is plausible, and the paper does discuss coalitional improvement in voting. But it changes the transition system: a move now coordinates positive mass to change the outcome, whereas the main voting definition has one voter changing their ballot at a time. The resulting reachability question is a new problem over a continuous state space, not a continuous version of the finite-structure model-checking algorithm. The algorithm’s fixed-point computation ranges over subsets of a finite vertex set; it supplies no representation or method for fixed points over distributions.

That objection does not defeat the best mirror. A distribution over voter preferences and ballots is a sensible high-multiplicity model, and positive-mass coalitional reachability is a well-posed computational question. It also stays close to the paper’s subject of improvement dynamics and sinks. The missing finite-state model and any bound on path length are open algorithmic difficulties, not reasons the question is unworthy.

So the negative case defeats a direct transfer of Theorem 1 and the literal atomless version of its voting dynamics. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists: the coalitional mass version remains a credible one, and the paper gives the authors reason to recognize it.
