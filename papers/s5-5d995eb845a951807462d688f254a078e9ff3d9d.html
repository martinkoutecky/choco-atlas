The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational classification. It does present named Row and Matrix heuristics and unnumbered running-time claims for their procedures, but it explicitly describes them as heuristics; it gives no result that exact microbribery is in P or is hard. So there are zero eligible anchors, and I can’t honestly provide an anchor-based continuous question.

The strongest prospective mirror I see—clearly an extension, not a result anchored in the paper—is Continuous GMM Pairwise-Comparison Microbribery with Consistency. Imagine a large panel of reviewers judging the same alternatives, with many reviewers using one of a relatively small number of complete judgment matrices. A type is one such matrix, including any costs or other attributes the problem uses; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of reviewers of type \(t\). A plausible regime is a very large review population using a small set of standardized judgment templates, so the population size greatly exceeds the number \(\tau\) of types.

Use geometric aggregation of the reviewers’ reciprocal matrices. Write each judgment as \(z^t_{ij}=\log_9 c^t_{ij}\in[-1,1]\), so \(z^t_{ji}=-z^t_{ij}\), and let the input masses \(\mu_t\) be binary rationals summing to \(1\). An action changes some mass of each type’s judgments. For each type \(t\) and pair \(i<j\), let \(a^+_{t,ij}\) and \(a^-_{t,ij}\) be the mass whose judgment is changed upward or downward, and let \(d^+_{t,ij}\) and \(d^-_{t,ij}\) be their aggregate log-scale changes. Require
\[ a^+_{t,ij}+a^-_{t,ij}\leq\mu_t,\qquad 0\leq d^+_{t,ij}\leq(1-z^t_{ij})a^+_{t,ij},\qquad 0\leq d^-_{t,ij}\leq(1+z^t_{ij})a^-_{t,ij}. \]
The resulting aggregate log judgment is
\[ y_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_t z^t_{ij}+\sum_t(d^+_{t,ij}-d^-_{t,ij}). \]
The cost is \(\sum_{t,i<j}(a^+_{t,ij}+a^-_{t,ij})\): mass times the number of changed comparisons, matching the paper’s unit cost per changed comparison.

A precise decision question is: given alternatives \(p,q\), a cost budget \(B\), an inconsistency bound \(\gamma\), and a desired score margin \(\epsilon>0\), is there such an action costing at most \(B\) that makes \(p\) outrank \(q\) under GMM by at least \(\epsilon\), while keeping inconsistency at most \(\gamma\)? With \(\ell_i(y)=\frac1n\sum_j y_{ij}\), the ranking constraint is \(\ell_p(y)-\ell_q(y)\geq\epsilon\). One compatible inconsistency constraint is normalized GCI:
\[ \frac{2}{(n-2)(n-1)} \sum_{i<j}\bigl(y_{ij}+\ell_j(y)-\ell_i(y)\bigr)^2\leq\gamma. \]
A solution is the per-type edit masses and changes, or a finite transfer list realizing them.

I expect this version to be tractable: it has \(O(\tau n^2)\) variables, linear edit and budget constraints, a linear ranking constraint, and a convex quadratic inconsistency constraint. It is a convex quadratic or second-order cone feasibility problem, so the natural target is a polynomial-time algorithm in \(n,\tau\), and input bit length. The positive margin makes the ranking requirement closed and avoids the zero-infimum issue that can arise when arbitrarily small masses and arbitrarily small judgment changes suffice for a strict rank reversal. Further questions include whether the same tractability survives under EVM, how to round solutions for finite electorates, and what changes with type-dependent prices or discrete judgment scales.

This proposal covers the paper’s core task—promoting one alternative by changing pairwise judgments, minimizing microbribe cost, and respecting an inconsistency cap. The paper’s introduction discusses group decision-making, and the model retains its quantitative judgments and manipulation goal, so the authors might recognize the extension. The weak point is substantial: the paper’s formal decision model concerns one expert’s matrix, not an aggregated population of experts. Geometric group aggregation and the margin constraint are modeling choices needed for this population version; they are not results proved or studied in the paper. Thus this is a plausible research question the paper motivates, but it cannot substitute for a mirror of one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no computational classification to anchor a continuization. Its Row and Matrix algorithms are named heuristics, accompanied by runtime claims for their procedures and Monte Carlo success rates. They do not classify an exact manipulation problem: the paper neither defines nor solves the optimum microbribe problem. So the proponent is right that there is no theorem-level computational result here to mirror.

Their proposed group model is the strongest extension, but it adds a substantive modeling choice. The paper’s formal input is one expert’s matrix \(C\), and its algorithms change entries of that matrix. The proposed model instead combines many experts’ matrices by geometric aggregation, then constrains the inconsistency of the aggregate. The paper discusses group decision-making, so this is a plausible direction for its authors; still, it is a new group-AHP problem, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

A more convincing version could use a large reviewer panel with a small set of standardized judgment templates, and include type-specific bribe prices. That would make the high-multiplicity regime more credible, and treating each complete matrix as a type preserves the relevant information. But it does not close the gap: the formal paper never specifies how multiple matrices produce the group ranking or how group-level bribery works. Choosing those rules creates the problem whose complexity one would study.

That is the strongest negative case I can make: there is no direct computational mirror in the paper, and its most plausible continuous question depends on a new aggregation model. I cannot honestly extend that to “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario.” Large panels of reviewers using repeated templates are plausible, and the paper’s discussion of group bribery gives that extension a foothold. The negative case is strong against calling this a continuization of a proved result, but weak against the broader claim that the paper motivates worthwhile continuous-population research.
