The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type graph \(H=(T,F)\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational partnership surpluses \(w_{tu}\), let \(D\) be a common denominator of the masses. For each integer \(r\geq 1\), form the weighted matching game \(G_r\) with \(rD\mu_t\) members of each type, all compatible type-pairs connected, and within-type edges between distinct members when allowed. Let \(\eta_r\) be its nucleolus and \(p_t^{(r)}\) the allocation per member of type \(t\). Determine whether \(p_t^{(r)}\) converges for every type and, if so, compute the exact limit vector; in the promised version, compute it when convergence is guaranteed. The target running time is polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|F|\), and the rational input bit length.

The model it lives in

A society has types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\); agents of a type have identical partnership options and surpluses. A coalition with type masses \(q_t\) has fluid worth \(\Phi(q)\), its maximum fractional matching value subject to type-mass capacities, with an internal pair consuming two units of its type’s mass. The decision variables are per-member payoff rates \(p_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=\Phi(\mu)\). The objective is the limit of per-type allocations in the exact finite nucleoli under the specified replications, not a lexicographic nucleolus inferred from fluid excesses alone.

The objection that survived

Finite coalition multiplicities and integer matching corrections may determine the payoff limit, leaving the fractional matching worth function without a selection rule for computing it; the proponent identifies this gap but does not resolve it.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 for complete type-block replications and their limiting per-type nucleolus payoffs. It does not cover the paper’s algorithm on arbitrary finite weighted graphs as a separate result.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a Replication-Limit Weighted Matching Nucleolus. Its types are roles in a partnership network: members of type \(t\) have the same partnership options and surplus with every other type. The input is a finite type graph \(H=(T,F)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational surpluses \(w_{tu}\). An edge within a type means two distinct members of that type can partner.

For each integer \(r\), make a finite population with \(rD\mu_t\) members of type \(t\), where \(D\) is a common denominator of the masses. Join every compatible pair and give it its type-pair surplus. The finite game is exactly the paper’s weighted matching game. Its unique nucleolus treats members of each type equally, so write \(p_t^{(r)}\) for each type’s per-member allocation.

The continuous decision variable is a payoff rate \(p_t\) for each type. Mass \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) means the fraction of the whole population in a coalition that comes from type \(t\). Its natural fluid worth per population member is the fractional matching value
\[ \Phi(q)=\max \sum_{\{t,u\}\in F} w_{tu}y_{tu} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{u\ne t:\{t,u\}\in F}y_{tu}+2y_{tt}\le q_t,\quad y\ge0, \]
with the within-type term included when that type can partner internally. The grand-coalition budget is \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=\Phi(\mu)\). The objective is to compute \(p_t=\lim_{r\to\infty}p_t^{(r)}\): at every finite scale, the allocation lexicographically maximizes the sorted coalition-excess list, just as in the paper. A full version of the problem asks whether this limit exists and, if it does, returns its exact type-payoff vector; the promised version asks for the vector when convergence is guaranteed. The algorithmic target would be polynomial time in \(|T|\), \(|F|\), and the rational input bit length.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: given a graph \(G=(V,E)\) and edge weights \(w:E\to\mathbb R\), the nucleolus of the corresponding weighted cooperative matching game—where each coalition’s value is its maximum-weight matching value—can be computed in polynomial time. The nucleolus is the allocation that lexicographically maximizes the nondecreasing list of coalition excesses. [Theorem 1 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.03249) is an algorithmic anchor; the paper provides no named hardness result for this problem to prefer instead.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large labor or exchange network can have many agents but relatively few role types, with identical partnership options and surplus within each type pair. The continuous object is the population distribution, and the mass-matching LP is the natural scale-up of coalition worth. The authors should recognize the limit question as a compressed, large-population version of the game they study. I would tentatively expect Class A for the promised limit problem: the type-level matching optimization and the paper’s matching-polytope structure offer plausible continuous-optimization tools. But Theorem 1 is polynomial in the *expanded* graph size; it does not imply an algorithm polynomial in the compressed type input.

The weakest point is that the nucleolus gives weight to the total excess of the smallest coalitions. After dividing by population size, those coalitions have vanishing mass and their excess vanishes too. So the fluid worth function alone does not inherit the paper’s lexicographic objective. Defining the mirror through limits of exact finite nucleoli preserves that objective, but makes convergence—and its independence from scale or parity effects—a substantive part of the research question. If a referee requires a nucleolus defined directly on the fluid game, this mirror needs further justification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational anchor: the paper gives a polynomial-time algorithm for computing the nucleolus. I would not argue that matching games have no plausible high-multiplicity regime. A network with many interchangeable players in a few roles is possible, and a complete type-block replication is a recognizable weighted matching game.

The strongest objection is that the paper’s objective is lexicographic over all individual coalitions, and the proposed fluid model retains only their limiting mass profiles. With \(n_t\) players of type \(t\), a type-symmetric allocation gives every coalition with count vector \(k\) the same excess, but that excess appears \(\prod_t \binom{n_t}{k_t}\) times in the list the nucleolus sorts. After scaling, matching worth may converge to \(\Phi(q)\), giving the fluid excess \(p\cdot q-\Phi(q)\). That function does not encode the finite list’s ranks and multiplicities. In the continuum, a minimum can be attained on an entire face, with no canonical “next-smallest” excess. Adding a measure on coalition profiles supplies extra structure; uniform sampling of finite coalitions, for example, concentrates around half-sized coalitions.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s own: define the target as the limit of exact finite-replication nucleoli. That is a coherent asymptotic question, but its selection rule comes from the growing finite coalition lattices, their binomial multiplicities, and the integer matching corrections. The fluid matching LP captures leading coalition worth; it does not by itself determine whether the type-payoff limits exist or what selects them. A direct continuous least-core problem is easier to define, but it does not automatically mirror the paper’s full nucleolus computation.

This weakens the claim that Theorem 1 has a natural continuous *nucleolus* mirror. It does not defeat the repaired limit problem: given the specified replication scheme, that problem is well-posed, concerns a high-multiplicity population, and could be worth studying. I cannot honestly make a strong universal case that no such scenario deserves the programme’s time. The negative case is therefore weak precisely at its best anchor.
