The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based case from this paper. Its numbered Results R1–R5 are empirical findings, not computational results: for example, R1 says that in every country the proportional scheme was chosen less often than the two progressive schemes combined. The paper gives no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity result for an underlying computational problem.

There is a natural population-level extension: in a large-country survey, let each type represent a complete preference over the proposed tax schemes, and let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s population share. One could ask which scheme wins under plurality, or how much mass must change preferences to alter the winner. But the paper studies neither problem: it records choices among fixed proposals and analyzes their observed frequencies. It supplies no preference-change costs or computational result to anchor either question.

So the survey setting makes a continuous-population *empirical* model plausible, but treating it as a continuous computational mirror would introduce a new problem rather than mirror a named result of this paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the absence of a computational result to continuize. Results R1–R5 report sample frequencies, cross-country comparisons, and regression associations. None states the complexity of a computational problem. That matters here because ChoCo’s target is the computational question over a continuous population, not simply representing a survey sample by masses.

The best-looking candidate is to treat each reported choice among proposals A–G as a voter type, with mass \(\mu_j\) for each choice \(j\). Then R1 becomes a statement about the measured masses—for example, \(\mu_B<\mu_C+\mu_D\). But the paper already reports those frequencies. Calling them masses gives a continuous description of the survey result; it does not create a computational problem from the paper. Asking which proposal wins under plurality, or how much mass must change to alter the winner, adds a social-choice rule or intervention the study neither specifies nor analyzes. Respondents give one selected proposal, not complete rankings or costs for changing their choices. Supplying those missing elements would define a new problem, not a stronger mirror of R1.

The burden-sharing model offers another plausible route: treat income groups as types and ask for an allocation of the tax burden. Yet the paper does not compute such an allocation. It presents a fixed menu of allocations and asks students which they prefer. A continuous income distribution could support a new taxation-optimization problem, but it would need an objective and constraints absent from the study. The paper’s axiomatic background on claims rules does not supply a computational result for this experiment.

A continuous population of respondents is sensible, and the paper’s frequency estimates naturally invite statistical generalization. That could motivate further empirical work, but it would not be the computational mirror sought here. The negative case is strong about this paper: none of the proponent’s proposed extensions anchors a computational mirror in a result the paper actually establishes. The broader claim that no recognizable computational problem could ever be built from its subject matter is less airtight; it would be unreasonable to claim that. The narrower conclusion is that such a problem would be new work inspired by the paper, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of its results.
