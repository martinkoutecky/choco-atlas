The continuous mirror question

Given a rational type distribution \(\mu\) with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\), integer-valued decreasing-marginal schedules, and supply \(K=N\kappa\), represent the main economy and each type-deletion economy \(E^{-t}\), formed by reducing \(\mu_t\) by \(1/N\). From a feasible lower-envelope UCE price state, when one such economy has aggregate minimum demand above \(K\) or maximum demand below \(K\), compute a feasible negative-objective witness for the type-aggregated restricted dual. Under Lemma 5’s assumptions, does applying the update with \(\Delta=K\) preserve feasibility and strictly decrease the multiplicity-weighted UCE dual objective?

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete bidder types, with type \(t\) represented by \(N\mu_t\) interchangeable bidders. Each bidder still receives an integral bundle, while type-level allocation mass may be fractional. Keep the main economy and one deletion economy \(E^{-t}\) per supported type, weighted by its multiplicity \(N\mu_t\); the restricted-dual variables \(q,\nu,r,\lambda\) specify a feasible negative-direction witness.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar complexity result. Its strongest anchors are instead two proved algorithmic results about the price updates used to construct the multi-unit and product-mix auctions. I would make a qualified positive case from those: the bidder population can have many repeated valuation profiles, and the paper’s aggregate-demand updates can be stated over the mass of each profile. This mirrors a genuine computational part of the paper, though the leave-one-bidder-out economies make it a less clean continuum limit than the programme’s voting examples.

My proposed regime is a repeated product-mix or multi-unit auction with many small lenders and a modest number of complete bidder types. A type specifies the bidder’s entire valuation schedule, quantity cap, and eligibility; agents sharing it are interchangeable under the model. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s fraction of the population, with \(\tau\) supported types and population scale \(N\gg\tau\). Supply scales with \(N\). A unit bidder-equivalent has mass \(1/N\); agents receive integral bundles, while the mass of a type assigned to each bundle can be fractional. To preserve the paper’s marginal economies, define \(E^{-t}\) by removing one bidder-equivalent of type \(t\), leaving mass \(\mu_t-1/N\). Identical bidders make one such economy per type sufficient to represent the distinct leave-one-out cases.

For a multi-unit instance, each type has an integer-valued valuation \(v_t(k)\) with decreasing marginal values and a quantity cap. For product-mix, a bundle is an integral vector of item quantities, with the type’s values, cap, and eligibility included in its description. In each economy, aggregate minimum and maximum demands are the sums of the type’s per-bidder demand endpoints weighted by its remaining mass. Prices retain the paper’s lower-envelope form: for a bundle \(k\), type \(t\)’s price is the minimum, over economies containing that type, of the economy’s affine price plus a type-specific offset. The action is a price update; the objective is the mass-weighted UCE dual objective. This is a continuous relaxation of bidder multiplicities, not of the bundles received by individual bidders.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved here. It says that if some economy is over-demanded, meaning the sum of bidders’ minimum demanded quantities exceeds supply \(K\), then the restricted dual has a feasible solution with negative objective: for the selected economy \(j\), \(q^{-j}=1/K\), while \(q^{-\ell}=0\) for other economies; its \(\nu_i^{-j}\) values are zero, and for other economies \(\nu_i^{-\ell}=\operatorname{mindemand}_i/K\). If an economy is under-demanded, meaning its maximum demanded quantity is below \(K\), the corresponding witness has \(q^{-j}=-1/K\), other \(q\)’s zero, \(\nu_i^{-j}=0\), and \(\nu_i^{-\ell}=-\operatorname{maxdemand}_i/K\). In the proof, the remaining witness components are also constructed explicitly. The theorem is proved in the paper, not cited.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Mass-UCE Imbalance Separation: given rational type masses, integer decreasing-marginal valuation schedules, supply \(K\), a feasible lower-envelope price state, and a main or type-deletion economy with aggregate over-demand or under-demand, produce a feasible negative-objective direction for the type-aggregated restricted dual. A solution is the update witness, including its price and offset components. The expected direction is Class A: after type aggregation, the over- and under-demand checks and the witness construction use only the supported types and their demand endpoints. The extension to rational masses needs proof, however; Theorem 4 itself is stated for individually indexed bidders. Further questions are whether this type aggregation preserves the original UCE program exactly, and how the update problem behaves with an implicit or very large type space.

A second, narrower anchor is Lemma 5, proved here. It assumes integer values and decreasing marginal values, and says that updating a feasible UCE dual with the restricted-dual solution from Theorem 4, using step size \(\Delta=K\), yields another feasible UCE-dual solution with strictly lower objective. Its separate continuous question is Mass-UCE Strict Descent: under the same type-mass model and assumptions, does that step preserve feasibility and strictly decrease the mass-weighted dual objective, and can the updated state be returned? This too looks like a Class A candidate: the paper supplies the descent argument that turns an imbalance witness into an actual price update. It supplies no polynomial bound on the number of rounds, though. With rational mass coefficients, a descent proof alone does not establish polynomial time in their bit length. Bounding the number of updates is a further question.

These mirrors cover the paper’s multi-unit and product-mix price-update results, not its entire framework or its empirical comparison of demand queries. They do not claim that the paper proves tractability of a continuous auction: they formulate the corresponding mass-weighted update and ask whether the paper’s constructive guarantees survive it. The product-mix example itself has only eight bidders, so it is not evidence for the high-multiplicity regime. A more convincing scenario is a lending platform with many small institutions whose full valuation schedules and caps fall into a small number of recurring mandate types. That is plausible for some markets, but weaker than a setting where large populations over a few types are already the natural instance regime.

My weakest point is the marginal-economy construction. The paper’s UCE prices are designed to support the main economy and every economy obtained by removing one named bidder. In a pure atomless limit, removing one agent changes no mass, so those economies—and the VCG payment information they carry—can collapse. Retaining a bidder-equivalent mass \(1/N\) keeps the mirrored question tied to the paper’s actual problem, but it also means the limit must track a finite-scale deletion. If the authors require a continuum model with no such individual-scale parameter, this positive case does not preserve their UCE payment problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither anchor establishes the kind of computational result ChoCo is trying to chart. Theorem 4 constructs a one-step certificate from demand endpoints already supplied to the auction; Lemma 5 proves that one update preserves feasibility and lowers the objective. Neither gives a running-time bound or a complexity classification. A continuous question about polynomially many rounds, for example, could be worthwhile, but it would be a new result rather than a mirror of either theorem.

For Theorem 4, the type-aggregated version has a basic fork. If each type still represents bidders of finite mass \(\varepsilon\), the construction can be copied by weighting its terms by type multiplicities. But \(\varepsilon\) then remains essential: the marginal economy deletes one bidder-equivalent. If the population becomes atomless, deleting one bidder changes no mass, so the main and marginal economies coincide and the VCG externality the UCE construction is meant to capture disappears. The finite-\(\varepsilon\) version preserves the auction’s key information only by retaining a population-size parameter; it is a compressed high-multiplicity instance, not an atomless limit. Moreover, the proposed search problem takes demand endpoints as input, so it asks for a local update certificate, not for the difficult pricing or separation computation that drives many ChoCo mirrors.

Lemma 5 has the same limitation. With a finite deletion quantum and integer-valued schedules, its continuous analogue is the same descent argument with type multiplicities in the objective. That establishes progress per round, not efficient convergence. A stronger question would need a round bound in terms of support size, input bit length, and the encoding of the valuation schedules. The lemma supplies no such bound, and rescaling rational values to integers does not by itself ensure a polynomial number of unit price updates.

The improved scenario—many bidders drawn from a small set of complete valuation schedules, caps, and eligibility conditions—is possible, but the paper gives little reason to expect that regime in its applications. The reported lending instance has eight bidders; its data do not establish recurring *complete* bidder types. Many small participants with similar mandates might supply such a regime, but that needs a more convincing market example.

This does not prove that no high-multiplicity auction model is worth studying. The negative case is weakest precisely there: the programme explicitly treats high multiplicity as supporting evidence, and an exact type-aggregated finite-population model may be a useful compression. What the two anchors fail to show is that their one-step guarantees yield a distinct continuous computational problem with a tractability result. The atomless version loses the VCG marginal economies; the version that retains them keeps the finite bidder scale.
