What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns weighted MLE for RUM location families and the boundedness of its maximizers; it does not cover the paper’s MC-EM experiments, model-selection comparisons, or provide complexity guarantees for those methods.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the worst-case complexity of finding an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal weighted Plackett–Luce MLE as a function of \(m,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • Can the corresponding normal location-family MLE be approximated with a worst-case guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a population RUM maximum-likelihood problem. A type is a complete ranking \(\pi\in L(C)\); its mass \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of the population reporting that ranking. Given the supported rankings and their rational masses, estimate the underlying utility parameters by maximizing per-capita log likelihood:
\[ F_\mu(\theta)=\sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_\pi\log\Pr_\theta(\pi). \]
For Plackett–Luce, \(\theta_j=\log\lambda_j\), with the scale fixed by \(\theta_1=0\), and
\[ \Pr_\theta(\pi)=\prod_{r=1}^{m} \frac{\lambda_{\pi(r)}}{\sum_{s=r}^{m}\lambda_{\pi(s)}}. \]
The problem is: given \(C\), at most \(\tau\) supported rankings with binary-rational masses summing to one, and \(\varepsilon>0\), return an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal parameter vector—one whose \(F_\mu\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of the maximum. This is the paper’s MLE objective with the log likelihood divided by population size. Every rational \(\mu\) can be realized by scaling a finite profile, so this is a high-multiplicity version of their problem.

My lead anchor is Corollary 1 (proved here): for location families with fixed-variance normal noise or fixed-shape, mean-zero Gumbel noise, the log likelihood is concave; in particular, the Plackett–Luce log likelihood is concave. The paper’s Theorem 1 (proved here) supplies the general condition behind it: for a location family, if every noise variable \(\zeta_j\) has a log-concave density, then the log likelihood is concave in \(\theta\). These are useful structural results for the population MLE, but neither proves a polynomial-time complexity bound.

A second, supporting question is Population-RUM MLE Boundedness: given \(C\), supported rankings and positive masses, and a fixed location family, decide whether the set of global maximizers of \(F_\mu\), with \(\theta_1=0\), is bounded. Theorem 2 (proved here) says this set is bounded if and only if, for every partition of \(C\) into nonempty \(C_1,C_2\), some supported ranking puts an alternative from \(C_1\) above one from \(C_2\). The condition can be checked in polynomial time by testing strong connectivity of the directed comparison graph. This is a tractable companion question about when their estimator has a bounded solution set.

The plausible regime is a large electorate or ranking platform with many users but relatively few distinct reported rankings. Each ranking is a complete type because the paper’s likelihood uses the observed order and integrates out the latent utilities; users with the same order contribute identically. The population objective is exactly the normalized likelihood for repeated rankings, so I think the authors would recognize it as their inference problem. Its expected classification is Class A for Plackett–Luce: the objective is explicit and concave, and Theorem 2 gives a condition for bounded maximizers. That is a reasoned expectation, not a complexity result established by the paper. Whether the generalized normal model admits comparably efficient, certified approximation is a further question.

There is an important limit to this positive case: the paper contains no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. The numbered results above establish likelihood geometry and boundedness, not running time. The paper reports MC-EM experiments, including empirical linear scaling in the number of agents, but those are not named complexity theorems. So if the required anchor must itself assert a computational classification, this paper has no qualifying anchor. The mirror is plausible and precise, but the paper does not show that continuization dissolves hardness or yields an algorithm with a stated complexity. It covers location-family MLE inference and its boundedness conditions—not the paper’s broader experiments or model-selection comparisons. The weakest point is that, especially for Plackett–Luce, this may be a high-multiplicity representation of an existing statistical objective more than a new computational landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchors themselves: neither names a complexity result. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish concavity of the likelihood; Theorem 2 characterizes when its maximizers are bounded. The paper gives no worst-case running time, hardness result, or polynomial algorithm. Its MC-EM runtime evidence is experimental, and its convergence discussion assumes an exact E-step. So these results support useful geometry for inference, but they do not show that the paper has a complexity landscape for continuization to chart.

The proposed population model is still coherent. If a large ranking platform has many users and relatively few distinct rankings, each ranking is a complete type for the likelihood: users with the same report contribute the same term, while their latent utilities are integrated out. Then \(F_\mu(\theta)\) is exactly the finite-sample log likelihood divided by the population size. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; neither individualized identity nor vanishing mass defeats it.

But that equivalence also limits what the cited results establish. A rational mass vector can be scaled to integer counts. Theorem 1’s concavity carries over because positive weights preserve concavity, and Theorem 2’s boundedness condition depends on the supported rankings, not their multiplicities. The boundedness question is therefore already answered by the paper’s theorem after this rescaling. The theorem’s graph condition is efficiently checkable, but it does not classify the cost of estimating the parameters.

A stronger, open mirror would ask for an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal weighted MLE algorithm with complexity in \(m,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\), especially for Plackett–Luce and the normal model. That is a legitimate computational question. The cited concavity and boundedness results suggest a route for Plackett–Luce, but they do not supply a polynomial-time guarantee or the quantitative bounds needed to derive one; for the normal model, the paper’s Monte Carlo method does not provide a worst-case guarantee either. Calling this Class A goes beyond the paper’s results.

So the negative case defeats the proponent’s claimed anchors as evidence of a new complexity result: one is likelihood geometry, and the other’s boundedness answer follows directly from Theorem 2. It does not honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. Weighted MLE remains a plausible mirror, and its high-multiplicity regime is sensible. The strongest defensible objection is narrower: the paper offers no named complexity classification to mirror, and the best replacement question is an extension of its estimation problem whose value and complexity remain open.
