The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(w\in(0,1)\) and finite-support rational probability laws \(\nu_\omega\) over \(\Delta_4\), one for each true state \(\omega\in\{++, +-, -+, --\}\), find a symmetric, upward-closed region \(U\subseteq\Delta_4\) minimizing \(w\,\nu_{+-}(U)+(1-w)\,\nu_{++}(\Delta_4\setminus U)\). Here \(\Delta_4\) is the simplex of masses on the four complete judgment types, and upward closure is under transfers of mass along \(--\to +-\), \(--\to -+\), \(+-\to ++\), and \(-+\to ++\); the laws respect the corresponding reflection symmetry and stochastic dominance.

The model it lives in

The society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta_4\) over complete judgment types. A rule labels each mass vector positive or negative, with symmetry under exchanging \(+-\) and \(-+\), and monotonicity under the stated upward transfers. The input laws describe population-level common environments; for finite support, the decision variables are the labels on the listed mass vectors, with precedence constraints from monotonicity and the paper’s weighted error objective.

The objection that survived

The mirror requires persistent common-environment shocks, so it is not the direct limit of the paper’s independent-voter model; the paper mentions dependent voters as an extension, leaving this population-level uncertainty as an added assumption.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper. It says that, among the admissible decision rules, the rule whose positive set consists exactly of the “good” voting tables is admissible and optimal. A table is good when moving it—and its transpose—from the negative set to the positive set reduces the paper’s weighted false-positive/false-negative loss. This is an exact characterization of the optimal rule, and the paper uses its results to compute and rank rules. It does not establish that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class; the mirror below therefore has no hardness-transfer claim to make.

My lead mirror is the Continuous Optimal Discursive Rule problem. The four voter types are the complete judgments \(++,+-,-+,--\) on \(P,Q\). A society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta_4\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of assessors with judgment type \(t\). The rule to choose is \(r:\Delta_4\to\{0,1\}\), with \(1\) meaning \(C=P\land Q\). Require symmetry under exchanging \(+-\) and \(-+\), and monotonicity under transfers of mass along \(--\to +-\), \(--\to -+\), \(+-\to ++\), and \(-+\to ++\).

An instance gives a rational \(w\in(0,1)\) and, for each true state \(\omega\in\{++, +-, -+, --\}\), an explicitly listed, finite-support rational probability measure \(\nu_\omega\) over mass vectors in \(\Delta_4\). These laws model uncertainty about which continuous society is realized—for example, across common evidence environments that affect whole cohorts of assessors. Require the one-false-clause laws to be symmetric and the both-false law to be no more likely than a one-false law to fall in any monotone positive region. For \(U_r=\{\mu:r(\mu)=1\}\), the objective is then
\[ L_w(r)=w\,\nu_{+-}(U_r)+(1-w)\,\nu_{++}(\Delta_4\setminus U_r). \]
The task is to find an admissible \(r\) minimizing this loss; a solution can be given as its positive upper set on the listed mass vectors, extended to an upper set on the simplex.

This is a credible high-multiplicity scenario: a national medical or professional body may have millions of assessors, but only four judgment types for a fixed two-clause question. The mirror preserves the paper’s core task—choose a symmetric, monotone rule on aggregate judgments to trade off the two kinds of error—and makes the society itself a distribution over types. It also gives the types a complete role in the model: assessors of the same judgment type are interchangeable for this decision.

For the stated finite-support version, I expect Class A. Each listed mass vector gets a binary label; monotonicity imposes precedence constraints between labels, and the loss assigns a cost to each label. The resulting minimum-weight upper-set problem can be solved by a minimum-cut construction, polynomial in the support size and input bit length. This algorithmic argument is mine, not a complexity result proved by the paper. It suggests follow-up questions about compact descriptions of optimal regions for continuous laws, approximation guarantees for finite committees, and which competence or common-shock assumptions yield especially simple threshold rules.

The mirror covers the paper’s optimal-rule characterization and its computation, not every result in the paper. Its weakest point is the changed source of uncertainty. With the paper’s independent voter errors in an atomless continuum, the law of large numbers makes each state’s aggregate mass deterministic; the finite-committee error tradeoff can then collapse. The proposed model retains nontrivial error probabilities by allowing common evidence conditions to vary the realized mass society. That is a real modeling change, though one adjacent to the paper’s discussion of dependent voters and extensions. I would expect the authors to recognize the decision-rule problem as theirs; I would not claim that this is the direct independent-voter limit of their model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 4.2, and its status matters: it characterizes which voting tables belong in an optimal rule, but it does not give a complexity classification for a computational problem. The paper does provide computations and software, so this is not an absence of algorithmic work. But the theorem’s “good table” test evaluates the change in loss for each table; it does not establish a complexity landscape of the kind ChoCo is meant to map.

Under the paper’s own independent-voter model, the direct population mirror largely removes the computation the theorem addresses. With four judgment types and competence \(\theta\), the limiting mass vector for each true state is deterministic. For example, under \(P\land Q\) it is
\[ \bigl(\theta^2,\theta(1-\theta),\theta(1-\theta),(1-\theta)^2\bigr). \]
The other three states likewise give fixed vectors. A continuous rule is therefore evaluated at at most four points, with its labels constrained by symmetry and monotonicity. Choosing those labels is a constant-size optimization in this paper’s two-premise setting. Allowing competence to vary across complete voter types changes the limiting vectors, but independent errors still average out. The continuous society does not preserve the paper’s nontrivial distribution over finite vote tables.

The proponent’s common-environment model is a stronger mirror: it retains uncertainty over realized society compositions. But it adds persistent population-level shocks, which are not part of the paper’s independent-voter model. For its explicitly listed finite-support laws, let \(y_q\) indicate whether a supported mass vector \(q\) is positive. The loss is a weighted sum of those labels, while monotonicity imposes precedence constraints \(y_q\le y_{q'}\) whenever \(q\le q'\). That is a minimum-weight upper-set problem, reducible to a standard minimum-cut computation. This is a legitimate continuous-population problem, but the computation is over the listed shock scenarios; the continuum of agents supplies the mass vectors, not a new algorithmic challenge. More general compactly represented shock laws might be harder, but then the complexity depends on how those laws are encoded.

So the best negative case is that the direct iid mirror collapses to a few deterministic evaluations, while the proposed stochastic mirror changes the source of uncertainty and, in its stated form, yields a small standard optimization problem. That undercuts the claim that Theorem 4.2 opens a substantial continuization programme.

I cannot honestly make the universal case airtight. A persistent common environment is plausible, and the authors could recognize optimal rule selection over its resulting population compositions as their problem. The finite-support version may still be a useful tractable baseline. The negative case defeats the direct mirror and limits the proponent’s computational claim; it does not establish that no worthwhile continuous scenario exists.
