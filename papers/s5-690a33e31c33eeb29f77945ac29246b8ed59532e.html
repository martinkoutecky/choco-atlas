The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under ChoCo’s anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorems 1–5 are equilibrium, uniqueness, and asymptotic information-aggregation results; none proves membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, approximation guarantees, or a similar computational classification. The numerical calculations and cited complexity-free literature do not repair that. So the following is the strongest substantive mirror, but not a fully compliant computational case.

The best mirror is to keep approval ballots discrete while replacing the large repeated electorate by population mass. Take three complete voter types \(T=\{t_A,t_B,t_C\}\). Types \(t_A\) and \(t_B\) have the paper’s state-dependent common-value preferences and imperfect signals; \(t_C\) is a partisan minority. A society is given by the state-conditioned mass distributions \(\mu^\omega_t=r(t\mid\omega)\), with \(\mu^\omega_{t_C}=r_C<1/2\). The many agents are citizens in a large referendum electorate, sharing one of these few signal/preference profiles; \(N\) is enormous while \(|T|=3\).

For each majority type \(t\), the action variable is a distribution \(\sigma_t\) over the discrete approval ballots \(\{A,B,AB\}\). Thus \(\sigma_t(a)\) is the fraction of that cohort using ballot \(a\); it is not a fractional ballot. The induced approval masses are

\[ v_A^\omega=\sum_{t\in\{t_A,t_B\}} \mu_t^\omega\bigl(\sigma_t(A)+\sigma_t(AB)\bigr), \]

\[ v_B^\omega=\sum_{t\in\{t_A,t_B\}} \mu_t^\omega\bigl(\sigma_t(B)+\sigma_t(AB)\bigr), \qquad v_C^\omega=\mu_{t_C}^\omega. \]

A continuous equilibrium is a limiting symmetric Bayesian equilibrium of the corresponding Poisson games as \(N\to\infty\): every positive-mass type uses only ballots maximizing its expected utility, taking account of the limiting pivotal probabilities. The computational task is to output the equilibrium mass policy \(\sigma\) and its winner map, or decide whether it satisfies full information and coordination equivalence:

\[ v_A^a>\max\{v_B^a,v_C^a\}, \qquad v_B^b>\max\{v_A^b,v_C^b\}. \]

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper’s Appendix A2. Its full content is: under approval voting, there exists an expected population size \(\bar n\) such that, for every \(n\ge \bar n\), the equilibrium is unique and satisfies full information and coordination equivalence. In the continuous formulation, the corresponding problem is:

\[ \textsf{Continuous-Approval-Equilibrium}. \]

Given rational priors, rational type masses, and the paper’s utility and signal parameters, compute the unique limiting mass allocation \(\sigma^\ast\), together with a certificate that \(A\) wins in state \(a\) and \(B\) wins in state \(b\). A natural expectation is Class A for this fixed three-candidate model: the paper’s structural lemmas reduce the equilibrium to a constant-dimensional system in the mass placed on \(A\), \(B\), and \(AB\). The theorem itself does not establish polynomial-time computation, however; bit complexity and approximation guarantees would be new results.

The second useful anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here, in Appendix A3. It states that for every scoring rule with score parameter \(\delta<1\), there exists \(\bar n\) such that, for every \(n\ge\bar n\), there is an equilibrium in which full information and coordination equivalence fails. The constructed bad equilibrium has the majority coordinate on one candidate: informally, both majority types use a ballot giving \(1\) point to \(B\) and only \(\delta<1\) to \(A\), so \(B\) becomes the only likely winner even in the state where \(A\) is the full-information winner.

Its continuous counterpart is:

\[ \textsf{Continuous-Scoring-FICE-Robustness}. \]

Given the same mass society and a scoring parameter \(\delta\in[0,1)\), decide whether there exists a limiting equilibrium whose winner fails full information and coordination equivalence, and output its mass ballot policy as a witness. Theorem 2 predicts a “yes” answer for every \(\delta<1\) in the paper’s regime, whereas Theorem 1 predicts uniqueness and FICE for approval voting, the boundary case \(\delta=1\). This is a meaningful continuous population comparison: it asks how equilibrium winner robustness changes when fractions of a large electorate, rather than named voters, coordinate.

The authors would recognise these as faithful mirrors. The paper already reasons in expected vote shares, cohort masses, and large-population limits, and its voters have only a small number of strategically relevant types. The scenario is plausible for a national referendum or mass consultation with millions of citizens belonging to a few repeated information and preference classes. No individual can be targeted; only cohort mass matters.

The weakness is substantial. The paper already performs the analytic large-population passage that the mirror would formalise, and with three candidates and three types the resulting computational problem is nearly constant-dimensional. Moreover, neither theorem is a complexity result: Theorem 2 is an equilibrium-existence obstruction, not NP-hardness. Thus I would defend this as a sensible continuous-population research question, with a likely tractable structural theory, but not as a strong ChoCo case unless a follow-up work introduces variable numbers of candidates or types and proves genuine exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithms.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s opening concession is decisive under ChoCo’s anchor rule: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorems 1–5 concern equilibrium existence, uniqueness, asymptotic winner selection, and comparative statics. None asserts membership in \(P\), hardness, approximation, fixed-parameter tractability, or any other computational classification. Turning “there is a unique equilibrium” into “compute the unique equilibrium” introduces a new problem; it does not mirror a computational result of the paper.

There is also a deeper obstruction to the proposed mirror. The paper’s strategic content comes entirely from finite-population pivotality. In an exact continuum society, changing one voter’s ballot changes every approval mass by zero. Hence every individual’s deviation is outcome-irrelevant, and the paper’s equilibrium incentives disappear. The relevant quantities in the proofs are instead exponentially small probabilities such as

\[ \Pr(\operatorname{piv}_{PQ}), \]

and, more precisely, their large-deviation rates

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n} \log \Pr(\operatorname{piv}_{PQ}). \]

Those rates are not ordinary properties of a mass society. They require retaining a finite-\(n\) sampling mechanism, a scaling convention, and often a tremble or tie-breaking specification. The paper itself makes this unavoidable: Poisson uncertainty acts as a tremble, while the multinomial model can generate additional corner equilibria unless an arbitrarily small fraction of sincere voters is imposed.

Thus the proponent’s “continuous equilibrium” has only two possibilities. If it uses deterministic masses, pivotality vanishes and the equilibrium problem degenerates. If it retains Poisson or multinomial pivotality and takes the same \(n\to\infty\) limit, it is simply the paper’s existing asymptotic game rewritten in mass notation. The mass variables

\[ v_A^\omega,\quad v_B^\omega,\quad v_C^\omega \]

describe the outcome generated by a strategy, but they do not determine the strategic incentives that produced that strategy.

This defeats the Theorem 1 anchor. The proposed \(\textsf{Continuous-Approval-Equilibrium}\) is not a continuous analogue of a computational theorem; it is an algorithmic question appended to an analytic equilibrium theorem. In the deterministic continuum it has no nondegenerate Nash interpretation. Under the Poisson-limit interpretation it is the paper’s own limiting equilibrium problem, with no new population object. Restricting to three candidates and three types may make a follow-up calculation possible, but that is a new algorithmic project, not evidence that this paper contains a worthwhile ChoCo mirror.

Theorem 2 fares no better. Its bad equilibrium is established by comparing the asymptotic orders of pivotal events under a scoring rule with \(\delta<1\). The proposed robustness problem again either loses those comparisons in the exact continuum or preserves them by importing the paper’s finite-population noise model. In neither case is Theorem 2 a complexity result. The existence of a bad equilibrium is not an algorithmic classification, and asking for a witness merely restates the equilibrium claim in computational syntax.

The stronger versions of the paper do not rescue the case. Theorem 3’s continuum of voter types and states makes the model more analytically continuous, but less suitable as a finite computational object: arbitrary \(F\), \(H\), signal functions, and utility functions have no specified finite encoding. If they are restricted to piecewise-rational or otherwise finitely represented inputs, that representation is an additional modelling decision and the resulting problem is new. Theorem 4 gives an explicit threshold involving partisan masses; evaluating that inequality is comparative statics, not a computational result. Theorem 5 changes the sampling and tremble assumptions, but still proves no algorithmic statement.

This is not an objection that high multiplicity is implausible. A national referendum with repeated information types is a perfectly sensible high-multiplicity regime, and existing high-multiplicity work would support rather than weaken that modelling choice. The narrower point is that this paper already works with type distributions, expected vote shares, random population size, and large-population limits. Its contribution is an analytic theory of strategic voting under those distributions, not a discrete computational problem whose population axis can be continuized.

A genuinely scalable problem—say, computing equilibria with variable numbers of candidates and finitely encoded type distributions—could be worth studying. But it would be a new computational game, not a mirror of any named computational result here. I therefore reject both proposed anchors. The universal claim that no conceivable follow-up could ever be worthwhile is not provable, but the strongest honest negative is clear: this paper supplies no ChoCo computational anchor, and its strategic equilibrium does not survive as a population-only continuum without either degenerating or importing the paper’s original finite-population machinery.
