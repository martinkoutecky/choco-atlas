Every anchor argued

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite value set \(V\), unknown rational masses \(\mu_v\) summing to one, and a known aggregate externality term \(a\), choose thresholds \(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_K\). In each round, observe the exact accepting mass, equivalently \(F(\tau_k)=\sum_{v<\tau_k}\mu_v\). Minimize regret against the best threshold in \(V\) for per-capita revenue \(H(\tau)=\tau-F(\tau)(\tau-a)\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population of firms grouped by value type \(v\in V\), with mass \(\mu_v\); a posted threshold determines allocation, and exact aggregate acceptance mass supplies the feedback. The seller’s objective is per-capita revenue regret over \(K\) rounds.

The objection that survived

With finite value support, exact mass queries may reveal the CDF after checking a finite set of thresholds, making the learning task much simpler than the paper’s stochastic update problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition A.2’s threshold-learning guarantee. It leaves Proposition 4.1’s hardness reduction and the paper’s finite-bidder welfare and revenue mechanisms without continuous mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • How many exact mass queries suffice to identify a revenue-maximizing threshold over finite support?
  • What regret guarantees hold with noisy mass measurements or when firms can strategize across rounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The most credible regime is a large market of small businesses buying the same non-rival prediction data: for example, many online merchants or ad publishers in a few product categories, with similar prediction tasks, values, and competitive effects. There may be millions of firms but only \(\tau\) distinct complete types, where a type records a firm’s value and its externality parameters. Normalize the externality coefficients so that the aggregate effect of allocated mass stays finite. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, though much less so for a market of a handful of large firms.

My lead hardness anchor is Proposition 4.1. Proved in this paper, it shows that finding a revenue-optimal mechanism for \(n\) items and one additive buyer reduces to finding one for a freely replicable digital good and \(n\) bidders with additively separable externalities. The paper says this carries over known hardness for the multi-item problem; that base hardness is cited from Daskalakis et al. (2014), not proved anew in Proposition 4.1.

A continuous question suggested by that result is Atomless Externality Digital-Good Revenue Design. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to one, and rational parameters for each type \(\theta\): value \(v_\theta\) and externality coefficients \(\eta_{\theta\leftarrow\phi}\) for every \(\phi\in T\). Each type represents an atomless cohort of firms. A direct mechanism maps the aggregate report distribution \(\rho\) to an allocation \(x_r(\rho)\in[0,1]\) and per-firm payment \(p_r(\rho)\) for each reported type \(r\). At truthful reports, \(\rho=\mu\), and type \(\theta\)’s utility is
\[ v_\theta x_\theta(\mu)-\sum_{\phi\in T}\mu_\phi\eta_{\theta\leftarrow\phi}x_\phi(\mu)-p_\theta(\mu). \]
The question is to compute a revenue-maximizing truthful, individually rational mechanism with nonnegative payments; the objective is \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta p_\theta(\mu)\).

I expect Class A for this particular atomless model. One firm’s deviation does not change the aggregate report distribution, so the externality term is the same under every report and under nonparticipation. The incentive and participation constraints therefore reduce to scalar screening in \(v_\theta\), with \(\eta\) dropping out; for finite support this has a direct optimization formulation. The reduction in Proposition 4.1 does not immediately transfer: its single multidimensional buyer and dummy bidders are not an atomless population of firms.

The weak point is also the model’s sharpest limitation. In the atomless limit, one firm cannot change the allocation to anyone else, which removes the externality threat that matters to the paper’s revenue results. The authors could reasonably accept this as a large-market limit of their auction, but might not accept it as a faithful mirror of Proposition 4.1’s finite-bidder screening problem. The useful follow-up is to establish how the optimal revenue and mechanisms of finite replicated markets converge to this limit, and whether hardness can persist when the number of firms grows but firms still have non-negligible influence.

A second, more direct anchor is Proposition A.2, proved in the paper using a stochastic-optimization result cited from Flaxman et al. (2005). Under Appendix A’s assumptions, the proposed threshold-update rule achieves regret \(R(K)=O(K^{3/4})\) after \(K\) rounds.

Its continuous mirror is Mass-Response Threshold Learning. Let firms’ private types have a bounded, finite rational support of values, with known aggregate externality term \(a\); the seller does not know the masses \(\mu_v\). Each round it posts a threshold \(\tau\), allocates data to firms with \(v\ge\tau\), and observes the exact mass accepting. From that response it obtains \(F(\tau)=\sum_{v<\tau}\mu_v\), and hence the per-capita revenue \(H(\tau)=\tau-F(\tau)(\tau-a)\). The task is to choose thresholds over \(K\) rounds to minimize regret against the best threshold in the support, returning both the threshold sequence and its final posted-price mechanism. With the paper’s regularity condition, I expect Class A: aggregate responses remove the finite-sample noise in Proposition A.2, leaving concave threshold optimization. Questions include the query complexity for exact optimization, approximation guarantees with noisy mass measurements, and how results change when firms can strategize across rounds.

Proposition A.2 is the stronger plausibility case: the authors already study threshold learning, and replacing sampled accept/reject decisions with population response masses is a natural high-multiplicity version. It keeps the continuous object as the society; the auction rounds remain discrete.

These mirrors cover Proposition 4.1’s revenue-design hardness question and Proposition A.2’s threshold-learning guarantee. They do not claim that the paper’s other finite-bidder welfare or revenue results have continuous analogues. The honest positive case is that a large-market version is a sensible computational question and may be tractable for structural reasons. The main risk is that atomlessness itself removes the individual externality incentives the paper set out to model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4.1’s reduction depends on named bidders. One buyer’s \(n\)-dimensional private type controls separate allocations to \(n-1\) dummy bidders, with each allocation standing for a different item. Aggregating identical dummies into a type cohort erases those separate coordinates. Keeping them as distinct types preserves the item roles, but does not solve the harder problem: an individual buyer in an atomless population has zero influence on the report distribution and cannot use an externality report to change other cohorts’ allocations. The focal buyer is still a unique strategic agent, so the reduction’s hard part remains finite.

A repair would give each cohort positive strategic weight so its reports move the aggregate distribution. Then the cohort acts as a coordinated bloc. That is a model of coalition-level reporting, not many independently strategic firms of the same type. Keeping the firms independent restores high multiplicity and removes the influence the reduction needs. Either way, Proposition 4.1 does not yield a continuous-population version of its hardness question.

The mass-response mirror of Proposition A.2 is more natural, but its exact aggregate feedback changes the problem. A unilateral report changes no mass, so the externality term is fixed across that firm’s reports; the incentive constraints reduce to screening on value alone. The resulting task is to learn a scalar posted-price revenue curve. With known finite support, one can evaluate the candidate thresholds directly. With unknown masses and exact responses, the task is a CDF-oracle query problem; adding measurement noise makes it a statistical pricing problem. Those are coherent questions, but they do not retain the paper’s externality-driven screening challenge or create much of a computational landscape for high-multiplicity mechanism design. The paper already reduces its outgoing-externality case to separate thresholds, and Proposition A.2 applies generic stochastic optimization to those thresholds.

The weakness in this negative case is Proposition A.2: a large market whose acceptance mass can be measured each round is a plausible setting, and the authors might recognize its oracle-learning version as their problem. I would argue that it is a useful pricing extension, but not a strong continuous mirror of the paper’s computational contribution. The universal claim is therefore strongest against Proposition 4.1 and less secure against the learning anchor.
