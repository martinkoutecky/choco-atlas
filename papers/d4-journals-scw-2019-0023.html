What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers evaluating the anonymous utilitarian choice rule characterized by Theorem 1, and could use the relative-utilitarian scale from Theorem 2. It does not computationally mirror the axiomatic characterizations or the other axiomatic results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive reading is that the paper’s utilitarian rule has a natural population version, but the paper has no named computational result to anchor it. Its nearest result, Theorem 1, says that a VNM social choice correspondence satisfies Neutrality, Anonymity, Separability, Archimedeanness, Weak Pareto, and VNM-Comparability if and only if it is anonymous and utilitarian: for each profile, it selects the alternatives maximizing the sum of individual VNM utilities, where the utility representation is neutral and represents each person’s VNM preferences. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about complexity, algorithms, or a formulation used in a computation. Theorem 2 likewise characterizes relative utilitarianism axiomatically. Neither qualifies as an anchor under the stated standard.

If I were allowed to offer an unanchored candidate, it would be: Continuous VNM Utilitarian Choice. An instance gives a finite alternative set \(X\), a specified utility-scale rule \(u\), and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete VNM preference types \(R_1,\ldots,R_\tau\), with rational masses summing to one. Each type shares its preference relation and the scale assigned by \(u\). The task is to return the full set
\[ \arg\max_{x\in X}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j u(R_j,x). \]
A plausible regime is a large public choosing population with many people in a small number of recurring, survey-derived preference types: population size is much larger than \(\tau\), and mass is the share of people in each type. Computing the choice is tractable by direct aggregation. The authors could recognize it as the population version of evaluating their anonymous utilitarian SCC.

Its weakness is decisive for this assignment: the model takes the utility scales as specified, while the paper’s distinctive contribution is to characterize aggregation from preference primitives without assuming utilities have already been measured. And the paper does not study the computational task of evaluating the rule. So this is a sensible adjacent question, but not a continuous mirror supported by a named result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes a rule by axioms; Theorems 2 and 3 and Proposition 1 are also characterizations. None studies an algorithm, a complexity question, or a computational problem with an input and objective. The paper’s variable electorate and Archimedeanness condition concern how the rule behaves under replication; they do not make the paper a study of high-multiplicity computation.

The proposed continuous choice problem is well posed, and its population story is plausible: many people may fall into a small number of VNM preference types. But if the utility scale \(u\) is supplied, choosing \(\arg\max_{x\in X}\sum_j \mu_j u(R_j,x)\) is just weighted aggregation. If the scale is meant to come from the preference relations, Theorem 1 characterizes a family of admissible scales rather than selecting one. Theorem 2’s relative-utilitarian normalization selects a particular scale, but computing the resulting choice still does not engage with the paper’s characterization.

A stronger follow-on could ask which alternatives can win across the scales allowed by Theorem 1, or how much mass must change type to make a target alternative win. Those might be worthwhile computational questions. But the first adds a scale-robustness problem, and the second adds an intervention and cost model; neither is a computational result in this paper. Calling either a mirror would make almost any axiomatic rule a launchpad for a continuous computational project.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated gate: there is no named computational result here to anchor a mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim that no useful continuous project could ever be built from the paper. Continuum populations and mass aggregation are quite natural in this setting; what is missing is a computational question the paper itself poses.
