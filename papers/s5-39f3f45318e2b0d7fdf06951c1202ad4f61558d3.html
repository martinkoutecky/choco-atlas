Why no mirror

Theorem 1 concerns individually identified vehicles making unilateral coalition moves. The proposed continuous model instead permits coordinated moves by positive mass, changing the stability concept and creating a different exchangeable game. That may be a worthwhile extension, but it is not a continuous analogue of the anchored result under the strategic-model gate.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targeted Theorem 1 alone; it leaves the Bayesian-core conditions, DTMC analysis, belief updates, and simulation findings without a continuous counterpart.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible anchor is Theorem 1, proved in Section 4.1. It states that Algorithm 1 converges to a Nash-stable coalitional structure: starting from a partition of the mobile nodes, the procedure considers a node’s move to another coalition or to a singleton. A move requires the node to strictly prefer its expected payoff after moving, and the destination coalition’s members must accept it under the paper’s preference rule. The endpoint is a partition where no node has an allowed profitable move. This is a named algorithmic result, though it gives no running-time or complexity-class bound. The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result to anchor a complexity comparison.

My lead mirror would be Mass-Stable Packet-Delivery Coalition Construction. Consider a fleet with a large number \(N\) of vehicles but a small set \(K\) of complete types. A type includes everything relevant to this game: mobility and encounter profile, packet demand, relay costs, payoff weights, time-to-live, cooperation behavior, and the beliefs used to evaluate other types. Let \(\mu_k\) be type \(k\)’s share of the fleet, with \(\sum_{k\in K}\mu_k=1\). This fits, for example, a large delivery or transit fleet with many vehicles in each of a relatively small number of route, shift, and behavior classes.

For a coalition with type-mass composition \(z\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{K}\), let \(U_k(z)\) be a type-\(k\) vehicle’s expected utility minus relay cost. Define it using the paper’s delivery-delay, cost, and belief calculations, with an exchangeable contact rate: a type-\(k\) vehicle encounters type-\(\ell\) mass in its coalition at a rate proportional to \(z_\ell\). Each vehicle still handles integral packets; \(z_\ell\) is a population share, and \(U_k\) is a representative vehicle’s expected payoff.

A continuous coalition structure is a finite or countable collection of coalition profiles \(z^1,z^2,\ldots\) satisfying \(\sum_a z^a=\mu\). An admissible move takes some mass \(\delta>0\) of type \(k\) from a source coalition \(z^a\) and places it in a destination coalition \(z^b\), or in a new coalition. The move is profitable if the moving mass strictly gains, for example \(U_k(z^b+\delta e_k)>U_k(z^a)\). For an existing destination, it must also be accepted: every incumbent type \(j\) with \(z^b_j>0\) must have \(U_j(z^b+\delta e_k)\ge U_j(z^b)\). A structure is mass-Nash-stable if no such move exists.

The computational question is: given rational type masses, rational packet-delivery primitives, the resulting type-level payoff functions, and an initial feasible structure, find a finite sequence of admissible moves ending in a mass-Nash-stable structure, or determine that no such sequence exists. A solution is the sequence and its stable endpoint. The question is about the underlying packet-delivery coalition game; any formula or circuit used to evaluate \(U_k\) is just its input representation.

I think the authors could recognize this as their problem: it keeps cooperative packet delivery, uncertainty about behavior, expected net payoffs, coalitions, individual incentives, and destination-member acceptance. The type-level encounter rates and movable mass are substantive co-modifications needed for the high-multiplicity regime. They do not replace the population with continuous outcomes or probabilities.

I would expect the new problem to be continuum-specifically difficult, though that is a research conjecture, not a hardness claim. Theorem 1’s proof uses a finite set of coalitional structures; a continuum of possible mass profiles and transfer amounts removes that finite-state argument. There is no discrete hardness result in the paper to transfer. The natural follow-up questions are whether a suitable potential function guarantees convergence, whether stable structures always exist, and whether construction is tractable in \(K\), the number of initial coalitions, and input bit length.

The weakness is that this is not an exact limit of the paper’s model: its encounter rates are node-specific, and its delivery analysis tracks finite-node packet states. The mirror needs exchangeable type-level contacts and a mean-field payoff calculation. More seriously, the named theorem promises convergence but says nothing about efficiency, and its argument supplies no continuous convergence principle. So the positive case is for a plausible new computational question that extends Theorem 1’s coalition-formation problem—not for a claim that the paper already establishes a tractable continuous theory.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 1: Algorithm 1 is claimed to converge to a Nash-stable coalitional structure. But it is a qualitative convergence claim, not a complexity result. The paper gives no runtime bound or input-size model. And the proof, as written, invokes the finite number of partitions without showing that the process cannot revisit a partition; finiteness alone does not establish termination. That leaves little computational content to carry into a continuous mirror.

More fundamentally, the theorem concerns individually identified vehicles. Their payoffs depend on pairwise encounter rates, packet-delivery costs, and private beliefs about particular other vehicles. In the generic network described by the paper, a complete type would have to capture much of a vehicle’s interaction profile and history. The two behavior labels, well behaved and misbehaving, are not enough. A fixed small set of types requires extra symmetry assumptions, such as a common contact-rate kernel between type classes and shared beliefs. Those assumptions could describe a plausible fleet, but they create a new anonymous network model rather than relax the paper’s identity-based one.

There is also a strategic problem with the proposed mass stability. In a population normalized to total mass one, a single vehicle has zero mass. Its departure does not change the coalition profile or the type-mass encounter rates. So a faithful unilateral Nash test loses the composition effects that drive the paper’s incentives. Letting a positive mass \(\delta\) move restores those effects, but now the deviation is a coordinated block move by many identical vehicles, not an individual move of Theorem 1. The paper’s finite-partition convergence argument says nothing about such moves; mass coalitions have continuously many possible profiles and transfer sizes.

The strongest salvage is an explicitly exchangeable fleet with finitely many route, demand, and behavior classes, type-pair contact rates, and common type-level beliefs. One could define a mean-field packet-delivery game and study stable allocations of mass across coalitions. That is coherent, and the authors might recognize it as a useful extension. But it needs both the anonymity assumptions and a new stability concept. If the payoff functions are supplied as arbitrary circuits or oracles, the proposed search problem also lacks a meaningful complexity measure until their representation and precision are fixed.

So I would argue against treating this paper as a strong ChoCo mirror: its anchor is not a complexity result, and the direct high-multiplicity move changes what counts as a deviation. The negative case is weakest against the deliberately redesigned exchangeable-fleet model. I cannot honestly rule out that model being worthwhile; I can say the proponent has not shown it is a continuous mirror of the result this paper actually proves.
