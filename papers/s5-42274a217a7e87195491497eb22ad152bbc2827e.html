The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates with a fixed tie-breaking order, a scale \(q\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on a listed set \(T\subseteq\Pi_C\), where \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/q\) and \(\sum_{\pi\in T}n_\pi=q\), run STV by eliminating the candidate with least first-place mass among the remaining candidates. Define \(d(\mu)=\inf_{\nu:\operatorname{STV}(\nu)\ne\operatorname{STV}(\mu)}\frac12\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_C}|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|\), with \(\nu\) ranging over distributions on all rankings. For each fixed \(\zeta\in(0,1)\), distinguish the promised cases \(d(\mu)\le 1/q\) and \(d(\mu)\ge c_\zeta q^{-\zeta}\), for a suitable constant \(c_\zeta>0\).

The model it lives in

Ranking types \(\pi\in\Pi_C\) carry mass \(\mu_\pi\); \(T\) is the input support, with size \(\tau\), and masses are rational with bit length \(L\). Transfer variables \(x_{\pi,\pi'}\ge0\) move mass from input types to rankings \(\pi'\), producing a distribution \(\nu\). Minimize total moved mass, equivalently \(\frac12\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_C}|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|\), subject to \(\operatorname{STV}(\nu)\ne\operatorname{STV}(\mu)\); the gap question uses the electorate scale \(q\) explicitly.

What the mirror covers

Covers the STV margin-of-victory and specified-target bribery hardness questions from Corollaries B.2 and B.3. It does not directly continuize the paper’s central-DP or LDP guarantees.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the elimination-order gaps in Proposition B.5 be extended to arbitrary fractional mass transfers so the reduction gives a gap of order \(q^{-\zeta}\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s STV margin of victory. A ranking is a type, and \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of the electorate holding ranking \(\pi\). STV on a distribution eliminates the candidate with the least first-place mass among the remaining candidates, using the paper’s fixed tie-breaking order. A mass transfer \(x_{\pi,\pi'}\) moves voters of type \(\pi\) to type \(\pi'\), at unit cost per unit of mass moved. The resulting society \(\nu\) has total moved mass \(\tfrac12\sum_\pi|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|\).

This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for a large STV election with repeated ballot templates: party slates, organizational blocs, or other groups sharing complete rankings. In the paper’s hardness construction, the electorate size \(n\) grows much faster than the number \(\tau\) of ranking types: there are \(O(N)\) ballot templates and \(m=4N+2\) candidates, while repetition amplifies the electorate. The template ranking groups are the types; the replication is the high multiplicity. That is a plausible regime for the mirror, even though the reduction itself is synthetic.

My lead anchor is Corollary B.2, proved in this paper. It says that, for every constant \(\zeta>0\), it is NP-hard to approximate the STV margin of victory within a factor of \(n^{1-\zeta}\). The underlying reduction is proved here in Theorem B.1, using a cited NP-hardness result for bounded-degree 3SAT. Its key gap has a profile with a designated alternate winner \(a\): in the YES case, \(a\) can be made the winner by changing at most one vote; in the NO case, changing at least \(n^{1-\zeta}\) votes is necessary to change the outcome. The reduction has \(m\le n^\zeta\).

The continuous question I would hand a prover is Gap-MoV-STV\(_\infty\). Its input is a candidate set and tie-breaking order, a finite support \(T\) of rankings, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), given with a common denominator \(N\). The denominator records the scale of the encoded electorate; feasible mass transfers may use arbitrary real amounts. Define
\[ d_{\mathrm{MoV}}(\mu) =\inf_{\nu:\operatorname{STV}(\nu)\ne\operatorname{STV}(\mu)} \frac12\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_C}|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|. \]
For each fixed \(\zeta>0\), distinguish the promised cases \(d_{\mathrm{MoV}}(\mu)\le 1/N\) and \(d_{\mathrm{MoV}}(\mu)\ge c_\zeta N^{-\zeta}\), for a suitable constant \(c_\zeta>0\). A solution is the correct case. This is a continuous mass-robustness problem: the answer is the infimum mass whose rankings must change to alter the winner. I would expect this gap problem to be NP-hard, corresponding to hardness transferring through the candidate agenda and STV elimination structure.

A second, distinct anchor is Corollary B.3, also proved here as an immediate consequence of Theorem B.1. It says that for every constant \(\zeta>0\), it is NP-hard to approximate STV constructive bribery within a factor of \(n^{1-\zeta}\). Constructive bribery asks for the minimum number of rankings to change to make a specified candidate win. Its continuous mirror, Gap-Target-Bribery-STV\(_\infty\), has the same distribution input and a specified target \(a\ne\operatorname{STV}(\mu)\). Define
\[ d_a(\mu) =\inf_{\nu:\operatorname{STV}(\nu)=a} \frac12\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_C}|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|. \]
For fixed \(\zeta>0\), ask the prover to distinguish \(d_a(\mu)\le 1/N\) from \(d_a(\mu)\ge c_\zeta N^{-\zeta}\). The answer again is the correct promised case. I consider this a useful second question because it preserves the specified-target feature of bribery; the margin-of-victory question remains my lead because MoV is the paper’s central decisiveness measure.

These are close to the authors’ problems: the intervention is still changing voters’ complete rankings, and the objective is still the number or fraction of voters needed to change an STV outcome. The paper itself studies MoV as a computational problem, and the target version is its constructive bribery formulation. The continuous version makes the per-capita quantity explicit, while allowing mass to split between rankings.

The main proof obligation is also the weakest point in the case. The paper proves its gap against integer voter changes; a continuous transfer may split a type and reach an elimination boundary between two integer profiles. So discrete hardness does not automatically imply hardness of the continuous problem. Still, the reduction’s replication is designed to create large score gaps at key elimination stages. Changing mass \(\delta\) can change any one round’s score by at most \(\delta\). That makes a fractional robustness extension plausible: the YES case costs at most \(1/N\), while the replicated NO case should remain stable up to mass on the order of \(N^{-\zeta}\). Establishing that every possible fractional perturbation preserves the relevant elimination sequence is the central step a prover must supply. If it holds, the problem looks like Class B: the hardness comes from the alternatives and elimination order, while repetition amplifies the gap. It is not a claim that the discrete and continuous optima are identical.

This case covers the paper’s STV margin-of-victory and target-bribery hardness results, especially the computational primitive behind its central-DP STV barrier. It does not continuize the paper’s privacy guarantees themselves. The anchor is the underlying computational robustness problem, which already has a direct per-capita interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong case that defeats the STV mirror. Corollaries B.2 and B.3 both descend from Theorem B.1: one asks for margin of victory, the other for a specified target’s bribery distance. Those are closely related continuous questions, so the paper offers one substantial anchor rather than two independent ones.

The proposed gap promise needs care about its scale. The fraction \(1/N\) means something only if \(N\) is part of the instance, for example the least common denominator of the input masses. Given just a distribution \(\mu\), there is no separate electorate size. Defining the mirror directly by the minimum moved mass \(d_{\mathrm{MoV}}(\mu)\) avoids that ambiguity.

The stronger objection is that Theorem B.1 proves a gap for integral vote changes; fractional transfers could cross an elimination boundary more cheaply. The paper’s reduction appears to blunt this objection. In Proposition B.5, the constructed election has score gaps of order \(t\) that preserve the relevant elimination sequence under fewer than \(t/2\) ranking changes. Moving mass \(\delta\) can change a restricted plurality score by at most \(\delta\), so a gap \(t\) in an \(n\)-voter profile appears to remain stable for \(\delta<t/(2n)\). The YES instance uses one changed vote, giving a transfer of mass \(1/n\). That suggests the reduction may extend to the proposed continuous gap with little additional work. The fractional-relaxation concern identifies a proof obligation, but it does not defeat the best version of the mirror.

The regime is also credible: many voters can share complete rankings, and the reduction itself uses repeated ranking templates. Existing computational work on STV margins studies ballot manipulations in finite elections; it does not appear to answer the continuous-population problem. For example, [Blom, Stuckey, and Teague’s STV margin work](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/ijoc.2018.0853) presents algorithms for bounds and exact margins on small instances, not complexity over a society given as a distribution.

The paper’s central-DP guarantees raise a separate modeling difficulty: a continuous society alone does not specify the single-person adjacency used by differential privacy. That limits how much of the paper’s privacy contribution this mirror captures. It still does not remove the underlying STV robustness question, which the two corollaries state directly. My honest negative case is therefore weak: the proposed questions are coherent, the population regime is plausible, and the reduction looks capable of supporting them.
