The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a valid named computational anchor in this paper. It gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational formulation that it then uses to compute a result. The nearest numbered results are analytical equilibrium claims, and calling them computational results would overstate the paper.

For completeness, Lemma 1 (proved here) says that in a weighted majority game, if there are no veto players and no player has recognition probability \(1\), a player earns strictly more as proposer than as a coalition partner in an SSPE; the no-veto condition can be replaced by \(\delta<1\). Proposition 2 (proved here) says that for \([5;3,2,2,1]\), recognition probabilities \((3/8,2/8,2/8,1/8)\), and \(\delta=1\), every SSPE has expected payoff vector \((5/14,4/14,4/14,1/14)\). Proposition 3 (proved here) gives \((3/8,2/8,2/8,1/8)\) for the same game with equal recognition probabilities. These establish equilibrium and comparative-static facts for specific finite games; they do not assert that a computational problem is tractable or hard.

There is a plausible *new* question suggested by the mechanism: take a large legislature with a few recurring member types, where a type specifies voting weight, preferences, and discount factor. Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population share and \(\rho_t\) its aggregate recognition probability. Ask whether, in the resulting stationary bargaining model, shifting recognition mass from one type to another can increase the donor type’s equilibrium payoff. That preserves the paper’s indirect-effect question. But it is not anchored by a computational result in this paper, and the continuum model would need care: an individual proposer becomes infinitesimal, so the proposer advantage and the donation effect may change. That is the strongest direction I can honestly offer, but it does not meet the requested anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against a mirror is the one the proponent concedes: the paper states no computational problem and proves no complexity result. Lemma 1 characterizes proposer and coalition partner payoffs in an SSPE. Propositions 2 and 3 give equilibrium payoffs for one four-party game under two recognition distributions. The paper solves small systems of equations; it does not study an input-size-dependent task, an algorithm, or a complexity bound. Asking how to compute equilibrium in a new continuous-type bargaining model could be worthwhile, but it would add a research agenda rather than mirror a named computational result here.

The best population version of Lemma 1 would use a large legislature with recurring member types and ask whether a type’s proposer premium stays positive. That changes what the lemma is about. In an atomless population, a fixed member has zero probability of being selected to propose. If the selected member receives the budget surplus, that rent goes to a zero-mass individual and does not aggregate into type payoffs. If the rent is instead pooled across the member’s type, the model needs an internal sharing rule; if the type itself becomes the proposer, it is again a finite set of party actors. Each repair adds an institutional choice the paper does not make.

Propositions 2 and 3 have the same problem in a sharper form. Their donation comparison changes \(\theta\), the recognition lottery, while keeping the four players and their weighted coalition game fixed. A continuous \(\theta\) is already a continuous parameter, but it does not make the population continuous. Replicating legislators by type would create a different coalition game, with many more possible coalitions and different actors receiving proposal rents. Keeping one actor per type preserves the paper’s finite-player structure. Either way, the proposition’s comparison does not itself supply a continuous-population result to mirror.

The negative case is weakest as a claim that no worthwhile model could ever be built. A large assembly with many similar members, a rule for distributing proposal rents by type, and an explicit computational question might make a sensible new study. But it would require the authors to choose a new payoff and aggregation model before there is a well-posed mirror. On the evidence in this paper, I would exclude it from the continuization programme: its central results concern bargaining among a few identifiable parties, and none anchors the computational landscape the programme is meant to chart.
