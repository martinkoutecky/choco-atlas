What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation covers Corollary 5.2 alone; it leaves the paper’s other choice rules and results, including Theorem 7.1, aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is Minimax Winner Determination over a continuous preference population. Let \(A\) be the options and let \(T\) be the support of complete ballot types, each specifying a voter’s ranking. Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), set \(v(p_{xy})=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t v_t(p_{xy})\), where \(v_t(p_{xy})\) records type \(t\)’s support for \(x\) over \(y\). Return the set
\[ \arg\min_{x\in A}\max_{y\ne x}v(p_{yx}). \]
Here mass is the fraction of voters of each type; the decision is which options win, and the objective is to minimize each option’s greatest pairwise opposition. A plausible regime is a large election with millions of voters but relatively few distinct ballots, so the population size is much larger than \(|T|\). The rule can be computed from the type masses and pairwise scores in polynomial time.

The nearest named anchor is Corollary 5.2, proved in this paper: with the initial values \(v(s_x)=v(\bar{s}_x)=0\) from (31), the supremacy winners are exactly the options \(x\in A\) minimizing \(\tau_x=\max_{y\ne x}v(p_{yx})\); the authors call this the minimax rule. It gives an exact winner characterization that makes the continuous problem straightforward to compute. The authors would recognize the connection: §3 already defines collective degrees of belief as weighted sums of individual opinions, and §5.1 derives the rule from the Llull matrix.

But Corollary 5.2 does not assert a complexity result such as membership in P; that is an algorithmic consequence of its formula. The paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for a firm anchor. Its other named results characterize rules or properties—for example, Theorem 7.1 proves monotonicity of the goodness method—not complexity classifications. So this is, at best, a weak lead rather than a qualifying anchor.

The weakest point is also decisive: the paper’s model already uses fractional aggregate opinions. A continuous-population version of minimax winner determination largely restates its existing formulation, rather than asking what happens when a discrete computational problem is continuized. The case is strongest as evidence that this kind of population regime is sensible; it does not establish the programme’s missing computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 5.2 is the only anchor, and it characterizes a rule rather than asserting the complexity of a computational problem. The strongest mirror would ask for the minimax winner set from rational masses on ranking types. But those masses enter only through the Llull entries
\[ v(p_{yx})=\sum_{t:\,y\succ_t x}\mu_t, \]
after which the corollary already specifies the answer: minimize \(\max_{y\ne x}v(p_{yx})\). Summing the type contributions and comparing the resulting scores takes polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Stating that as a formal complexity theorem would add bookkeeping, not a new computational question.

This is a direct collision for that mirror. The paper already aggregates individual opinions by weights in (15), treats the resulting normalized fractions as degrees of belief, and derives the minimax rule from those aggregate values. Representing the same input as a distribution over complete ballot types is a different encoding of information the rule does not use: its output depends only on pairwise support. The authors would recognize the continuous question, and their result already gives its evaluator.

One could add mass-based bribery or campaigning, with costs for changing ballot types. Such a model may be sensible, but this paper specifies no intervention, costs, or target; adding them creates a new computational problem rather than continuizing one of its results. So the negative case is strong for the proposed anchor, though it cannot rule out every worthwhile extension someone might build around these rules.
