The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\) binary events and a stationary trader population with rational type masses \(\mu_r\), where type \(r\) has a fixed contract \(S_r\) and target price \(p_r\), study the shared-state AMM with objective \(F_\mu(\phi)=\sum_r \mu_r\,\mathrm{CE}(p_r,q_{S_r}(\phi))\). Starting from \(\phi_0=0\), determine the convergence guarantee for the state and coherent contract prices after \(t\) type draws; in the complete-parlay case \(\mu_S=1/(2^M-1)\), certify the cumulative expected loss.

The model it lives in

A stationary cohort of trader types \(r\), each fully described by its contract, fixed target, and update policy, with rational population masses \(\mu_r\). Each round samples a type according to \(\mu\); the AMM updates its shared Ising state \(\phi\) against the mass-weighted cross-entropy objective and quotes \(q_S(\phi)\). The Ising distribution remains the AMM's internal model, while the continuous object is the trader population.

The objection that survived

After types are aggregated by contract, the mirror reduces to arrival weights and averaged targets already represented in the composite objective, so it adds little trader-specific structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's stationary convergence guarantee and Proposition 3's complete-parlay loss bound under uniform contract-type masses. It leaves Proposition 5's parlay-acceleration result, the multinomial extension, and the empirical Kalshi evaluation outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which Ising model regimes can the marginals and gradients needed for \(F_\mu\) be computed with exact or approximation guarantees polynomial in \(M\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\)?
  • How does the convergence guarantee change when trader targets depend on the evolving market state?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a population of traders, not the paper’s distribution over binary outcomes. In a large prediction market, many traders can use the same standardized signal and order policy on the same contract menu. Let \(N\) be the number of traders and \(\tau\) the number of distinct contract–signal–order types. A plausible regime has \(N\gg\tau\): for example, many customers following a smaller set of recurring strategies, trading a menu of base events and parlays. A type must include everything the model uses—contract, target probability, order size, and relevant trading policy—so the grouping is genuine high multiplicity. Its mass \(\mu_r\) is the fraction of the trader population, or normalized order flow, of type \(r\).

This is close to the paper’s own setup. It treats trades as observations about a shared belief state and weights its composite objective by market arrival rates. Replacing sampled arrivals with a population measure gives a deterministic, mass-weighted learning problem. The paper’s Ising distribution \(P_\phi\) over outcomes remains the market maker’s internal model; that outcome distribution is not what I am continuizing.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1 (Geometric convergence), proved in this paper. As stated, in the pure arbitrageur environment, with \(\phi_0=0\) and step size \(\eta\le 1/(2L)\), it gives
\[ \mathbb E\|\phi_t-\phi^*\|^2 \le (1-2\eta\mu)^t\|\phi^*\|^2+\frac{\eta\sigma^2}{2\mu}, \]
where \(\sigma^2=\mathbb E\|g_t(\phi^*)\|^2\). The paper further says that with \(\eta=0.2\) and \(\mu\approx 1/4\), the contraction factor is \(0.9\), so roughly 22 rounds reduce initial error by a factor of ten. Here \(\phi^*\) is the moment-matched Ising parameter: it matches the target singleton and pairwise moments, and represents the best pairwise approximation when the true law has higher-order dependence. The appendix supplies the proof, with local strong-convexity and iterate-neighborhood conditions; those conditions matter to the guarantee.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Trader-Population Ising Calibration. An instance gives \(M\) binary events, a finite list of trader types \(r\) with rational masses \(\mu_r\), and for each type a contract \(S_r\subseteq[M]\) and target YES probability \(p_r\). The Ising state is \(\phi=(\theta,W)\), and \(q_S(\phi)=P_\phi(X_i=1\text{ for all }i\in S)\). Define
\[ F_\mu(\phi)=\sum_r\mu_r\,\mathrm{CE}\bigl(p_r,q_{S_r}(\phi)\bigr). \]
The question is: given \(\epsilon>0\), return a shared state \(\hat\phi\) whose population objective is within \(\epsilon\) of its infimum, along with its coherent contract prices; in the online version, give an iterate and a bound on its expected parameter error after \(t\) draws from \(\mu\). If the target prices are generated by a common distribution, or by signal types whose mass-weighted targets give those prices, this is the paper’s weighted cross-entropy learning problem. With positive coverage of singleton and pairwise contracts, the state can be identified from those moments. A solution is the fitted state and the associated error guarantee—not a fractional-outcome allocation.

I expect the support-moderate learning version to be Class A, provided the Ising marginals and gradients can be computed efficiently. The paper’s convergence theorem supports that expectation, but it does not itself prove a polynomial-time bound for every dense Ising instance. Exact marginal computation can be the bottleneck; the paper uses loopy belief propagation in its large empirical clusters. So the prover should also state the inference assumptions and how they affect running time. The follow-up questions are how the guarantee depends on \(\tau\), type coverage, and \(L\), and how it changes when traders’ targets vary with market state.

A second useful anchor is Proposition 3 (Complete-parlay loss scaling), also proved here. In the complete-parlay regime, with every nonempty subset \(S\subseteq[M]\) traded at equal rate and SGD step size \(\eta\), it states that expected aggregate LMSR loss decomposes as
\[ \mathbb E[L_T]\le L_{\mathrm{transient}}+T L_{\mathrm{ss,round}}, \]
with \(L_{\mathrm{transient}}=O(b\rho^2M^2/\eta)\) and \(L_{\mathrm{ss,round}}=O(b\eta)\). Thus aggregate steady-state loss per round is polynomial rather than exponential in \(M\), even though there are \(2^M-1\) active contracts. The appendix derives this using three uniform-in-\(M\) conditions: a transient-plus-noise-floor error bound, a bounded average price sensitivity, and \(\|\phi^*\|^2=O(M^2\rho^2)\). Those are substantive conditions on the result, not details to omit.

Its mirror is Complete-Parlay Population Loss Certification. The instance gives \(M\), liquidity \(b\), step size \(\eta\), a horizon \(T\), a target joint law with weak-correlation scale \(\rho\), and a trader population with equal mass on each contract type \(S\ne\emptyset\). A type’s target price is the corresponding joint-event probability. The question is to certify the cumulative expected loss of the shared-state AMM under arrivals drawn from this population, returning the transient and per-round steady-state bounds above. A solution is that loss certificate for the pooled market maker, with loss measured in the paper’s aggregate LMSR sense. For this exact regime I expect Class A as a loss-analysis problem under the proposition’s conditions; extending the certificate to arbitrary type masses is an open question. It would require asking how concentrated arrival mass or poorly covered contracts change the curvature and sensitivity bounds.

The regime for this second mirror is a very large platform cohort trading a standardized menu, with many traders per contract and a market maker pooling their flow. Its limitation is that the complete-parlay type support itself has size \(2^M-1\). It is plausible as a high-multiplicity trader population, but it does not give a small-\(\tau\) account of the paper’s full exponential contract family. The more compelling moderate-support mirror is the calibration problem above, with a tractable subset of frequently traded contracts.

There is no numbered result here establishing that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or another standard complexity class. The anchors are named learning and loss guarantees, not discrete hardness results. So this case does not claim a hardness-dissolves-in-the-continuum result. Its positive claim is narrower: the paper already models a stream of trader types and optimizes a weighted learning objective, making a population-mass formulation recognizable as its continuous-agent version.

My weakest point is that the paper’s main scaling result is in the number of events \(M\), not the number of traders \(N\). Passing from sampled traders to masses may cleanly remove sampling noise, yet leave the paper’s central computational challenge—the event-space inference and the \(2^M\) parlay family—untouched. Trader signals can also create many distinct types unless the scenario really has repeated policies and discretized targets. That is why I would present the calibration question as a credible mirror of the learning subproblem, while being cautious about claiming that the paper itself establishes a continuous-population complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Neither anchor is a computational complexity result. Theorem 1 bounds the convergence of a specified stochastic gradient process under convexity and variance assumptions; Proposition 3 bounds that process’s expected market loss under further uniform-in-\(M\) assumptions. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem by running time or gives exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithms. The proposed mirrors turn those analyses into optimization and certification problems, but that is a new question layered onto the paper’s results.

For Theorem 1, the proposed trader masses add little to the actual objective. In
\[ F_\mu(\phi)=\sum_r\mu_r\,\mathrm{CE}(p_r,q_{S_r}(\phi)), \]
types with the same contract can be combined: cross-entropy is affine in its first argument, so their contribution is one weighted term with arrival weight \(\lambda_S=\sum_{r:S_r=S}\mu_r\) and target \(\bar p_S=\sum_{r:S_r=S}\mu_r p_r/\lambda_S\). The many trader types therefore reduce to contract sampling rates and average targets—the \(\lambda\)-weighted composite objective the paper already writes down. This creates no new population dimension for the algorithmic question.

There is also a fidelity problem. In the paper, signals depend on the evolving score state and time, and each trade updates the shared state in sequence. A fixed \((S,p)\) type erases that variation and order dependence. A type rich enough to preserve it would need to encode the state-contingent signal and policy; the supposed repeated-type population then loses its moderate support. One could instead study a stationary cohort with fixed targets and batch or infinitesimal updates. That may be a coherent estimation model, but it changes the learning process and asks about a different system.

Proposition 3 is an even less direct anchor. Its uniform weights range over all nonempty event subsets, so they describe the market-selection schedule over contracts, not the mass of a society. Replicating traders does not change that schedule or the per-round loss bound. Replacing the schedule with masses over trader policies would produce a broader arrival model, but the proposition does not analyze it. A strong extension might ask how adversarial cohorts can steer prices or increase loss; that requires adding a strategic control problem absent from the paper. It could be worthwhile as new work, but it is not a continuous mirror of Proposition 3.

The negative case is strongest against these two anchors, rather than as a proof that no conceivable re-modeling could be valuable. A real population of traders sharing policies is plausible. But to make it the subject here, one must add population-level control or change the sequential mechanism into a mass-flow model. That would be a new market-design question, not the population continuization of a computational result in this paper.
