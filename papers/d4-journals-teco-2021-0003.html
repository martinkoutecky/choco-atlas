The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) summing to one on each side, an acceptable-pair graph \(E\), and acyclic pairwise preferences for every type, does there exist a flow \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\) that is super-stable? For each \((a,b)\in E\), let \(Q_a(b;x)\) be the unmatched mass of \(a\) plus its mass assigned to types other than \(b\) that \(a\) does not strictly prefer to \(b\), and define \(Q_b(a;x)\) symmetrically; require \(Q_a(b;x)Q_b(a;x)=0\) for every acceptable pair.

The model it lives in

An atomless two-sided placement market with finitely many complete agent types, rational cohort masses, one-to-one matching represented by a feasible flow, and the paper’s super-stability condition applied to positive masses willing to form an acceptable pair.

The objection that survived

The paper’s four-partner restriction bounds individual partners, whereas a four-type restriction is a separate condition that need not preserve the reduction; the proponent acknowledges the adaptation and disclaims hardness transfer, so this weakens the bounded variant’s grounding but not the unrestricted mass question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers super-stable existence from Theorem 9 and weakly stable existence from Theorem 4; it leaves the paper’s polynomial algorithms and its other preference and stability combinations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether a super-stable mass flow exists, measured in the number of types and the binary input length?
  • Does every such rational-mass instance have a stable flow equivalent to an integral stable matching after scaling the masses to a finite replicated market?

The case FOR (proponent)

A natural regime is a large, centralized placement market: many applicants and one-seat positions, with agents grouped by standardized preference profiles. For example, applicants may share a type when they have the same acceptable position types and the same pairwise comparisons among them; positions are typed by their corresponding applicant comparisons. There might be hundreds of thousands of agents but only hundreds or thousands of types, so population size \(N\) is much larger than support size \(\tau\). The type must include the full information the matching problem uses. This is a plausible setting for stable matching, and the paper itself motivates its stability notions through college admissions and uncertain preferences.

Here is the mass version I would ask about. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite sets of types on the two sides, with rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), each summing to \(1\). Let \(E\subseteq A\times B\) be the acceptable type pairs, and let each type have the paper’s pairwise comparisons over its acceptable counterpart types. A solution is a flow \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) on \(E\), with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\). The remaining mass is unmatched. There is no welfare objective in the paper’s problem: the question is whether a feasible matching flow exists that has no blocking type pair.

For precision, for a potential pair \((a,b)\), let \(S_a(b;x)\) be the mass of type \(a\) unmatched or assigned to a *different* type it strictly likes less than \(b\). Let \(Q_a(b;x)\) be the mass unmatched or assigned to a different type that \(a\) does not strictly prefer to \(b\). Define \(S_b(a;x)\) and \(Q_b(a;x)\) symmetrically. Excluding flow already on \((a,b)\) means stability is judged by whether a positive reallocation between types is possible; merely exchanging identities within an already matched type pair does not change the aggregate allocation. Then a pair blocks weakly if \(S_a,S_b>0\); strongly if \(S_a,Q_b>0\) or \(Q_a,S_b>0\); and in the super-stable sense if \(Q_a,Q_b>0\).

My lead is Super-Stable Mass Marriage. Its instance is the mass model above, with acyclic pairwise preferences for every type and at most four acceptable counterpart *types* per type. It asks whether there is a feasible flow with no super-blocking pair; a solution is that flow. The anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper: when both sides have acyclic pairwise preferences, deciding whether a super-stable matching exists is NP-complete, even if each agent finds at most four other agents acceptable. The paper’s proof reduces from \((2,2)\)-e3-SAT. In the mass version, the degree restriction is on acceptable types, so it is an adaptation of the theorem’s restriction rather than an assertion that the two input formats are identical.

A second, independently meaningful question is Weakly Stable Mass Marriage with One-Sided Cycles. Use the same mass-flow solution, require strict lists on one side and asymmetric pairwise preferences on the other, and ask whether a weakly stable flow exists. The anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here: deciding whether a weakly stable matching exists is NP-complete when one side has strict lists and the other has asymmetric pairwise preferences, even if each agent finds at most four other agents acceptable. Its proof also reduces from \((2,2)\)-e3-SAT. The continuous instance can impose the analogous bound of four acceptable counterpart types.

Both questions are recognizable as the paper’s problem: they keep its two-sided matching, acceptable relationships, pairwise preference structures, and stability definitions. The model changes the allocation variable from individual edges to mass on type pairs, which is the expected high-multiplicity change. It also gives the questions a sensible setting: large placements markets where many applicants and positions share standardized profiles, rather than a small committee. The mirror covers the paper’s super-stability and weak-stability existence results, not its full complexity table or its polynomial algorithms.

My working prediction for both mass problems is continuum-specific hardness, with low confidence. The paper’s reductions are built around the combinatorics of preference and acceptability gadgets, not a large numeric population, so there may still be hard choices at the level of the type graph. But neither theorem’s hardness automatically carries over: divisible mass can split across partners, potentially destroying the Boolean choices used in the proofs. A hardness proof would have to survive that splitting, or else show that stability itself forces the relevant flows into discrete choices.

That is the weakest point in the case. A continuous type-flow instance is not simply a discrete matching with many copies, and I would not claim the original NP-completeness results establish hardness of either mirror. The natural next questions are whether stable flows can always be made integral under any of the paper’s preference restrictions; whether the reductions can be adapted to rational type masses; and which of the paper’s polynomial-versus-hard boundaries persist when complexity is measured in the number of types \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is to the proposed anchors’ fidelity. Theorems 4 and 9 bound each agent’s acceptable *individual partners* by four. If agents are grouped into types, an acceptable type may contain a large cohort, so each member accepts many individuals. If the grouping is refined enough to preserve the original sparse gadgets, the types may become nearly as numerous as the agents. Thus the type-degree restriction in the proposed mass problems is not the bounded-degree restriction proved in either theorem, and those reductions do not establish that the specific hardness survives.

That gap does not defeat the better version of the mirror. Dropping the degree bound leaves a clear question: does stable matching existence change when a large placement market is represented by rational masses of agents with identical preference relations? A flow is a coherent aggregate of one-to-one matches between atomless cohorts, and the paper’s existence questions supply named computational targets. The paper’s algorithmic results also invite questions about whether their procedures extend to typed populations. The fact that this is a high-multiplicity matching problem is a connection to investigate, not a reason to reject it.

I can’t make a strong case that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror. The bounded-degree mismatch weakens the proponent’s specific grounding in Theorems 4 and 9, but it does not invalidate the mass-matching questions or show that their computational classification lacks value. My honest negative case is therefore weak: it challenges the claimed anchor transfer, not the underlying continuization.
