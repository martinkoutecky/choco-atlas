The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bundle set \(X=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\{0,\ldots,b_j\}\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over valuation types \(t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), can an algorithm find an exactly welfare-maximizing allocation using polynomially many demand-and-value queries? Each query gives a type \(t\) a rational price vector \(p\ge 0\) and returns \(x_t(p)\in\arg\max_{x\in X}\{v_t(x)-p\cdot x\}\) and \(v_t(x_t(p))\). The allocation variables \(y_{t,x}\ge 0\) must satisfy \(\sum_x y_{t,x}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,x}y_{t,x}x_j\le q_j\) for each resource \(j\); welfare is \(\sum_{t,x}y_{t,x}v_t(x)\). Bound the query count and running time in \(m\), the support size, and the binary input length, apart from oracle evaluation.

The model it lives in

Types are complete valuation functions with rational masses \(\mu_t\); each unit of mass receives an integral bundle \(x\), while type mass may split across bundles. Choose \(y_{t,x}\) to maximize \(\sum_{t,x}y_{t,x}v_t(x)\) subject to type-mass and per-capita supply constraints, using demand-and-value oracles for pricing.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper; Appendix Theorem D.11 restates it. For every \(\epsilon>0\), it says there are infinitely many combinatorial auction instances where no combination of demand queries can produce efficiency above \(50\%+\epsilon\). The bound holds even with infinitely many demand queries and truthful values for every bundle demanded. The proof uses two bidders and one item type: one bidder values any positive quantity highly, while the other has a strictly convex value function whose demand jumps from zero to the full capacity, skipping the quantity needed for the efficient discrete allocation.

This is a worst-case query-model performance lower bound, rather than a theorem classifying running time as \(P\), NP-hard, or FPT. The paper contains no named result of those latter kinds. Theorem 3.2 is still a useful computational anchor on its natural query-complexity reading: it limits what a class of elicitation algorithms can achieve.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federation of regional resource markets with many small firms and a short menu of recurring valuation schedules. For example, firms might demand bundles of spectrum blocks or compute capacity, with the supply of each resource scaling with the number of firms. A type \(t\) would be a firm’s complete valuation function \(v_t\), including its preferences over bundles; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of firms with that valuation. The regime is \(N\) firms and small support \(\tau\), perhaps thousands of firms but a few dozen exact valuation types. Each firm still receives an integral bundle.

Here is a precise continuous question, Mass Demand-Oracle Welfare. Let each agent’s bundle lie in a finite set \(X=\prod_{j=1}^m\{0,\ldots,b_j\}\), let \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) sum to one, and let \(q_j\) be the per-capita supply of resource \(j\). The valuation functions are available through a demand oracle: at a rational price vector \(p\ge 0\), each type returns a bundle maximizing \(v_t(x)-p\cdot x\), along with its true value. Returning the value gives the oracle the same raised-bid information that Theorem 3.2 allows.

The output is a feasible mass allocation \(y_{t,x}\ge 0\), where \(y_{t,x}\) is the fraction of society that has type \(t\) and receives integral bundle \(x\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{x\in X}y_{t,x}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,x}y_{t,x}x_j\le q_j\quad\text{for every }j. \]
Its welfare is \(\sum_{t,x}y_{t,x}v_t(x)\). The question is whether an algorithm can return an exactly welfare-maximizing allocation using polynomially many type-price queries, with running time and query count polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\), apart from the cost of answering each demand query.

I expect this problem to be tractable in the demand-oracle model, by the LP machinery the continuization programme is meant to bring into reach. The full allocation problem is the linear program maximizing \(\sum_{t,x}y_{t,x}v_t(x)\) subject to those mass and supply constraints. Its dual has a price \(p_j\ge0\) for each resource and a variable \(\alpha_t\) for each type, with constraints \(\alpha_t+p\cdot x\ge v_t(x)\). To check those constraints at a proposed dual solution, one asks the demand oracle to maximize \(v_t(x)-p\cdot x\) for each type. That is exactly the pricing step needed for separation and ellipsoid optimization. The resulting primal allocation can split a type’s mass among integral bundles, while using only a sparse set of queried bundles.

This gives a plausible continuous counterpart to Theorem 3.2: its finite-auction obstruction may disappear when the population is continuous and demand responses include values. In the theorem’s construction, the efficient quantity is unavailable as an individual demand response. A mass of identical agents can instead be divided among the available integral bundles, and the LP prices identify the bundles needed for an optimal aggregate allocation. This mirrors the paper’s demand queries, reported bundle values, welfare objective, and winner-determination step closely enough that its authors could recognize the question. It covers Theorem 3.2’s DQ-only welfare limitation; it does not attempt to continuize MLHCA’s training algorithm, query ordering, payments, or incentive claims.

The main weakness is that this is an idealized high-multiplicity regime, unlike the paper’s SATS experiments with seven to ten bidders. It also changes the obstruction at the heart of the theorem: a cohort can be split across bundles even though an individual bidder cannot. That change is precisely what makes the LP question worthwhile, but it leaves the practical case dependent on whether large markets really have a small number of complete valuation types and supplies that scale with participation. Two follow-up questions are how the result changes when types are only approximately identical, and whether the demand-oracle problem remains tractable when the cost of computing each demand response is counted.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 3.2 is an elicitation limit, not a complexity classification. It shows that even unlimited demand queries, with values reported for demanded bundles, can leave the auction’s winner determination far from efficient on certain finite instances. It gives no running-time bound, decision problem, or reduction. The paper’s named results concern what query sequences reveal and how MLHCA performs; the proposed LP question reaches beyond those results.

The proponent’s formulation is still a serious mirror. It is especially vulnerable because it supplies a type-level oracle that returns both a demanded bundle and its true value. The resulting mass-allocation LP has only \(\tau+m\) capacity constraints beyond the type-mass constraints; its dual separation step asks each type to maximize \(v_t(x)-p\cdot x\), exactly the query the oracle provides. Allowing identical types’ mass to split among integral bundles convexifies their choices and removes the obstruction in Theorem 3.2’s example. Demand-query welfare is also an established algorithmic model, though the cited work concerns submodular welfare rather than this exact continuous-population problem ([Feige and Vondrák, 2010](https://www.theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v006a011/)).

That makes the question look more like applying generic LP machinery to a deliberately powerful oracle model than charting a complexity landscape opened by this paper. The pricing task is assumed away, and the paper’s difficult issues—learning valuations from sparse reports, choosing queries, and handling bidder incentives—do not enter. A more practical version would need to count the cost of answering demand queries or study approximation when types differ; those would be new questions beyond the proposed mirror.

The regime is also a weak fit to the paper’s auction settings. Its SATS experiments have only six to ten bidders, each with an individually generated valuation. “Regional” and “national” are model categories, not complete valuation types; grouping by the paper’s definition would likely leave \(\tau\) close to the number of bidders. Scaling spectrum supply with a large population of exact valuation clones requires a stylized market, and the proposed federation of firms is conceivable but unsupported by the paper’s examples.

Still, I cannot honestly make the universal negative stick. A large market with many agents sharing a small menu of valuation schedules is possible, and the resulting mass-allocation question is recognizable as a continuous version of its welfare problem. The case against rests on the thin connection to a named complexity result and the narrow realism of the proposed regime; the anchor survives if the reader accepts demand-query complexity as within scope.
