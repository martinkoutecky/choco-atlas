The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive mirror anchored to this paper’s results. It contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity.

The closest results are Theorems 1–3, all proved in the paper, but none is a qualifying anchor. Theorem 1 establishes that sustainable preferences satisfying the paper’s axioms exist, with a representation combining a weighted sum of generation utilities and a purely finitely additive term. Theorem 2 characterizes the continuous, independent sustainable preferences as having that form. Theorem 3 shows that, in general, a unique sustainable optimum cannot be approximated by feasible paths approaching the unique discounted optimum, for any sequence of discount factors. That last result concerns the relationship between two economic optima; it is not a result about an approximation algorithm.

A tempting population mirror would treat people affected by development as a continuum of stakeholder types and ask how to aggregate their preferences over development paths. But the paper does not study that aggregation problem: its welfare criterion ranks utility streams across generations. And the “continuity” in Theorem 2 is mathematical continuity of a welfare function on a space of utility streams, not a continuous population. Turning the paper’s problem into computational aggregation over stakeholders would change the question, so I would not expect the authors to recognize it as the continuous-population form of their results.

The strongest honest positive claim is therefore limited: the paper offers a formal welfare framework that could motivate a *different* computational social-choice project. It does not provide a named computational result to mirror, so there is no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that none of the paper’s main results gives a computational problem to mirror. Theorems 1 and 2 establish and characterize welfare criteria over bounded utility streams; Theorem 3 compares the optima of two criteria. The paper specifies no finite input, algorithmic task, or complexity claim. Theorem 3’s “approximation” is convergence between utility streams, not an algorithmic approximation guarantee.

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 might suggest treating generations as voter types. That runs into the structure the results rely on: a generation’s position in the sequence matters. The axioms distinguish every finite prefix from the infinite tail. A type that omits position loses that distinction; a type that includes it leaves a countably infinite set of distinct types. Theorem 2’s continuity is continuity of a functional on a space of utility streams, and its characterization includes a purely finitely additive term that vanishes on every finite-support stream. That term cannot be recovered by examining any finite prefix of an instance. Continuizing generations or the time horizon would therefore change the axis of the model, while retaining the original sequence leaves no finite type-support representation of the kind the programme studies.

Theorem 3 admits a more plausible proposed mirror: given a high-multiplicity population and feasible development paths, compute an optimum under a sustainable welfare criterion, perhaps comparing it with discounted optima. That could become a worthwhile project with added assumptions. It would need a finite encoding of paths and constraints, a specified representation of the welfare criterion, and a new computational question. The theorem itself says that the two kinds of optima can remain separated; it gives no algorithmic result about computing either one.

A stakeholder distribution over environmental policies is another plausible setting. It introduces a new aggregation problem among people within the present population. The paper instead axiomatizes how to rank utility across generations; it supplies no result about computing a choice from a distribution of stakeholder preferences. That application might motivate a separate project, but it does not turn these theorems into continuous-population results.

The negative case is strongest for the paper’s stated results, rather than as a proof that no adjacent project could be valuable. A researcher could build a computational model around sustainable welfare. The work needed to do so would create the computational problem and its input regime; it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.
