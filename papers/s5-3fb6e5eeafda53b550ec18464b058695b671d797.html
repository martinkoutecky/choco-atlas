What the mirror covers

The proposed task captures Section 4's distance-based aggregation rule over population shares; it does not computationally mirror Theorem 1's impossibility result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a continuous companion problem, but the paper has no named computational result to anchor it to. Its only numbered result is Theorem 1, proved here: for a finite set of \(k>2\) worlds and \(n>1\) individuals, there is no aggregation rule from \(n\)-tuples of nonempty proper subsets of worlds to a nonempty proper collective subset that satisfies all three conditions: unanimity (identical individual judgments are adopted), independence (the collective judgment about a world depends only on individual judgments about that world), and non-dictatorship (no fixed individual’s judgment is always the output). The appendix derives the theorem from earlier work, but it proves the stated result in this paper. It is an axiomatic impossibility theorem, not a complexity classification; Section 4’s distance-based escape route is described, not stated as a numbered computational result.

A plausible computational companion is Continuous Distance-Based Possible-World Aggregation. An instance has a finite world set \(\Omega\), \(|\Omega|=k>2\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the types \(A\in\mathcal J=2^\Omega\setminus\{\varnothing,\Omega\}\), given by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types. A type is an individual’s complete judgment about which worlds are possible; \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of the population holding that judgment. The task is to output a valid collective judgment \(K\in\mathcal J\) maximizing total mass-weighted agreement:
\[ \sum_{A\in\mathcal J}\mu_A \left(\lvert K\cap A\rvert+\lvert(\Omega\setminus K)\cap(\Omega\setminus A)\rvert\right). \]
This is the paper’s Section 4 distance-based idea with population shares in place of counts. It keeps the outcome as a valid judgment over the original finite worlds; the population is the continuous object.

A credible regime is a large consultation about which states of a finite system are possible—for example, residents assessing possible outcomes of a local infrastructure plan. Many respondents may give the same subset of worlds, so the number of people can be large while the number of distinct judgment types \(\tau\) remains modest. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their aggregation problem: it aggregates precisely the kind of individual judgments they define, using the distance-based escape route they discuss.

This companion problem is tractable. Let \(p_\omega=\sum_{A\ni\omega}\mu_A\), the mass judging \(\omega\) possible. Before enforcing that \(K\) be nonempty and proper, each world can be handled independently: include it when \(p_\omega>1/2\), exclude it when \(p_\omega<1/2\), and resolve ties to obtain a valid set. The \(p_\omega\) values can be computed in \(O(\tau k)\) rational arithmetic. This is a Class A algorithmic question, but it does not show that the impossibility in Theorem 1 dissolves under continuization: the proposed rule relaxes independence, the very condition the theorem says cannot coexist with the other two.

That is the weak point, and it is substantial. The theorem’s contribution is about whether a rule with specified axioms exists, not about the cost of computing one. The continuous companion computes a particular compromise rule; it does not mirror the theorem’s impossibility as a computational phenomenon. So the paper supports a sensible continuous-population aggregation problem, but it supplies no named computational anchor for a claim that its results belong to ChoCo’s complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper contains no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is a global impossibility claim about whether any aggregation function can satisfy unanimity, independence, and non-dictatorship on the full domain. Replacing voter counts with a distribution over judgment types leaves the question global and axiomatic: does such a function exist? The paper supplies no per-instance computational task or complexity claim for a continuous population to change.

The proposed distance-based problem is the best counterexample to that negative case. A large consultation about possible worlds is a credible high-multiplicity setting: many respondents can share a judgment type, and their masses are natural inputs. I would not reject it on the grounds that the population is finite in practice or that the answer might match a finite-count version.

But computing that rule follows Section 4’s escape route, which relaxes independence. It asks how to implement one proposed compromise, rather than whether continuizing the population changes the impossibility in Theorem 1. A more faithful continuous version of the theorem still asks whether a global rule can meet the axioms; that remains an axiomatic question. Adding restrictions on allowable collective judgments or on the representation of worlds could create harder computational problems, but those additions would supply the computational structure the paper itself lacks.

So the case against is strongest if “mirror” means a computational counterpart to a result in this paper: none of its results provides a computational anchor. The proposed task is a plausible continuous companion, and the authors might recognize it as their problem because they discuss the rule. That leaves my broader claim—that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror—weak. I cannot honestly rule out that the companion is worthwhile; I can only argue that it does not continuize the paper’s central result.
