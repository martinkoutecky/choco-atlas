What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 is the only anchor the proponent named; it gives a best-response fairness guarantee in the two-agent setting. The paper’s equilibrium-existence and other fairness results are not covered by a surviving population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive seed is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: with two agents having additive true valuations, under Standard, Priority, or Preferred-Item E-C-E, any agent who plays a best response to the other agent’s bid receives an allocation that is EF1 from her true-valuation perspective. That is a clear strategic-fairness guarantee and a natural result to want to extend.

But it is not a qualifying anchor under your stated rule: it gives a fairness guarantee, not a complexity classification for a computational problem. The paper’s other named results concern equilibrium existence or fairness guarantees in small instances; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. There are no qualifying named computational anchors in this paper, so there are no anchor-based continuous problems to hand to a prover or classify in the programme’s trichotomy.

A possible research direction would be to ask whether best-response fairness persists for a high-multiplicity population with a small support of additive valuation types and goods supplied at a fixed rate per person. The obstacle is that E-C-E acts on named agents: its turn order and envy cycles depend on individual bundles. A unilateral deviation has zero mass in a continuum description, yet can change those dynamics. Keeping the identities and order may make the number of types grow with the population; restricting deviations to whole types changes the paper’s individual best-response question. That makes this a weak basis for the requested mirror, even though Theorem 1 is a compelling result in its original setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its theorems concern whether pure Nash equilibria exist and whether best responses preserve fairness; none classifies the complexity of a problem. Theorem 1 is therefore a real and interesting result, but it is a strategic-fairness guarantee, not a complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to continuize. Asking for the complexity of finding a best response or equilibrium would add a new question rather than mirror a result in this paper. The paper also does not specify a finite encoding for that task: additive bids may be arbitrary nonnegative reals, and subadditive bids are given by value oracles.

The proposed high-multiplicity version runs into a deeper mismatch. Theorem 1 is about one named agent’s best response in a sequential mechanism. E-C-E’s source choices, envy cycles, and item assignments depend on agents’ individual bundles and order in the run. Aggregating agents with the same valuation can discard those distinctions. A more careful model could include priority position and bundle history in the type, but then it has to preserve the very individual-level state that population aggregation was meant to compress. And in an atomless population, one agent’s deviation has zero mass, so it cannot straightforwardly represent the strategic move in Theorem 1. Treating a positive mass of agents as deviating instead asks about a coalition, not that theorem’s unilateral best response.

Scaling up a shared market with per-capita goods could produce an interesting continuum fair-division game. But its result would concern a newly defined E-C-E-like process and new strategic deviations; it would not be a continuous-population version of Theorem 1’s individual guarantee. Repeating small two- or three-agent markets would preserve the theorem, but those markets remain separate: aggregating them does not create a population-level computational problem.

So the strongest case against is that this paper is outside ChoCo’s target: its contribution is about strategic behavior and fairness, not computational complexity, and the most promising population-scale reformulation changes the strategic object. This does not rule out a worthwhile nonatomic fair-division project; it is a strong reason to treat that as a different research direction, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
