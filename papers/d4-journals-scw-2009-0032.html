The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly name a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results do not establish that a problem is in P, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified. Theorem 1, proved here, says that RU is maximal under any ordering of bargaining solutions satisfying Global Pareto, Strong Linearity, and Dictatorship Indifference; it is the only solution maximal for every such ordering, and it dominates every other solution when the ordering is total. Theorem 4, also proved here, says RU uniquely maximizes ex ante expected utility among measurable, rescaling-invariant solutions under the paper’s anonymity and distribution assumptions. These are characterizations of a solution, not computational results. Theorem 4’s distribution is over future bargaining problems while the set of roles stays finite, so it does not supply a continuous-population anchor either.

The strongest positive proposal I can make is therefore a *new computational question suggested by Theorem 1*, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper. Call it Type-Mass Relative-Utilitarian Bargaining. Take a finite set \(T\) of complete participant types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Members of each type share the same preferences and utility normalization. Let \(B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{T}_{\ge 0}\) be a compact convex set of feasible per-person utility vectors, including lottery outcomes; let \(q\in B\) be the status quo. For each type, set \(M_t=\max\{b_t:b\in B,\ b\ge q\}\), assuming \(M_t>q_t\). The task is to return a feasible, individually rational vector \(b^\star\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\frac{b_t-q_t}{M_t-q_t}, \]
together with a lottery or allocation realizing it when one is part of the input. Here mass is the fraction of the population in a type, \(b_t\) is that type’s per-person utility, and the objective is the population-weighted sum of normalized gains.

A plausible regime is a large bargaining population in a citywide labor or resource compact: many participants, but relatively few recurring contract or household types, with shared preferences within each type. That gives \(n\gg\tau\), where \(n\) is the number of people and \(\tau=|T|\). It also makes a continuous population description natural: the question is what fraction of the population is in each type, rather than how many named individuals. The authors could recognize the objective as RU aggregated over identical participants. If \(B\) is given as a rational polytope, the proposed problem is an LP, so I would expect it to be tractable (Class A).

This proposed mirror covers RU’s pointwise bargain selection, the solution at the heart of Theorem 1. It does not mirror Theorem 1’s claim about orderings over solution functions, which is axiomatic. Nor would I count Theorem 4’s random sequence of future problems as a population mirror: that would continuize the problem distribution, not the society.

The weak point is substantial: the paper does not pose this computational problem or analyze its complexity, and its bargaining interpretation emphasizes relatively small groups and unanimous consent. A mass population fits more naturally if RU is read as a social choice rule, an interpretation the paper itself allows, than if every resident must personally consent to a bargain. So this is a plausible RU population extension, but it does not meet the requested standard of a paper result serving as a computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives the negative case a strong starting point: it proves no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes RU by axioms on an ordering over bargaining-solution functions; Theorem 4 characterizes it through preferences over solution functions under a distribution on future bargaining problems. Neither gives a finite input, an algorithmic task, or a complexity classification. Theorem 4’s randomness is over problems while the set of roles remains finite, so it is not a population mirror.

The proposed Type-Mass RU problem is sensible as a new model, but it does not mirror Theorem 1’s result. It takes the formula for RU’s pointwise choice and asks how to compute it in a population allocation setting. If \(B\) is an explicitly given rational polytope, that is a linear program. If \(B\) is represented through a large or implicit set of feasible outcomes, the computational question depends on that added representation and allocation structure. Either may be worth studying, but neither is supplied or analyzed by the paper. The work needed to make it interesting would be a new allocation problem, not a computational consequence of the theorem.

There is also a modeling choice hidden in the proposed normalization. In the finite-person RU solution, \(M_i\) is the best utility one person can get while everyone else remains at least at the status quo. In the proposed type-level model, \(M_t\) is the best *common per-person utility* for a whole type. Those can differ: a single member’s dictatorship need not be feasible as an equal utility for every member of their type. One can repair the model by carrying forward individual dictatorship values, or deliberately define cohort-level RU. Both choices are defensible, but each adds structure the paper does not settle.

Trying to continuize Theorem 1’s axioms directly does not provide a computational alternative. Its dictatorship solutions refer to individual roles; in an atomless population, changing one person’s outcome has no effect on an aggregate outcome defined up to sets of measure zero. The dictatorship-indifference axiom then loses its intended force. Treating positive-mass types as the “dictators” instead changes the axiom and its object. That could motivate new axiomatic work, but it is outside ChoCo’s computational remit.

So I would reject this as an anchored continuous mirror of the paper. I would not claim the stronger universal conclusion that no useful population-RU optimization problem could ever be built: an implicit feasible-outcome model might yield a real LP or pricing question. The honest negative case is narrower and stronger: that possibility is a new research direction suggested by RU’s formula, not a computational problem or result this paper gives us to continuize.
