The normalized fixed-point analogue is credible and has the same threshold characterization, but that does not supply the missing computational anchor, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 1, case (1), in the pure case; it leaves the other absorbing classes in Theorem 1, the mixed-agent cases in Theorem 2, and the large-population phase analyses untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited by the anchor rule: this paper has no named computational result in the specified sense. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 give complete classifications of absorbing classes and their conditions of occurrence, but they make no complexity or algorithm claim. The closest named result is Theorem 1, item (i)(1), proved in this paper: in the pure case with both conformists and anti-conformists present, the state \(N_a\)—all anti-conformists say “yes,” all conformists say “no”—is absorbing if and only if
\[ n_c \ge \max(n-l_c,\;n-l_a). \]
That is a strong mathematical anchor for a continuous analogue, but it is not an eligible computational anchor under the requested standard.

The most plausible regime is a very large population deciding whether to adopt something, where people react to the overall adoption share rather than to anyone’s identity. A type is a complete opinion-updating rule; its mass is the fraction of the population with that rule. With finitely many types and many people per type, let \(x_t\) be the mass of type \(t\) currently saying “yes,” and \(q=\sum_t x_t\) the total “yes” share. If \(p_t(q)\) is that type’s probability of saying “yes” next, the continuum update is
\[ x'_t=\mu_t p_t(q). \]
This is the deterministic population limit of the paper’s independent updates. It keeps the paper’s anonymity and conformist/anti-conformist behavior; the decision variable is the distribution of “yes” mass across types. There is no optimization objective in the paper to preserve.

A precise exploratory problem is Polarized Fixed-Point Existence. Its input is rational type masses and response rules with common normalized thresholds \(\lambda_c=l_c/n\) and \(\lambda_a=l_a/n\): conformist rules give probability zero to “yes” when \(q\le\lambda_c\), and anti-conformist rules give probability one to “yes” when \(q\le\lambda_a\). The question is whether the state with every anti-conformist saying “yes” and every conformist saying “no” is absorbing; the solution is a yes/no answer and, when yes, that state. At this state \(q=\mu_a\), so the condition is
\[ \mu_a\le\lambda_c\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_a\le\lambda_a, \]
or equivalently \(\mu_c\ge\max(1-\lambda_c,\;1-\lambda_a)\). This is exactly the normalized form of Theorem 1(i)(1). The proposed problem is tractable by comparing binary rationals. It covers only this pure-case polarization result, not the paper’s full classification, mixed-agent case, or cycles and chaotic classes.

The authors could recognize this as their population process with mass in place of counts, and the theorem’s threshold condition survives the scaling cleanly. Further questions would ask which attractors exist in the continuum dynamics, whether a large initial majority can undergo opinion reversal, and how mixed types change the basins of attraction.

The weak point is substantial: this is an exploratory analogue of a theorem, not a mirror of a computational result. The paper already gives the corresponding finite-population condition and studies a large-\(n\) pure case in Section 3.5. The proposed fixed-point question is therefore a simple scaling of known analysis, not evidence of a new computational-complexity landscape. The model is a credible continuous-population object; the paper itself does not supply the computational anchor needed to make a stronger ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives the other analyst only one anchor: Theorem 1(i)(1), which characterizes when \(N_a\) is absorbing. The theorem says this happens exactly when \(n_a\le l_c\) and \(n_a\le l_a\), or, after normalization, \(\mu_a\le\lambda_c\) and \(\mu_a\le\lambda_a\). It makes no algorithm or complexity claim. The paper’s named results classify long-run behavior; they do not analyze the computational complexity of a problem.

The proposed continuous question preserves that characterization, but its answer is just the same two rational comparisons. At the proposed polarized state, \(q=\mu_a\), and the state is fixed exactly when \(p_c(q)=0\) and \(p_a(q)=1\). Asking whether that holds repackages the theorem as a decision problem; it does not expose a computational bottleneck or a new algorithmic boundary. The full classification of absorbing classes would be a richer analogue, but the paper’s classes concern collections of subsets of a finite society. In the standard independent-update population limit, the type masses instead follow the deterministic map \(x'_t=\mu_t p_t(q)\). That changes the object being classified: finite-population stochastic transitions and their absorbing classes give way to dynamics of mass shares.

A stronger candidate is opinion reversal: can a small anti-conformist mass drive a conformist majority to the opposite opinion? That is a plausible continuous-population question, and the paper’s large-\(n\) analysis supports the population regime. But posing it as a complexity problem requires adding an algorithmic task and an encoding of the response functions—for example, deciding whether a specified mass-share trajectory reaches a target region. The paper supplies no computational result for that task. Likewise, adding a control or campaigning objective could create an in-scope computational problem, but it would be a new question rather than a mirror of one of this paper’s results.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated eligibility criterion: none of the proposed anchors is a computational result, and the only exact theorem mirror reduces to checking its existing threshold condition. It is weaker as a universal claim about the model. Anonymous influence in large populations is a credible continuous object, and the paper itself studies a large-population limit. That supports further research on the dynamics; it does not supply the computational anchor needed for a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper.
