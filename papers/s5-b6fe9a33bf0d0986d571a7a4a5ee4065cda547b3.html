What the mirror covers

No result is covered: Theorems 1 and 3 establish stability existence under structural conditions, Theorem 2 establishes payoff uniqueness, and Theorem 4 establishes weak bargaining-set existence for finite contract choice problems.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that contract choice has a plausible high-multiplicity setting. In a large staffing market, for example, many workers could share a type specified by their skills, outside options, and contract-relevant preferences. With relatively few such types, coalition feasibility and payoffs might depend on the masses of each type in the coalition. Mass would then represent population share, and outcomes would partition that mass into coalitions with feasible contracts. That is a credible population-continuous version of the paper’s subject.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor it. Theorem 1, proved here, says the core is nonempty when the weak top-coalition property holds. Theorem 2 says the top-coalition property makes strict-core payoff vectors unique. Theorem 3 says the weak top-cycle property guarantees an individually stable outcome. Theorem 4, proved here, says every finite contract choice problem has a nonempty weak bargaining set. These are existence and uniqueness claims; none establishes an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an approximation result.

One could propose a follow-up asking how to compute a weak bargaining-set outcome in a finite-type, mass-based contract market. That would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored by Theorem 4: its theorem concerns existence for finite-agent problems, and its proof does not give a complexity guarantee. Extending it to a continuum would also require new modeling choices and a proof; the finite-agent termination argument does not automatically carry over.

The soft spot in the positive case is therefore decisive under ChoCo’s criteria: the population regime is plausible, but the paper’s results are about stability existence, not computation. I can’t honestly name a qualifying anchor or assign a complexity direction to its continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

A large staffing market with workers grouped by skills, outside options, and contract relevant preferences is a sensible high multiplicity setting. That is not where the negative case lies. The problem is that none of the paper’s four named results gives a computational claim to carry into such a setting.

Theorem 1 gives a condition for core existence; Theorem 2 gives strict core payoff uniqueness under a stronger condition; Theorem 3 gives individual stability existence under the weak top cycle property; and Theorem 4 asserts weak bargaining set existence for every finite contract choice problem. None says how to find an outcome, or classifies the complexity of deciding or computing one. So none is a computational anchor. The paper also does not specify an input encoding for its arbitrary real payoff sets \(F(S)\), making computational complexity undefined even for the finite model as stated.

One could define mass based feasible contracts and ask how to compute a core, individually stable outcome, or weak bargaining set. But that would require specifying a new continuous model and its input representation, then posing a new algorithmic question. The paper’s results do not anchor its computational difficulty or supply an algorithm to analyze. Theorem 4’s proof, for example, terminates by building a chain of disjoint nonempty coalitions in a finite population; a continuous version would need its own existence and limiting arguments. That might make an interesting project, but it would be a new continuous contract choice paper, not a computational mirror of this one.

So I would reject all four as ChoCo mirrors, even in a plausible staffing scenario. The honest limit of this case is that it rules out a worthwhile *mirror under ChoCo’s computational remit*; it cannot establish that continuous contract choice is uninteresting in broader cooperative game theory.
