The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Equations (1) and (2) define the pivot probabilities and prospect rating; the runtime bounds and the population-size findings are discussed in prose and illustrated by simulations. Figures 9 and 10 are empirical results, not numbered complexity results. So there is no eligible anchor to restate, and no continuous problem that I can honestly present as the mirror of one.

The strongest unanchored direction is the paper’s study of how population size affects strategic voting in networks. Imagine large electoral districts with a small number of recurring voter blocs. A type could be a voter’s ideal point \(p_t\) together with a network role that fixes their exposure to other blocs. The mass \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the electorate. Let \(x_{t,c}\) be the fraction of type \(t\) currently voting for candidate \(c\); plurality selects the candidate maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t x_{t,c}\). Given candidate positions and a network mixing matrix, ask whether the paper’s boundedly rational response dynamics, started from sincere voting, converge or cycle, and return the resulting ballot masses and winner. The natural regime is \(n\gg\tau\): many voters but relatively few preference-and-network types.

That scenario speaks directly to the paper’s question about population size and preserves its combination of voter preferences, local network information, plurality, and expected-utility responses. But it needs a symmetric network-block model so that voters grouped into one type really do have the same relevant observations. It also needs care with pivotality: in a continuum, an individual ballot has zero mass, while the paper’s strategic incentives depend on the chance that one ballot changes the winner. A high-multiplicity formulation would have to retain that finite-election pivotal calculation as the population scale grows.

Those are substantial weaknesses. The proposed convergence problem is a research direction, not a mirror of a named result in this paper; its complexity is undetermined. And the block structure may be a useful scenario for large networks, but it simplifies the paper’s individual neighborhood observations. The positive case is therefore plausible as a continuation of the paper’s simulations, but this paper supplies no qualifying formal anchor for the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to anchor a mirror. Equations (1) and (2) define the pivot probabilities and response rule. The \(O(m^2 n^{m-2})\) estimate for computing a full voter response and the Poisson model’s \(O(m^3)\) runtime appear as prose analyses; the comparisons of speed, fidelity, and population size are simulation results. The proponent accordingly names no anchor. A continuous question about convergence or winner computation would be a new formal problem, not a mirror of a stated computational result in this paper.

The strongest proposed version is a society with finitely many preference and network-role types, masses \(\mu_t\), and a mixing matrix that determines each type’s local ballot observations. One could ask whether the resulting mass dynamics converge or cycle. That model is coherent under conditions such as an equitable block network with agents in each block receiving identical observations. The paper’s experiments, however, use sparse realized graphs with average degree around 25–30 as the population grows. Each voter’s finite neighborhood remains variable, and directed structure and correlations created by iterative updates affect what they observe. A block mixing matrix gives expected neighborhood composition; it does not capture those realized local observations. Encoding enough network state to reproduce them exactly would make the type support grow with the graph. Restricting to equitable blocks would instead change the network regime and its dynamics.

There is also a population-limit obstacle to carrying over the voter response itself. Its strategic incentive comes from the chance that one ballot is pivotal in a finite election of size \(n\). In an atomless population, one agent’s ballot has zero mass. A mirror that preserves pivotality must retain a finite size parameter or a finite-population probability model, as the paper’s Poisson heuristic does. That may support a useful follow-up, but it leaves the paper’s simulation and heuristic claims without a continuous computational result to mirror.

This is a strong case against a continuous mirror *of this paper under the stated anchor standard*. I cannot honestly establish the broader claim that no continuous study of block-network voting could ever be worthwhile: an equitable-block model could merit independent study. Its plausibility does not provide the missing anchor, and the proponent’s proposal depends on changing the network setting the paper investigates.
