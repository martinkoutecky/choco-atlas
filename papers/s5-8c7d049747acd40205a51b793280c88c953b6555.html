The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), ranking types \(T\subseteq\mathcal L(C)\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), compute a linear order \(\rho\) minimizing \(D_\mu(\rho)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\lvert\{\{a,b\}\subseteq C:\rho\text{ and }t\text{ disagree on }a,b\}\rvert\); equivalently, decide whether a designated candidate \(c^\star\) is first in some optimal ranking.

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types \(T\subseteq\mathcal L(C)\). The decision variable is an integral ranking \(\rho\), and the objective is its population-weighted Kendall disagreement, equivalently the Kemeny score induced by the population pairwise margins.

The objection that survived

The continuous Kemeny instance is determined entirely by its pairwise-margin vector, so it adds no population-sensitive optimization beyond the paper's weighted-majority formulation and may offer limited scientific yield.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Kemeny hardness result in Theorem 5 and the Banks-set hardness result in Theorem 2. It leaves the majority-dimension characterizations, SAT implementation, experiments, and the other voting-rule results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does continuous Kemeny ranking remain NP-hard when the support size \(\tau\) is bounded?
  • Which domain restrictions on ranking types make the continuous problem tractable?
  • What are the complexity and approximation guarantees for Kemeny ranking under uncertain or perturbed masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a genuine high-multiplicity mirror for the paper’s ranking and tournament-solution problems. I would lead with Kemeny; the expected outcome is Class B, because the paper’s hardness is carried by the alternatives and their gadget structure, not by having many named voters.

Take a candidate set \(C\), and let each voter type \(t\) be a complete ranking in \(\mathcal L(C)\). A continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these rankings. The intended regime is a large population—say millions of users ranking a fixed catalogue of products, policies, or search results—with many users sharing one of a relatively small number \(\tau\) of ranking templates. Thus \(N\) is large while \(\tau\ll N\). The population enters only through its mass distribution; the output remains an ordinary integral ranking or candidate set.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5 of the paper, proved in the paper:

“The problems of computing the Slater set and of computing a Kemeny ranking are NP-hard if the number of voters is at least 7.”

The Kemeny part is the relevant anchor here. The paper’s proof uses Conitzer’s SAT-to-Slater construction, modifies it, and shows that every resulting tournament is induced by a seven-voter profile whose pairwise majority margins are all \(1\). The underlying reduction is inherited from cited work, but the seven-voter realization is established by this paper. The theorem appears in the [journal version](https://pub.dss.in.tum.de/brandt-research/fewvoters-journal.pdf).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous Kemeny Ranking. An instance consists of \(C\), a finite list of ranking types \(T\subseteq\mathcal L(C)\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and optionally a target candidate \(c^\star\). For a ranking \(\rho\), define its population Kendall disagreement by

\[ D_\mu(\rho) = \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\, \bigl|\{\{a,b\}\subseteq C: \rho\text{ and }t\text{ order }a,b\text{ differently}\}\bigr|. \]

The task is to output a linear order \(\rho\) minimizing \(D_\mu(\rho)\); the associated winner question asks whether some optimal \(\rho\) places \(c^\star\) first.

Equivalently, define the population pairwise margin

\[ M_\mu(a,b) = \sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t - \sum_{t:b\succ_t a}\mu_t. \]

Then the task maximizes

\[ \sum_{a\succ_\rho b}M_\mu(a,b), \]

which is precisely the normalized weighted-majority formulation used by Kemeny. No ranking or outcome has been fractionalized: only the society is continuous.

This is a strong mirror because the paper’s seven-voter construction embeds directly. Given a hard profile \(R_1,\ldots,R_7\), set

\[ \mu_t=\frac{|\{i:R_i=t\}|}{7}. \]

The resulting margins are the paper’s margins divided by \(7\), so every Kemeny score is scaled by the same positive constant and the optimal rankings are unchanged. Conversely, a population of \(7q\) individuals can contain \(q\) copies of each of the seven ranking types. Thus the continuous problem contains the paper’s hard instances with only \(\tau\le 7\) positive-mass types. The expected classification is therefore Class B: NP-hardness transfers even in an extreme high-multiplicity regime.

This is not merely “Kemeny with a different notation.” The paper studies profiles of a fixed number of named voters; it does not study an input population measure, type multiplicities, rational mass encoding, or complexity as a function of \((m,\tau,L)\) independently of \(N\). The proposed mirror turns the paper’s central weighted majority object into a population-level computational problem.

A second, independently defensible anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in the paper:

“Computing the Banks set is NP-hard if the number of voters is at least 5.”

The paper adapts the earlier Banks-set reduction of Brandt, Fischer, Harrenstein, and Mair, and proves that its tournament gadgets are inducible by five voters. The earlier Banks hardness is cited, but the bounded-voter realization and the resulting theorem are proved here.

The matching continuous problem is:

Continuous Banks Set. An instance consists of \(C\), ranking types \(T\subseteq\mathcal L(C)\), rational masses \(\mu\), and the promise that every pair has a nonzero population margin \(M_\mu(a,b)\). Let

\[ T_\mu=(C,E_\mu), \qquad (a,b)\in E_\mu \iff M_\mu(a,b)>0. \]

The task is to compute the Banks set of \(T_\mu\): the alternatives that are maximal elements of inclusion-maximal transitive subtournaments. Equivalently, given \(c^\star\), decide whether \(c^\star\) belongs to that set.

Again, a five-voter hard profile becomes a distribution with at most five positive-mass ranking types, and replication preserves the majority tournament exactly. Hence this mirror is also expected to be Class B. The hardness is plainly driven by the number of alternatives and the SAT gadget, not by voter multiplicity.

The authors should recognize both questions as their problems. A type is a complete ranking, exactly the information their voting rules use; mass determines the pairwise majority digraph or its weighted version; and the decision variable is still the same discrete object—an optimal ranking or a Banks winner. The paper’s search-engine example has few voters and therefore is not itself the best high-multiplicity story, but a large platform or organization with many users falling into repeated ranking templates is a plausible regime for the same mathematical problem.

This case deliberately covers only Theorems 2 and 5. It does not claim that the SAT encoding, majority-dimension experiments, or structural \(k\)-inducibility lemmas have already been continuized.

The weakest point is that these mirrors do not demonstrate a tractability gain. They show that the continuous-population questions are meaningful and that the paper’s hardness survives them. Moreover, the reduction from a constant profile to a mass distribution is formally straightforward. The positive case therefore supports the existence and legitimacy of the mirror, not a claim that continuization dissolves discrete hardness.

Natural follow-up questions are whether Kemeny remains hard when \(\tau\) is bounded but ranking types obey a domain restriction, whether approximate Kemeny rankings are easier under noisy or estimated masses, and how Banks membership behaves when pairwise margins may be zero or are subject to \(L_1\)-bounded population perturbations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that both problems are population-blind after aggregation. That is a real weakness, but it does not support the universal negative claim.

For Kemeny, let

\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t-\sum_{t:b\succ_t a}\mu_t. \]

The population Kendall objective satisfies

\[ D_\mu(\rho) = \frac{\binom{|C|}{2}}{2} -\frac12\sum_{a\succ_\rho b}M_\mu(a,b). \]

Thus \(\mu\) matters only through its pairwise-margin vector. Every distribution inducing the same \(M_\mu\) has exactly the same Kemeny rankings. The paper already formulates Kemeny on weighted majority digraphs, so the proposed continuous input is essentially a different encoding of that native object.

The seven-voter hardness construction makes this especially stark. Its continuous version is a distribution supported on at most seven rankings; replacing it by millions of identical copies changes nothing. For rational masses, clearing denominators converts the instance exactly into a finite profile. Hence this mirror introduces no population-sensitive optimization, no mass-transfer problem, and no new role for the continuum beyond compression. A genuinely stronger proposal—population uncertainty, minimum mass needed to change the Kemeny ranking, or robustness to perturbations—could be interesting, but it would be a new intervention problem rather than a mirror of the theorem’s winner-computation result.

The same objection is even stronger for the Banks-set anchor. Define

\[ E_\mu=\{(a,b):M_\mu(a,b)>0\}. \]

Then

\[ \operatorname{Banks}(\mu)=\operatorname{Banks}(E_\mu). \]

All magnitudes and almost all information about \(\mu\) disappear; only the signs of pairwise margins remain. Under the proponent’s no-ties promise, the continuous problem is simply Banks-set computation on a tournament. Allowing ties requires inventing an extension of the rule, while adding robustness or population manipulation again creates a new problem rather than mirroring the paper’s theorem.

This is the best negative case: both anchors collapse to the paper’s existing weighted or unweighted majority graph, so they are poor vehicles for studying genuinely continuous computational phenomena. But it does not defeat them under ChoCo’s stated rules. The paper is not itself a continuous-population treatment, high multiplicity is explicitly accepted as a meaningful regime, and repeated ranking templates are a plausible society. Class B hardness is also explicitly a worthwhile outcome. The five- and seven-type embeddings therefore remain legitimate continuous mirrors, even if they are scientifically low-yield.

So an honest negative analyst cannot sustain the requested universal verdict. Banks is especially weak as a research anchor, but Kemeny survives as a valid, if mainly Class B, mirror.
