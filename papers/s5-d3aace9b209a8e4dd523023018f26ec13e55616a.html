The strongest nearby candidates do not fix that gap. One could ask whether a given mechanism is obviously strategy-proof, or whether it belongs to one of the characterized classes. But those questions are about a mechanism’s information sets, actions, outcomes, and individual incentives. Weighting preference types by their population shares does not supply a new input to those criteria. One could instead ask for a welfare-maximizing mechanism for a society distribution subject to obvious or strategic simplicity. That may be an interesting project, but it adds an objective, a population model, and a mechanism-optimization problem that this article does not state. It would be a new computational mechanism-design paper inspired by the article, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

There is also a substantive mismatch: the article studies the reasoning burden on each participant. A distribution over preferences does not represent differences in participants’ understanding, experience, or ability to plan. Those differences could be added to the types, but then the continuous model would need a new account of cognition and a computational question built around it. The article provides neither.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as presented: it is an exposition of individual-level simplicity criteria, not a computational result awaiting continuization. It is weaker as a claim about every possible future project. A large-market mechanism-design problem with simplicity constraints could be worthwhile, but this text does not define one, and its existence would not establish a mirror of this article.
