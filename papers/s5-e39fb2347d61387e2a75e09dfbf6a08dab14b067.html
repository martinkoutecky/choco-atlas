The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case for a mirror is that the paper’s model already describes voters using just three complete preference types, and its informed treatment makes their realized aggregate shares central to the analysis. A large electorate made up of stable preference blocs, with reliable polls reporting each bloc’s share, is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: \(N\) is large while the number of types is \(\tau=3\). That could support a continuous study of the paper’s QRE predictions about strategic-vote rates and plurality outcomes.

But the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational-complexity result. Its numbered behavioral predictions, equilibrium analysis, and experimental findings are not such anchors. So there are no qualifying results to quote and restate, and no anchor-linked continuous problem to hand to a prover under your criteria.

The main obstacle to turning that plausible setting into a faithful mirror is pivotality. In a nonatomic electorate, one voter’s ballot cannot change the aggregate vote shares. The finite game’s strategic incentives depend on the probability of being pivotal; retaining them in a continuum model would require a finite-population or noise scaling, which changes the object being studied. Thus the paper offers natural ingredients for a future continuous model, but no named computational result on which to ground the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered predictions are comparative statics of a QRE model, and its main findings are experimental claims about voting behavior and outcomes. Computing those predictions in another model could be a new project, but it would not transfer or classify a computational problem posed by this paper.

The proposed large-electorate setting is sensible: a society can have three preference types with known shares. But it removes the mechanism behind the QRE predictions. Write the type shares as \(\mu_t\), and let \(q_{ta}\) be the fraction of type \(t\) voting for option \(a\). Aggregate vote shares are \(v_a=\sum_t\mu_tq_{ta}\). In an atomless society, one voter’s ballot cannot change any \(v_a\), so it cannot change the winner or their expected utility. The sincere, strategic, and third ballots are all payoff-equivalent for an individual. A logit response therefore assigns equal probability to each ballot, regardless of preference type or poll information. The paper’s predictions about information, intermediate-option value, and voter rank disappear; if one instead fixes sincere ballots, the majoritarian winner is simply read off from the type shares.

A stronger version could ask how much mass must change its vote to overturn the majoritarian candidate’s win. That is a coherent continuous robustness question, but it adds an intervention problem the paper does not study. With the paper’s three types and three options, it reduces to a small optimization over vote shares. Generalizing the preference domain might create more computation, but then the question comes from the added robustness model, not from a computational result in this paper.

The honest negative case is strongest against a paper-anchored mirror, not as a proof that no worthwhile extension could ever be designed. High multiplicity itself is not the objection. The obstacle is that the paper’s central strategic effect relies on finite voters being pivotal, while its reported results are behavioral rather than computational. Preserving pivotality requires finite-population uncertainty or another added mechanism; removing it makes the proposed equilibrium computation trivial or indeterminate.
