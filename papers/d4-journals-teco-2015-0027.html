The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses μ_t over finitely many advertiser types \(t=(v_t,b_t)\), rational slot qualities \(α_1>\cdots>α_m>0\), and per-capita supply \(q\) of each slot class, can one compute in time polynomial in \(m,τ,L,\log(1/ε)\) a compact randomized direct mechanism? Each advertiser receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\) and has value \(v_t\sum_{j\in S}α_j\); the mechanism must respect supply \(q\), be ex-interim individually rational and incentive compatible for a tagged advertiser facing a fixed background distribution of other reports, and be ex-post no-positive-transfers, budget-feasible, and Pareto optimal.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with type masses μ over value-budget pairs \(t=(v,b)\), per-capita supplies of heterogeneous slots, and integral bundles for each advertiser. The mechanism uses the tagged advertiser’s report and the aggregate distribution to determine their randomized bundle and payment while assigning the remaining population feasibly.

The objection that survived

The mechanism must define the tagged advertiser’s outcome for off-support reports separately from the aggregate distribution; otherwise a mechanism determined only by μ makes their incentive constraint vacuous.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s randomized, private-budget mechanism for single-dimensional values and indivisible items. It leaves the theorem’s divisible-item and public-budget branch, and the paper’s impossibility results, aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the tagged advertiser’s allocation and payment be defined compactly from their report and the background distribution while preserving the stated incentive and Pareto conditions?
  • Can a clinching construction be implemented with running time depending on support size τ rather than population size?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is narrow. This paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise in a complexity class. Its impossibility results concern mechanism existence and are stated to hold independently of computational complexity. I would not present one of them as a hardness anchor. The closest algorithmic anchor is Theorem 4, together with the paper’s statement that its positive results have polynomial-time algorithms. That supports a high-multiplicity computation question, though it does not already answer one.

Consider a large sponsored-search market with many small advertisers and repeated supplies of several slot qualities. A type is the complete pair \((v,b)\): value per unit of slot quality and budget. Advertisers with the same pair are interchangeable for the model. It is plausible to have \(N\) advertisers but only \(\tau\) such types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of advertisers of type \(t\). To keep competition meaningful as the population grows, let each slot class have supply \(q\) per unit of population. Each advertiser still receives an integral bundle of slots; only the mass of advertisers assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

My lead question is High-Multiplicity Clinching for Heterogeneous Slots. Its input is a finite support of types \(t=(v_t,b_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational qualities \(\alpha_1>\cdots>\alpha_m>0\), common per-capita supply \(q\) of each item, and private budgets. For single-dimensional values, an advertiser’s value for bundle \(S\) is \(v_t\sum_{j\in S}\alpha_j\). The output is a compact representation of a randomized direct mechanism: for each report profile it assigns measurable population mass to integral bundles and payments, with item use at most \(q\) per item. It must be ex-interim IR and IC, and ex-post NPT, budget-feasible, and Pareto optimal. IC compares a tagged advertiser’s reports while holding the mass distribution of everyone else fixed; a unilateral deviation has zero mass. There is no welfare objective to maximize: the solution is a mechanism satisfying the stated properties.

Theorem 4, proved here using the single-item methods of [4], is the anchor. It states that for single-dimensional valuations, indivisible or divisible items, and private budgets, there is a randomized mechanism satisfying IR ex interim, NPT and PO ex post, and IC ex interim. It also states that for single-dimensional valuations and divisible items there is a deterministic mechanism satisfying IR, NPT, PO, and IC for public budgets. I would mirror its private-budget, indivisible-items branch. The paper reduces the single-dimensional heterogeneous-item case to a single item with value \(\tilde v_t=v_t\sum_j\alpha_j\); the continuous question is whether that clinching approach can be computed from type masses rather than individual bidders. The target is running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\) for an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate outcome. I expect Class A: after grouping identical types, the clinching events may depend on \(\tau\) rather than \(N\). That compression is a conjecture to establish, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 4.

This question covers the paper’s single-dimensional mechanism construction. It keeps population continuity distinct from divisible outcomes: each unit of mass still receives an integral bundle, while type-level assignment masses can be fractional. It does not address the paper’s multi-dimensional results.

The weakest point is the strategic limit. In the continuum, one advertiser has no effect on aggregate demand, so the tagged-agent IC condition loses some of the strategic influence present in a finite market. Also, scaling item supply with population is a deliberate co-modification. Both are allowed in this programme, but the authors might regard them as changing the economic question. And although Theorem 4 supplies a credible algorithmic starting point, it does not establish the desired dependence on \(\tau\); that compression is the actual research question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the only anchor, and it is a weak fit for ChoCo’s computational aim. It establishes mechanism existence; the paper’s algorithmic claim is that the finite-agent mechanisms can be computed in polynomial time. The proposed mirror asks whether that computation can be compressed from \(N\) bidders to \(\tau\) exact \((v,b)\)-types. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity question, but it offers no obvious complexity boundary: the paper already reduces the single-dimensional case to one composite good, and identical bidders in the clinching process have identical states. Aggregating each cohort’s mass and budget should collapse many bidder-level events into type-level events. The likely result is an efficient implementation of a mechanism the paper already gives, rather than a new tractability landscape. The existing clinching literature also supplies algorithms for the underlying single-good setting ([Bhattacharya et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.3501)).

The supply choice exposes a further gap between the anchor and its mirror. With the paper’s fixed stock of \(m\) items, each person’s share tends to zero as the population grows. Scaling supply with population avoids that degeneration, but changes the market into one with repeated copies of each slot class. That co-modification is allowed, yet the paper’s single-good reduction does not by itself establish the desired result for that repeated-supply market. And if the mirror restricts allocations to the paper’s all-slots-together bundles, the problem moves even closer to a standard single-good clinching process, where cohort aggregation looks especially direct.

The sharper concern is incentive compatibility. A distribution \(\mu\) records positive-mass reports; a tagged advertiser’s deviation has zero mass. If the mechanism is only a function of \(\mu\), that deviation cannot change its output, so IC is vacuous. To make IC substantive, the model needs a type-contingent allocation and payment rule that also specifies outcomes for off-support reports by a tagged agent. Such a price-taking continuum mechanism is a sensible object, but its strategic condition is not simply the finite auction’s condition with \(N\) replaced by \(\tau\): an individual no longer affects aggregate demand or prices.

These points defeat the proposed support-compression anchor as stated: the theorem is already algorithmic in the finite model, the likely compression seems structurally simple, and the meaningful continuum IC model needs extra machinery. They do not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A tagged-agent continuum clinching question could still be a coherent project; the negative case is strongest as a reason to rank this anchor low, not as proof that every natural version fails.
