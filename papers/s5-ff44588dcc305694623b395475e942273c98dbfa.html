The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(A\), a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational costs \(c_t(a)\), an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon>0\), and the type-level axes-cutting condition that each \(t\in T\) has an alternative \(a_t\) with \(c_t(a_t)>0\) and \(c_{t'}(a_t)=0\) for every \(t'\ne t\), compute a lottery \(x\in\Delta(A)\) and prices \(\pi_t(a)\ge0\) satisfying \(\pi_t(a)=0\) when \(c_t(a)=0\), \(1-\varepsilon\le\pi_t\cdot x\le1+\varepsilon\), \(\pi_t\cdot y\ge\pi_t\cdot x\Rightarrow c_t(y)\ge c_t(x)\) for all \(y\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{A}\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\pi_t(a)\ge1\) with equality whenever \(x(a)>0\); determine whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|A|\), the input encoding length, and \(1/\varepsilon\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity public-bads model with homogeneous cost types \(t\in T\) carrying masses \(\mu_t\); the outcome remains a lottery \(x\in\Delta(A)\), while prices \(\pi_t\) and type-level costs are the decision variables. Equivalently, solve the weighted program \(\max_{\beta_t>0}-\sum_t\mu_t\log\beta_t\) subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t c_t(a)\beta_t\ge1\) for every \(a\in A\), with \(\pi_t(a)=c_t(a)\beta_t\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 6.4's unweighted iteration bound does not establish polynomial-time computation for the weighted type program: the analysis may depend on \(1/\min_t \mu_t\), which is not polynomial in binary encoding length.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers approximate computation of zero-respecting Lindahl equilibria, and hence strictly positive proportional-fair allocations, under type-level axes-cutting. It leaves the general-instance existence failures, core and individual-fair-share results, Flipped-MNW, axiomatic characterizations, and other computation outside scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can weighted Greedy Frank–Wolfe achieve iteration and bit-complexity bounds polynomial in the binary encoding of the masses, rather than in \(1/\min_t\mu_t\)?
  • Can exact strictly positive proportional-fair or zero-respecting Lindahl allocations be computed under type-level axes-cutting?
  • What are the existence and complexity consequences when type-level axes-cutting fails?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow but credible positive case, with one lead anchor: Theorem 6.4.

The natural regime is a large population of residents divided into a small number of genuinely homogeneous exposure types. For example, millions of households may fall into \(\tau\) neighbourhood or infrastructure types, where all residents of type \(t\) incur the same cost \(c_t(a)\) under every public decision \(a\). Let \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\tau\ll N\). The type description includes the complete cost vector and every other parameter relevant to the problem.

The public outcome remains exactly the paper’s object: a lottery \(x\in\Delta(A)\) over public bads. We are not continuizing the alternatives or introducing fractional bads. Continuity enters only through the population distribution. The expected cost to a type is \(c_t(x)=\sum_{a\in A}x(a)c_t(a)\).

My lead problem is \(\textsc{Approx-ZRL-Public-Bads}_{\infty}\). Its instance consists of \(A\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), rational costs \(c_t(a)\), and an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon>0\). We impose the paper’s nondegeneracy assumptions and the type-level analogue of axes-cutting: for every type \(t\), there is an alternative \(a_t\) with \(c_t(a_t)>0\) and \(c_{t'}(a_t)=0\) for every \(t'\ne t\).

The task is to output a lottery \(x\) and type-level personalized prices \(\pi_t(a)\) satisfying:

\( \pi_t(a)=0 \) whenever \(c_t(a)=0\);

\(1-\varepsilon\le \pi_t\cdot x\le 1+\varepsilon\) for every type \(t\);

for every \(y\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{A}\), if \(\pi_t\cdot y\ge\pi_t\cdot x\), then \(c_t(y)\ge c_t(x)\);

and \( \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\pi_t(a)\ge1 \) for every \(a\in A\), with equality whenever \(x(a)>0\).

This is the population-normalized version of the paper’s approximate zero-respecting Lindahl equilibrium. The normalization is harmless: in a finite electorate of size \(N\), one rescales the paper’s prices by \(N\), replacing each individual budget \(1/N\) by a per-agent budget of \(1\), while replacing the unweighted profit sum by the mass-weighted sum above.

The underlying allocation question can equivalently be written as finding a strictly positive-cost lottery satisfying the continuous proportional-fairness inequalities

\( \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\frac{c_t(a)}{c_t(x)}\ge1 \quad\text{for every }a\in A. \)

For rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), this is exactly the finite PF condition after replacing the \(n_t\) identical agents of type \(t\) by their mass. Thus the finite-to-continuous dictionary is exact, while the compressed input has size depending on \(\tau\), not \(N\).

The anchor is the paper’s Theorem 6.4, proved in this paper. It states:

For an axes-cutting instance, let \(a_i\) be an alternative that imposes cost only on agent \(i\), let \(\alpha_i=c_i(a_i)\), and let \(\delta\) be the smallest positive cost in the instance. For every \(0<\varepsilon\le1\), the Greedy Frank–Wolfe algorithm finds an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate zero-respecting Lindahl equilibrium in \(O\!\left(\sum_i\log(\alpha_i/\delta)/\varepsilon^2\right)\) iterations.

The proof uses the convergence theorem cited as Theorem 6.5 from Chaudhury et al. (2024), but Theorem 6.4 and its application to public bads are the authors’ own result.

The continuous question is whether the same computation can be carried out directly on the type-compressed instance, with a bound polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|A|\), the encoding length of the rational costs and masses, and \(1/\varepsilon\), rather than in the expanded population size \(N\). The natural type-level analogue of the paper’s program (16) is

\( \max_{\beta_t>0}-\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log\beta_t \)

subject to

\( \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c_t(a)\beta_t\ge1 \quad\text{for every }a\in A. \)

Its LP subproblems have only \(\tau\) variables. The type-level axes condition supplies the same boundedness mechanism that the paper uses for individual axes. This gives a serious Class A expectation: the paper’s tractable special case appears to survive high-multiplicity compression, and the continuous model exposes the relevant parameters directly.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. It preserves public bads, lotteries, cost minimization, proportional fairness, Lindahl prices, zero-respecting, axes-cutting, and the exact equilibrium concept computed in Section 6. The only substantive change is replacing repeated indistinguishable agents by their mass. Indeed, retaining an individual-level axes-cutting condition would be unnatural: if many agents have exactly the same type, no public alternative can single out one of them while sparing all its clones. The correct population analogue is an alternative that burdens one homogeneous cohort and spares the others.

The scope is deliberately narrow. It mirrors Theorem 6.4’s approximate computation on axes-cutting instances, not the paper’s general-instance impossibility phenomena, Flipped-MNW, or its axiomatic characterizations. Further questions include whether exact strictly positive PF allocations can be computed, whether the dependence on \(\min_t\mu_t\) can be removed, and what happens when axes-cutting fails and positive PF allocations may not exist.

The weakest point is that Theorem 6.4 does not transfer automatically. Its stated bound sums over named agents, and a weighted type-level Frank–Wolfe analysis may introduce dependence on \(1/\min_t\mu_t\). Also, the type-level axes condition is a genuine co-modification of the structural promise. If the authors insist that only individual axes count, high multiplicity destroys the promise. I think that objection is survivable: the type-level condition is forced by indistinguishability and is natural for localized environmental or infrastructure burdens. But the weighted convergence theorem and its bit-complexity guarantee would have to be proved; they should not be presented as already established by Theorem 6.4.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is not strong enough to defeat this anchor. Theorem 6.4 is a named computational result, not merely an axiomatic or analytic statement, and its finite-to-high-multiplicity translation is unusually clean. If \(n_t\) agents share type \(t\), then

\[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_i\frac{c_i(a)}{c_i(x)} = \sum_t \mu_t\frac{c_t(a)}{c_t(x)}. \]

Thus the type-compressed proportional-fairness condition is exactly the finite condition on a repeated electorate. Nothing essential depends on agent identities. A population of residents grouped by exposure or neighbourhood type is also a credible high-multiplicity regime, and alternatives that burden one entire cohort while sparing the others are a natural public-bads scenario.

There are real defects in the proponent’s formulation, but they are proof gaps rather than fundamental obstructions. The paper’s theorem does not transfer automatically to the weighted program

\[ \max_{\beta_t>0}-\sum_t\mu_t\log\beta_t \]

subject to

\[ \sum_t\mu_t c_t(a)\beta_t\ge 1. \]

Under a type-level axes condition, the axis alternative for type \(t\) implies

\[ \beta_t\ge \frac{1}{\mu_t c_t(a_t)}, \]

so the naïve Frank–Wolfe analysis can depend on \(1/\min_t\mu_t\). Since masses are binary-encoded, that dependence need not be polynomial in the input length. Theorem 6.4 therefore cannot simply be cited as an established polynomial-time high-multiplicity theorem; a weighted convergence and bit-complexity analysis is required.

The axes promise is also not inherited from the individual model. Repeating a type destroys individual axes-cutting: no public alternative can burden one clone while sparing the others. But replacing it by cohort-level axes-cutting is precisely the kind of population-forced co-modification that the programme permits, and it has an intelligible interpretation. Rejecting it merely because it differs from the literal finite promise would be too weak.

Nor do the paper’s negative examples rescue the case against continuization. Failure of strictly positive PF, completion-core failure, and the need for Flipped-MNW all remain meaningful weighted questions. One could study whether these phenomena depend on vanishing type masses, whether weighted convex programs compute the relevant rules, or whether new continuum-specific hardness appears. Their answers may be trivial, hard, or surprising; none makes the question malformed.

So the honest negative conclusion is limited: the proposed mirror is not yet a theorem, and its stated complexity claim overreaches until the dependence on the masses is resolved. But I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. This paper supplies a particularly credible Class A candidate: a public-bads computation with a genuine high-multiplicity interpretation, an exact finite-to-continuous dictionary, and a concrete weighted optimization problem. The anchor survives.
