The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(k\), let a society have finite-support rational distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) over seller and buyer valuation schedules \(w\) and \(v\), respectively, with each schedule increasing and submodular. Give each pair \((w,v)\) mass \(\mu_w\nu_v\), and let \(q_p(w,v)\) be the quantity traded under the paper’s sequential fixed-price mechanism at common per-unit price \(p\). Determine the worst-case population guarantee \(\rho_k^{\mathrm{pop}}=\inf_{\mu,\nu}\sup_{p\ge 0} W_{\mu,\nu}(p)/O_{\mu,\nu}\), where \(W_{\mu,\nu}(p)=\sum_{w,v}\mu_w\nu_v\bigl(v(q_p(w,v))+w(k-q_p(w,v))\bigr)\) and \(O_{\mu,\nu}=\sum_{w,v}\mu_w\nu_v\max_{0\le q\le k}\bigl(v(q)+w(k-q)\bigr)\).

The model it lives in

The society is a mass of buyer–seller trade opportunities, typed by complete valuation-schedule pairs \((w,v)\); independent seller and buyer masses induce pair mass \(\mu_w\nu_v\). One common per-unit price \(p\) governs every opportunity, and the objective is aggregate welfare divided by aggregate first-best welfare.

The objection that survived

The population formulation inherits the paper’s distributional optimization and bounds verbatim, so its contribution is a new population interpretation rather than new optimization content.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the single-unit bounds in Theorem 1.1 and the multi-unit upper bounds in Theorem 6.1. The same aggregation also carries Theorem 1.2 and the symmetric ratio corollaries; this anchored claim does not pursue them separately.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the exact value of \(\rho_k^{\mathrm{pop}}\) and close the gaps between the guaranteed ratios and explicit bad populations.
  • Determine whether \(\rho_k^{\mathrm{pop}}\) decreases with \(k\).

The case FOR (proponent)

I think there is one credible mirror: a large pooled platform running many bilateral trades under one common tariff. The agents are the buyer–seller pairs in those trades. A pair’s type is its complete valuation schedule; mass is the fraction of trading opportunities with that type. This is an actual population of repeated market opportunities, not uncertainty about the values in one trade.

For \(k\) units, let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be finite-support distributions with rational masses over seller schedules \(w\) and buyer schedules \(v\). Assume buyer and seller types are paired independently, as in the paper. A pair of type \((w,v)\) then has mass \(\mu_w\nu_v\). The regime is millions of opportunities but relatively few distinct schedules on each side, so the number of types is small compared with the population. A platform with one posted per-unit tariff \(p\) applies the paper’s sequential fixed-price mechanism to each pair. If \(q_p(w,v)\) units trade, aggregate welfare and the pairwise first-best benchmark are

\[ W_{\mu,\nu}(p)=\sum_{w,v}\mu_w\nu_v\bigl(v(q_p(w,v))+w(k-q_p(w,v))\bigr), \]

\[ O_{\mu,\nu}=\sum_{w,v}\mu_w\nu_v\max_{0\le q\le k}\bigl(v(q)+w(k-q)\bigr). \]

The price is chosen from the publicly known population masses, and is the same for every pair. For \(k=1\), this is exactly the paper’s seller-value/buyer-value trade rule. Rational masses make the society a high-multiplicity instance: a sufficiently large finite population can realize those frequencies exactly.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Population Fixed-Price Guarantee. For a supplied society \((k,\mu,\nu)\), find a price maximizing \(W_{\mu,\nu}(p)/O_{\mu,\nu}\). Its global version asks for the worst-case guarantee

\[ \rho_k^{\mathrm{pop}} =\inf_{\mu,\nu}\sup_{p\ge0}\frac{W_{\mu,\nu}(p)}{O_{\mu,\nu}}, \]

over admissible finite-support societies. A solution to the global problem consists of a price-selection rule guaranteeing a lower bound for every society, together with bad finite-support societies witnessing an upper bound. This is a precise continuous-population question; it is a question about welfare guarantees, not a claim that the paper proves NP-hardness.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It states that \(r_k^*\le r_1^*\) for every \(k\ge2\), and gives upper bounds for \(k=2,\ldots,8\): respectively \(0.7291, 0.7277, 0.7284, 0.7276, 0.7272, 0.7286,\) and \(0.7278\). The accompanying construction supplies, for each listed \(k\), a population on which every common price has welfare ratio at most the stated bound. The paper calls these *hardness* results in the approximation-ratio sense: they limit how good a fixed-price mechanism can be. They are not complexity-theoretic hardness results. The theorem is proved here, through Lemma 6.1 and Propositions 6.1 and 6.2; the paper reports that the numerical optimization used Gurobi, and omits full proofs for space.

For this mirror, Theorem 6.1 says that the multi-unit pooled population’s worst-case fixed-price guarantee is no better than the single-unit guarantee, with the listed stronger caps for \(k=2,\ldots,8\). The hard instances are societies of mass over full valuation schedules, not merely randomized outcomes for one trade. I consider this the strongest anchor because it gives concrete adverse populations and connects the single- and multi-unit questions.

My second anchor is Theorem 1.1, also proved here. It says there is a single-unit fixed-price mechanism with approximation ratio at least \(0.7292\) on every instance, and there is an instance on which no fixed-price mechanism achieves more than \(0.73805\). Thus it brackets \(\rho_1^{\mathrm{pop}}\) between those numbers. The lower bound uses the paper’s mechanism selecting among 32 normalized basic prices; the upper bound comes from the constructed hard instance. Propositions 4.1 and 4.2 support the theorem, again using numerical optimization whose full proof details are omitted from the paper.

The connection is unusually direct. In the paper, \(w\) and \(v\) are random independent valuations and welfare is their expected welfare. In the mirror, they are actual masses of opportunities, independently paired. Integrating welfare over the product population gives exactly the same expressions as taking the paper’s expectations. So the named theorems carry over to this population problem, including the approximation barriers. This is a transfer of the paper’s answers to a distinct population interpretation, not new evidence that computational hardness dissolves in a continuum.

For an explicitly supplied finite-support society, the price optimization itself is tractable: welfare changes only at the finitely many marginal values, so one can check those breakpoints and the intervals between them. That exact per-society problem is not what Theorems 1.1 and 6.1 settle; their anchors concern the worst-case guarantee across societies. Further questions include the exact value of \(\rho_k^{\mathrm{pop}}\), whether it decreases with \(k\), and how the answer changes for symmetric populations or for non-independent pairings.

The scope I am claiming is narrow: the fixed-price welfare guarantees for single-unit and multi-unit bilateral trade in Theorems 1.1 and 6.1. I am not treating every mechanism-design result in the paper as a population problem.

The weak point is that the paper already studies distributions over valuations, so the equations line up exactly once the setting is recast as a mass of independent trade opportunities. The authors may see this as a useful large-market interpretation of their Bayesian model rather than a new computational problem. The mirror remains defensible because the agents and masses have changed meaning: it describes a deterministic high-volume platform with one tariff across many markets, rather than one uncertain buyer–seller pair. But the paper’s anchors provide approximation guarantees, not a new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is a novelty collision. The paper already takes distributions over complete valuation types as its inputs and computes expected welfare under a single price. Given rational masses \(\mu_w\) and \(\nu_v\), form a deterministic population of buyer–seller opportunities with mass \(\mu_w\nu_v\) for each pair \((w,v)\). For every price, aggregate welfare in that population equals the paper’s expected welfare exactly. With rational masses, a finite population can realize those proportions exactly. The change is in the story attached to the distributions; the optimization problem stays the same.

That defeats Theorem 1.1 as a distinct mirror. Its lower and upper bounds already concern the best fixed-price guarantee over distributions of single-unit valuations. Interpreting those distributions as population frequencies gives the same guarantee and the same bad instances. For a supplied finite-support population, choosing its best price is a separate, straightforward task: welfare changes only at the finitely many marginal values, so evaluate those values and one price in each interval between them. The paper’s theorem concerns the guarantee across all such instances; asking for that guarantee again is asking the paper’s question under a population interpretation.

Theorem 6.1 has the same collision for multi-unit schedules. Its product distributions over seller and buyer valuation functions define precisely the mass over opportunity types in the proposed mirror. Thus its bound \(r_k^*\le r_1^*\), and its bounds for \(k=2,\ldots,8\), transfer unchanged. The paper’s “hardness” here limits achievable welfare approximation; it does not establish computational hardness. Treating the instances as a large population adds no complexity question to those results.

A stronger high-multiplicity version could ask for an algorithm whose running time depends on the number of distinct schedules and their encoding length. For explicit finite support, the same breakpoint scan answers the price-selection problem in polynomial time. A question about richer mechanisms, correlated pairing, or type-specific prices might be worthwhile, but those changes no longer mirror the fixed-price results these anchors rely on.

This is a strong objection to the proposed anchors, though it cannot establish that *no* useful population question can be devised for bilateral trade. The proponent’s large-platform story is plausible; its weakness is that the paper’s existing distributional model already supplies the mathematics and answers the named questions.
