The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(A\) with binary-rational population fractions \(\alpha_a\) summing to \(1\), seat types \(B\) with binary-rational per-capita capacities \(\beta_b\), compatible pairs \(E\), and bounded integer utilities \(u_i(a,b)\), find a nonnegative allocation \(x_{ab}\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\beta_b\) whose profile \((P_1(x),\ldots,P_r(x))\), with \(P_i(x)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}u_i(a,b)x_{ab}\), is lexicographically maximal.

The model it lives in

A bipartite type-mass matching model: applicant fractions \(\alpha_a\), seat capacities \(\beta_b\), compatible type pairs \(E\), and allocation variables \(x_{ab}\geq 0\); maximize the vector of aggregate utilities lexicographically, with each individual still receiving at most one seat.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers lexicographic profile optimization for high-multiplicity school assignment; it leaves the paper’s weight lower bounds, fair and weight-maximal runtime improvements, maximum-weight verification reduction, and finite-data experiments outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s mixed-radix reduction be extended to rational type masses with weights of polynomial encoding length, without expanding each unit into an individual vertex?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4, proved in the paper. In the first English rendering supplied, it says that assigning each edge the mixed-radix weight
\[ w_{\mathrm{mixed}}(e)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}u_i(e)\prod_{j=i+1}^{r}(2U_j+1) \]
makes a maximum-weight matching profile-optimal: it lexicographically maximizes the sums of the utilities. The duplicated OCR rendering labels this same claim “Lemma 4.” Section 4’s Algorithm 1 gives the resulting running time \(O(m\sqrt n(\log n+\sum_i\log U_i))\). The paper has no named hardness result, so the case here is for an algorithmic mirror.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large city or regional school-placement market. Let \(N\) be the number of applicants; applicants can be grouped by their complete preference list, eligibility, and commuting-zone information, while seats are grouped by school program and quota class. Those descriptions determine compatibility and every utility used in the problem. A scaled-up market might have hundreds of thousands of applicants but only hundreds or thousands of applicant types and relatively few seat types. The paper’s own school-choice example supplies the ingredients, though its 1,414 students are not themselves a high-multiplicity regime.

I would ask the following continuous problem. Continuous Profile-Based Matching. The input is finite sets of applicant types \(A\) and seat types \(B\); binary-rational masses \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_b\), each summing to one; a set of compatible type pairs \(E\subseteq A\times B\); and \(r\) integer utility functions \(u_i:E\to\{0,\ldots,U_i\}\), with the paper’s priority order. Find nonnegative masses \(x_{ab}\) such that
\[ \sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\alpha_a,\qquad \sum_{a:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\beta_b, \]
and whose profile \((P_1(x),\ldots,P_r(x))\), where \(P_i(x)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}u_i(a,b)x_{ab}\), is lexicographically maximal. The output is the type-pair mass allocation \(x\). Each person still receives at most one seat: \(x_{ab}\) records how much of the type-\(a\) population is matched to type \(b\), not a fractional seat for an individual.

This problem is a linear optimization problem solved by optimizing \(P_1\), fixing its optimum, then optimizing \(P_2\), and continuing through \(P_r\). Thus I expect it to be Class A, tractable, in time polynomial in the type supports, \(|E|\), \(r\), and the binary input length. It asks the paper’s underlying question—lexicographic profile matching—over a population represented by type masses. It does not ask whether the paper’s particular one-weight reduction continues to work.

That distinction is the weak point. Theorem 4’s mixed-radix reduction is for discrete matchings; over arbitrary rational masses, a higher-priority profile difference can be arbitrarily small, so those same fixed bases need not preserve lexicographic order. The continuous question therefore does not inherit the theorem’s particular algorithm. I still think the mirror is credible: the agents, compatibility constraints, utility priorities, and objective are the same, while mass is the natural representation of a large population with repeated full types. Since the masses are binary rationals, an exact discrete-to-continuous dictionary is also a natural follow-up: determine when scaling masses yields an exactly realizable discrete matching and how the required scale compares with the compressed LP input. Another follow-up is whether the paper’s minimum-cost rank-maximal variant remains tractable when commuting cost is added as a final priority.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s general input is an arbitrary bipartite graph with utilities attached to individual edges. To preserve that instance exactly when grouping vertices, a type must retain its full compatibility pattern and every utility value. In general, most vertices will then be their own type, so replacing them with masses gives no compression. The school-choice experiment makes this concern concrete: exact commuting distances can distinguish students even when their rankings match.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the proposed mirror. Rank-maximal school placement can group applicants by complete preference list and eligibility, with school programs represented by capacities. Those are natural recurring types in a large market, and the paper’s profile objective applies directly. For the minimum-cost variant, commuting information can be included in the type when it genuinely repeats. The support may grow in some settings, but the programme asks whether a sensible high-multiplicity regime exists, not whether every instance compresses.

The mixed-radix theorem itself would need restatement. With rational masses, profile differences need not be whole-number differences, so the paper’s fixed bases need not preserve their order. But the better mirror is lexicographic optimization over the mass allocation, as proposed. That is a well-posed computational question for the same profile objective; failure of this particular scalarization would not defeat it.

So the negative case is weak. The paper’s broad arbitrary-graph formulation offers no guarantee of useful type compression, and its exact-distance experiment may have high support. But the school-placement ranking problem supplies a plausible regime with repeated complete types, and neither concern rules out the continuous profile-matching question. I cannot honestly defeat the proponent’s Theorem 4 anchor across the best scenario.
