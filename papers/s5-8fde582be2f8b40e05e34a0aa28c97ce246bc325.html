What the mirror covers

The nearest candidate is the sincere-voting Condorcet method applied to a distribution of rankings, but it is not anchored by a named computational result in this paper. The strategic Weakest Link and Sequential Condorcet claims are not continuized here.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational-complexity result. Its closest numbered result is Bag, Sabourian and Winter (2009, Proposition 3), cited in the paper: it says that one-round voting procedures, including STV, cannot in general guarantee selection of a Condorcet winner when voters act strategically. That is a result about voting-procedure outcomes, not a classification of a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. The paper’s claims about Weakest Link and the Sequential Condorcet procedure are also not stated as numbered complexity results. So there are no valid anchors here.

The best nearby continuous question is Continuous Condorcet Identification, mirroring the paper’s section on sincere voters. Let \(C\) be a finite set of options, and let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over the support of complete rankings of \(C\). Given this society, return its Condorcet winner if one exists, or report that none exists. An option \(c\) is the winner exactly when, for every \(d\ne c\),
\[ \sum_{\succ:\,c\succ d}\mu_{\succ}>\frac12. \]
This is a plausible population model for a large referendum: a type is a complete ranking, and its mass is the fraction of voters with that ranking. The authors explicitly discuss using submitted rankings to identify the Condorcet winner. The problem is tractable by computing the pairwise totals over the \(\tau\) supported rankings, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). It covers only the sincere-voting discussion, and it is not anchored by a qualifying result in this paper.

The serious limitation is that sincere Condorcet counting is already straightforward in a finite electorate, so the continuous formulation offers little complexity story. More importantly, the paper’s central case concerns strategic voting. In an atomless population, a single voter has zero mass and cannot change a tally; that removes the pivotal influence driving the paper’s strategic analysis. Preserving it would require an additional model of strategic blocs or some other source of positive individual influence, which the paper does not supply.

So the strongest positive case is that the population distribution is a natural representation for the paper’s sincere Condorcet question. But there is no honest anchor-specific continuous problem to hand to a prover under the requested criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to continuize. The paper does not analyze the complexity of computing an outcome, an equilibrium, or a manipulation. The cited Bag, Sabourian and Winter Proposition 3 says that one-round procedures cannot guarantee a Condorcet winner under strategic voting; it is an outcome guarantee, not a complexity result. The Weakest Link discussion makes similar claims about what a procedure selects under behavioral assumptions. On the requested anchor standard, the paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

The proposed Continuous Condorcet Identification problem is coherent and fits a large referendum: for each pair \(c,d\), compute
\[ P_\mu(c,d)=\sum_{\succ:\,c\succ d}\mu_{\succ}, \]
then report a Condorcet winner if one exists. Extending this to the top cycle is also natural. But this is a newly framed computational task, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper. The sincere-voting section already describes mechanical pairwise counting; it asks what voting rule identifies the winner, not how to solve a computational problem. A mass-based robustness or manipulation problem could make the computation more substantial, but it would add a cost or intervention objective the paper does not formulate. The tally is not disqualified merely for being simple; the difficulty is that the paper gives no result or computational question for it to continue.

The more faithful candidate is a continuous version of the strategic Weakest Link or Sequential Condorcet procedure. That runs into a deeper problem. In an atomless referendum population, one voter has zero mass, so a unilateral change of ballot cannot alter any round’s support or elimination. The individual strategic incentives central to the paper disappear; under outcome-based payoffs, any unilateral report leaves the outcome unchanged. Treating each ranking type’s entire mass as one strategic player would restore influence, but it turns preference groups into coordinating blocs. That is a different strategic model from the paper’s individual voters. The other salient setting, Parliament, preserves individual influence but has a small, individuated electorate rather than a high-multiplicity regime.

A minimum-mass manipulation problem with ranking-change costs is the strongest computational rescue: it would combine the paper’s discussion of strategic misreports with a continuous electorate. Still, it asks how much coordinated mass must change its report, not how the paper’s individual voters behave in equilibrium. It is a plausible adjacent project, but the paper does not supply that objective or identify it as its problem.

I would argue against a worthwhile continuous mirror on the evidence in this paper, while conceding that the universal claim is not airtight. Continuous Condorcet counting is a sensible population computation, and a separate mass-robustness problem might be valuable. Neither, as presented, is a computational result of this paper being continuized.
