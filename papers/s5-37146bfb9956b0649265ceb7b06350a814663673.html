The continuous mirror question

Given a finite list of exchange-network templates \(G_q\), each with rational edge weights, a valid initial message vector, positive mass \(\lambda_q\), and a unique Nash bargaining solution with positive gap \(\sigma_q\), suppose a continuum of copies of each template bargains only within its own copy. How many rounds suffice to compute role earnings \(\hat\gamma_{q,i}\) satisfying \(\max_{q,i}|\hat\gamma_{q,i}-\gamma^*_{q,i}|\le\varepsilon\), as a function of the support size, input length, gaps, weights, and \(\varepsilon\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a rational mixture of isolated network copies: type \((q,i)\) is role \(i\) in template \(G_q\), with mass \(\mu_{q,i}=\lambda_q/\sum_r\lambda_r|V(G_r)|\). Each template has one shared message vector evolving by the paper’s update rule; the output is an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate earnings value for each positive-mass role. The theorem supplies a per-template round bound, with no separate optimization objective.

The objection that survived

The stated bound depends on \(W/\sigma\), which can be exponential in the binary input length, so it does not establish polynomial bit complexity without an additional conditioning bound.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s earnings-convergence guarantee for each template. It leaves aside Theorem 3’s recovery of exact trading pairs and Theorem 1’s fixed-point correspondence.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the dependence on \(W/\sigma\) be improved to a polynomial dependence on its binary encoding length?
  • Does a convergence guarantee persist when copies interact through shared type-level bargaining pools?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: this paper supports a continuous mirror for populations made up of many repeated exchange networks. Its named results are algorithmic, not hardness results, so the best anchor is the main convergence theorem.

Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that if a finite exchange network \(G\) has a unique Nash bargaining solution with positive gap \(\sigma\), then its natural dynamics converges to that solution’s earnings. For every valid initial message vector and error tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), after

\[ T^*(n,\sigma,\varepsilon) = C n^7\left(\frac{W}{\sigma}+\log\left(1+\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)\right) \]

rounds, every player’s estimated earnings are within \(\varepsilon\) of their Nash bargaining earnings. Here \(n\) is the number of vertices, \(W\) is the maximum edge weight, and \(C\) is independent of the instance.

A concrete mirror is Role-Continuum Bargaining Convergence. An instance is a finite rational mixture of exchange-network templates \(G_q\), each with edge surpluses, an initial message vector, and a unique Nash bargaining solution with positive gap. Imagine a continuum of copies of each template \(G_q\), with mass \(\lambda_q\). A type is \((q,i)\): a participant’s template and role \(i\) within it. Its mass is the fraction of the population occupying that role; after normalization, \(\mu_{q,i}=\lambda_q/Z\), where \(Z=\sum_q\lambda_q|V(G_q)|\). Copies of a template have the same graph, surpluses, and initial messages. Bargaining stays within each copy, and each participant can trade with at most one neighbor, as in the paper.

The action variables are the messages on role-to-role edges and the mass \(\pi_{q,M}\) of template-\(q\) copies whose resulting matching is \(M\). Each copy receives an ordinary integral matching; aggregate matching and earnings can be fractional because they are measured over mass. The underlying objective remains the paper’s maximum-total-surplus matching objective, with the stable and balanced outcome selected by Nash bargaining. The computational task is to return earnings \(\hat\gamma_{q,i}\) satisfying

\[ \max_{q,i}\left|\hat\gamma_{q,i}-\gamma^*_{q,i}\right|\le\varepsilon. \]

This is a convergence problem, so its solution is the approximate earnings vector; it has no separate optimization objective.

The expected direction is tractable. Run the paper’s dynamics on each template. If \(n_q=|V(G_q)|\), there are \(\tau=\sum_q n_q\) role types, and the total number of rounds is bounded by a polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length, \(W/\sigma\), and \(\log(1+\sigma/\varepsilon)\), taking \(\sigma\) to be the smallest template gap. Theorem 2 gives the per-template guarantee directly; integrating earnings over the copies preserves the same error bound. The continuous question is therefore answered by a discrete-to-continuous identity: the copywise convergence guarantee yields the aggregate guarantee. The result mirrors the paper’s computational question—how quickly the natural bargaining process reaches the Nash bargaining outcome—rather than replacing it with an equilibrium-existence or outcome-continuity question.

This regime has a plausible setting: a large society of repeated local exchange institutions, such as recurring regional or cohort-based markets, with many agents in the same role and relatively few distinct role types. Each type records the template, neighborhood, and surplus values used by the problem, so agents grouped together really are indistinguishable for its purposes. The population can be very large while \(\tau\) stays small. Authors studying a local bargaining process should recognize the mirror: it preserves their messages, one-exchange constraint, stability, balance, and convergence target.

The weakness is that the copies do not bargain across templates. The mirror aggregates repeated finite institutions; it does not establish convergence for one large network where agents of the same type interact across the population. That makes this a limited, relatively direct mirror, but still a credible high-multiplicity regime. The natural next questions are whether the theorem extends to shared type-level pools, heterogeneous initial messages across copies, or a single measured network with per-capita capacities.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it is a genuine computational result. The proposed mirror, though, leaves its dynamics unchanged: copies of a template never bargain across copies, and each copy runs the paper’s finite-network process independently. The mass variables only average the resulting earnings afterward. Neither the number of copies nor their masses affect the runtime; \(\tau\) counts the roles in the finite templates. That is a valid replication identity, but a thin continuous mirror: it adds no population-level computational question.

A more integrated mirror would let large pools of agents bargain across type classes and track matching and earnings by mass. That is more interesting, but preserving this paper’s dynamics requires preserving who can bargain with whom, since each message update depends on a player’s particular neighbors and best alternatives. In a general sparse network, a type must therefore encode enough of the neighborhood and edge-weight structure to distinguish most vertices, so the support can grow with the population. If instead one uses interchangeable type-to-type pools, one has chosen a special block-structured market. It may be a sensible model, but its continuum dynamics and mass matching are an extension of the paper’s fixed-graph problem, not a population limit that retains its general network structure. The exact partner result in Theorem 3 also cannot be recovered from type-level masses, though that is not needed for the earnings guarantee in Theorem 2.

There is a further qualification to the proponent’s runtime claim: the stated bound depends on \(W/\sigma\), which need not be polynomial in the binary input length of rational weights. That does not make the convergence question uninteresting, but it weakens the claim that the proposed mirror directly yields a polynomial-time algorithm in the usual encoded-input sense.

So the strongest negative case is that the concrete mirror is computationally vacuous at the population level, while the more substantive version needs a restrictive new network model. I cannot make the universal claim airtight: a large typed market with interchangeable bargaining partners is plausible, and its authors might recognize a continuous mass-bargaining version as their problem. The case against is strongest on limited payoff, not on there being no worthwhile scenario at all.
