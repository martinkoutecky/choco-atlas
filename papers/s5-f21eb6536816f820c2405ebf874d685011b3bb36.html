The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying anchor in this paper, so I can’t make a positive case that meets the stated bar. Its named theorems are axiomatic characterizations, not results about an algorithm’s complexity.

Theorem 3, proved here, says that on acyclic weighted hypergraph communication situations, the \(\beta\)-power surplus solution is the unique solution whose decomposable \(\beta\)-conference function set satisfies component efficiency and balanced conference contributions. Theorem 5, also proved here, gives a second uniqueness characterization using component efficiency, strong conference monotonicity, conference symmetry, and connectedness. Neither theorem says that a computational problem is tractable or hard.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be an industry with many firms sharing conference memberships, bargaining weights, and project roles. One could represent firm types by those attributes and their masses by population fractions. The paper’s majority point game also suggests replacing vote-count thresholds with fractional thresholds. But turning either idea into a continuous problem of computing allocations would require choices about coalition worths and how to extend the paper’s Shapley-based solution to a continuum. The paper neither makes those choices nor proves a named computational result about the underlying allocation problem. Its applications and axiomatic results therefore do not supply the required anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Neither Theorem 3 nor Theorem 5 is a computational result. Each characterizes a solution by axioms on finite, acyclic weighted hypergraph situations; neither states the complexity of deciding, optimizing, or computing anything. The paper’s applications do not supply a missing complexity theorem. So there is no named computational result here for a continuous mirror to preserve or transform.

A plausible high multiplicity story does exist: many firms could share the same complete type, including their conference memberships, weights, and bargaining role. One could describe their population by masses, use fractional thresholds in the majority example, and seek per-type allocation rates. I would not reject that on the ground that firms cannot have repeated types.

The difficulty is that this would require specifying a new computational problem. The paper permits an arbitrary characteristic function \(v\), with a value for every coalition, and a broad class of power indices \(\beta\). It gives no compact input format for \(v\), no computational specification for \(\beta\), and no algorithmic task or complexity claim. In a continuous version, one would also have to define coalition worths over type masses and choose how to extend the finite Shapley-based allocation. Those choices could support a worthwhile project, but they would supply the computational model and question that this paper itself does not provide.

The majority point game is the strongest lead: replacing vote counts with mass thresholds could yield a coherent continuous allocation model. But its result here is still an application of the proposed solution, not a computational claim. Even the best mirror someone could build from it would be a new complexity study of a newly specified allocation problem, rather than a continuization of a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchor for ChoCo. It is weaker as a universal claim that no related continuous computation could be worthwhile: a carefully specified high-multiplicity allocation problem might be. The paper gives no basis for saying that project is worth the programme’s time.
