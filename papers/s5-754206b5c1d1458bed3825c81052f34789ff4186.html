The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a credible positive case for a continuous-population mirror of this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result of the kind your anchors require.

The closest item is Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper: it says the axiom system \(AX\) is sound and complete for the logic of relative likelihood over partially ordered preferential structures. That is an axiomatization result, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. Theorem 2.10 gives representation results for orders on sets of worlds; it also does not classify a computational problem. The paper does report that validity for the logic is co-NP-complete, but it attributes that fact to Friedman and Halpern (1994) in the related-work discussion. It is not a numbered result of this paper.

A population mirror would require types such as world descriptions together with their positions in a likelihood preorder, with mass representing fractions of agents or worlds of each type. But the paper gives no population action, cost, or objective to carry over. Its semantics compare sets of possible worlds using an order; replacing that order with population mass would change the question. Treating its potentially infinite world space as a continuous society would continuize the space of worlds, not the population.

So there is no eligible anchor here and no continuous problem I can state as the paper’s analogue under your criteria. The soft spot is decisive: a population scenario would need to be supplied from outside the paper, and the computational question would then belong to a new model of collective reasoning rather than a mirror the authors are likely to recognize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the level of the paper’s subject: it has no computational result about a population. Theorem 3.1 proves a soundness and completeness result for an axiomatization. Theorem 2.10 represents certain orderings on sets of worlds. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem. The paper’s co-NP-completeness statement concerns validity, but it attributes that result to Friedman and Halpern (1994), in related work; it is not a result of this paper.

The best attempt to make a mirror would give each agent a type describing their preferential structure over worlds, put a mass \(\mu_t\) on each type, and ask how a collective likelihood judgment changes when mass moves between types. That could be a worthwhile new computational social choice problem. But the paper supplies no collective judgment rule, intervention, cost, or optimization objective for it. Its worlds are possible states being compared, not agents being aggregated. Putting mass on worlds would change the qualitative, order-based semantics into a measure-based one; putting mass on reasoners would add a social choice layer absent from the paper.

The other plausible attempt—continuizing the world space—doesn’t rescue a population mirror. The paper already allows \(W\) to be infinite, and its definitions address infinite domains. The multiplicity of worlds can matter as a supply of distinct witnesses in representation arguments, but that is not a fractional population quantity. Reweighting those witnesses does not express a question the paper asks.

A convincing collective-reasoning scenario is imaginable, so universal impossibility would be too strong. The firmer conclusion is that any such scenario needs a new computational problem and a new aggregation model. It would not mirror a named computational result of this paper.
