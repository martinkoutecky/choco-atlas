What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort version covers classifier-induced allocations and critical payments, while leaving aside the paper’s individual-bid result and its \(O(1)\) and \(\Omega(m)\) payment bounds; treating a cohort as one bidder makes it a group-mechanism extension.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a type-cohort accuracy auction. It continues the population, while keeping the paper’s classifier-induced allocation and truthful elicitation question. Its anchor is Corollary 1, proved here: for the paper’s learning-induced allocation rule, there is a payment rule making the auction dominant-strategy incentive-compatible, so each user’s best bid is their true value regardless of others’ bids. Theorem 1 supplies the key property: holding other bids fixed, raising a user’s bid cannot make their prediction less accurate.

Here is the continuous problem. An instance gives a finite support \(T\) of types \(t=(x_t,y_t,v_t)\), with \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), \(y_t\in\{-1,1\}\), \(v_t\in[0,V]\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. The regime is a large institution’s repeated classification task: many users share the same complete feature, label, and value description, so the population size is much larger than the support size \(\tau=|T|\). A type cohort submits a common per-person bid \(b_t\). For linear scores and a convex decreasing margin loss \(\ell\), the reported bids induce
\[ w(b)\in\arg\min_w\left\{\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t b_t\,\ell\!\left(y_t\langle w,x_t\rangle\right)+\lambda\|w\|_2^2\right\}. \]
The allocation is \(a_t(b)=\mathbf{1}\{y_t\langle w(b),x_t\rangle\ge 0\}\). The problem, which I would call Type-Mass Accuracy Auction, is: given this instance and a report vector \(b\), compute the classifier, the cohort allocations, and per-person critical payments \(q_t\). Charge \(q_t=0\) to a losing cohort; for a winning cohort, set \(q_t\) to the least report in \([0,V]\) that would still win with the other reports fixed. The total payment collected from cohort \(t\) is \(\mu_t q_t\). Truthful reports aim to maximize the true mass-weighted welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t a_t\); as in the paper, the learned allocation optimizes a reported-value surrogate objective, not necessarily exact \(0\)-\(1\) welfare.

I expect the finite-support problem to be tractable to additive price accuracy: each reported classifier is a convex optimization problem, and monotone allocation makes each critical price a one-dimensional threshold that can be approximated by search. That is a Class A expectation for numerical computation, with running time naturally stated in \(d,\tau,L\), and the desired precision. Exact critical prices and an exact polynomial bit-complexity claim need their own proof; the paper’s numerical payment procedures do not establish those claims for this cohort model.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the paper’s learning-induced allocation and DSIC payment result, not all of the paper’s sample-size payment bounds. This is a problem its authors could plausibly recognize: the same welfare objective, same classifier-feasible allocations, and same incentive question are present, with population mass replacing headcount. The weak point is the strategic unit. The paper gives each sampled user an individual bid; this mirror makes a homogeneous cohort report together. For an atomless feature-and-value distribution, a single user has zero influence on the classifier, so the literal nonatomic auction would lose the paper’s pivotal-payment question. The cohort regime is therefore the credible positive case, but it is a real modeling choice the authors would need to accept.

The paper does not give a named P/NP, FPT, or parameterized-complexity classification to anchor. Corollary 1 is a named mechanism-design result, not a complexity theorem. This mirror’s computational question is consequently about computing the classifier and critical prices for a mass-based version of the paper’s auction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proponent’s anchor is not a computational result. Theorem 1 proves monotonicity, and Corollary 1 uses it to establish that truthful payments exist. Neither classifies the complexity of a problem. Section 6 gives ways to compute payments for the finite training auction, but the proposed mirror asks again for implementation—solve a weighted classifier and find payment thresholds—not for a continuous-population complexity question that the paper’s results leave open.

The more fundamental issue is the strategic unit. In the paper, each sampled user can change their own bid. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass, so their bid cannot change the learned classifier. Their critical payment is then zero: a winning prediction does not depend on their bid, and a losing one cannot be bought by that individual. This is a genuine limit degeneration of the auction’s pivotal-payment question.

The proposed type-cohort auction avoids that degeneration by making the whole mass of a type a single bidder. That is the best version of the mirror: its weighted objective is coherent, monotonicity extends to a type’s bid, and convex optimization plus threshold search can compute its outcome. But the incentive guarantee is now for a coordinated bloc, not for the individual users in the paper. Having identical features, labels, and values makes users one type; it does not make them one strategic actor. This could be a sensible group-mechanism extension, but it changes the question Corollary 1 answers.

There is no faithful intermediate version that preserves both nonzero influence and individual deviations. With \(n\) separate users of one type, one person’s effect shrinks with their \(1/n\) share of the objective. Keeping a positive share as the population grows means treating that share as a bloc, which returns to the changed strategic unit. The finite-support formulation also makes the computational work an explicit weighted empirical-risk problem; it does not expose a pricing problem over an implicit or exponentially large space.

The payment bounds are a possible alternative anchor, but they fare poorly in the limit. The linear-classifier result bounds the *number* of paying sample users by \(O(1)\), so their population mass vanishes as the sample grows. The \(k\)-NN lower bound depends on identities and finite \(k\)-neighbor sets; a continuum version needs a new neighborhood-mass scale, and different choices define different questions. That is useful follow-up modeling, but it does not rescue the proposed individual-auction mirror.

So the negative case is strongest against calling the cohort formulation a continuization of this paper’s computational result: the paper has no named complexity theorem to mirror, and the proposed nontrivial continuous auction requires replacing individuals with coalitions. It is not airtight against the narrower claim that cohort accuracy auctions deserve study. That formulation is coherent and might interest the authors; its weakness is that its computational question looks like a new group-mechanism application of standard weighted learning, rather than a missing complexity landscape from this paper.
