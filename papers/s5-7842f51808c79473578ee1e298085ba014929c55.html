What the mirror covers

The proposed scope is §5.2’s proportional-sharing best-response problem, anchored by Theorem 5.7; it leaves the Price of Stability, Shapley-sharing, and other Sybil results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

I see one defensible, narrow positive case: the paper’s Theorem 5.7, proved in the paper, says that under proportional sharing the optimal solution to the Sybil-attack optimization problem in §5.2 can be computed in polynomial time. The underlying problem is specific: given a player with stake \(a_i\) and \(m\) existing winning pools \(C_1,\ldots,C_m\), choose portions \(s_{i_j}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_j s_{i_j}\le a_i\), to maximize the player’s total reward across those pools. Under proportional sharing, that objective is \(\sum_j s_{i_j}/(m(C_j)+s_{i_j})\). The theorem asserts polynomial-time solvability.

The mirror I would put forward is Oceanic Proportional Sybil Response. Consider a Proof of Stake system with many tiny delegators and a relatively small number \(\tau\) of existing pools. A type is a delegator’s current pool and the terms that determine their per-unit payoff; delegators of the same type are indistinguishable for this response problem. Let \(\mu_j\) be the fraction of the ocean’s stake in pool \(j\), \(M\) the ocean’s total stake, and \(q_j\) the fixed stake of other atomic members there. Thus pool \(j\)’s baseline stake is \(b_j=q_j+M\mu_j\). Require each \(b_j\ge h\), so every target pool already earns its reward. A focal stakeholder with stake \(a\) chooses \(x_j\ge0\), with \(\sum_jx_j\le a\), and seeks to maximize
\[ \sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\frac{x_j}{b_j+x_j}. \]
The task is to return an allocation within additive \(\varepsilon\) of the optimum, given rational masses and stakes and rational \(\varepsilon>0\). This is the paper’s best-response question with the small stakeholders represented by their pool masses rather than listed individually.

I expect this problem to be tractable. Its objective is separable and concave, so continuous optimization gives an approximation route; the optimality conditions also reduce the allocation to a scalar water level. Theorem 5.7 is the anchor, and the continuous version preserves its attacker, available pools, sharing rule, objective, and decision variable. The natural regime is many small delegators spread across a moderate number of established pools, with the delegators in each pool sharing the same relevant terms. It does not require pretending that a seven-member committee is a continuum.

This is the strongest anchor I would use. The paper’s Price of Stability theorems are substantive game-theoretic results, but they do not assert a computational complexity classification, so I would not stretch them into additional anchors. The proposed question covers only computation of one player’s profitable stake split under proportional sharing. It says nothing about computing Nash equilibria, the Shapley scheme’s best response, or the other sharing rules.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already has a non-atomic population, and §5.2 already writes the stake-splitting problem with continuous decision variables. So this is evidence that the pool-mass regime is natural and computationally meaningful, but the continuous-population move itself is not new here; the mirror is narrow and close to a restatement. There is also a proof caveat: Theorem 5.7 claims polynomial-time computation of the optimum, while Appendix C.1’s general convex-optimization argument explicitly supports solving to a requested accuracy. Its additional greedy description says to equalize pool sizes, which does not in general equalize the marginal rewards for the displayed objective. I would therefore treat additive-\(\varepsilon\) tractability as the solid positive case and exact bit-model complexity as a question to verify, rather than relying on that greedy step.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor collapses under the paper’s own model. In §5.2, the paper fixes a partition into winning pools \(C_1,\ldots,C_m\) and asks a player with stake \(a_i\) to choose portions \(s_{i_j}\) maximizing their total proportional reward. The objective is already
\[ \sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{s_{i_j}}{m(C_j)+s_{i_j}}, \qquad \sum_j s_{i_j}\le a_i. \]
In the proposed mirror, \(m(C_j)=q_j+M\mu_j=b_j\) and \(s_{i_j}=x_j\). It is the same optimization problem with the same decision variables and objective. The paper already models the ocean’s stake as mass, including the mass assigned to each pool. So this is not an outside answer to a newly posed continuous question; the paper itself has already posed that continuous-population computational question.

The focal attacker’s remaining atomic is no objection: a mixed oceanic model can sensibly retain a few influential players. The problem is that the oceanic setting and its computational response problem are already present. The proposed pool types and masses just unpack the pool totals used in §5.2.

A broader version could let a positive mass of stakeholders change pools simultaneously. But that changes the question from one player’s best response to a coupled allocation or equilibrium problem. It may be worth studying on its own, but Theorem 5.7 does not anchor it. Keeping a single attacker while changing the pool masses gives the same objective again. Also, the paper’s convex-optimization discussion already supports approximation of that objective; its separate greedy claim to equalize pool sizes is questionable, since the optimum should equalize marginal rewards, not generally final pool sizes. Any work needed here is to check or repair the existing algorithmic claim in its existing model.

That makes the negative case strong against this anchor, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists anywhere in the paper. Other variants, such as computation involving Shapley sharing, could pose distinct questions. The honest conclusion is narrower: this proposed mirror is a restatement of the paper’s own continuous computational problem, not evidence for a new one.
