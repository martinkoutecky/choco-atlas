The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible case for the *setting*: a large housing or school-allocation system could have many applicants sharing a small number of complete preference rankings, so \(n\) is large while the number of preference types \(\tau\) is relatively small. But this paper offers no eligible computational anchor for that case.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says a mechanism is strategy-proof and satisfies globally constant tie-breaking if and only if it is a serial dictatorship. Theorem 2, also proved here, says a mechanism is group strategy-proof (equivalently, strategy-proof and non-bossy) and satisfies locally constant tie-breaking if and only if it is a sequential dictatorship. These are axiomatic characterizations; neither asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm for a computational problem. The corollaries are axiomatic too.

So I can’t state a continuous prover problem per anchor without inventing a new computational question or continuizing the paper’s axioms. There is also a substantive modeling challenge: replacing individuals with infinitesimal mass can make unilateral strategy-proofness vacuous, while replacing it with positive-mass coalition deviations changes the paper’s question. The plausible high-multiplicity scenario does not resolve either issue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s housing example makes a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many applicants can share a small number of rankings. I would grant that point. But a plausible setting does not supply a result to mirror.

The only candidate anchors in their case are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2. Theorem 1 characterizes serial dictatorships by strategy-proofness and globally constant tie-breaking; Theorem 2 characterizes sequential dictatorships by group strategy-proofness and locally constant tie-breaking. Both are axiomatic characterizations. Neither poses a computational problem, gives an algorithm, or establishes a complexity bound. The corollaries and the variable-population result in Appendix B are axiomatic too. So there is no named computational result here for a continuous mirror to take up.

The strongest reconstruction of Theorem 1 would give applicants a distribution over preference types, scale house supplies with population, and retain priority by adding a normalized priority coordinate to each type. One could then ask whether continuous versions of the axioms characterize a priority mechanism. That could be a coherent axiomatic project, but it remains outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Alternatively, one could ask how to compute the resulting mass allocation. That is a new computational question: the paper supplies neither that problem nor a result about its complexity. The same applies to Theorem 2. Encoding its adaptive, history-dependent priorities may make a continuum model possible, but characterizing mechanisms under those axioms is still axiomatic; computing outcomes from a specified adaptive policy would be a follow-on problem.

The proponent’s concern about strategy-proofness is also substantial. In an atomless model, an individual report has zero mass and may not affect the outcome, making individual strategy-proofness vacuous. Replacing individual deviations with positive-mass coalition deviations could restore content, but changes the strategic property being studied. Adding identity or priority coordinates may preserve more information, yet does not turn either theorem into a computational result.

That is the strongest case against a continuous mirror of this paper: its principal results are precisely the kind that ChoCo excludes as anchors, even if a suitable high-multiplicity allocation setting exists. The broader claim that no worthwhile computational project could be built nearby is harder to defend. A new problem about computing allocations in a continuous housing market might be worthwhile; it simply would not mirror a computational result in this paper.
