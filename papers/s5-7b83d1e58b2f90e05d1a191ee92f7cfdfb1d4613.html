The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) candidates, \(\tau\) ranking types \(\sigma_t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and shared nonnegative rational transposition costs \(\varphi\), compute a ranking \(\pi\) whose expected distance \(C_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma_t)\) is at most \(4\) times optimum. The paper's method minimizes the expected surrogate \(D\), where \(D(\pi,\sigma)\) sums shortest-path costs between each candidate's positions; the guarantee is \(2\) for metric costs and weighted Kendall costs.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types. The decision is an aggregate ranking \(\pi\), and the objective is its expected weighted-transposition distance to the society, \(C_\mu(\pi)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma_t)\). For the matching algorithm, assigning candidate \(j\) to position \(i\) costs \(\sum_t\mu_t\,d_{\mathrm{sp}}(i,\sigma_t^{-1}(j))\), with \(d_{\mathrm{sp}}\) the shortest-path cost induced by \(\varphi\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the constant-factor approximation to weighted rank aggregation through expected surrogate-distance minimization. It does not settle exact expected-distance minimization or assess the Markov-chain method's empirical behavior.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(4\)-approximation guarantee be improved for general nonnegative transposition costs, or the \(2\)-approximation extended beyond the stated cases?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s weighted rank aggregation algorithm. The anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper: if \(\pi'\) minimizes \(\sum_l D(\pi,\sigma_l)\) and \(\pi^*\) minimizes \(\sum_l \mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma_l)\), then \(\pi'\) is a \(4\)-approximation to the optimal aggregate for any nonnegative transposition weights \(\varphi\). If \(\varphi\) is a metric or gives weighted Kendall distance, it is a \(2\)-approximation. The paper gives the \(4\)-approximation proof; its justification for the stronger factor refers to results of Farnoud et al. (2012). The supporting Theorem 2 states that \(\tfrac12 D(\pi,\sigma)\le \mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma)\le 2D(\pi,\sigma)\), where \(D\) sums shortest-path costs between each candidate’s positions; its proof is omitted.

Lead mirror: high-multiplicity weighted rank aggregation with a constant-factor guarantee. An instance has \(n\) candidates, a support of \(\tau\) rankings \(\sigma_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and common nonnegative rational transposition costs \(\varphi\). A type is a complete ranking \(\sigma_t\); its mass is the fraction of the population with that ranking. The decision is the aggregate ranking \(\pi\), and its objective is the expected distance
\[ C_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma_t). \]
The problem asks for a ranking with \(C_\mu(\pi)\le 4\min_\rho C_\mu(\rho)\); for metric \(\varphi\), including weighted Kendall costs, the target is a factor of \(2\).

This is the paper’s aggregation question with a compressed, high-multiplicity input. For the weighted Kendall case, let \(d_H(a,b)\) be the path cost between positions \(a\) and \(b\), so \(d_H(a,b)=\sum_{h=a}^{b-1}w_h\) when \(a<b\), with the symmetric definition when \(b<a\). Then \(D(\pi,\sigma)=\sum_j d_H(\pi^{-1}(j),\sigma^{-1}(j))\). The paper’s matching method minimizes expected \(D\): the assignment cost for putting candidate \(j\) in position \(i\) is \(\sum_t\mu_t d_H(i,\sigma_t^{-1}(j))\). Thus the guarantee extends to rational masses and can be computed in time polynomial in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length.

A plausible regime is a large professional association surveying hundreds of thousands of members on a fixed set of six policy priorities. Members with the same ranking are the same type for this problem; there can be at most \(6!\) types even as the population grows. The survey’s chosen position weights apply to everyone. A normalized ballot profile has the same objective as the raw total distance up to a factor of population size, but the continuous input records each type and its binary rational mass. That is a sensible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ aggregation problem, and I expect them to recognize it.

For the stated approximation problem, I expect Class A: the paper’s surrogate objective has an exact matching algorithm, and the distance bounds yield the approximation guarantee. This does not establish that exact expected-distance minimization is tractable. Indeed, the paper reports that exact Kendall aggregation is NP-hard, though that claim is unnumbered and cited from elsewhere, so I do not use it as an anchor. It suggests a further Class B question: the exact continuous problem can encode a finite voter profile by assigning each distinct ranking its empirical rational mass. Other follow-ups include whether the \(2\)-approximation guarantee can be improved and how the exact problem behaves under additional restrictions on the rankings or costs.

The weakest point is that Proposition 3 anchors an approximation algorithm, not a named hardness result, and its guarantee already applies to finite ballot lists. The continuous contribution here is the high-multiplicity input regime: polynomial dependence on support size and mass bit length, rather than on the possibly enormous number of voters. It is a credible, direct mirror, but it does not by itself reveal a new complexity boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the proposed approximation result is almost already written for a distribution over rankings. For a society \(\mu\), the matching algorithm can assign position \(i\) to candidate \(j\) at cost
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{weight}\!\left(p^*(i,\sigma_t^{-1}(j))\right). \]
It then minimizes the expected surrogate distance. The pointwise bounds in Theorem 2 still hold after weighting and summing by \(\mu_t\), so Proposition 3’s approximation guarantee follows directly. This computation uses \(\tau\) rankings and rational arithmetic; it need not expand the society into individual ballots. Any rational \(\mu\) can also be represented as a finite ballot list by clearing denominators.

That makes the approximation mirror a very short weighted-input corollary of the paper’s own algorithm, with no new optimization structure. If “worthwhile” means uncovering a new tractability boundary, this is a thin anchor: the continuous formulation mainly compresses the electorate’s input. But ChoCo explicitly treats high-multiplicity compression as part of the point, so thinness is not a decisive objection under the programme’s criteria.

The more fundamental negative arguments do not hold up. A large electorate ranking a fixed slate gives a natural regime with many people per ranking type; the paper’s objective uses only those rankings and shared swap weights, so the type definition loses no information the problem uses. The aggregate remains a ranking, but that does not prevent the population from being the continuous object. An exact weighted-Kendall mirror is also well-posed, even if its eventual answer is already suggested by discrete results.

So I cannot honestly defeat the anchor. The paper has a named computational result, and its population mirror is sensible. The best negative case is that the approximation mirror may be too immediate to merit much programme effort; it does not support the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
