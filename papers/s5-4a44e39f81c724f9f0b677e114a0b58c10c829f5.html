The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 (proved here) establishes existence of an envy-free allocation for compensable tenants; Theorems 4, 5, and 6 (also proved here) establish existence in the roommates, secretive-agent, and extra-agent settings. Corollary 3 supplies an existence result for prices and weights. None states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm. The introduction cites algorithms for the standard quasilinear setting from earlier work, while Section 7 treats algorithms for compensable tenants and the extensions as future work.

There is a plausible *unanchored research lead*: in a large housing market, let types be complete tenant preferences, \(\mu_t\) their population shares, and \(q_j\) the per-capita supply of room categories. Ask for prices \(p\) and assignment masses \(x_{tj}\) satisfying \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tj}=q_j\), and \(x_{tj}>0\) only when room \(j\) is best for type \(t\) at \(p\). That is recognizably a population-continuous rental-harmony question. But the paper neither studies this mass model nor supplies a finite representation for its general demand functions.

So the honest positive case is limited: the subject has a natural high-multiplicity extension, but this paper contains no named computational result to anchor it. I can’t responsibly assign that proposed problem to Class A, B, or C based on these theorems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no computational result as an anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorems 4–6 establish existence; Corollary 3 supplies another existence statement. None gives an algorithm or a complexity bound. Section 7 does invite future algorithmic work, but it does not pose a computational problem with an input representation or a target guarantee. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, there is no named computational result here to continuize.

The proposed large-market model is the natural lead, and a large rental market can plainly have many tenants per type. Its obstacle is computational specification: a compensable type may be an arbitrary demand function of the entire price vector. Listing the support and rational masses does not encode those functions or say how an algorithm can evaluate demand. A finite utility language or a demand-oracle model could make the question precise, but either would add a computational model the paper does not provide. Restricting to quasilinear tenants avoids that gap, but leaves the paper’s broader compensable-tenant results behind.

So the case against is strong for an *anchored mirror* of this paper: the paper has no computational result to mirror, and its general preference model has no stated finite encoding. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise. Section 7 points toward a possible new algorithmic project once preferences are explicitly represented. That would be a follow-up to the paper, though, not a computational mirror anchored in one of its results.
