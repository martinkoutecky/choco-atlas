The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can make is narrow. The paper’s closest named anchor is Theorem 1, proved in Section 5: for the Uniform Price Auction with symmetric submodular multi-demand bidders, every pure Nash equilibrium in undominated strategies achieves at least a \(1-e^{-1}\) fraction of optimal social welfare, equivalently the Price of Anarchy is at most \(e/(e-1)\). This is a welfare guarantee, not a running-time or hardness result, so it is not a direct complexity anchor of the kind ChoCo most wants.

A plausible limit scenario is a very large platform of small investors buying identical securities, with many accounts but only \(\tau\) distinct marginal valuation curves. A type \(t\) is a complete valuation curve \(v_t:\{0,\ldots,k\}\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of accounts with that curve. Let the supply scale with the population, with \(\kappa\in\mathbb Q\) units per account. Accounts still receive integral bundles, while the mass of a type receiving each bundle can be fractional.

Call the resulting question Continuum Uniform-Price Equilibrium Welfare. Its input is \((T,\mu,(v_t)_{t\in T},\kappa)\), with explicit submodular valuation curves and \(0\le\kappa\le k\). A feasible allocation is a set of masses \(z_{t,j}\ge0\), where \(z_{t,j}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(j\) units, satisfying \(\sum_j z_{t,j}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,j}jz_{t,j}=\kappa\). Its welfare is \(\sum_{t,j}z_{t,j}v_t(j)\). In the atomless auction, each account submits a nonincreasing marginal-bid vector; an individual deviation can change that account’s bundle but not the aggregate market or the uniform price. The problem is to find the lowest welfare ratio among pure equilibria—returning a price, an equilibrium allocation \(z\), and its ratio to the maximum feasible welfare—or to decide whether that ratio is at most a given rational threshold.

I expect this version to be tractable, with ratio \(1\): at a market-clearing price \(p\), every bundle assigned in equilibrium maximizes \(v_t(j)-pj\) for its type. Such an allocation maximizes total welfare subject to the supply constraint. With the valuation curves given explicitly, the welfare benchmark is a polynomial-size linear program. This is the positive case for mirroring Theorem 1: the continuum question is well-defined, computational, and its welfare guarantee is at least as strong.

The weakness is also the central issue: atomlessness removes the market power that causes demand reduction. The resulting price-taking equilibrium is recognizable as a continuous-player limit of the auction, but it may not be accepted by the authors as a mirror of their main inefficiency question. Making a whole type cohort the deviating player could preserve price impact, but then the model gives each type coordinated strategic agency; it no longer represents many independent bidders. So this case covers Theorem 1’s equilibrium-welfare question, not the paper’s finite-player demand-reduction phenomenon, and it should not be presented as a computational-complexity result already established by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is Theorem 1’s Price of Anarchy bound, \(e/(e-1)\). It is a named theorem, but not a computational result: the paper gives no algorithm, running-time bound, or complexity classification. Theorem 2 tightens the welfare bound, and Theorem 3 gives a Bayesian bound; all three analyze inefficiency. So the proposed “find the lowest welfare ratio” task changes the paper’s result into a new algorithmic problem rather than continuizing a computational one.

The proposed model also changes the strategic object. An infinitesimal bidder cannot affect the market price, so its equilibrium is price-taking. With finite type support, the allocation variables \(z_{t,j}\) and supply constraint already form the standard welfare LP; a supporting price is its dual variable. Computing the proposed equilibrium therefore reduces to market clearing and welfare optimization, rather than analyzing the strategic bid deviations behind the paper’s demand-reduction result. A tractable LP could still be a useful Class A result; the concern is that the paper supplies no computational problem for that result to mirror. Large-market analysis of uniform-price auctions already connects their equilibria to nonatomic exchange economies and approximate efficiency, as in [Bodoh-Creed’s 2013 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jet.2013.09.012). That is analytic prior art, not a computational collision, but it makes this particular continuum question look familiar.

The strongest repair is to give each positive-mass type cohort control of its own bids, preserving price impact. But then the cohort acts as a coordinated strategic bidder; it no longer represents many independent high-multiplicity bidders. If supply stays fixed as the population grows, the mass receiving units vanishes instead. These choices leave a narrow middle ground: independent atomless bidders yield a price-taking LP; price-impacting players require strategic atoms or coordination.

I would vote against this anchor as a ChoCo mirror, though the universal claim remains vulnerable. A mixed market with a few price-making institutions and a continuum of small bidders could be an interesting new model. I cannot rule out that authors would recognize it as their problem, but its strategic complexity would come from the finite large players, not from continuizing the population in the paper’s equilibrium analysis.
