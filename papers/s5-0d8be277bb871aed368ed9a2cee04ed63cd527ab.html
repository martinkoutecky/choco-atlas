The continuous mirror question

Given finite good types \(G\), agent types \(T\), subadditive valuations \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), population masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(b_g\), and a feasible reference allocation \(\widetilde{x}\), find masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}=b_g\), such that every positive-mass bundle \(S\) of type \(t\) is EF1 toward every positive-mass bundle \(R\) of type \(u\), and \(\operatorname{NSW}_\infty(x)\ge e^{-2/e}\operatorname{NSW}_\infty(\widetilde{x})\), where \(\operatorname{NSW}_\infty(x)=\prod_{t,S}v_t(S)^{x_{t,S}}\).

The model it lives in

A multi-unit high-multiplicity fair-division model: \(\mu\) distributes population mass over valuation types, \(x_{t,S}\) distributes each type across integral bundles, \(b_g\) gives per-capita supply, and the objective is weighted geometric-mean welfare subject to support-wise EF1.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.1 does not itself provide a polynomially compressed algorithm: support-wise EF1 remains a nonconvex configuration-selection condition, and its agent-level cycle argument may not survive aggregation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers the EF1-to-NSW conversion theorem in Theorem 3.1. It leaves the existential EFx and EF1 bounds, lower bounds, asymmetric NSW result, superadditive impossibility, and constrained-allocation results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational instance \((\mu,b,\widetilde{x})\) admit a polynomial-support support-wise EF1 solution with the \(e^{-2/e}\) guarantee?
  • Can the mass version be solved in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), reference-support size, and encoding length?
  • Does the \(1/2\) lower bound from Theorem 4.3 persist when \(m\) and \(\tau\) are fixed while population multiplicity grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is an algorithmic one, anchored in the paper’s own Theorem 3.1. The paper contains no named NP-hardness theorem of its own; the NP-hardness of NSW maximization appears only as a statement in related work. Thus there is no hardness anchor to use. Theorem 3.1 is proved in this paper; Theorem 1.3 is its introductory statement, not a separate result.

Theorem 3.1 says: given a fair-division instance \(I=\langle[n],[m],\{v_i\}_{i\in[n]}\rangle\) with subadditive valuations, value-oracle access to those valuations, and an arbitrary allocation \(\widetilde A\), one can compute in polynomial time a complete EF1 allocation \(A\) satisfying

\[ \mathrm{NSW}(A)\ge \frac{1}{e^{2/e}}\mathrm{NSW}(\widetilde A). \]

Here \(\mathrm{NSW}\) is the geometric mean of the agents’ values. This is the paper’s strongest computational anchor: it is a genuine polynomial-time fairness-preserving welfare-conversion theorem, not merely an existence statement.

My continuous mirror would be the following.

There is a finite set of good types \(G=[m]\) and a finite set of agent types \(T=[\tau]\). Type \(t\) has a normalized, monotone, subadditive valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\). The population has mass distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents of type \(t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each good type \(g\) has per-capita supply \(b_g\in[0,1]\). Thus, in a finite realization with \(N\) agents, there are \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nb_g\) copies of good \(g\). The limit regime has \(N\gg \tau\), with \(\tau\) and \(m\) fixed or comparatively small.

Individuals still receive integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\). No individual receives a fractional good. The continuous decision variable is only the mass \(x_{t,S}\) of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(S\). A complete allocation satisfies

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

for every type \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}=b_g \]

for every good type \(g\). Thus \(x\) is a distribution over integral bundles, not a fractional bundle allocation.

The exact continuous analogue of EF1 should be support-wise, not merely average EF1. For every pair of positive-mass bundle atoms \(S\) received by type \(t\) and \(R\) received by type \(u\), with \(R\neq\varnothing\), there must exist \(g\in R\) such that

\[ v_t(S)\ge v_t(R\setminus\{g\}). \]

This says that every individual in the population would be EF1 toward every other individual, exactly as in the paper. Replacing this by a condition on type-average utilities would be easier, but would weaken the paper’s question.

The continuous Nash welfare is the high-multiplicity limit of the finite geometric mean:

\[ \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x) = \prod_{t,S}v_t(S)^{x_{t,S}} = \exp\!\left(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\right), \]

with value \(0\) if positive mass receives zero utility. If \(x\) is rational, multiplying all masses by a common denominator gives a finite cloned population whose normalized NSW is exactly this quantity.

I would call the problem Continuous EF1–NSW Conversion\(_\infty\):

Given \((G,T,v,\mu,b)\) and a complete reference allocation \(\widetilde x\), find a complete support-wise EF1 allocation \(x\) maximizing \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)\), or, in the direct theorem-mirroring version, find one satisfying

\[ \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x) \ge \frac{1}{e^{2/e}}\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(\widetilde x). \]

The input reference allocation may itself split a type across several integral bundles, so this is genuinely the analogue of an arbitrary allocation \(\widetilde A\), not a type-homogeneous simplification.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario for the authors’ problem. Imagine a large institution distributing many copies of standardized goods—benefit packages, equipment, housing amenities, or educational resources—to a population with a small number of repeated valuation profiles. Agents of the same type have identical values for every bundle, not merely similar preferences. The population is continuous because only the proportions of these types matter; the goods remain indivisible at the individual level. This is therefore population continuization, not outcome-space fractionalization.

I would expect this mirror to be a Class A candidate, at least when the reference allocation is supplied with an explicit finite support. The proof of Theorem 3.1 has several features that survive normalization: the EF1 invariant is pointwise, the welfare analysis is expressed through products and AM–GM inequalities, and the path-growing procedure operates on connected pieces of bundles rather than on semantic identities of individual voters. A mass version could process positive-mass blocks of such bundle pieces. Equivalently, one could formulate the allocation through integral-bundle configuration columns and seek a sparse mass solution.

The crucial new theorem would be a compressed implementation: polynomial dependence on \(m\), \(\tau\), the support size of \(\widetilde x\), and the encoding length of the masses, rather than polynomial dependence on the enormous clone population \(N\). If that mass path-growing or configuration algorithm exists, the continuous problem is tractable and Theorem 3.1’s finite-agent dependence has genuinely disappeared.

My weakest point is that the paper does not prove this compressed algorithm. Simply cloning \(N\) agents and running Theorem 3.1 is not enough: if \(N\) is given through large rational denominators, that is only pseudo-polynomial. Moreover, support-wise EF1 is substantially stronger than type-average EF1 and creates a nonconvex support condition. A weaker average-fairness formulation would be easier, but would no longer be a faithful mirror.

The main follow-up questions are whether every such mass instance admits a polynomial-support support-wise EF1 allocation; whether the \(e^{-2/e}\) guarantee survives with complexity polynomial in \((m,\tau)\); and whether the paper’s \(1/2\) lower bound from Theorem 4.3 persists when \(m\) and \(\tau\) stay fixed while population multiplicity grows. That last issue is open-ended rather than automatic: the paper’s lower-bound construction lets the number of goods and agents grow together, so it does not by itself establish a continuum barrier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious computational anchor is Theorem 3.1 (Theorem 1.3 is the same result restated). A negative case must therefore attack the proposed EF1–NSW conversion mirror directly.

The literal population limit degenerates. If the paper’s \(m\) named goods remain fixed while the number of agents tends to infinity, at least \(n-m\) agents receive empty bundles. Since \(v_i(\varnothing)=0\), the normalized Nash welfare becomes \(0\), and the guarantee relative to an arbitrary allocation becomes vacuous. Thus the paper has no meaningful continuum with fixed resources.

The proposed repair—scaling the supply of each good with population size—is admissible under the programme’s co-modification rule, but it changes the central object. The paper studies a finite set of named indivisible goods; the repaired model is a multi-unit fair-division problem with anonymous good types and per-capita supply. That may be a sensible new problem, but it is no longer obviously a mirror of the theorem’s indivisible-goods setting.

Even granting that repair, support-wise EF1 does not genuinely relax the discrete difficulty. For each type–bundle pair \((t,S)\), define a configuration node. Two positive-mass nodes may coexist only if the corresponding EF1 comparisons hold in both directions. Hence the feasible set is a finite union of faces determined by which integral bundles have positive mass. Once that support is fixed,

\[ \log \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x) = \sum_{t,S} x_{t,S}\log v_t(S) \]

is linear and the supply constraints are linear. The continuous variables merely specify how much mass is assigned to a selected collection of discrete configurations; the substantive problem is still selecting a compatible support of integral bundles.

Replacing support-wise EF1 by an average condition would produce a smoother optimization problem, but it would no longer preserve the paper’s fairness notion. Conversely, retaining exact EF1 leaves the proposed mirror with a nonconvex configuration-selection problem rather than the LP-like population model suggested by the programme.

The proof of Theorem 3.1 also gives no compressed algorithm for this setting. Its welfare bound relies on one cycle per named agent and on integer counts such as \(|S_i|\). In a mass model, a positive-mass reference block may be split among many recipient configurations, with no canonical individual cycle or analogue of those counts. Aggregating clones may eventually yield a theorem, but that would be a new result about multi-unit fair division, not a consequence of the paper’s algorithm.

This is the strongest negative case, but it is not decisive. For rational masses, the repaired model can be cloned into a finite instance, and the paper’s theorem then gives an EF1 allocation with the same normalized NSW guarantee. Type and bundle identities contain all information used by EF1 and NSW, so no genuine identity obstruction exists. Consequently, I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed mirror survives as a legitimate high-multiplicity problem; the real uncertainty is whether its clone-level guarantee admits a polynomially compressed algorithm.
