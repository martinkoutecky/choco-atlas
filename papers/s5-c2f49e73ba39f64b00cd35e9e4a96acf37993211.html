The continuous mirror question

Given \(N\) types on each side, each of mass \(1/N\), a bipartite compatibility graph of maximum degree \(3\), strict type rankings satisfying the theorem's single-peaked and single-crossing restrictions, and a stable matching of mass \(1-1/N\), decide whether there is a measurable perfect matching that is popular against every feasible measurable matching and has at most one blocking type pair. A pair \(ab\) is blocking when positive mass on each side is unmatched or prefers the other type to its current assignment; count each such type pair once.

The model it lives in

Two atomless populations are partitioned into finitely many types with rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\). Allowed type pairs \(ab\) carry the rankings and unit utility and cost; allocations have type-level flows \(x_{ab}\), while popularity compares the same agents' assignments across matchings and instability counts each mutually blocking type pair once.

The objection that survived

Popularity depends on how the same agents' assignments under two matchings are coupled, so type-flow matrices alone may not give a finite representation; the proponent identifies this gap but leaves its resolution open.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5's NP-completeness result for perfect popular matchings with one blocking edge. It leaves the paper's other hardness results, master-list algorithms, and Pareto-optimal matching results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can popularity be characterized with a finite representation that captures identity-level couplings between assignments?
  • Does the type-pair instability version retain hardness when cohorts can split, and how does it relate to markets with many discrete agents per type?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 5 (*). It proves that Max-Utility Popular Matching with Instability Costs is NP-complete even when the graph is bipartite with maximum degree \(3\), preferences are single-peaked and single-crossing, every edge has cost \(1\), the budget is \(1\), every edge has utility \(1\), and the target utility is \(t=|M_s|+1=|V|/2\), where \(M_s\) is a stable matching. Thus the target is a perfect matching, one unit larger than the stable matching. The authors prove this result here, in the appendix, by modifying their Exact-3-SAT reduction. It is the cleanest anchor because its objective is total matched mass, rather than utility concentrated on one special edge. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.02189)

A plausible mirror is Popular Perfect Cohort Matching with One Blocking Type Pair. Consider a large two-sided labour or placement market. Each side has \(N\) cohorts, each cohort containing many interchangeable applicants or positions with the same eligibility, strict ranking of the opposite-side cohorts, utility, and cost. Let each cohort have mass \(1/N\); the market may have \(R\) agents per cohort, with \(R\gg 1\). The instance is a bipartite graph on the cohort types, with degree at most \(3\) and the same single-peaked and single-crossing restrictions as the theorem. Include a stable partial matching of mass \(1-1/N\). The question is whether there is a perfect matching of the population that is popular and has at most one blocking type-pair.

Precisely, represent each cohort as an atomless interval of its stated mass. A solution is a measurable, one-to-one matching of agent mass along the allowed type edges; its type-level flow \(x_{ab}\) records the mass matched from cohort \(a\) to cohort \(b\). For two matchings \(M,M'\), count the mass of agents on both sides who prefer their partner under \(M\) to their partner under \(M'\), treating being unmatched as worst. Call \(M\) popular if it receives at least as many votes as every feasible \(M'\). For an allowed type-pair \(ab\), let \(p_a(b\mid M)\) be the mass in \(a\) who are unmatched or prefer \(b\) to their current partner; define \(p_b(a\mid M)\) symmetrically. The pair is blocking when \(\min\{p_a(b\mid M),p_b(a\mid M)\}>0\). Charge \(1\) for each blocking type-pair and require the total to be at most \(1\). Since all edge utilities are \(1\), perfect matching is exactly utility \(1\); since the stable baseline has mass \(1-1/N\), that is one cohort-mass quantum above it.

This is the population being continuized: the decision is still whom to match, popularity is still a head-to-head vote among the agents, and instability still concerns mutually preferred partnerships. The graph and preferences live on the cohorts; the mass records how much of society each cohort represents. That is a plausible market regime when there are many thousands of agents but far fewer distinct qualification, location, and ranking profiles. Charging once for a blocking *type-pair* is the natural type-level version of the paper’s unit edge cost: a positive-mass blocking relation between two cohorts counts as one relation.

My provisional expectation is Class B, because the theorem’s reduction encodes its choices in the preference graph and consistency relations among types, rather than in the number of agents sharing a ballot or profile. But the reduction does not itself prove hardness for this mirror: divisible mass could allow a cohort to split across alternatives that were discrete choices in the construction. A tractability result would be meaningful too, especially if popularity can be captured by a finite LP or an efficient separation problem. Further questions include whether the result changes under a mass-weighted instability budget \(\sum_{ab}\min\{p_a(b\mid M),p_b(a\mid M)\}\), and what exact or approximate relationship connects this model to markets with \(R\) discrete agents per cohort.

The main soft spot is the representation of popularity. A type-flow matrix alone may not record which members of a cohort receive which partner types, and therefore may not suffice to compare the same agents’ votes across matchings. The measurable-matching definition keeps those voters and their comparisons explicit, but a computational treatment would need a finite representation or a proof that type flows suffice. This is a real modelling question for the authors and a referee; it does not erase the case for the mirror, but it is the part most likely to need refinement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a strong computational anchor: it proves NP-completeness for a named problem, and the utility target is a perfect matching, not a reward attached to one special edge. So the “no computational result to mirror” objection does not apply.

The strongest case against its mirror is that the reduction’s hardness is carried by the identities and incidences of individual vertices. Each variable, clause, and consistency edge has a specific role. If each individual becomes a distinct type to preserve those details, the support grows with the instance and there is no high-multiplicity compression. If repeated roles are merged into cohorts, the resulting type graph permits mass to move across type pairs; it no longer preserves the reduction’s degree-three graph of named-agent edges. The proposed labour-market version is coherent, but its “one blocking type pair” condition is a new instability measure: one positive-mass relation can represent very many individual blocking edges.

Popularity also needs more than separate type-level flow matrices. Take a type whose agents rank partners \(x \succ y \succ z\). One matching can assign half its mass to \(x\) and half to \(z\); another can assign half to \(y\) and half to \(z\). With the same two flow matrices, one identity alignment gives half the agents a vote for the first matching and half a tie; another gives half a vote to each matching. A finite model could add joint allocation variables to track these comparisons, but then popularity depends on couplings between assignments, not just on the cohort flows. That is a real modeling and computational change.

These points weaken the claim that the theorem itself transfers to the proposed mirror. They do not defeat the best repaired question: define popularity over measurable assignments with explicit joint comparisons, and study either the support of blocking type pairs or a mass-weighted instability budget. Those are finite-type computational questions that the authors could recognize, and their answers could be interesting even if they differ sharply from the discrete theorem. The theorem’s “one more than a stable matching” gap may also cease to be the right target when mass is divisible, but perfectness remains a meaningful objective.

So the honest negative case is weak at its crucial point. I can challenge the proposed encoding and the direct connection to this reduction, but I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. This anchor survives that challenge.
