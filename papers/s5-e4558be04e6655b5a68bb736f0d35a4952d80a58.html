The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(G\), finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(\kappa_j\), and type data \(t=(S_t,\theta_t)\), compute a feasible mass allocation \(x_t\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\theta_t x_t\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:j\in S_t}x_t\le\kappa_j\) for every \(j\in G\).

The model it lives in

An explicit high-multiplicity LP in which \(\mu_t\) is the population mass of type \(t\), \(x_t\) is the mass served its entire bundle \(S_t\), \(\kappa_j\) is supply of item \(j\) per capita, and the objective is total welfare \(\sum_t\theta_t x_t\).

The objection that survived

The mirror removes the randomized rounding and most of the mechanism-design contribution, leaving an explicit fractional allocation LP with no nontrivial pricing problem when the type list is explicit.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.8's high-multiplicity welfare guarantee through mass allocation over package-and-value types. It leaves Theorem 4.1's individual truthfulness, the revenue comparison in Theorem 5.1, the derandomization question, and the open problem of lying about desired sets.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the sharp additive and multiplicative relationship between the continuous optimum and the integral \(N\)-agent optimum after rounding masses?
  • Can a nonvacuous price-taking or representative-agent incentive model preserve any meaningful analogue of Theorem 4.1 without reverting to named finite bidders?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s known single-minded combinatorial auction. My lead mirror is the welfare-allocation result, not merely the fact that the paper writes an LP.

The paper has no numbered NP-hardness, polynomial-time, W[1]-hardness, or FPT theorem. Its statement that SetPacking is NP-hard is background, not a numbered result, so I do not use it as a formal hardness anchor. My anchors are therefore the algorithmic results proved in the paper.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.8, proved here. It states that, when every item \(j\) has multiplicity \(m_j=\Omega(\ln K)\), where \(K\) is the maximum desired-set size, and \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is the maximum total valuation of any feasible allocation, the paper’s randomized allocation algorithm obtains expected total valuation at least

\[ (1-\epsilon_I)q^\ast\mathrm{OPT}, \]

where \(q^\ast\) is the survival factor defined earlier in the paper, essentially \(1-2/K^c\). Thus the theorem gives a near-optimal welfare guarantee under high item multiplicity.

A natural continuous mirror is:

\[ \mathrm{HM\text{-}SM\text{-}Welfare}_\infty. \]

An instance consists of a finite item set \(G\), a finite set \(T\) of bidder types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\kappa_j\ge 0\). A type \(t\) consists of a desired set \(S_t\subseteq G\) and a value \(\theta_t\ge 0\). Thus two agents have the same type exactly when they want the same package and have the same value. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\), and \(\kappa_j\) is the number of copies of item \(j\) available per unit population.

The decision variable is \(x_t\), the mass of type \(t\) that receives its entire desired set. It must satisfy

\[ 0\le x_t\le \mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_{t:j\in S_t}x_t\le \kappa_j \qquad\text{for every }j\in G. \]

The objective is to maximize

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\theta_t x_t. \]

A solution is the optimal mass vector \(x\), together with a measurable assignment realizing it: within each type class, a subset of agents of mass \(x_t\) receives all items in \(S_t\), while every other agent receives nothing. Individuals still receive integral bundles. Only the aggregate number of served agents is fractional.

This is a genuine population continuization. It is not outcome-space continuity: we are not giving an individual \(0.4\) of an item. We are taking \(N\mu_t\) repeated bidders and \(N\kappa_j\) repeated item copies, then normalizing by \(N\). In a plausible regime, a cloud, spectrum, or infrastructure provider serves millions of customers from a finite catalogue of standard packages. There may be \(N\) customers but only \(\tau\ll N\) package-and-value classes, with many customers in each class. The paper’s known-set assumption is also natural when package eligibility is determined publicly by contract or customer category.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. Its continuous problem is an LP with \(\tau\) variables and \(|G|\) capacity constraints, solvable exactly in time polynomial in \(|G|\), \(\tau\), and the input precision. The condition \(m_j=\Omega(\ln K)\) becomes \(N\kappa_j=\Omega(\ln K)\), which is automatic for any fixed positive per-capita supply as \(N\) grows. The continuous problem is therefore stronger than the paper’s approximation guarantee: exact optimization already gives the theorem’s \((1-\epsilon_I)q^\ast\) bound.

The bridge back to the discrete problem is also meaningful. Given an optimal mass vector \(x\), setting the number served of type \(t\) to \(\lfloor N x_t\rfloor\) preserves feasibility and loses at most an additive \(O(\tau)\) agents’ worth of value. After normalization, the loss is \(O(\tau/N)\). Thus the continuous solution gives high-multiplicity preprocessing and an additive approximation for the discrete auction. Conversely, the continuous object is not sensible when \(\tau\) is comparable to \(N\), or when the type universe is represented implicitly by all \(2^{|G|}\) packages. Those are different regimes in which the pricing or representation problem may retain the original combinatorial hardness.

A secondary anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here. It states that, under the same \(\Omega(\ln K)\) item-multiplicity assumption, the paper’s RandRound allocation algorithm combined with its payment scheme is truthful in expectation and is also truthful with error probability at most

\[ \epsilon+\frac{1}{K^c}. \]

Here “truthful with error probability” means that, for every bidder, every vector of other bids, and every true value, the probability over the mechanism’s randomization that truthful bidding fails to maximize that bidder’s profit is bounded by this quantity.

The corresponding continuous question would be:

\[ \mathrm{HM\text{-}SM\text{-}TruthfulAuction}_\infty. \]

Given the same \((G,T,\mu,\kappa)\), construct a finite description of an anonymous randomized direct mechanism for the atomless population. It must assign each individual either her whole desired set or nothing, satisfy the per-capita capacity constraints for every realization, and use payments per served unit. Holding all other reports fixed, truthful reporting must maximize each individual’s expected profit. In addition, the probability that truthful reporting fails to be a best response must be at most \(\epsilon+1/K^c\). To prevent a vacuous zero-allocation mechanism, the mechanism must achieve expected welfare at least \((1-\epsilon_I)q^\ast\) times the continuous optimum above.

I would expect this strengthened mechanism problem also to be Class A, using the paper’s monotonicity argument and threshold-payment construction, with the type-level LP replacing the bidder-level LP. In the atomless setting, overselling can be handled by selecting measurable subsets of each type class with exactly the required mass, so the probabilistic loss from rounding should disappear or become easier to control.

This second mirror is less secure than the welfare mirror. Truthfulness for a measure-zero individual can degenerate if the continuum model is defined carelessly: one agent cannot change aggregate capacities. The mechanism therefore has to retain a meaningful representative-agent deviation notion, with individual allocation and payment depending on that agent’s report even though her effect on aggregate mass is negligible. If a referee rejects that incentive interpretation, the first mirror remains intact.

The case covers the paper’s known single-minded, single-parameter auction with repeated item copies. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s open problem where bidders can lie about their desired sets, its derandomization question, or the revenue comparison in Theorem 5.1.

My weakest point is that the welfare mirror makes the paper’s fractional LP exact by moving to an atomless population. An opponent can say that this removes precisely the indivisibility exploited by the original mechanism. My answer is that this is the intended continuization: the individuals still receive integral bundles, while the population is represented by mass. The question is not whether the paper’s finite auction remains unchanged, but whether its SetPacking-style welfare problem becomes tractable in the high-multiplicity regime. Here the answer appears to be a clean and plausible yes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case defeats Theorem 4.1, but it does not defeat Theorem 3.8.

Theorem 4.1 is fundamentally about named individuals. Truthfulness fixes \(b_{-i}\), varies bidder \(i\)’s bid, and evaluates that individual’s payment and profit. A society represented only by a distribution \(\mu\) erases precisely this information: changing one measure-zero agent’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. A \(\mu\)-only mechanism therefore gives the same aggregate outcome under every unilateral deviation. Truthfulness is either vacuous, or the model needs an additional identity, priority, or rank for each agent. Adding those coordinates restores the finite-agent problem; replacing unilateral deviations by positive-mass type deviations gives a coalition problem, not the paper’s theorem. A price-taking continuum mechanism could be interesting, but it would be a new market model rather than a continuous mirror of RandRound’s dominant-strategy guarantee. The private-value interpretation also creates a tension: if the distribution of values is publicly supplied, the strategic problem has largely disappeared; if it is not, the welfare optimizer cannot use the stated type distribution.

Theorem 3.8 is much harder to defeat. One can object that the proposed mirror replaces the paper’s substantive contribution—randomized rounding of an integral allocation—by the already-written fractional LP

\[ \max \sum_t \theta_t x_t \]

subject to

\[ 0\le x_t\le \mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t:j\in S_t}x_t\le \kappa_j. \]

It also has no nontrivial pricing problem when the type list is explicit. Thus it is a narrow mirror of the paper’s welfare guarantee, not of its mechanism-design contribution.

But that is not a decisive objection under the programme’s rules. If many customers share the same package and value, and supplies scale as \(m_j=N\kappa_j\), then this is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocation problem. Each type can receive an integral bundle; only the aggregate served mass is fractional. Rounding an optimal mass solution down to \(\lfloor Nx_t\rfloor\) preserves feasibility and loses only an additive amount bounded by the number of types times the largest value. This is exactly the discrete-to-continuous bridge the programme is meant to study. Idiosyncratic values or non-scaling supply would make the mirror weak, but they do not rule out the plausible repeated-customer regime.

So the honest negative conclusion is limited: Theorem 4.1 has no faithful distribution-only continuization, and the paper’s main mechanism-design insight is largely lost in the continuum. But Theorem 3.8 still supplies a well-posed high-multiplicity welfare mirror. I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; the welfare anchor survives.
