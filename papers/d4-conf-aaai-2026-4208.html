The continuous mirror question

Given a seed universe \(U\), a limit \(k\), a finite support \(T\) of audience types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), monotone submodular per-capita benefit functions \(f_t:2^U\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), and rational \(\lambda\in(0,1)\), compute an efficiently samplable distribution \(P\) over sets \(S\subseteq U\) with \(|S|\le k\) whose value is at least \((1-1/e-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_P\left[\min_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim P}[f_t(S)]+\lambda\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\mathbb{E}_{S\sim P}[f_t(S)]\right]\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete campaign-response types \(t\), each with per-capita utility \(f_t\). The decision variable is a lottery \(P\) over seed sets of size at most \(k\); the objective combines the least-served type’s expected benefit with the population-mass-weighted expected benefit. The simplex oracle is an LP, and the stated boundedness assumptions let Theorem 3.1 yield the approximation guarantee in time polynomial in the universe size, support size, encoding length, and \(1/\varepsilon\).

The case FOR (proponent)

I see a credible, narrow mirror in the paper’s Setting 1: ex-ante fair influence maximization. The population to continuize is the audience receiving the campaign. The paper’s lottery over campaigns remains part of the problem, but it is not what makes the population continuous.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of audience types, with support size \(\tau\) and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type is a complete campaign-response profile: every person of type \(t\) has the same utility function \(f_t\), where \(f_t(S)\) is that type’s per-capita benefit from seeding campaign channels \(S\subseteq U\). Assume each \(f_t\) is nonnegative, monotone, and submodular, as in Setting 1. A plausible regime is a public-health campaign reaching millions of residents through a few hundred candidate channels, where replicated communities create a few dozen or hundred response types. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the share of the audience in type \(t\); for a finite population with \(n_t\) people of each type, \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Thus \(N\) may be large while \(\tau\) stays moderate.

Here is one precise mirror, Mass Ex-Ante Fair Seed Selection. The instance gives \(U\), a seed limit \(k\), the type masses, value oracles for the \(f_t\), and a fixed rational \(\lambda\in(0,1)\). A feasible campaign is a set \(S\subseteq U\) with \(|S|\le k\). For a distribution \(P\) over campaigns, write \(y_t(P)=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim P}[f_t(S)]\), and score it by
\[ G_\mu(y)=\min_{t\in T} y_t+\lambda\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_t. \]
The first term protects the least-served represented type; the second gives the population share a direct role in welfare. The question is to compute an efficiently samplable campaign distribution \(P\) whose score is at least \((1-1/e-\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{OPT}\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is the maximum score over all distributions on feasible campaigns. A compact sampler specified by \(k\) probability vectors—independently draw one channel from each and discard duplicates—counts as a solution. Assume the boundedness and nondegeneracy conditions needed by the paper’s approximation theorem. The concave-optimization oracle for this \(G_\mu\) is an LP.

My anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper in Section 3. It says that for Setting 1, with \(n\) nonnegative monotone submodular functions on a universe of \(m\) elements, a suitable concave Lipschitz aggregation function, and an oracle giving additive \(\varepsilon/(nk)\)-approximations to the stated concave simplex problem, Algorithm \(\varepsilon\)-prob greedy runs in polynomial time and returns \(k\) probability vectors. Independently sampling from them produces a set of at most \(k\) elements whose objective is at least \((1-1/e-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\), with probability at least \(1-\max\{n,m\}^{-\alpha}\), for any \(\alpha\in\mathbb N\). The benchmark \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is over arbitrary distributions on sets of size at most \(k\), not just distributions with the algorithm’s product form. The theorem is proved here, rather than cited from elsewhere.

For the mirror, substitute \(\tau\) types for the paper’s \(n\) functions and use \(G_\mu\) as the aggregation function. Its simplex oracle is polynomial in the type and input sizes, including the bit length of the masses. This gives an expected Class A result: a polynomial-time approximation with the theorem’s \(1-1/e-\varepsilon\) guarantee, measured in \(m,\tau\), the mass encoding length, and \(1/\varepsilon\). This is approximation tractability, not a claim that exact optimization is easy. In the one-type subcase the objective is just a positive scalar multiple of the ordinary coverage objective, so the familiar maximum-coverage barrier remains. The paper states a \(1-1/e+\varepsilon\) barrier for Setting 1 in its prose and Table 1, but not as a numbered theorem; I treat it as supporting context, not a second anchor.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: they explicitly use influence maximization as an application, and the mirror retains its cardinality-constrained seed choice, submodular influence functions, and ex-ante fairness objective. The co-modification is the welfare functional’s dependence on type masses, which makes the population share matter while keeping the function concave and within their framework. This case covers Setting 1 and Theorem 3.1 only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s matching or item-assignment settings.

The weakest point is the low-support assumption. In a real social network, a person’s exact position and neighborhood can change their response to many possible seed sets, so treating millions of people as a few exact types may require a strongly replicated or block-structured population. If those distinctions make \(\tau\) large, the computational appeal weakens; approximating types would require a stability analysis the theorem does not supply. Further questions include whether the stated approximation boundary holds for this mass-weighted objective in richer type regimes, and how accurately a continuous type model approximates a measured audience with near-identical, rather than identical, response profiles.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that Theorem 3.1 already covers it as a problem instance. Set \(n=\tau\), use the type functions \(f_t\), and give the theorem the aggregator
\[ G_\mu(y)=\min_{t\in T} y_t+\lambda\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_t. \]
This function is concave, monotone, and Lipschitz; its simplex optimization oracle is an LP with rational coefficients. Subject to the paper’s stated nondegeneracy conditions, Theorem 3.1 therefore gives the same campaign distribution, benchmark, approximation guarantee, and polynomial dependence on the support size and input length. The type masses are parameters of \(g\); the theorem’s model already permits that.

That makes this more than a case where an outside result happens to answer a newly posed question: the paper’s own computational model subsumes the proposed mass-based one. Reinterpreting its utility coordinates as types adds a high-multiplicity reading, but no new algorithmic question or complexity boundary. A more explicitly mass-sensitive concave welfare function would still fit the same framework, so it would not rescue this anchor.

This objection is narrow. I cannot make a convincing case that no plausible high-multiplicity audience exists, or that the continuous population model is incoherent. The proponent has a sensible mirror; the negative case is that this particular mirror is already contained in the paper’s theorem, not that continuization has no worthwhile application anywhere.
