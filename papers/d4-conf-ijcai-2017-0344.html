The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes equivalent WEU representations; Propositions 1 and 2 normalize those representations and connect an assumption to monotonicity. None classifies an algorithmic problem. The paper does give an elicitation procedure, LP formulations, and experiments, but no complexity theorem or proved query or approximation guarantee. So there is no computational result here for a continuous mirror to preserve or transform.

The tempting population version is a society of customers with different WEU preferences, from which one wants to choose a lottery. But the paper asks for the best lottery for one DM. If everyone in a cohort has the same complete preference type, one representative’s answers suffice and the cohort’s mass adds nothing to that task. If types differ, the problem needs a social objective—majority preference, aggregate welfare, or robustness across types—that the paper does not provide. The WEU representation is not a shared cardinal scale: equivalent representations can change the numerical intensities between lotteries. A majority rule could avoid that issue, but it would introduce a collective choice problem, rather than mirror this paper’s elicitation result.

The other analyst’s customer-population suggestion therefore identifies a plausible adjacent project, not an anchor from this paper. I can’t honestly rule out that such a project would be worthwhile. The negative case is strongest when scoped to the requested mirror: none of the paper’s named results supplies a computational target, and adding a population forces the central question to change.
