The continuous mirror question

Given a finite spatial template with role set \(T\), conflict graph \(H=(T,E)\), and common role bids, repeat the template across zones \(u\in[0,1]\), with zones more than \(d\) apart. Each role has mass \(\mu_t=1/|T|\), and an allocation \(a(u)\in\{0,1\}^{T}\) must satisfy \(a_t(u)+a_s(u)\leq1\) for each \((t,s)\in E\) almost everywhere. Find a measurable allocation whose served mass is at least the Theorem 2 factor \(\min\{\kappa/\bar d(H),\,\kappa' \log(\log \Delta(H))/\Delta(H)\}\) times the maximum feasible served mass, where \(\bar d(H)\) and \(\Delta(H)\) are the average and maximum degrees.

The model it lives in

A continuum of separated copies of a finite deployment template \(H\), with one buyer per role in each zone and mass \(\mu_t=1/|T|\) per role overall. Each person receives an integral allocation; the measurable role-level averages may be fractional. A constant allocation across zones can be produced by applying STAMP to \(H\), and mixtures of independent sets cannot exceed the best independent set’s served mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s allocation-efficiency approximation guarantee for repeated spatial templates. It leaves the strategy-proofness and semi-group-strategy-proofness claims, payment schemes, and empirical comparisons outside the stated question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can geometric restrictions on \(H\) improve the approximation guarantee for served mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case I believe is a mirror of the paper’s allocation-efficiency result in a repeated wireless-deployment regime. Imagine a sensing or spectrum service deployed across a very large, expanding region in many identical, separated zones. Each zone contains the same small spatial pattern of buyer locations; zones are more than \(d\) apart, so buyers in different zones never conflict. In a zone, each location-role has one buyer, and buyers in the same role across zones have the same value and costs. If the pattern has \(\tau\) roles and there are \(M\) zones, then \(n=M\tau\), while the type support stays at \(\tau\). In the limit, type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t=1/\tau\). Mass is the fraction of buyers in that role; each buyer still receives an integral allocation, either \(0\) or \(1\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that STAMP achieves an asymptotic allocation-efficiency approximation ratio of
\[ \min\left\{\frac{\kappa}{\mu},\frac{\kappa'\log(\log\Delta)}{\Delta}\right\}, \]
where \(\kappa>0\), \(\kappa'\) depends on \(\kappa\), and \(\Delta\) and \(\mu\) are the conflict graph’s maximum and average degrees. The paper proves the STAMP guarantee by using the cited maximum-independent-set approximation bound from [6] and observing that STAMP’s reallocation does not reduce the number of winners. The theorem’s mechanism-specific claim is therefore tied directly to a familiar computational problem: finding a large independent set in the conflict graph. The paper has an unnumbered, cited intractability statement about exact allocation, but no numbered hardness result that I would use as an anchor.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Repeated-Deployment Mass Allocation. Its input is a finite spatial template with \(\tau\) roles, rational coordinates, conflict distance \(d\), and the resulting graph \(H=(T,E)\). A continuum of zones is indexed by \(u\in[0,1]\); each zone has one buyer at every role, with conflicts exactly along \(E\). The task is to output a measurable allocation \(a(u)\in\{0,1\}^{T}\) that is feasible in almost every zone—\(a_t(u)+a_s(u)\leq1\) for every \((t,s)\in E\)—and serves at least the Theorem 2 factor times the maximum feasible mass. The served fraction is
\[ \frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^1\sum_{t\in T}a_t(u)\,du. \]
A solution can be given compactly as a finite mixture of independent sets \(S\) of \(H\): allocate according to \(S\) in a fraction \(\lambda_S\) of the zones, where \(\lambda_S\geq0\) and \(\sum_S\lambda_S=1\). Every person’s allocation remains integral; the type-level averages can be fractional.

The theorem’s factor carries over exactly. The finite graph for \(M\) zones is \(M\) disjoint copies of \(H\), so its maximum and average degrees are those of \(H\), and both the mechanism’s allocation and the optimum scale by \(M\). Their ratio is unchanged as \(M\) grows. Thus the stated approximation is tractable for this continuous question, with the computational input measured by \(\tau\), the graph representation’s bit length, and the degree parameters—not by the number of people in the limiting population. Adding common bids by role lets one run STAMP in each zone; I am isolating the allocation-efficiency claim, not claiming a new continuous payment or incentive result.

The exact version remains hard for the familiar reason: if every zone uses the same independent set, maximizing served mass is precisely maximum independent set on \(H\). That is a transferred-hardness follow-up, not a continuum-specific obstacle. Further questions include whether the paper’s approximation factor can be improved in this regime, and whether additional geometry or regularity in the repeated templates yields better algorithms.

The weak point is that repetition across separated zones is a structured instance regime. It makes a convincing high-multiplicity sensing or spectrum scenario, but the hard combinatorics remain in the \(\tau\)-role template; taking the population limit does not make exact allocation easier. That limits the novelty of this mirror, but not its legitimacy: it continuizes the population, keeps the spatial-reuse allocation question intact, and carries a named result of the paper over to mass measured as a fraction of society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, and it is a real computational result: it gives STAMP an approximation guarantee for allocation efficiency. The objection has to be to whether a continuous population preserves a meaningful spatial-allocation problem.

With buyers spread continuously through a fixed region, hard conflicts within distance \(d\) create a basic problem: any feasible set of winners is \(d\)-separated. In a bounded region, such a set has bounded size, so its share of an increasingly dense population tends to zero. The natural atomless spatial limit therefore loses the paper’s allocation-efficiency objective.

The best repair is the proponent’s expanding collection of separated, repeated zones. But preserving conflicts requires keeping the finite role pattern inside each zone. In the limit, the problem is a continuum of copies of one finite conflict graph \(H\). A feasible allocation is a distribution over independent sets of \(H\); since the objective is linear, some independent set in that distribution serves at least as much mass as the mixture. Thus the continuous problem reduces to finding an independent set in \(H\), and Theorem 2’s guarantee carries over by running the finite algorithm on that template.

That makes this a thin mirror: the continuum indexes repeated local auctions, while all the combinatorial work stays in one finite graph. It does not yield a new continuous optimization problem or expose a pricing problem for the continuous-optimization toolbox. More elaborate spatial limits face the same choice: preserve hard local conflicts by retaining finite local structure, or use an atomless population in which the feasible winner mass vanishes. Softening conflicts or changing them to local capacity constraints might produce a richer model, but would need a separate case that the authors regard as the same allocation problem.

This is not a decisive defeat under ChoCo’s stated criteria. Repeated zones are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, the types can be complete descriptions of the buyers’ roles, and a known or transferred answer does not make a well-posed mirror worthless. So the negative case can argue that the mirror offers little *new continuous structure*, but it cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 2’s anchor survives; this case is weaker than the proponent’s.
