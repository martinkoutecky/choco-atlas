The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational coefficients \(b_t\ge 0\) with \(B=\sum_t\mu_t b_t>0\), and continuous, surjective, weakly increasing type-specific curves \(g_t:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), define the aggregate by \(F_\mu[x]=B^{-1}\sum_t\mu_t b_t\int_0^1 g_t(x(t,u))\,du\). What are each type’s total \(\varphi\)- and \(\psi\)-importance, defined as the limits of sums of individual indices in finite replications with type fractions tending to \(\mu_t\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents has types \(t\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), mass \(\mu_t\), coefficient \(b_t\), and curve \(g_t\); each type’s agents share the same curve and state law. The aggregate is \(F_\mu[x]=B^{-1}\sum_t\mu_t b_t\int_0^1g_t(x(t,u))\,du\), and the type-level importance is the limit of the individual indices summed within each type. Theorem 6.3 gives \(I_t^\varphi=I_t^\psi=\mu_t b_t/B\).

The objection that survived

The response curves \(g_t\) drop out: the type totals are mechanically inherited from the finite-replica formula, so this mirror adds little computational content.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 6.3’s additive aggregation family; leaves the general importance measures, weighted-median results, and broader aggregation classes unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-level calculation extend efficiently to weighted-median aggregation or other families studied in the paper?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a type-level, high-multiplicity version of the paper’s importance calculation for additive interval aggregation.

The anchor is Theorem 6.3, proved in this paper. It says that for nonnegative weights \(w_i\) summing to one and continuous, surjective, weakly increasing functions \(f_i:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), the aggregation function \(f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_i w_i f_i(x_i)\) is a simple aggregation function and both importance measures satisfy \(\varphi_i(f)=\psi_i(f)=w_i\). The paper states no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result; this exact formula is its strongest computational anchor. For rationally encoded instances, it yields a polynomial-time calculation.

Continuous Type Importance for Additive Aggregation. An instance consists of \(\tau\) types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, nonnegative rational influence coefficients \(b_t\) with \(B=\sum_t\mu_t b_t>0\), and rational piecewise-linear curves \(g_t:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) that are continuous, surjective, and weakly increasing. A society is a continuum of agents \((t,u)\), with mass \(\mu_t\,du\) of type \(t\); members of a type share its coefficient, response curve, and uniform state law. For a state profile \(x(t,u)\), the aggregate is \(F_\mu[x]=B^{-1}\sum_t\mu_t b_t\int_0^1 g_t(x(t,u))\,du\). The task is to return, for each type, its total Shapley–Shubik-style importance and its total Penrose–Banzhaf-style importance, defined as the limits of the sums of the respective individual indices as the number of agents grows with each type’s population fraction tending to \(\mu_t\).

To see that the limit is well-defined, take any such finite replication. Each type-\(t\) agent has weight \(b_t/\sum_s n_s b_s\), so Theorem 6.3 gives that value for each of the two individual indices. Summing within type and taking the limit gives both type-level importance vectors as \(I_t^\varphi=I_t^\psi=\mu_t b_t/B\). Thus the mirror is tractable by direct rational arithmetic, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

A plausible regime is a large public consultation in which many respondents submit normalized policy positions, while a small number of constituency or reliability classes share a contribution weight and calibration curve. The population could be in the millions while \(\tau\) remains small. The type is precisely the attributes the calculation uses—\(b_t\), \(g_t\), and the state law—and mass is the fraction of respondents in that class. This preserves the paper’s question of how agents influence a real-valued collective decision; it aggregates the same index across indistinguishable agents rather than replacing it with an intervention problem. The interval outcome is already in the paper; the mirror’s continuization is of the population.

The expected result is tractability for this restricted class. The proposed problem covers Theorem 6.3’s additive family, not the paper’s general importance measures, weighted medians, or all aggregation functions. Natural next questions are whether the type-level calculation extends to other aggregation families and whether it remains efficient under nonuniform or correlated state distributions.

The weak point is that this is a narrow, easy slice of the paper: the type-level answer follows almost immediately from its finite-agent formula, and the paper does not claim a complexity classification. The uniform independent state assumption also limits the interpretation. Still, a large population with few repeated roles is a credible regime, and this mirror keeps the paper’s importance measure intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the computational anchor, not about whether repeated agents make sense. The paper has no theorem classifying an importance-calculation problem by complexity. Its computational motivation is that direct evaluation becomes infeasible as the number of agents grows; its named results give properties and formulas for importance measures. Theorem 6.3 is an exact identity for an additive family, not a complexity result.

The proposed population model is coherent. For a finite replication with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\), Theorem 6.3 gives each such agent importance \(b_t/\sum_s n_s b_s\). Summing within types and taking the limit yields \(\mu_t b_t/B\). This is a valid high-multiplicity interpretation, and the type classes are a plausible regime. But it does not expose a new computational question left by the paper: the theorem already determines every finite calculation in the sequence, and the limiting type totals follow from it. The response curves \(g_t\) do not affect the result. That is a useful interpretation of the formula, though a thin basis for a complexity-landscape project.

A stronger candidate would use the paper’s weighted-median results: let a large electorate grow with a fixed number of weight types, then study type-level Shapley–Shubik- or Banzhaf-style importance. That is still a plausible population mirror, and I would not reject it on grounds of lost identities, vanishing individual importance, or fractional outcomes. The difficulty for the negative case is that this would require a specified family of growing aggregation functions and an input representation for computing the type totals. The paper supplies neither a complexity theorem nor such a high-multiplicity family. It would be a new computational project inspired by the paper, rather than a mirror of a computational result it establishes.

So the negative case is strongest under ChoCo’s requirement for a named computational anchor. It is not airtight as a claim that no worthwhile population-continuous project exists: the public-consultation scenario, and especially a weighted-median extension, remain credible. I would not manufacture a stronger obstruction than the paper supports.
