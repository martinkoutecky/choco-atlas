The continuous mirror question

Given applicant and project types with rational masses, capacities, compatible edges, and strict rankings of counterpart types, choose a feasible mass matching \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) minimizing \(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}x_{ij}\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i q_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\nu_j r_j\). For each type and any challenger flow \(y\), pair lost and gained partner mass by a fractional transport that minimizes the vote under that type's ranking; call \(x\) popular if its summed vote is nonnegative against every feasible \(y\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided nonatomic population described by rational masses over complete preference-and-capacity types, with feasible matchings represented by mass flows \(x_{ij}\). Minimize edge cost \(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}x_{ij}\) subject to popularity under the type-level fractional-transport vote rule.

The objection that survived

A type-level flow records partner incidences but not how integral bundles are distributed among people, so its fractional-transport votes need not reproduce the paper's individual bundle votes; the proposed rule is an extension for homogeneous cohorts.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is Min-Cost Popular Perfect Mass Matching. Its anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for a many-to-many bipartite instance with strict, possibly incomplete preferences and a cost on each edge, if a perfect matching exists, a minimum-cost popular perfect matching can be computed in polynomial time. “Popular” here means that no other perfect matching wins a head-to-head election against it under the paper’s vertex-voting rule. The paper gives no comparable hardness theorem for this exact problem, so this is an algorithmic anchor.

Consider a large placement market with many applicants and projects, each side divided into a modest number of cohorts. A type records a side, capacity, strict ranking of compatible types on the other side, and the other information the problem uses. Cohort members share that information. For example, applicants might be grouped by qualification and project preferences, and projects by capacity and applicant priorities. Let the two sides’ type masses be rational, with balanced total capacity, and let the cost of matching a unit of one type to another be fixed for that type pair.

A mass matching is a flow \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of matches between applicant type \(i\) and project type \(j\). It satisfies \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i q_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\nu_j r_j\), where \(q_i,r_j\) are the types’ capacities and \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) their masses. Each unit of population still receives an integral bundle; the flow records the aggregate, so its per-capita averages may be fractional. The cost is \(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}x_{ij}\).

To extend the paper’s vote rule, compare a proposed flow \(x\) with any feasible challenger \(y\). At each type \(v\), the positive differences \(x_{vw}-y_{vw}\) are mass of partner incidences lost, and the positive differences \(y_{vw}-x_{vw}\) are mass gained. Pair those losses with gains using a fractional transport \(\pi^v\), and minimize the total vote over such pairings, assigning \(+1\) when the old partner type is preferred, \(-1\) when the new one is preferred, and \(0\) when they are the same. Sum these minimum votes over all types to obtain \(\Delta(x,y)\). Call \(x\) popular if \(\Delta(x,y)\geq 0\) for every feasible \(y\). The problem is: given the rational type masses, capacities, rankings, compatibility graph and costs, compute an exact minimum-cost feasible flow that is popular under this rule. Its running time should be measured in the number of types, compatibility edges, and input bit length.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable, though that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 4. The paper’s useful bridge is its characterization of popularity through a stable matching in a colorful auxiliary instance. A continuous counterpart might yield a stable mass-matching formulation or a finite separation problem. The main further questions are whether that characterization extends to mass flows, whether popularity has a polynomial-size certificate or efficient separation procedure, and how rational mass instances relate to finite repeated-cohort markets. The theorem’s polynomial algorithm operates on an explicit graph, so its runtime does not by itself imply a bound polynomial in compressed support size and binary mass encoding.

The case is plausible because the population itself is what becomes continuous: the object is still a two-sided matching, with capacities, preferences, perfect feasibility, popularity, and minimum edge cost. The weakest point is the vote semantics. The paper pairs changed partners separately at each named vertex; the mass-flow rule pairs changed partner incidences at the type level. That is a natural extension of its adversarial pairing rule, but it can lose information about which individuals received which bundles. If the authors require strict rankings over named counterpart individuals, rather than strict rankings over interchangeable counterpart types, this mirror needs a richer type model and may lose its compactness. The proposal therefore covers Theorem 4’s min-cost popular perfect-matching problem, not the paper’s other matching variants.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real algorithmic anchor, so the case against cannot start by saying the paper has no computational result to mirror. The problem is whether its popularity rule can be carried by a population distribution over types. In the paper, each named vertex compares its exact partner sets, and the vote can depend on how those sets are paired.

Here is a case where the proposed mass-flow mirror loses that information. Let \(a_1,a_2\) each have capacity \(3\), and let \(u_1,\ldots,u_6\) each have capacity \(1\). Both \(a\)-vertices rank \(u_1\succ\cdots\succ u_6\), and every \(u_j\) ranks \(a_1\succ a_2\). Let \(M\) assign \(\{u_1,u_3,u_5\}\) to \(a_1\) and \(\{u_2,u_4,u_6\}\) to \(a_2\); let \(N\) swap those bundles. Both are perfect matchings. The paper’s own example gives votes of \(-1\) and \(-3\) for those two bundle comparisons, so the \(a\)-vertices contribute \(-4\) to \(\Delta(M,N)\). The \(u\)-vertices’ votes cancel, leaving \(\Delta(M,N)=-4\). Yet the aggregate flow between the two \(a\)-vertices’ type and the \(u\)-vertices’ type is identical in \(M\) and \(N\). A rule based only on the difference between those flows returns zero.

That mismatch presents a dilemma for the proposed finite-type mirror. If it groups people by their own preference lists, it can group the \(u_j\)’s even though the \(a\)-vertices strictly distinguish them; then type-level flows do not determine popularity. If the type description preserves those distinctions, it must record rank positions in the other side’s lists too. In ordinary strict-priority markets, those positions can make the number of types grow with the population. A continuum over rank positions is conceivable, but it requires a different input model and a way to compare whole partner bundles across alternatives. The paper does not supply that bridge.

This defeats the stated mass-transport formulation and makes a compact high-multiplicity lift of Theorem 4 much less immediate. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A market whose preferences are genuinely over interchangeable cohorts, or a carefully specified continuum of rank positions, could still yield a coherent computational question. Those versions change or enlarge the paper’s preference model, and I cannot honestly rule them out as unworthy. So the negative case is strongest against this proposed flow mirror; the universal claim that no scenario is worthwhile remains weak.
