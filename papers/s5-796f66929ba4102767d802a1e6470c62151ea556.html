The continuous mirror question

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper; it contains no named hardness result to anchor on. It says that, in the random-order secretary model, with the right-side vertices known in advance, arbitrary edge utilities and bids, edge utilities whose maximum-to-minimum ratio is at most \(\beta\), and the paper’s large-market assumption, Algorithm ON is \(24\beta\)-competitive in expected matching utility, truthful, and feasible for the total payment budget. That is a substantive computational result about online allocation and incentives, not just an encoding or a claim about fractional outcomes.

A plausible regime is a large crowdsourcing market running a campaign with many tasks. Workers share a type when they have the same eligibility, utility for each task class, and cost per accepted unit; these are complete worker types, not just preference labels. Think of standardized skill and compensation categories, with \(N\) workers but a much smaller support \(\tau\). Task capacity must scale with the market too: a task class represents a fraction of the campaign’s available slots, rather than one fixed slot as \(N\) grows. That co-scaling is part of the mirror. It is plausible for recurring large campaigns, though less so for a one-off set of individually negotiated bids.

I would pose the lead mirror as the fluid limit of Online Budgeted Truthful Matching. An instance has a finite task-class set \(R\), a finite support \(T\) of worker types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, type costs \(c_t\), utilities \(u_{tr}\), eligibility edges, per-capita task capacities \(q_r\), and a normalized payment budget \(b\). Workers arrive online in random order. On each arrival, the mechanism sees the worker’s utility vector and reported cost, and must irrevocably assign that worker to at most one eligible task or leave them unmatched. In the fluid policy, an arriving infinitesimal mass may be split across task classes: the aggregate allocation is mass \(x_{tr}(s)\) at arrival time \(s\). Cumulative allocation from type \(t\) cannot exceed \(\mu_t\), allocation to task class \(r\) cannot exceed \(q_r\), and total payments cannot exceed \(b\). The offline benchmark is the fractional matching LP maximizing \(\sum_{t,r}u_{tr}y_{tr}\), subject to \(\sum_r y_{tr}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{tr}\le q_r\), and \(\sum_{t,r}c_ty_{tr}\le b\).

The question for a prover is: does there exist a causal, truthful fluid mechanism whose expected welfare is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/(24\beta)\), with the stated budget and eligibility constraints? Define the random-order input through equal-mass refinements: replace each type’s mass by many identical worker parcels, uniformly permute them, scale capacities and budget with population, and take the limit of the mechanism’s aggregate allocations and payments. Truthfulness is per worker in the refinements and per infinitesimal worker in the limit. The expected approximation is measured against the fluid LP benchmark. The expected direction is Class A: the fixed-support benchmark is a linear program, and the paper’s threshold and sample-and-price structure offers a concrete route to an online mass policy. The theorem does not by itself establish convergence to such a policy; proving that, or finding a better fluid guarantee, is the question.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s online budgeted matching problem, including irrevocable assignment, budget feasibility, random-order input, and truthfulness. It does not claim that the paper’s applications are already continuous merely because they involve matching, nor does it mirror every reverse auction.

The weak point is the random-order limit. A literal atomless limit of uniform permutations can make each type’s arrival rate deterministic, eliminating the sample variation that the paper’s algorithm uses. The proposed refinement definition makes that issue explicit, but an author or referee could reasonably ask whether the resulting fluid policy still captures the secretary problem they studied. That is the part I would test hardest. The high-multiplicity worker types and scaled task capacities are a sensible regime; the continuity of the *online information structure* is less settled.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational result, so the strongest objection is not that the paper lacks an anchor. Nor is high multiplicity inherently implausible here: repeated worker types and scaled task capacities make sense for a large crowdsourcing market. The problem is that the paper’s central online feature—the uncertainty created by a uniformly random order—does not survive the proposed fluid limit in a useful form.

Suppose there are \(N\) workers and a fixed finite support \(T\), with type proportions tending to \(\mu_t\). Under a uniform random permutation, the fraction of type \(t\) seen by time \(s\) converges to \(s\mu_t\). Thus the arrival stream becomes the deterministic flow \(s\mapsto s\mu\). If the mechanism is not given \(\mu\) in advance, any fixed positive sampling interval reveals its proportions with vanishing relative error as \(N\) grows; in the atomless limit, the type rates are exact. The secretary uncertainty that motivates ON’s sample-and-price structure has disappeared. Letting the sampling interval shrink after taking the limit makes the information-gathering phase negligible in population mass.

That exposes a problem in the proposed mirror’s information model. If \(\mu\), costs, and utilities are known as part of the instance, the mechanism already knows the future profile. If they are hidden until workers arrive, fixed-support random order reveals their aggregate rates essentially immediately in the limit. Either way, the mirror is not testing whether ON’s random-order guarantee extends to a continuous population. It is closer to a full-information fluid allocation problem. Individual truthfulness can still be defined, but it does not restore the missing uncertainty about future arrivals.

A more faithful rescue would preserve nonvanishing uncertainty in the arrival stream—for example, by making the type mix vary over time or adding a random market-wide shock. That could be a worthwhile online mechanism-design problem, but it changes the paper’s fixed population in uniformly random order into a stochastic, time-indexed population model, with a new information structure and benchmark. Alternatively, retaining the finite-sample fluctuations means keeping the discrete arrivals, or studying fluctuations on a scale smaller than the theorem’s per-capita welfare guarantee. Those are meaningful extensions, but they do not supply the claimed fluid mirror of Theorem 1.

So the negative case is strongest against the specific anchor and its natural fixed-support continuization: the feature that makes the theorem an online secretary result washes out when the population becomes a deterministic mass flow. It is not airtight against every scenario. A process-valued continuous model with persistent aggregate uncertainty may be worth studying, but the paper alone gives little reason to treat that different model as its continuous mirror.
