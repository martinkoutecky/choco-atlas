The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of rational piecewise-constant valuation types \(u_t\) on \([0,1]\), a rational type-mass vector \(\mu\), and market size \(r\), compute a finite-support distribution \(\Pi\) over \(r\)-agent configurations \(a=((t_1,X_1),\ldots,(t_r,X_r))\), with \((X_1,\ldots,X_r)\) partitioning \([0,1]\) and type marginal \(\mu\), maximizing \(\int \frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log u_{t_j}(X_j)\,d\Pi(a)\); determine exact or \(\varepsilon\)-optimal algorithms and, separately, whether a positive-mass bloc analogue of NOM exists.

The model it lives in

High-multiplicity replicated-cake Nash welfare: \(\mu\) is the population type distribution, \(\Pi\) is the empirical distribution of integral \(r\)-agent cake allocations across replicated markets, and the objective is average log utility.

The objection that survived

The type marginal \(\mu\) does not by itself determine how types are grouped into \(r\)-agent markets, so optimizing \(\Pi\) adds a correlation or grouping decision absent from the paper; this is a genuine extension, though not a fatal one.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3's Nash-optimal allocation mechanism and its envy-free and Pareto-optimality conclusions in a high-multiplicity regime. It leaves Theorems 1 and 2, the indirect-protocol NOM analysis, connectedness, and individual-agent incentives largely unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the replicated-cake Nash-welfare problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(|T|\), the encoding length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • Can optimal \(\Pi\) be represented with polynomially bounded support and rounded while preserving the type marginal and Nash welfare?
  • What positive-mass bloc-NOM definition gives a nonvacuous continuum analogue of the paper's individual NOM property?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: the paper has no numbered theorem proving NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or any other standard computational classification. Its only plausible computational anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper:

“There exists a NOM direct-revelation mechanism that finds envy-free and Pareto-optimal allocations.”

The mechanism is the Nash-optimal rule: given utility functions \(u_1,\ldots,u_n\), it chooses a partition \((X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) maximizing \(\prod_i u_i(X_i)\). The paper observes that this allocation is Pareto-optimal and envy-free, hence proportional; Lemmas 3 and 4 then imply NOM. The paper further cites Aziz et al. for an efficient algorithm when utilities are piecewise constant. Thus Theorem 3 is proved here, while its efficient-computation component is cited rather than established as a numbered \(P\)-theorem.

My lead mirror would be High-Multiplicity Nash-Cake\(_\infty\).

Consider a large land or cloud-resource authority dividing many identical cakes, plots, or resource intervals. Each cake has \(r\) claimants, but the total number of claimants is large because there are many repeated markets. A finite set \(T\) of valuation types is given. Type \(t\) is a rational piecewise-constant nonatomic valuation \(u_t\) on \([0,1]\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the whole claimant population having that type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is \(R\gg |T|\): many claimants and repeated markets, but relatively few distinct valuation types.

A micro-allocation is

\[ a=((t_1,X_1),\ldots,(t_r,X_r)), \]

where \(X_1,\ldots,X_r\) partition \([0,1]\), and claimant \(j\) has type \(t_j\) and receives the integral cake piece \(X_j\). A continuous population allocation is a finite-support probability measure \(\Pi\) over such micro-allocations. It must have the prescribed type marginal

\[ \mu_t= \frac{1}{r} \int \sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbf 1[t_j=t]\, d\Pi(a). \]

The decision variable is therefore the mass of claimant-market configurations, not a fractional cake given to an individual. Every unit of population mass still receives an integral piece in every configuration.

The problem is to compute a \(\Pi\) maximizing average log utility,

\[ \operatorname{NW}(\Pi) = \frac{1}{r} \int \sum_{j=1}^{r} \log u_{t_j}(X_j)\, d\Pi(a). \]

The exact version asks for an optimal finite-support \(\Pi\). The computational version takes a rational \(\varepsilon>0\) and asks for a finite-support \(\Pi\) satisfying the marginal constraints and

\[ \operatorname{NW}(\Pi) \ge \operatorname{OPT}-\varepsilon. \]

This is the correct high-multiplicity analogue of the Nash product: it uses average \(\log\)-utility, not \(\log\) of a type’s average utility. If \(\Pi=\sum_{\ell}w_\ell\delta_{a^\ell}\) has rational weights, clearing denominators produces \(R\) concrete cake markets. There are \(rR\) individual claimants, each receives an integral piece, and

\[ \operatorname{NW}(\Pi) = \frac{1}{rR} \log \left( \prod_{\ell,j} u_{t^\ell_j}(X^\ell_j) \right). \]

So the continuous question is exactly the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s finite Nash-welfare computation, with type mass replacing a long list of repeated agents. A canonical optimum can be chosen so that every positive-mass micro-market is Nash-optimal for its own type profile; Theorem 3 then supplies envy-freeness and Pareto-optimality within each market.

I would expect the unrestricted piecewise-constant version to be Class A, probably via a finite atomization of valuation breakpoints followed by convex optimization or column generation. The pricing problem is over cake partitions and has the same Nash-welfare structure that the paper cites as efficiently solvable. The real research obligations are to prove polynomial dependence on \(r\), \(|T|\), encoding length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), and to establish finite-support and rounding bounds. Connected-piece restrictions, or implicit valuation types, could instead produce a Class C boundary.

This mirror is plausible because it retains the paper’s actual object: utility functions, integral cake pieces, Nash welfare, envy-freeness, and Pareto efficiency. The population is the newly continuous object; the cake was already continuous in the paper. The repeated-cake setting is a deliberate per-capita resource scaling, analogous to repeated hospitals or replicated assignment markets, rather than a claim that an atomless population can all receive positive pieces from one fixed cake.

It does not cover Theorems 1 or 2. In particular, individual NOM does not transfer automatically: a measure-zero agent cannot affect \(\Pi\). A separate follow-up would need a positive-mass or bloc-NOM definition and would ask whether changing the report distribution of a type can improve both its worst- and best-case utilities.

The weakest point is also decisive: Theorem 3 is primarily a fairness and mechanism-existence theorem, not a named complexity theorem. The efficient algorithm is cited, not classified in the paper. Moreover, repeated cakes and support-wise allocations are a substantive modelling extension. If the programme requires an explicit numbered \(P\)/NP-hardness anchor, this paper probably fails that gate. If an algorithmic formulation counts, however, High-Multiplicity Nash-Cake\(_\infty\) is a defensible, precise, likely-tractable mirror of the paper’s computational core.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s entry gate: this paper contains no numbered computational result of the required kind. Theorems 1 and 2 classify manipulability and fairness properties of procedures; Lemmas 3 and 4 are structural observations; Theorem 3 is a mechanism-existence theorem. None states a decision problem, input model, running time, complexity class, approximation guarantee, or parameterized algorithm. The paper merely cites an efficient algorithm for a restricted piecewise-constant case. Thus there is no computational theorem here whose population-continuous counterpart the programme needs to chart.

Theorem 3 is nevertheless the only plausible anchor, so grant it. Its object is a single finite cake-cutting instance: \(n\) named agents, one cake, one profile of utility functions, and one allocation \(X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) maximizing

\[ \prod_{i=1}^n u_i(X_i). \]

The proposed High-Multiplicity Nash-Cake changes all of that. It introduces many independent cakes or markets, a distribution \(\Pi\) over finite market configurations, and an objective

\[ \frac{1}{r}\int \sum_{j=1}^{r}\log u_{t_j}(X_j)\,d\Pi. \]

This is an expectation of Nash welfare over a lottery of finite problems, not Nash welfare for one continuous society. Clearing denominators produces many separate finite cake-cutting markets; it does not produce one high-multiplicity instance of the paper’s problem. The type marginal \(\mu\) does not determine which types meet in a market, so the model also adds a new grouping or correlation problem absent from the paper.

The paper’s fairness conclusions do not survive this reinterpretation either. Envy-freeness and Pareto-optimality hold within each finite market, but agents in different markets have no common allocation to compare. They are properties of the support-wise microinstances, not of the aggregate population allocation. Calling \(\Pi\) a population allocation therefore hides that the genuinely new continuous object is a lottery over outcomes—precisely the sort of outcome-space continuization the programme says is not its goal.

The more faithful alternative is to let a continuum of agents divide one cake. That exposes a fundamental degeneration. A named agent has measure zero, so changing her report does not change the population distribution. Individual strategy-proofness and NOM then become vacuous: every rule depending only on \(\mu\) is immune to unilateral manipulation. Meanwhile, with finite cake and atomless population, almost every individual receives zero cake, and proportionality collapses to the vacuous bound \(0\).

One can repair this by scaling cake supply, renormalizing per-capita utilities, or replacing individual NOM by a positive-mass bloc deviation. But each repair changes the theorem’s subject. Scaling supply yields repeated finite markets; renormalization changes the utility objective; bloc-NOM is a new coalition-incentive concept for which the paper proves nothing. A type-level Nash objective such as \(\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t\) could be an interesting new fair-division problem, but it would not be a continuous version of Theorem 3’s individual mechanism result.

This is not an objection that the resulting problem might be easy, hard, or have the same answer. The objection is that the proposed model either degenerates, or replaces the paper’s single-market incentive problem with a new optimization over markets and lotteries. The only serious mirror is therefore a plausible new ChoCo project inspired by the paper, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of a computational result actually established in it. The honest negative case is consequently strong under the programme’s stated computational gate, though not a proof that no independently interesting continuous cake-cutting problem could ever be formulated.
