The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent correctly finds no named computational result to anchor a mirror. The paper’s central formal results are axiomatic: Table 1 lists which principles the allocation rules satisfy, and the CEA Lorenz-dominance comparison is attributed to earlier work. Tables 3–7 report allocations and fairness comparisons for a small set of regional claims. None states an algorithmic complexity result or poses a computational problem whose complexity the paper investigates.

That absence is decisive for the proposed programme, though it does not make a continuous claims model unnatural. A high-multiplicity version is easy to describe: type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\) and per-claimant claim \(c_t\), and a per-capita endowment \(e\) is divided into awards \(a_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=e\). Anonymity and equal treatment make repeated claimant types a sensible regime. This is evidence for the model’s plausibility, not a reason to dismiss it.

But the best version of that mirror still yields routine computations. Proportional awards are \(a_t=e c_t/\sum_t\mu_t c_t\). CEA awards have the form \(a_t=\min(c_t,\lambda)\), with \(\lambda\) found from the weighted budget equation; CEL is the corresponding weighted threshold calculation. The other listed rules are similarly explicit or piecewise explicit. Sorting the \(\tau\) claim types and evaluating these formulas is polynomial in the encoded input. Even the random-arrival rule’s factorial-looking sum does not rescue the mirror: when arrivals are among a large population of individual claimants, its per-type expected award converges to the proportional award, since an individual’s random position is asymptotically uniform and the aggregate claims ahead of them concentrate. Weighted fairness indices add sums over types or pairs of types, not a new complexity landscape.

The same applies if the mirror focuses on the paper’s empirical selection: with many types, one can calculate the candidate allocations and weighted equity and stability measures from the support. To make that computationally difficult would require adding constraints or interactions absent from this paper, and would shift the object away from the claims allocation it studies. So the strongest negative case is narrow but substantial: a continuous population is sensible here, yet the paper offers no computational result to mirror, and its most natural high-multiplicity extension appears computationally immediate. I cannot establish the broader claim that no conceivable related scenario could be worthwhile; the case is weaker on that universal point than on the absence of a ChoCo-style computational question in this paper.
