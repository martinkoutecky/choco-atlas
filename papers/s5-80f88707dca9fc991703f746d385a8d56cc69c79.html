The strongest positive avenue is a high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s core and social-loss questions: many households share complete economic types—preferences, endowments, ownership shares, and externality parameters—and each type has a rational population mass. A coalition would then be described by the mass it draws from each type, and its deviation would specify consumption, production, and any required feasible response by outsiders. That is a plausible continuous-population research direction, but it is not anchored in a computational result of this paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 7 equates two definitions of the core; Theorem 13 characterizes core allocations as zero points of a social-loss mapping. The corresponding \(\gamma\)- and \(\alpha\)-core theorems do the same under additional assumptions. None gives a complexity classification, algorithm, or finite input model. The proponents acknowledge this, so they have not supplied an eligible anchor.

The strongest version of their proposed direction is a large economy with finitely many complete economic types, each carrying a mass. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; I would not dismiss it because the paper has finitely many agents or because continuous-agent economics already exists. But turning it into computation requires choices the paper does not make. Its preferences are relations over the whole allocation, and its production and outsider-response correspondences may depend on the particular coalition and status quo. A list of type masses alone need not encode those primitives. One would have to specify finite representations and restrict the model so coalition feasibility and externalities can be computed from them, then choose a task such as core membership or social-loss computation. Those could be worthwhile new questions, but they are not algorithmic versions of results established here. The paper supplies neither an encoding nor a computational structure—such as an optimization formulation with a tractable pricing problem—from which to derive them.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: the proposed type-mass economy may be a good project in computational cooperative economics, but the paper itself offers no computational anchor for ChoCo. That does not prove no such project could be worthwhile; it does mean a continuous mirror of this paper has not been established by the proponent’s case.
