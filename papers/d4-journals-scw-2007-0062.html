The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to anchor one. Theorem 4 and Theorem 6 characterize aggregation functions under conditions such as independence and unanimity; Corollary 5 derives dictatorship for bivalent judgements. These results quantify over profiles and functions. They do not define a decision, optimization, or algorithmic problem whose complexity the paper establishes.

A plausible population model would group many assessors by their judgement profiles and authority, then aggregate with a functional such as \(a_j=\int x_j(t)\,d\nu(t)\). That could be a sensible continuous model. But asking how to compute its output does not mirror Theorems 4 or 6: it supplies an algorithmic task where the paper gives an axiomatic characterization. Asking how to choose the authority measure \(\nu\) would add a further problem the paper leaves contested. For Corollary 5, a type-level “dictator” would also change the object being characterized: the result concerns an individual’s authority, while a positive-mass type represents a group.

The deliberation model offers the best route to a computational question: study consensus or convergence for a population of assessors updating their beliefs. But the paper’s convergence discussion is analytic, and its main claim is a critique of the rationality of iterative pooling. To turn it into a complexity problem would require specifying a new population interaction model and computational task. That might be worthwhile research, but it would not mirror a named computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for this paper’s results, but it does not prove that every continuous population model one could build around its subject is unworthy. The paper gives a plausible setting for such models; it does not give the computational anchor the proposed mirror needs.
