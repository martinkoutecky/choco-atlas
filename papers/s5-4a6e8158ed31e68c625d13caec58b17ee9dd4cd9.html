What the mirror covers

No result qualifies for a computational mirror; Theorems 1–8 and Lemmas 1–4 concern network formation conditions, formation impossibility, or efficiency.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is the paper’s organizational-network setting. In a large organization, many members may share the same utility parameters, while the evolving network has only a few structural roles. The paper itself groups nodes by graph-automorphism type; for a star, that gives just two classes, the center and the leaves, even as the number of nodes grows. A continuous population description could assign them masses \(1/n\) and \((n-1)/n\). That makes the low-support, high-multiplicity regime plausible, though the roles are endogenous rather than fixed agent types.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved here: if \(b_1-b_2+\gamma b_2\leq c<b_1\) and \(c_0<(1-\gamma)(b_2-b_3)\), the recursive formation process results in a star. This gives a concrete structural result one might try to extend to a mass-based network model.

But it is not a qualifying anchor under the requested standard. Theorem 1 gives sufficient parameter conditions; it does not classify the complexity of a problem or present an algorithm or computational formulation. The other numbered results are also structural conditions, impossibility statements about \(k\)-star formation, and efficiency claims. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, so I can’t honestly state a continuous problem *per qualifying anchor*.

The weakest point is deeper than that missing anchor: turning graph-automorphism classes into population types does not by itself preserve the problem. In a star, the center’s mass vanishes as \(n\) grows, while pairwise stability depends on individual link changes and the paper’s model is built around discrete graphs. Defining a continuum link model that preserves those incentives would be substantial new work. So there is a plausible high-multiplicity setting to explore, but this paper does not supply the computational result needed for a strong continuous-complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not supply the kind of result ChoCo is meant to mirror. Theorem 1 gives sufficient inequalities on \(b_1,b_2,b_3,c,c_0,\gamma\) for a star to form. It states no computational problem, gives no algorithm, and makes no complexity claim. The other numbered results are of the same structural kind: conditions for topologies, formation impossibility, or efficiency bounds. Translating one of those inequalities into a continuum model might be a new analysis, but it would not extend a computational result in this paper.

The organizational-network story is the strongest high-multiplicity case. An even better candidate than the star is Theorem 3’s bipartite Turán graph: both sides can contain a positive fraction of the population, so the case does not depend on a vanishing-mass center. Theorem 2’s complete graph also avoids that problem. Yet the paper assumes homogeneous agents. Its automorphism classes describe positions in a particular network state; they are not fixed population types. In a bipartite model, the two sides are roles produced by the network, not different agent attributes. A continuum graph or graphon could represent those roles, but then the continuous object is the network topology. The population distribution itself remains a unit mass of one type.

Preserving the paper’s incentives makes the fit harder. Its process adds one named agent at a time and tests whether particular pairs should add or delete a link. In an atomless mass model, changing one edge changes zero mass, so aggregate payoffs do not register the deviation and pairwise stability loses its content. Keeping edge-level payoffs preserves that deviation, but it also keeps the identities and degrees that the mass description was supposed to replace. A hybrid model with a finite center and a continuum of leaves could rescue the star setting; it would leave a pivotal individual atom in the model and describe a new finite-core network game.

So the negative case is strongest on programme fit: there is no named computational result here, and the paper’s most natural population is already homogeneous. A continuous network-formation project could still be worthwhile, especially with a carefully designed mean-field or graph-limit model. I would not claim that no such scenario can ever be made convincing. I would claim that it would require inventing both the computational question and the continuum model, rather than making a continuous mirror of a computational result this paper established.
