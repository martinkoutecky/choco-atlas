The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent identifies: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 gives conditions for uniqueness of coalitional equilibria; Theorem 7 establishes structural properties of the effective cartel game, including an exact potential and semi-strict equilibrium existence. Neither specifies a computational problem, an input representation, or a running-time claim. Turning “find an equilibrium” into a complexity question would require choices the paper does not make: how to encode the payoff functions and action sets, how to represent an equilibrium, and what to do when regularity fails. That could be a worthwhile new project, but it would not be a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The best proposed mirror is a continuum cartel game: represent members by a population share \(\alpha\), then compute equilibrium participation masses. A high-multiplicity regime of firms or contributors is plausible, so I would not reject this on the ground that the population cannot be aggregated. The deeper problem is that the paper’s membership equilibrium is defined by individual deviations that change the cartel size by one. In an atomless population, one person’s deviation changes \(\alpha\) by zero and leaves the second-stage cartel equilibrium unchanged. For identical agents, an interior share requires them to be indifferent between joining and staying out. Semi-strict equilibrium with both members and nonmembers is impossible: members must weakly prefer joining, while nonmembers must strictly prefer staying out, although both groups face the same participation payoff difference. A finite-mass deviation could restore changes to the cartel, but it would introduce a group-deviation stability concept in place of the paper’s Nash equilibrium. The continuum would then be answering a redefined game-theoretic question.

The paper’s own Example 1 makes the limiting issue concrete: its nontrivial semi-strict cartel sizes are two or three, so their population shares tend to zero as \(n\) grows. One could instead interpolate the exact potential in Theorem 7 and optimize over \(\alpha\). That is a sensible aggregate analysis, but the potential records the finite game’s unilateral incentives; using it to assess changes in a positive mass of members changes the deviation being evaluated. Any resulting algorithmic question would need its own model and computational target.

Heterogeneous types are the strongest escape route. The paper even points toward two-type games, and a mass-based model of firms or countries with several parameter types could be meaningful. Yet the paper’s general result for that territory is a proposed direction for future analysis, not a complexity result; Theorem 7’s symmetry and potential conclusions do not cover it. So the honest negative case is not that population continuization is implausible. It is that the most direct mirror loses the paper’s deviation structure, while the richer versions that avoid that problem require new equilibrium definitions and new computational questions. Those may merit separate study, but this paper supplies no computational anchor for them.
