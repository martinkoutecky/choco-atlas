What the mirror covers

Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollaries 1 and 2 remain axiomatic domain and possibility characterizations; the proposed electorate interpretation gives none of them computational coverage.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is that the paper’s preference profiles could arise in a large electorate with relatively few distinct rankings: a type is a ranking \(t\in L(A)\), and its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with that ranking. The paper’s restrictions on admissible preferences might then suggest questions about which population types support non-dictatorial, Pareto-efficient, strategy-proof rules.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. The nearest results, Theorems 1 and 2, are proved here and characterize maximal preference domains: Theorem 1 says that, when only agent 1’s domain is restricted, a maximal strategy-proof possibility domain has either an inseparable pair or an inseparable set; Theorem 2 gives a more intricate characterization for Maskin-monotonic possibility domains. Corollaries 1 and 2 give admissibility conditions for the existence of such rules. None states a complexity result, algorithm, or running time. Thus there are zero qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor prover question or tractability prediction to give.

The obstacle is more than missing complexity notation. The paper studies restrictions on the *domain of possible reports*, and its strategy-proofness condition tests an individual agent’s deviation. In a model that sees only the population distribution \(\mu\), one person’s report change has zero mass and leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, making individual strategy-proofness vacuous. Keeping the distinguished agent pivotal requires retaining that agent’s identity; turning the agent into a positive-mass group changes the theorem’s assumptions. A continuous version of the maximal-domain characterization would also continuize an axiomatic question, which the programme puts out of scope.

So the large-electorate setting is a plausible source of a new research extension, but I don’t think this paper supplies a defensible continuous-computational mirror under the stated criteria. Its weakest point is decisive: the natural population representation loses the individual deviations on which its results depend.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no anchor to defend: there is no named computational result in this paper. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize maximal domains on which certain rules exist; Corollaries 1 and 2 give conditions for possibility. These are axiomatic results, and turning them into a population-distribution question would continuize the object the programme explicitly leaves out of scope: the domain of admissible preferences. A separate computational problem about recognizing those domains might be worth asking, but it would have no population variable to continuize.

The proposed electorate interpretation also loses information the paper’s questions require. A preference profile assigns a ranking to each named agent, and strategy-proofness asks what happens when one particular agent changes theirs. The rule need not treat agents with the same ranking alike; the paper’s own hierarchical construction distinguishes agents 1 and 2. A distribution over rankings forgets those identities. Keep identity in the type and the supposed compression largely disappears; discard it and the rule may no longer be well-defined from the society.

That loss is especially sharp in a continuum. Changing one agent’s report changes no mass, so the paper’s individual-deviation condition becomes untestable from the distribution. Giving a group positive mass and letting it change preferences would replace individual strategy-proofness with a mass-coalition condition. That may define a new problem, but it is not a mirror of Theorems 1 or 2. Likewise, domain maximality asks whether adding an admissible preference changes what rules exist; a measure over rankings makes zero-mass preferences invisible even though domain membership can depend on them.

So the strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper studies axiomatic restrictions on named agents’ possible reports, not computation over a population. Neither its main theorems nor its corollaries provide a computational result to translate, and the natural population model erases the deviations and identities that make those results meaningful. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous social-choice project could grow out of these ideas. But that would be a new project, not this paper’s continuous computational mirror.
