What the mirror covers

Propositions 1 and 2 provide the paper’s relevant mechanism-existence results; neither is a computational result under the stated criterion. The proposed mechanism-optimization question is not anchored in a computational result of the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive signal is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It gives a credible high-multiplicity regime: the state, action, and signal sets stay finite as the number \(n\) of experts grows. Signals are conditionally independent draws from a fixed state-dependent distribution; experts’ payoff characteristics are independent of the state and signals. Under bounded utilities with a strict best action, a nondegeneracy condition on experts’ normalized payoff vectors, and a choice rule continuous at pure-state beliefs, then for every \(\varepsilon>0\), all sufficiently large \(n\) admit an incentive-compatible mechanism whose expected \(L^1\) distance from the planner’s target rule is less than \(\varepsilon\), uniformly over payoff-characteristic profiles. The paper proves this result.

That is good evidence that a population-mass description is sensible for this problem: in a regime with many experts but a fixed, finite set of signal and preference types, \(n\) can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of types. A type would be the complete expert type \((s,q)\); its mass would be the fraction of experts with signal \(s\) and characteristic \(q\). The paper’s result does not collide with the computational gap: it establishes existence of mechanisms, not their computational complexity.

But Proposition 2 is not a qualifying computational anchor under the requested standard. It proves asymptotic approximate implementability, not that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Proposition 1 has the same limitation in the finite accurate-signal jury setting. I therefore cannot honestly provide a computational anchor or a class prediction for either result.

A possible follow-on question would be: given finite rational state, action, signal, and preference-type data, a rational population measure over \((s,q)\), and a target rule \(\pi\), find a mechanism minimizing expected distance from the target subject to truthful reporting. This is recognizable as an approximate-implementation question, and the authors would likely recognize its subject. Yet in a genuinely atomless population, one expert’s report does not change the reported mass measure. If the mechanism depends only on that measure, individual incentive constraints become vacuous; auditing a uniformly sampled individual does not restore incentives for any fixed individual, who has zero selection probability. A meaningful continuous version needs an additional incentive notion, such as scaled marginal incentives or coordinated mass deviations. Either choice changes the problem and needs justification.

So the positive case is that this paper itself makes the large-population regime unusually plausible. Its weakest point is also decisive for the requested deliverable: the paper contains no named computational result to anchor, and its individual-incentive mechanism does not pass directly to an ordinary atomless model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that the paper proves no computational result. Propositions 1 and 2 establish existence of incentive-compatible mechanisms with small distortion; they do not classify an algorithmic problem or analyze its running time. Proposition 2 is unusually good evidence that a large-population regime makes sense: the signal space stays fixed as the number of experts grows. But that supports the *modeling precondition*, not a computational mirror. Proposition 1 has the same gap.

The best follow-on would be to optimize over mechanisms: for a target rule and rational type data, minimize expected distortion subject to truthful reporting, or find the least scrutiny probability that sustains incentives. That is a recognizable computational question, but it is not a computational result from this paper, and its natural finite version has a serious continuization problem. In the paper, an expert’s report matters because an individual can be pivotal and can be selected for scrutiny with probability proportional to \(1/n\). In an atomless population described only by type masses, one expert’s report changes no mass. A lottery over experts gives each fixed individual probability zero of scrutiny. The two incentive effects that the paper balances therefore vanish in the continuum.

One could try to repair this by scaling incentive constraints with \(n\), retaining first-order changes in mass, or requiring truthful behavior against coordinated deviations by positive-mass groups. Those are possible research questions, but none is selected by the paper’s result: each adds a new incentive notion or limiting convention. The paper’s mechanism and theorem do not identify which one its authors would regard as the right continuous version. Encoding the full finite report profile as an aggregate distribution would preserve more information, but then the individual deviation and scrutiny probability that drive the theorem still depend on the finite population behind that distribution.

So the strongest case against is narrow but firm: there is no named computational result here to mirror, and the most natural attempt to create one loses the theorem’s individual incentives at the atomless limit. The negative case is weaker on the broader claim that *no* worthwhile mirror could be designed. Proposition 2 makes the high-multiplicity setting plausible, and a carefully specified computational mechanism-design problem might be worth studying. It would be a new problem motivated by this paper, though, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its results.
