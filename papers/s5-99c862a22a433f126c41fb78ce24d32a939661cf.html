The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) institutions, \(K\) applicant profiles, piecewise-constant rational queue densities \(f_k(q)\) on \(q\in[0,1]\), and per-capita institution capacities \(\kappa_d\), compute the continuous Rank-Maximal allocation. Each type \((k,q)\) receives at most one acceptable institution; first maximize matched mass, then lexicographically maximize assigned masses in the baseline institution order, and then assign the highest-priority available profile and queue mass at each institution while preserving completion. Return the exact allocation as finitely many queue intervals with rational endpoints.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of types \((k,q)\), with mass density \(f_k(q)\), where \(k\) records acceptability and institutional profile priorities and \(q\) is a shared queue rank. Decision variables are measurable assignment indicators \(x_{kd}(q)\in\{0,1\}\); institution \(d\) receives mass at most \(\kappa_d\). The objective is the continuous Rank-Maximal ordering: maximize total assigned mass, then apply the baseline institution order and institutional priorities subject to completing the allocation.

What the mirror covers

Covers the outcome-computation claim in Theorem 5 for the queue-density regime; it does not extend the paper’s strategyproofness, non-bossiness, or other mechanism-property results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can every outcome for piecewise-constant rational queue densities be represented by finitely many rational interval cuts?
  • Can completion checks and exact allocation computation run in time polynomial in \(m\), \(K\), the number of density intervals, and their bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: “The outcome of the SAFE/Rank-Maximal Mechanisms can be computed in polynomial time.” For any reported acceptability profile, strict institutional priorities, and fixed baseline order of institutions, the theorem says the mechanism’s matching can be computed in polynomial time. The proof constructs the Rank-Maximal outcome using repeated maximum-matching computations. Proposition 1, also proved here, shows that SAFE and Rank-Maximal give the same outcome for each baseline order. The paper has no comparable hardness result of its own to anchor instead.

My lead mirror is Mass Rank-Maximal Matching. Consider a large healthcare or school-placement system with \(m\) institutions and a finite number \(K\) of recurring applicant profiles. A profile \(k\) specifies which institutions an applicant finds acceptable and how institutions rank that profile relative to other profiles. To retain strict individual priorities, give each applicant a distinct queue position \(q\in[0,1]\), used as a common tie-break within each profile. A full type is \((k,q)\), with population described by a rational, piecewise-constant density \(f_k(q)\). The regime is millions of applicants, relatively few profiles, and a densely populated queue. Institution capacities are given as fractions of the population.

An allocation is a measurable assignment \(x_{kd}(q)\in\{0,1\}\), where an applicant at \((k,q)\) is either unmatched or assigned to an acceptable institution. No applicant is assigned twice, and institution \(d\) receives mass at most \(\kappa_d\). The problem is to compute the continuous Rank-Maximal outcome: first maximize total matched mass; among those allocations, lexicographically maximize the masses assigned to institutions in the baseline order; then, while preserving those quantities and the possibility of completing the assignment, give each institution the highest-priority available applicants according to its strict ordering of profiles and the queue position \(q\). A solution is the resulting exact allocation, represented by a finite partition into assigned and unassigned queue intervals with rational endpoints.

I would expect this restricted, succinctly encoded version to be tractable, likely through capacitated flow or a continuous LP with repeated completion checks. The prover’s task would be to establish that the priority-based solution has a finite exact representation and can be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(K\), the number of density intervals, and their bit length. It mirrors the paper’s computed object—the priority-respecting, maximum matching selected by Rank-Maximal—not merely the generic task of finding a large matching. It covers Theorem 5’s outcome-computation result; it does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s strategyproofness or non-bossiness theorems.

The scenario is plausible for centralized rationing: many applicants can share eligibility and priority profiles while a common queue number gives a strict tie-break. The main weakness is representational. Since \(q\) distinguishes applicants, the full type space is continuous rather than a finite support of repeated complete types; in a finite election-like instance, each applicant’s exact priority rank would make them a distinct type. The mirror therefore needs a succinct density input, and it co-modifies capacities to scale with population. Those are real departures from the programme’s finite-support model. I still think authors could recognize it as their maximum, fair matching problem in a continuum regime: priority remains strict, and the object being computed remains the mechanism’s assignment. The result would be evidence for a tractable mirror, but not evidence that finite-support high multiplicity alone captures their setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against turns on Theorem 5, the paper’s clearest computational result: computing the SAFE/Rank-Maximal outcome takes polynomial time. Its input is an explicit finite population, with each institution giving a strict ranking of named applicants. Since each strict ranking places every applicant at a distinct position, two applicants cannot have the same complete type if type includes their institutional priorities. In the paper’s model, then, the strict priorities that shape the computed outcome leave no repeated complete types to aggregate.

The proposed queue coordinate \(q\) preserves those identities by assigning each applicant a distinct rank. But that makes the type space a continuum of singleton identities, rather than a high-multiplicity population of repeated types. And a common \(q\) restricts the paper’s arbitrary institutional rankings: within each applicant profile, all institutions must share the same order. Giving each institution its own rank coordinate would preserve more of the model, but the joint distribution of those coordinates would itself encode the individualized priority data the aggregation was meant to remove.

Scaling capacities with population is a reasonable co-modification, so that alone is no objection. The harder choice is between retaining strict individual priorities, which defeats finite-support aggregation, and collapsing applicants into priority classes, which changes strict priorities into ties and yields only a type-level allocation. The latter may be a sensible model, but its output is not the exact individual matching computed in Theorem 5. It is a related mass-allocation problem.

That makes the proposed mirror vulnerable, but it does not establish the universal negative. A finite-class model with strict priorities between classes, repeated applicants within each class, and capacities that scale with population is plausible. Authors might recognize its rank-maximal mass allocation as their problem. I cannot honestly rule that out; the negative case is strongest against this paper’s strict-priority result and weakest against that natural class-level variant.
