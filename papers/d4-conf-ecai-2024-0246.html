The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named computational result in this paper, so I cannot give a positive case that meets your anchor requirement. The numbered results characterize incentive and fairness properties. For example, Proposition 3, proved here, says an OWA is strategy-proof exactly when some weight is \(1\); Theorem 6, also proved here, characterizes when an OWA satisfies NOM; and Theorem 11, proved here, says an OWA satisfies proportional fairness exactly when every weight is \(1/n\). None asserts a complexity class or gives an algorithmic result about a computational problem.

A plausible population model would describe ideal locations by a distribution \(\mu\) over \([0,1]\) and define a continuum OWA as \(y(\mu)=\int_0^1 a(p)Q_\mu(p)\,dp\), where \(Q_\mu\) is the quantile function and \(a\) is a weight density. One could ask which \(a\) satisfy proportional fairness for every \(\mu\) and every measurable coalition. That is a possible research question, but it is an axiomatic classification rather than a computational mirror of a named result in this paper. Individual manipulation also becomes vacuous for zero-mass agents; making deviations affect the outcome would require a coalition-mass formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Proposition 3, Theorem 6, and Theorem 11 characterize which OWA weights satisfy incentive or fairness axioms. The paper gives no algorithm, complexity bound, reduction, or computational problem whose continuous version could be charted. That is the decisive gap under the programme’s remit.

Proposition 3 has a natural-looking lift: represent locations by a distribution \(\mu\) and an OWA by a weighting function over quantiles, then characterize which functions yield strategy-proof rules. But with an atomless population, one person’s report changes \(\mu\) by zero, making literal individual strategy-proofness vacuous. A stronger repair would let a positive mass of agents change reports; that asks about coalition manipulation, rather than the individual dominant-strategy property characterized in the proposition. Either way, the result would be an axiomatic classification, not a computational mirror.

Theorem 6 has the same obstacle. Its NOM condition compares an individual’s best- and worst-case utilities across reports. In an atomless society, a single report has no effect; allowing a block to report differently changes the incentive question. A continuum classification of the resulting property could be mathematically worthwhile, but the theorem itself poses no algorithmic task.

Theorem 11 is the proponent’s strongest candidate. A distribution of residents’ ideal locations is plausible, and a quantile-weighted facility rule could support a meaningful proportional-fairness condition for measurable coalitions. That makes a continuous axiomatic question quite natural. It still does not make it computational: the proposed question classifies weight functions that satisfy a property over societies and coalitions. Turning it into finite-input verification or weight optimization would require a new encoding and a new computational problem absent from the paper.

The multiplicity objection would be weak here. A city’s residents can sensibly be modeled by masses of people with similar locations, and the paper does not already study that continuous population model. Those points support the plausibility of a continuous mechanism-design project; they do not supply the missing computational result. One could attach a computational problem to facility location, but that would be a separate project rather than a mirror of this paper’s contributions.

The universal claim has a limit: this cannot rule out every useful computational question one might build around continuous facility location. The negative case is strong within ChoCo’s stated scope because none of the paper’s results gives such a question to continuize.
