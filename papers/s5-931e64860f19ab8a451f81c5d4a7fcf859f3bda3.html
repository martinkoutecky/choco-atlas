The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a scoring vector \(\alpha\), target candidate \(p\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finite ranking-price types \(t=(\sigma_t,\pi_t)\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), decide whether deletion masses \(z_t\) exist with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\pi_tz_t\le B\), and \(\sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\alpha_{\mathrm{pos}_{\sigma_t}(p)}\ge\sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\alpha_{\mathrm{pos}_{\sigma_t}(c)}\) for every \(c\in C\); equivalently, minimize \(\sum_t\pi_tz_t\) subject to these winner constraints.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity scoring election with ranking-price types \(t\), population masses \(\mu_t\), deletion variables \(z_t\), linear per-capita deletion costs \(\pi_t\), and a linear scoring-winner objective or budget constraint.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 6's priced \(\mathcal R\)-CCAV, \(\mathcal R\)-CCDV, and bribery results, including scoring rules, and overlaps with the Approval control results. It does not by itself cover the generic mixed-integer technique, the multicover approximation results, or Shift Bribery.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can continuous solutions be rounded to finite elections with useful additive or multiplicative guarantees?
  • Can the ranking-type space be represented implicitly and solved through a separation or pricing oracle?
  • Which discrete hardness results return when cohort actions must be integral?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a faithful Class-A mirror of the paper’s priced election-control result, supported by a hardness anchor showing exactly what population continuization can remove.

The lead anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It states that, for every voting rule \(\mathcal R\) whose winner condition can be expressed by integer linear inequalities in the numbers of voters of each preference order, \(\mathcal R\)-\(\$CCAV\), \(\mathcal R\)-\(\$CCDV\), and \(\mathcal R\)-\(\$Bribery\) are fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by the number of candidates. The theorem explicitly applies to all scoring rules. Here \(\$CCDV\) means deleting priced voters, \(\$CCAV\) means adding priced voters, and \(\$Bribery\) means changing priced voters’ preferences.

A natural continuous analogue is Continuous Scoring-\(\$CCDV\). An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a target candidate \(p\), a scoring vector \(\alpha\), and a finite population of ranking-price types \(t=(\sigma_t,\pi_t)\). Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and deleting one unit of its mass costs \(\pi_t\). Given a budget \(B\), choose deletion masses \(z_t\) satisfying \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\) and

\[ \sum_t \pi_t z_t\le B. \]

After deletion, candidate \(c\) receives score

\[ \operatorname{sc}(c)= \sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{\sigma_t}(c)}. \]

The question is whether there is such a vector \(z\) for which \(p\) is a scoring-rule co-winner; equivalently, \(\operatorname{sc}(p)\ge\operatorname{sc}(c)\) for every \(c\in C\). The optimization version minimizes the deletion cost.

This is precisely the paper’s control problem with voter counts replaced by population masses. A type includes both the ranking and its price, so voters with different prices are not incorrectly identified. The regime is a large election of shareholders, employees, or citizens in which \(N\gg\tau\): many voters belong to a small number of recurring ranking-and-price cohorts. Persuading \(37\%\) of one cohort not to participate is more natural there than specifying a particular voter.

The continuous problem is an LP. Its variables are the \(z_t\), its budget constraint is linear, and the scoring inequalities are linear. Thus the expected result is stronger than the paper’s discrete parameterized result: exact optimization is polynomial in the explicit type description and input bit length, rather than merely FPT in the number of candidates. Rational masses may encode an exponentially large finite electorate, so this is not just expansion into individual voters. The paper’s use of variables counting voters of each ranking makes the authors especially likely to recognize this as their high-multiplicity version.

The supporting hardness anchor is Proposition 1, proved here. It states that Weighted Multiset Multicover is NP-complete even when the universe has one element. An instance consists of multisets \(S_i\), integer weights \(w_i\), covering requirements, and a budget; one asks whether a subfamily covers every requirement within budget. The proof reduces Partition: for numbers \(k_1,\ldots,k_n\), use one element \(x\), let \(S_i\) contain \(k_i\) copies of \(x\), give it weight \(k_i\), and set both the covering requirement and budget to \(\frac12\sum_i k_i\). A feasible subfamily exists exactly when the numbers have a perfect partition.

Its continuous mirror is Continuous Weighted Multicover, with finitely many package types \(t\). Type \(t\) has available mass \(\mu_t\), coverage vector \(a_t\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{U}\), and unit cost \(w_t\). Choose mass \(y_t\) satisfying

\[ 0\le y_t\le\mu_t, \]

\[ \sum_t a_t(u)y_t\ge r_u \quad\text{for every }u\in U, \]

and minimize

\[ \sum_t w_t y_t. \]

The decision version asks whether the minimum is at most \(B\). Each infinitesimal agent still carries an integral package \(a_t\); only the amount of an identical cohort selected is divisible. This is therefore population continuization, not fractionalization of the outcome space.

The continuous problem is again an LP, including the one-element case. The Partition obstruction disappears because one may select a fraction of a package type. This is an honest Class-A outcome: Proposition 1’s discrete hardness is driven by all-or-nothing population units, while the continuous mirror exposes the aggregate covering structure. Through the paper’s Construction 1, the Boolean-vector subcase is also a continuous Approval-\(\$CCDV\) problem: candidates are universe elements, voter types are approval sets with prices, and deleting voter mass covers the approval deficits of competing candidates.

The authors would likely accept both mirrors as their questions. The first follows their central voting formulation directly: \(x_\sigma\) counts become \(\mu_\sigma\), and the deletion variables \(c_\sigma\) become deletion masses. The second follows their explicit reduction between priced voter control and multicover. Neither changes the winner space, introduces lotteries over outcomes, or continuizes time or axioms.

Further questions naturally arise:

  • whether continuous-to-discrete rounding gives additive or multiplicative guarantees for finite electorates;
  • whether the LP remains tractable when ranking types are implicit and pricing requires a separation oracle;
  • whether robustness, bribery, and swap actions admit analogous mass-transport formulations;
  • exactly which hardness returns when an entire type cohort must be acted on integrally rather than fractionally.

The weakest point is the multicover anchor: Proposition 1’s own Partition construction uses essentially one distinct package type per number, so it is not itself a hard instance with many agents and few types. I would not claim that NP-hardness transfers to the continuum. The positive claim is narrower and more defensible: the paper’s problem has a natural high-multiplicity regime, and in that regime its discrete population-indivisibility hardness becomes a precise LP problem. Theorem 6 supplies the stronger social-choice anchor; Proposition 1 explains why that change is computationally meaningful.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is weak. Proposition 1 can be rejected as an independent anchor, but Theorem 6 survives the strongest objections.

The hardness reduction for Proposition 1 does not exhibit high multiplicity. Given numbers \(k_1,\ldots,k_n\), it creates one multiset of coverage \(k_i\) and price \(k_i\) for each number. These are generally all distinct complete types. The reduction’s hardness comes from choosing each individually and integrally, not from a large population of repeated agents. Its continuous version, with package masses \(y_t\), is simply a fractional covering or knapsack LP: \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\), coverage constraints are linear, and cost is \(\sum_t w_ty_t\). That is a sensible relaxation, but it is not a continuous form of the Partition hardness in any informative sense.

There is also a modelling problem. In WMM, the multisets are selectable packages, not agents whose social preferences are being aggregated. Their multiplicities are resources supplied by an intervention. Thus the abstract WMM mirror continuizes the selected objects rather than the population. Recasting it through Construction 1 produces a genuine population model, but then the relevant anchor is Approval-\(\$CCDV\), not Proposition 1 itself. This makes Proposition 1 redundant rather than independently persuasive.

The same objection cannot defeat Theorem 6. One might say that arbitrary prices individualize voters: if every voter has a different price, there are no repeated ranking-price types. But that only describes some instances. A perfectly coherent high-multiplicity regime has many shareholders, members, employees, or citizens sharing both a ranking and a control price. The programme’s definition of type explicitly requires prices and costs to be included in the type, so grouping such voters is legitimate rather than an information-losing approximation.

The continuous mirror is also not degenerate. For deletion, with type \(t=(\sigma_t,\pi_t)\), mass \(\mu_t\), and deletion mass \(z_t\), the constraints \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\pi_tz_t\le B\), and \(\sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{\sigma_t}(c)}\ge \sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{\sigma_t}(p)}\) form a genuine population-level control problem. Scores and costs remain finite after the usual per-capita normalization. Deleting \(37\%\) of a homogeneous cohort is a meaningful bulk abstention action, not merely a fractional outcome.

The better mirror is even stronger for bribery: use mass-transfer variables \(x_{\sigma,\sigma'}\) between ranking-price types, with linear winner constraints and linear transfer costs. This is directly the continuous-population formulation central to the programme. The fact that the explicit version is an LP is not an objection; identifying such a tractable Class-A problem is precisely one of the programme’s goals.

Nor can prior high-multiplicity work be used against it. That literature would support, rather than collide with, the claim that priced control has a sensible repeated-type regime.

So the strongest negative conclusion is limited: Proposition 1 is a poor standalone population anchor, and its original Partition construction should not be presented as population-multiplicity hardness. But there is no honest way to defeat the Theorem 6 mirror in every scenario. The universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror is therefore not supportable.
