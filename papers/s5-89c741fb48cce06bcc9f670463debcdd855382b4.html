The continuous mirror question

Given candidate facilities \(F\) with costs \(c_i\), supported service types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and each type’s complete profile \((r_t,(a_{it})_{i\in F})\), maximize total revenue over KC-dual-induced per-person charges. For \(S\subseteq F\), define \(r_t^S=\max\{r_t-\sum_{i\in S}a_{it},0\}\), and set \(a_{it}^S=0\) for \(i\in S\), otherwise \(a_{it}^S=\min\{a_{it},r_t^S\}\). Choose \(q_t^S\ge 0\) to maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{S\subseteq F}r_t^S q_t^S\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{i\}}a_{it}^S q_t^S\le c_i\) for every \(i\in F\). The output charge is \(p_t=\sum_{S\subseteq F}r_t^S q_t^S\); ask whether these charges satisfy the core condition for every coalition containing any mass \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for a KC-LP dual-feasible solution \(y=(y_j^S)\), the charges
\[ \xi_j=\sum_{S\subseteq F} r_j^S y_j^S \]
satisfy the core: for every user group \(J\subseteq U\), \(\sum_{j\in J}\xi_j\le c_J^*\), where \(c_J^*\) is the minimum cost of an integer facility set that serves \(J\). This is a named, computationally useful result about constructing cost shares. It is not a numbered P-versus-NP result; the paper’s general CIP hardness premise is not presented as a numbered hardness theorem.

The continuous mirror I believe in is Mass KC-Core Recovery. There are candidate facilities \(F\), each with a cost \(c_i\), and a finite set of supported user types \(T\). A type \(t\) is its complete service profile: a requirement \(r_t>0\) and a contribution \(a_{it}\ge0\) from every facility \(i\in F\). The population has mass \(\lambda_t=N\mu_t\) of type \(t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A facility plan remains integral: \(X\subseteq F\) serves type \(t\) when \(\sum_{i\in X}a_{it}\ge r_t\). Each unit of type \(t\) is charged a per-person amount \(p_t\).

Given a feasible plan for the whole population, the problem is to find nonnegative type charges that maximize total revenue among KC-dual-induced core allocations. A coalition may contain any mass \(z_t\in[0,\lambda_t]\) of each type. Its deviation cost is the cheapest facility set serving every type with \(z_t>0\); the charges must satisfy
\[ \sum_t z_t p_t\le \min_{\substack{X\subseteq F:\\ \sum_{i\in X}a_{it}\ge r_t\ \forall t:z_t>0}} \sum_{i\in X}c_i \quad\text{for every }z\in\prod_t[0,\lambda_t]. \]
The objective is to maximize \(\sum_t\lambda_t p_t\).

The KC-dual version of that question is explicit. For \(S\subseteq F\), define \(r_t^S=\max\{r_t-\sum_{i\in S}a_{it},0\}\), and let \(a_{it}^S=0\) for \(i\in S\), or \(\min\{a_{it},r_t^S\}\) otherwise. Choose \(q_t^S\ge0\) to maximize
\[ \sum_t\lambda_t\sum_{S\subseteq F}r_t^S q_t^S \]
subject to
\[ \sum_t\lambda_t\sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{i\}}a_{it}^S q_t^S\le c_i \quad\forall i\in F. \]
The solution returns per-person charges \(p_t=\sum_S r_t^S q_t^S\). Every feasible \(q\) gives a core allocation: for any coalition, facility dual feasibility bounds its charges, and the clipped KC constraints ensure that any facility plan serving its types covers each residual requirement. This is the direct mass analogue of Theorem 1’s construction.

The regime I would defend is a large IoT rollout with many sensors installed at repeated standardized sites or building templates. Sensors in the same template share their requirement and their full vector of contributions to the candidate gateways. The number of sensors \(N\) could be in the millions while the number of distinct profiles \(\tau=|T|\) is in the tens or hundreds. Those identical profile assumptions matter: if propagation estimates make nearly every sensor’s full vector unique, this is not a convincing high-multiplicity instance. In the proposed regime, mass means the number or fraction of interchangeable devices, and a coalition may include a fractional cohort. The authors should recognize the mirror: it preserves the covering requirements, the integer gateway decisions, and the coalition stability condition. It changes the representation of repeated users, not the service question.

I expect the approximate version to be Class A. The type-level dual has \(n\) facility constraints and exponentially many \(q_t^S\) variables, so an exact algorithm still needs pricing. The paper describes exact column generation through pseudopolynomially solvable knapsack problems and near-optimal feasible dual methods. Thus the approximation story transfers naturally, with running time expressed in the number of facilities \(n\), support size \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and approximation parameter \(\varepsilon\). I would not claim from Theorem 1 alone that exact maximum-revenue recovery is polynomial-time. The useful next questions are the bit complexity of exact pricing, whether arbitrary core allocations can outperform the KC-dual-induced optimum, and how the approximation guarantee compares as \(\tau\) grows.

The paper’s high-multiplicity setting supports this mirror rather than colliding with it. My weakest point is that exact equality of complete coverage profiles can be demanding in a geographically detailed network, so the regime depends on genuinely repeated or standardized site classes. A second limitation is that the anchor establishes core feasibility, not a numbered complexity classification. This is a focused algorithmic mirror for cost recovery under the core; it does not turn the paper’s integral gateway-opening problem into a continuous one or claim that its hardness disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor, but its core constraints collapse when users are grouped into identical types. Let \(\lambda_t\) be the mass of type \(t\), and let \(p_t\) be its per-person charge. A coalition with positive mass of type \(t\) must get that type’s full coverage, so its cost depends on which types are present, not how much of each type joins. For any support \(K\subseteq T\), the most demanding coalition includes all of each type in \(K\). Thus the continuum of coalition constraints is equivalent to

\[ \sum_{t\in K}\lambda_t p_t\le c^*(K) \qquad\text{for every }K\subseteq T. \]

Set \(q_t=\lambda_t p_t\), the total charge collected from type \(t\). Then the constraints become \(\sum_{t\in K}q_t\le c^*(K)\); the masses disappear. The same cancellation occurs in the proposed KC-dual: setting \(w_t^S=\lambda_t q_t^S\) removes \(\lambda_t\) from both its objective and facility constraints. The continuous choice of coalition mass adds no optimization problem. It only determines how an already-feasible type total is divided into per-person charges.

A stronger mirror could maximize revenue over *all* core allocations, rather than only KC-dual-induced ones. But with \(q_t\) as the variables, that problem still depends on the finite type support, not its mass distribution. Its separation problem can be written as

\[ \max_{X\subseteq F} \left(\sum_{t:\,X\text{ covers }t}q_t-c(X)\right), \]

a prize-collecting covering problem on the supported types. That may be computationally interesting, but the continuum has not created it: the same problem arises for one finite representative of each type. The paper’s KC construction likewise remains the same dual calculation on those representatives.

The obstacle is specific to the service model. Each facility has a fixed opening cost, and a type’s coverage requirement applies to every user of that type. Any positive mass therefore triggers the same full coverage constraint. To make mass materially affect the optimization, one could instead scale demand with mass or allow serving only a fraction of a type. Those may be worthwhile models, but they change the paper’s per-user coverage and coalition-cost structure; they are not merely its population-continuous version.

So I would reject this particular anchor as a ChoCo continuization: Theorem 1’s type-mass extension is algebraically immediate, and the population distribution drops out of the recovery problem. The negative case is not airtight, though. Repeated standardized deployment profiles are plausible, and the exact core-revenue problem could still be valuable as a high-multiplicity cost-sharing study. I can argue that this paper’s central cost-recovery result has no meaningful continuous population axis; I cannot honestly establish that no related scenario is worth studying.
