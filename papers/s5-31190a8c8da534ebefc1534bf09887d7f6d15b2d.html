The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed unit-supply item environment \(S\) and a finite set of buyer types \(k\), each specifying a complete valuation function \(v_k\) and per-capita budget \(b_k\), let the population type distribution be \(\mu\), with \(\sum_k \mu_k b_k=1\). For \(N\) buyers sampled independently from \(\mu\), each with budget \(b_k/N\), and the observed quasilinear Fisher equilibrium of that market, does its revenue estimate the long-run revenue \(R(\mu)=\int_\Theta \max_k\{\beta_k v_k(\theta)\}\,S(d\theta)\), where \(\beta\) minimizes \(\int_\Theta \max_k\{\beta_k v_k(\theta)\}\,S(d\theta)-\sum_k\mu_k b_k\log\beta_k\) over \(0<\beta_k\leq1\)? Establish consistency and finite-sample error guarantees as \(N\) grows.

The model it lives in

A quasilinear Fisher market with a finite support of complete buyer types, masses \(\mu_k\), per-capita budgets \(b_k\), and fixed item supply \(S\). Type-level pacing multipliers \(\beta_k\in(0,1]\) minimize the weighted dual objective; revenue is the integral of the resulting price function. The empirical estimator uses the equilibrium revenue from \(N\) sampled buyers with budgets scaled by \(1/N\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s quasilinear revenue-inference result. It leaves the linear-market welfare and utility results, the pacing-multiplier and utility asymptotics, and the dual-objective differentiability results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do the consistency and error bounds depend on the number of supported types and on rare types?
  • How do boundary pacing multipliers at \(\beta_k=1\) affect confidence intervals?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the required kind. Its numbered theorems give statistical guarantees, rather than classifying a problem as polynomial-time, hard, or fixed-parameter tractable. So there is no fully compliant complexity anchor for a positive case.

The closest named result is Theorem 9 (Revenue Convergence), proved here. In the paper’s quasilinear Fisher market, with its stated bounded-value and budget assumptions, the revenue from the observed market of \(t\) sampled items, \(\REV^\gamma=\frac1t\sum_{\tau=1}^t p^{\gamma,\tau}\), converges almost surely to the long-run revenue \(\REV^*=\int_\Theta p^*\,dS\). It also gives the rate
\[ |\REV^\gamma-\REV^*| =\tilde O_p\!\left( \frac{\bar v\sqrt n\,(\bar v+2\bar\nu n+1)} {\underline b\sqrt t} \right), \]
where \(n\) is the number of buyers, \(\bar\nu=\max_i\int v_i\,dS\), and \(\underline b=\min_i b_i\). The theorem is about sampling items while keeping buyers finite; it does not establish the result for a continuous buyer population.

The strongest plausible mirror is Continuous-Buyer Quasilinear Revenue Inference. Consider a large platform with \(N\) ad campaigns, many generated from repeated templates. A campaign’s type records its budget and its complete valuation function over the platform’s inventory, so campaigns of one type are identical for the market model. Suppose there are \(\tau\) such types, with population fractions \(\mu_k\), per-campaign budgets \(b_k\), and \(\sum_k\mu_k b_k=1\). For unit item supply distributed according to \(S\), the continuous market has type-level pacing multipliers \(0<\beta_k\le1\), prices \(p(\theta)=\max_k\beta_kv_k(\theta)\), and revenue
\[ \REV^*=\int_\Theta \max_k\{\beta_kv_k(\theta)\}\,S(d\theta), \]
where the multipliers are chosen by minimizing
\[ \int_\Theta \max_k\{\beta_kv_k(\theta)\}\,S(d\theta) -\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}\mu_k b_k\log\beta_k. \]

The question for a prover is: given \(N\) sampled campaign types from an unknown population distribution, and the observed quasilinear equilibrium of the sampled market, construct an estimator of \(\REV^*\) and prove consistency and finite-sample error guarantees as \(N\) grows. Each sampled campaign has budget \(b_k/N\); repeated types can be aggregated by their empirical frequencies. A solution is an estimator computable from that empirical equilibrium, together with a proved error bound. With finite item categories, or an efficient representation for evaluating the integral, I would expect this to be Class A: the equilibrium calculation reduces to a convex program whose dimension depends on \(\tau\), rather than on \(N\). Further questions include how the rate depends on \(\tau\) and rare types, and whether quasilinear pacing multipliers at the boundary \(\beta_k=1\) complicate confidence intervals.

This is a narrow proposed extension of Theorem 9’s revenue-inference question, not a claim that Theorem 9 already proves the mirror. It covers the revenue task only; it does not mirror the paper’s utility, pacing-multiplier, or welfare results. The main weakness is that the paper deliberately samples items and keeps buyers finite. Campaign templates provide a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but the authors could reasonably regard sampling buyers as a different problem, and the theorem’s rate does not transfer automatically.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 9 is the only anchor the proponent names, and it does not establish a computational complexity result. It proves statistical convergence and an error rate for revenue when the observed equilibrium is already available. The other named results have the same character: they concern consistency, concentration, asymptotics, or confidence intervals. That leaves no result in this paper that classifies the complexity of a computational problem for a continuous population to mirror.

The proposed buyer-type model is coherent. A type can include a complete valuation function and per-capita budget, with mass \(\nu_k\); under the proposed normalization, its quasilinear dual objective has the form
\[ H_\nu(\beta)=\int_\Theta \max_k\{\beta_k v_k(\theta)\}\,S(d\theta) -\sum_k \nu_k b_k\log\beta_k,\qquad 0<\beta_k\leq 1. \]
Repeated campaign templates make a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Aggregate revenue need not vanish, and the existing high-multiplicity literature supports treating such populations as sensible. The paper’s continuous item space does not already settle this buyer-population question.

But the strongest version still asks for statistical guarantees when the buyer distribution is sampled. That is a new sample-average-inference question, not a complexity result of the kind ChoCo is charting. If the task is changed to computing equilibrium from a compactly represented buyer-type distribution, it becomes a distinct equilibrium-computation problem. Theorem 9 does not study that task: it takes observed equilibrium quantities as input, and the paper explicitly says its estimators require no convex-program solving.

So I would reject this as a continuous mirror for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme. The case against is strong on project fit and weak on the underlying model: a buyer-population inference study could be worthwhile in its own right, but this paper provides no computational-complexity anchor for it.
