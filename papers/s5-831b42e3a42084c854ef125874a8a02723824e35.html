The continuous mirror question

For a fixed relational schema and fixed data-tractable \(\Sigma\), given \(O=(D,\Sigma)\), a CQ \(Q\), \(k\), a shared probabilistic model if used, and \(\tau\) complete preference types over set-valued query answers with rational masses \(\mu_t\), merge each type's preferences as specified and form the mass-weighted CSU relation by net mass on each directed pair, omitting ties, taking transitive closure, and applying a fixed cycle-removal rule. Decide whether the resulting relation has a skyline answer or a nonempty iterated \(k\)-rank answer.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference types; alternatives remain the paper's entailed, set-valued CQ answers. Aggregate each type's preference contribution by its mass, then ask whether the resulting relation admits a skyline answer or a nonempty \(k\)-rank.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5's CQ skyline and \(k\)-rank nonemptiness questions, with Theorem 4's DAQ \(k\)-rank algorithm as a companion; it leaves the aggregation-property results and experiments outside the complexity mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s complexity result for conjunctive-query rankings. A credible setting is a large online community choosing venues from an ontology of businesses, cuisines, and food types. Its members share a query and the same background data, while many use the same preference profile: for example, the same partial ordering of cuisine or venue categories and the same policy for incorporating availability probabilities. Let a type be that complete profile, including any merge threshold or tie-handling rule the ranking uses. A type’s mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the community; the support size \(\tau\) counts distinct profiles. Millions of members can then be represented by a much smaller list of rational masses. The candidates remain the paper’s discrete ontology query answers. The society is what becomes continuous.

I would define Mass-Weighted CQ Skyline and \(k\)-Rank Nonemptiness as follows. An instance gives a Datalog+/– ontology \(O=(D,\Sigma)\), a conjunctive query \(Q\), an integer \(k>0\), a finite list of complete preference types over the query’s set-valued answer objects, and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). It may also give the paper’s shared probabilistic model \(M\), with each type’s preferences merged with \(M\) by the specified operator. Aggregate type preferences by the paper’s CSU procedure, replacing each unit contribution from a user by that type’s mass, then apply the paper’s transitive-closure and cycle-removal steps. The decision question is whether the resulting preference relation has any skyline answer, or whether its iterated skyline ranking gives a nonempty \(k\)-rank answer. A witness is an entailed query answer object \(A\), or a sequence \(\langle A_1,\ldots,A_\ell\rangle\), \(1\leq\ell\leq k\), returned by the successive-skyline procedure.

The lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper; its proof is described as a straightforward generalization of [21, Theorem 16]. The theorem says that, when the relational schema and \(\Sigma\) are fixed, and each user’s preference relation over sets of atoms has polynomial-time membership testing, deciding whether the set of skyline answers to a CQ is nonempty, or whether a \(k\)-rank answer is nonempty, is \(\Sigma_2^p\)-complete. The paper states the result for preference-merging and aggregation operators as part of the model. This is a good computational-social-choice question: a group must rank ontology-grounded alternatives under incomplete preferences and uncertain facts. For a finite group of \(n\) users, assign each profile type mass \(n_t/n\), where \(n_t\) is the number of users of that type. Weighted edge contributions are then the original CSU counts scaled by \(1/n\), preserving the comparisons and yielding the same aggregate. Thus finite instances are embedded in the continuous problem. I expect the hardness to transfer, placing this mirror in Class B.

A complementary question is Mass-Weighted DAQ \(k\)-Rank Answering. Its instance gives a data-tractable ontology, a disjunction of atomic queries, \(k\), and a finite rational-mass distribution over complete user preference types. Its solution is the first \(k\) answer atoms under the mass-weighted aggregate and iterated-skyline ranking. Here the aggregate again weights each type’s contribution by its mass, so it can be computed by summing over the \(\tau\) types rather than visiting individual users.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that the algorithm correctly computes \(k\)-rank answers for DAQs; with CSU aggregation and a cycle-removal procedure that does not unnecessarily remove edges, its running time is \(O(\operatorname{poly}(\lVert D\rVert)\cdot S+C)\); and with a polynomial-time voting aggregation operator, it is \(O(\operatorname{poly}(\lVert D\rVert)\cdot S)\). Here \(S\) is the cost of computing the relevant probabilities and \(C\) is the cost of cycle removal. For the mass version, summing rational contributions adds a polynomial dependence on \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\). With polynomial-time cycle removal, this is a plausible Class A problem in the paper’s data-tractable setting. It mirrors the group query-ranking task directly while compressing repeated preference profiles.

The scope is the paper’s computational results on ranking ontology query answers under group preferences: CQ skyline and \(k\)-rank nonemptiness, and DAQ \(k\)-rank computation. The population carries the continuum; ontology answers remain the decision objects, and probability remains part of the preference-merging model. The paper’s user-satisfaction experiments and its semantic properties of aggregation are useful context, but they are not needed as complexity anchors.

The weakest point is that the paper studies small groups, including groups of only a few users, while the convincing continuous scenario is a large community with recurring profiles. Also, Theorem 5’s hardness does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is small: the finite-instance embedding permits \(\tau\) to grow with the group. The support-sensitive question—whether the CQ problem remains hard, or becomes tractable, for bounded or moderate \(\tau\)—would need its own analysis. That gap limits the claimed high-multiplicity gain, while leaving a well-posed continuous-population ranking problem for the authors to recognize and classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about algorithmic payoff. Theorem 4 gives a data-complexity result, and Theorem 5’s \(\Sigma_2^p\)-completeness does not establish a lower bound for a bounded-support population. Neither tells us how the results behave as the number of preference types \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\) vary. A mass-weighted extension would need to specify how CSU handles fractional edge weights and ties, and how cycle removal works with them. The proponent has described a plausible extension, but has not proved those support-sensitive claims.

That leaves Theorem 5 standing as a plausible mirror. A large community can share a finite menu of preference profiles over query answers; give each profile type rational mass, and aggregate those masses with a weighted version of the paper’s operators. Finite groups embed by assigning each profile mass \(n_t/n\). The theorem’s hardness may come from CQ answers and their set-valued preferences rather than population multiplicity, but the programme explicitly treats such hardness as a valid result, not a reason to reject the question.

Theorem 4 also survives. Its DAQ ranking can be applied to the same profile distribution, with weighted voting or mass-weighted CSU. Summing over the \(\tau\) profiles is a natural way to avoid iterating over individuals, and exact rational arithmetic brings in \(L\). The paper does not supply that bound, but the missing analysis looks like a manageable formulation and algorithm question, not a fundamental obstacle.

There is a real limitation: the paper’s experiments concern small groups, while a broad online audience may have mostly distinct, high-dimensional preferences, leaving \(\tau\) close to the population size. Yet its preferences are built from a finite set of categories, which gives a credible repeated-profile scenario. I cannot honestly rule that out, or defeat either anchor. The negative case is weak: it can question how much new algorithmic leverage weighted aggregation brings, but it cannot show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
