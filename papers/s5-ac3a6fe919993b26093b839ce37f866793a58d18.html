The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t provide a qualifying anchor: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Its numbered conclusions are experimental findings. The closest foothold is equation (1) and footnote 16, which say that the revenue-maximizing bid allocation is NP-complete when the number of bidders and objects is large. That claim is asserted in the paper, not proved or attributed there, and does not meet your anchor rule.

The strongest positive lead I can offer, therefore, is provisional rather than anchored: continuize the winner-determination problem from equation (1) for an auction where each bidder submits one package bid per round. Let type \(t\) specify a bid \((p_t,S_t)\), where \(S_t\) is a subset of item categories \(K\). The instance gives each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and each category a per-capita supply \(q_k\). Choose \(x_t\), the mass of type \(t\) whose bid wins, to maximize
\[ \sum_t p_t x_t \]
subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and
\[ \sum_{t:k\in S_t}x_t\le q_k \qquad(k\in K). \]
A solution is the winning mass for each bid type. This is a linear program, polynomial in the explicit input size, so I would expect this mirror to be Class A.

A plausible regime is a large procurement of repeated capacity categories, with many buyers sharing a modest number of complete package-bid types and supplies scaling with the buyer population. This mirrors the paper’s revenue-maximizing allocation rule, rather than treating its auction as an equilibrium model. The paper’s own five-bidder experiment does not establish that regime.

The soft spot is substantial: RAD permits bidders to submit multiple bids, while this clean LP uses one bid per bidder. The full multi-bid case may require tracking feasible combinations of a bidder’s bids, bringing back a difficult pricing problem. Nor would this LP mirror the paper’s empirical claims about RAD’s efficiency, revenue, or speed. So it is a concrete possible mirror of a subproblem, but it cannot serve as the required named-result case. Further questions would include whether the full multi-bid version remains tractable under high multiplicity, and whether RAD’s price and stopping rules have continuous counterparts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a qualifying mirror is that this paper reports experimental results, not named computational results. Its numbered conclusions compare RAD with other auction designs on efficiency, profits, revenue, and duration. None asserts a theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. Equation (1) and footnote 16 identify winner determination as NP-complete, but only as a scaling concern; the paper offers no complexity analysis of its own. On the requested standard for anchors, there is no named result to mirror.

That disposes of the proponent’s one-bid LP as an anchor, but the model has a stronger version. For each complete bidder type \(t\), let \(\mu_t\) be its mass and let \(j\) index its submitted package bids, with price \(p_{tj}\) and bundle \(S_{tj}\). A continuous winner-determination problem can maximize \(\sum_{t,j}p_{tj}x_{tj}\), subject to \(x_{tj}\geq 0\), \(\sum_j x_{tj}\leq\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,j:k\in S_{tj}}x_{tj}\leq q_k\) for each item \(k\). This captures multiple bids and keeps the model polynomial in the explicit bid input. If the paper’s allocation rule permits a bidder to win compatible bids, the menu constraint can be omitted to match that rule more closely.

That is a credible high-multiplicity question about the paper’s allocation subproblem. It does not mirror the experimental finding that RAD performs better: it fixes the bids and studies one allocation step, leaving aside the iterative price updates, eligibility, stopping rule, and bidder behavior that drive those findings. Still, the paper itself flags the allocation solver as a possible scaling bottleneck, so this is not an arbitrary detour. Calling the continuous version easy would not defeat it; tractability is precisely a possible result.

So the negative case is strong on the narrow question of whether the paper supplies a named computational result, and weak against the broader claim that a worthwhile mirror exists. I cannot honestly argue that no scenario or re-modeling yields one: the continuous winner-determination problem remains a plausible mirror of the paper’s stated scaling concern.
