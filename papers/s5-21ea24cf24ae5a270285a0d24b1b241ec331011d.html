What the mirror covers

There are no named computational results to cover; the continuous aggregate-score idea would extend the paper's model rather than mirror an established result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a mirror is that Borda scores are additive over voters. In a setting with many participants and relatively few preference rankings, a type could be a complete ranking, its mass \(\mu_t\) the fraction of participants with that ranking, and aggregate scores would be \(\mathrm{sc}_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t s(t,c)\). Large membership votes or organizational polls could plausibly have many voters but few distinct rankings. The paper’s focus on prior scores and the remaining electorate also gives a reason to ask how strategic behavior changes as those quantities become population shares.

But there are no eligible anchors in this paper: it states no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, and makes no claim about complexity or algorithms. Examples 1–3 illustrate voting outcomes; Section III describes a voting and prediction model. Neither is a named computational result. So there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover, and I cannot responsibly assign a tractability or hardness expectation. A new problem about computing outcomes in a continuous version of the model would be a research extension, not a mirror of a result this paper establishes.

The serious obstacle to that extension is the sequential order. The paper’s voters react to the scores left by earlier voters, and its prediction rule uses how many voters remain. Thus a type containing only a ranking may not capture everything that determines behavior: adding turn or cohort information could make the number of types grow with the population. Also, in a nonatomic population an individual has no pivotal effect, which may alter the paper’s trigger for strategic voting. That is the weakest point in the positive case: the aggregate Borda scores make population mass look natural, but preserving the paper’s sequential decision rule in a high-multiplicity regime is not yet demonstrated.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with the missing anchor: the paper states no named computational result. It presents a sequential voting model, examples of outcomes, and a prediction rule, but no complexity claim or algorithmic theorem. So there is no result here whose computational question can be mirrored. The proponent’s Borda-score example is a possible starting point for a new question, not an anchor from this paper.

The strongest version of that new question would preserve the sequence as well as the ranking distribution—for example, a large organization voting in waves, with each voter’s type including their ranking and wave. But the paper’s central behavior depends on an individual changing the current winner by changing their ballot. In an atomless population, an individual’s effect on the score margin vanishes. If waves act collectively so their mass can change the winner, the model has become a sequence of strategic blocs rather than the paper’s individual voters. If arrival order is retained finely enough to preserve individual moves, the ranking distribution alone no longer captures the dynamics.

One could still study a continuous-time population model of sequential Borda voting. That might be worthwhile in its own right, but it would require a new formal model and a new computational question; it would not mirror a computational result established here. The opposing case is therefore strong on the absence of an eligible anchor, but weaker as a universal claim: a carefully designed cohort model could be a sensible research extension. The paper itself does not give a reason to count that extension as a ChoCo mirror.
