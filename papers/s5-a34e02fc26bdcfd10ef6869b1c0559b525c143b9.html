The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(C\), a societal axis \(L\), a target \(p\), a bound \(k\), and finite-support rational distributions \(\rho\) and \(\nu\) over single-peaked rankings, where \(\rho\) is registered mass and \(\nu\) is eligible mass, choose \(S\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\) with \(|S|\le k\). Activating \(S\) adds once the \(\nu\)-mass on rankings at Kendall swap distance at most one from some ranking in \(S\). Does there exist such an \(S\) making \(p\) a Plurality co-winner or a Condorcet winner?

The model it lives in

A finite-support mass election over single-peaked ranking types, with registered mass \(\rho\) and eligible mass \(\nu\). The decision variable is a set of at most \(k\) eligible leader types; each selected type activates the eligible mass in its full-\(1\) ranking neighborhood, counted once across overlapping bundles. The objective form is to minimize the number of selected leader types, with the winner condition given by Plurality or Condorcet.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 8 and 2. It leaves the paper's other hardness and tractability results, including the single-crossing result, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity in \(k\) when support size \(\tau\) is bounded, and do tractable regimes emerge in \((k,\tau)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 8, proved in this paper by a reduction from Partial Vertex Cover. It says that both Plurality-C-CC-AV and Condorcet-C-CC-AV are W[1]-hard parameterized by solution size \(k\), even when the election is single-peaked and the bundling function is full-1. In a full-1 bundle, a leader brings in every unregistered voter whose ranking is at Kendall swap distance at most one from the leader’s ranking.

A direct continuous mirror is Single-Peaked Mass Control with Full-1 Bundles. An instance has alternatives \(C\), a common societal axis, and finite-support distributions \(\rho\) and \(\nu\) over single-peaked rankings: \(\rho_t\) is the mass already voting with ranking \(t\), and \(\nu_t\) is eligible unregistered mass. The masses are binary rationals, with total population normalized to one. For a set \(S\) of supported unregistered rankings, let \(\Gamma_1(S)\) be the supported rankings at swap distance at most one from some ranking in \(S\). Selecting a leader type in \(S\) activates all eligible mass in \(\Gamma_1(S)\), counted once even when bundles overlap. The resulting society has mass \(\rho_t+\nu_t\) on each activated type and \(\rho_t\) elsewhere. Given \(k\), ask whether some \(|S|\le k\) makes the target \(p\) a Plurality co-winner or a Condorcet winner; the optimization version minimizes \(|S|\). Its solution is the set of leader types \(S\).

This is a population continuization: the society and the votes that campaigns add are measures over ranking types. Campaign choice remains indivisible, as in the paper. The type is just the ranking, since the full-1 influence rule is determined by ranking distance. A plausible regime is a large electorate or consultation with many people but a modest number of observed single-peaked rankings. A mobilization campaign aimed at one opinion profile reaches the nonvoting mass with immediately adjacent profiles. That is a fairly specific influence assumption, but it is exactly the paper’s full-1 model expressed in population shares.

The hardness transfers. Give each voter in a paper instance mass \(1/N\), where \(N\) is the total number of registered and unregistered voters, and aggregate voters with the same ranking. Scores and pairwise comparisons are uniformly scaled, and selecting one leader of a ranking activates the same full-1 bundle as selecting an individual leader in the paper. The budget \(k\) is unchanged. Thus the continuous problem contains the paper’s instances, preserving the W[1]-hardness. I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. The reduction does not establish hardness when the support size \(\tau\) is fixed or small; that is a separate question.

A second, broader anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here, by a parameterized reduction from Set Cover. It says that both Plurality-C-CC-AV and Condorcet-C-CC-AV parameterized by \(k\) are W[2]-hard, even with only two alternatives. Its mirror is Mass Control with Type-Specific Bundles: types include both a ranking and the complete bundle behavior relevant to the problem. The instance gives registered and eligible rational mass on each type, and a bundle map \(\Gamma(t)\) from each eligible leader type to the eligible types it activates. Choose at most \(k\) leader types, add the mass in the union of their bundles, and ask whether the target wins under Plurality or Condorcet. The optimization objective is again to minimize the number of selected leader types.

This matches the paper’s arbitrary bundling model: agents of the same type have the same preferences and bundle behavior, and bundle overlap is handled by taking a union. A conceivable regime is a large electorate in which people share a ballot ranking and a campaign-network or affiliation profile, with repeated individuals per profile. The exact Set Cover reduction has many distinct bundle signatures, however, so it supports hardness as \(\tau\) grows; it does not show hardness in a bounded-support regime. As with Theorem 8, equal masses reproduce the paper’s instances, so the W[2]-hardness transfers to this continuous problem.

Theorem 8 is the stronger mirror for me: its types need only be rankings, and its local influence rule is fixed by the paper rather than supplied as an arbitrary network. Theorem 2 shows that the mirror can also accommodate the paper’s central arbitrary-bundle setting when bundle behavior is part of the type. My scope here is those two hardness results, not the paper’s entire complexity classification or its single-crossing tractability result.

The weakest point is that both mirrors retain discrete campaign selection and all-or-nothing activation of a type bundle. Their continuity is in population mass, not in campaign intensity. That is faithful to the paper’s control action, and the authors explicitly discuss bundles of voters and weighted elections; still, a referee could reasonably ask whether the mass-based version with indivisible campaigns is the continuous question they want, or whether campaign effort itself should scale fractionally. The proposed questions make that choice explicit rather than hiding it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against starts with the mismatch between the paper’s control action and a population measure. Its decision is to select at most \(k\) individual leaders; each leader activates a specified set of individually identified voters. For Theorem 2, putting the ranking and the entire bundle signature into the type can make a mass model formally precise, but the type then carries the control network itself. The resulting problem is weighted maximum coverage with indivisible leader choices. It is a sensible problem, but its combinatorics remain in the bundle incidence structure, and the continuum supplies no obvious new optimization method. A better version would group people into recurring network roles and let a selected role activate specified masses of follower roles. That is plausible, but it is a type-level campaign model rather than a broad mirror of arbitrary individual bundles. This makes Theorem 2 a weak basis for claiming a useful continuous research direction.

That objection does not dispose of Theorem 8, the stronger anchor. Its full-1 bundles are determined by rankings: selecting a supported ranking activates eligible mass at swap distance at most one. This is a clean mass-control problem. The paper’s election can be represented by rational masses, and choosing a leader type has the same effect as choosing one leader with that ranking. Repeated copies of every voter preserve the result while making the population arbitrarily large relative to the number of types. A plausible setting is a large electorate with recurring single-peaked ballot profiles, where outreach to one profile reaches eligible voters with nearby profiles.

The tempting objection is that campaign selection remains discrete: the optimizer chooses a set \(S\) of leader types, so only the population—not the control action—is continuous. But the programme’s scope continuizes the population, not necessarily every decision variable. The mass affects the Plurality scores and Condorcet comparisons, while the discrete choice reflects the paper’s control action. And a hard answer would still chart the landscape; it cannot be dismissed because it resembles the discrete result or lacks an immediate LP formulation.

Theorem 2 has the same problem in its stronger, role-based formulation. Bundling signatures can be treated as leader types and follower types, with rational mass on each, and selecting leaders activates the union of their audiences. That retains a genuine mass election, even though it preserves a discrete maximum-coverage core. The arbitrary-network examples may be less persuasive as a general population model, but the paper’s network and advertising motivations give the authors a recognizable scenario to accept or reject.

So the negative case is weak where it needs to be strongest. Theorem 2 invites a serious question about whether network signatures produce a useful type space. Theorem 8, however, has a coherent continuous mirror, a plausible repeated-profile regime, and a faithful population interpretation. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror survives.
