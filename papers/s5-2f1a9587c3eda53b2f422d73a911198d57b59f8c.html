The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), positive rational per-member powers \(g_t\), and rational \(\alpha\in[1/2,1)\), let a coalition be a mass vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and power \(\Gamma(x)=\sum_t g_t x_t\). Does there exist a scale-covariant correspondence \(\phi\) on every nonzero subpopulation \(x\le\mu\) that satisfies the paper’s translated Inclusion, Power, Enforcement, and Group Rationality axioms, equivalently selecting minimum-power winning self-enforcing subcoalitions? If so, characterize and compute \(\phi(\mu)\) in terms of the type count, support size, and input bit length.

The model it lives in

A large federation of local political organizations, grouped into types by their complete relevant description, including per-member political power \(g_t\). The input gives each type’s population mass \(\mu_t\) and the common threshold \(\alpha\); the decision object is the selected ruling mass vector \(x\), chosen by the minimum-power rule subject to winning and self-enforcement. Under proportional allocation, type \(t\)’s aggregate share is \(g_t x_t/\Gamma(x)\); there is no separate optimization objective.

The objection that survived

The opponent shows that scale covariance, enforcement, and group rationality conflict when a proper fraction of a self-enforcing mass coalition is itself winning; the proponent does not resolve this conflict.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s axiomatic characterization and inductive construction. It does not cover the dynamic-game equivalence in Theorem 2, the cooperative-game equivalence in Theorem 3, or the later structural results on coalition size and robustness.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the four-axiom mass extension itself force scale covariance, or must scale covariance be imposed as part of the natural continuous model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved in Section 3: for any society, power mapping, and threshold \(\alpha\), there is a unique mapping satisfying Axioms 1–4; generically it selects a single ruling coalition. The theorem also gives an inductive procedure: a singleton selects itself, and each larger society selects a minimum-power member of the set consisting of itself and its proper, winning, self-enforcing subcoalitions. This is an algorithmic result, but the paper gives no running-time classification. I would use it as the sole anchor; there is no comparable hardness result to prefer.

The most plausible regime is a large federation of local political organizations—such as chapters or councils—where many organizations have the same voting power and face the same rules. The paper itself says that groups with solved internal collective action problems can be treated as individuals. A type is therefore an organization’s complete relevant description, here its per-capita political power \(g_t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the federation made up of type \(t\). The regime has many organizations but relatively few distinct types, \(\tau\). This is a credible large-population version of the paper’s society, though less compelling for a small cabinet or committee.

My proposed lead is Continuous Ruling Coalition. An instance is a finite list of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational powers \(g_t\), and rational \(\alpha\in[1/2,1)\). A coalition is a mass vector \(x\) with \(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\), and its power is \(\Gamma(x)=\sum_t g_t x_t\). A subcoalition \(y\leq x\) wins within \(x\) when \(\Gamma(y)>\alpha\Gamma(x)\). Under the paper’s proportional-power allocation, each member of type \(t\) in coalition \(x\) receives \(g_t/\Gamma(x)\), and the type’s aggregate share is \(g_tx_t/\Gamma(x)\).

The task is to determine the selected ruling mass vector for the whole society, allowing multiple outputs when there are ties. More precisely, seek a correspondence \(\phi\) on every nonzero subpopulation \(x\leq\mu\), where \(\phi(x)\) selects from \(x\) a winning coalition that is self-enforcing, and the selection obeys the paper’s minimum-power rule:
\[ \phi(x)=\arg\min_{y\in W(x)\cup\{x\}}\Gamma(y), \qquad W(x)=\{y\leq x:y\neq x,\ \Gamma(y)>\alpha\Gamma(x),\ y\in\phi(y)\}. \]
The solution is the set \(\phi(\mu)\). A prover should establish whether such a correspondence exists and is unique, characterize the possible outputs exactly, and determine whether they admit an effective algorithm in the type count, support size, and input bit length. This is the continuous version of the ruling-coalition problem, not a model that makes outcomes or time continuous.

I would expect this to be a continuum-specific difficulty candidate, rather than predict tractability or transferred hardness. Fractional membership preserves the paper’s power and enforcement constraints, but removes its induction on the finite number of players: a coalition can be split into smaller mass vectors, so there may be no minimal coalition at which to start the recursion. Whether the resulting selector remains unique and computable is a substantive part of the proposed problem, not something Theorem 1 settles.

The mirror is plausible because it keeps the paper’s central object, selection criterion, payoffs, and two constraints. Its weakest point is that fractional coalition membership may change stability itself: in the original model, an individual cannot be divided across coalitions. The authors could reasonably accept this as a high-multiplicity population limit, but the continuation’s first challenge is to show that the limit has a well-defined ruling coalition at all.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest on Theorem 1, but it is not airtight. The theorem gives an axiomatic characterization and an induction over finite societies; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem or bound that induction in input parameters. The proposed task—find an effective algorithm in the number of types and bit length—would add a computational question rather than continuize a computational result the paper establishes.

The best population setting is a large federation of otherwise identical local bodies. But those bodies are indivisible members in the paper’s model. Making the federation a continuum means allowing a coalition to contain fractional mass. That changes the structure the theorem relies on: for every nonzero coalition mass \(y\) and every \(q\in(\alpha,1)\), the proper submass \(qy\) is itself winning within \(y\).

This creates a problem for the paper’s scale-free selection rule. Its power comparisons and proportional shares are unchanged when all masses in a society are scaled by the same positive factor, so the natural continuous extension should satisfy \(\phi(qx)=q\phi(x)\). Suppose \(y=\phi(x)\). Enforcement gives \(\phi(y)=y\), and scale invariance then gives \(\phi(qy)=qy\). Thus \(qy\) is a proper, winning, self-enforcing subcoalition of \(y\). But Group Rationality applied to \(y\), with \(Z=qy\), requires
\[ Z\notin\phi(y)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Gamma(y)<\Gamma(Z). \]
The left side is true and the right side false. So no scale-invariant mass selector can preserve all four axioms. Avoiding this conflict requires a new primitive—such as a minimum coalition size, an indivisible mass unit, or a scale-dependent rule—that the paper does not supply.

Keeping indivisible bodies avoids that issue, but leaves a finite population with a minimum atom. Studying whether its outcomes converge as the number of bodies grows could be worthwhile, but that is a limit question whose answer depends on how the finite populations are approximated; it is not the proposed computation over fractional coalitions. The dynamic-game result does not supply an obvious escape: an atomless federation has no canonical way to choose an individual agenda setter, so a type-level proposer or another selection procedure would need to be added.

That is a substantial case against this mirror as a ChoCo complexity project: the named anchor is axiomatic, and its natural mass version loses the scale-free structure that made the finite theorem work. The universal claim remains weak, though. A carefully specified finite-to-continuous convergence study might still be valuable; it would be a different question from the proposed mass-vector selector.
