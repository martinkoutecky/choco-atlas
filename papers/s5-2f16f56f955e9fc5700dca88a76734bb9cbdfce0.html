The other named results characterize optimization solutions or establish robustness properties, without a running-time, approximation, hardness, or solver-formulation result under the stated gate. What the mirror covers

The proposed mass restatement covers Theorem 2.2 alone; it leaves the clone-robustness results, win-rate characterizations, and empirical case study untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper. It says that with \(n=2\) annotators and a representative preference dataset over alternatives \(\mathcal M\), for every algorithm \(\mathrm{ALG}\) and every \(C>0\), there are annotator reward functions \(r_1^*,r_2^*\) such that the algorithm’s output \(r^{\mathcal D}\) has

\[ \min_{\alpha\in\mathbb R}\sum_{x\in\mathcal M} \left(\frac{r_1^*(x)+r_2^*(x)}{2}-r^{\mathcal D}(x)-\alpha\right)^2>C. \]

In other words, even exact pairwise preference rates need not identify the population’s mean reward, up to the additive constant that BTL rewards leave undetermined.

A natural population regime is a very large pool of human raters whose preferences fall into a small number of stable reward profiles—for example, two profiles with equal prevalence. Let a type be a complete reward vector over the alternatives being compared, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of raters of type \(t\). A comparison of \(x\) and \(y\) then has population rate

\[ p_\mu(x\succ y)=\sum_t\mu_t \frac{e^{r_t(x)}}{e^{r_t(x)}+e^{r_t(y)}}. \]

Here the population may be large while the number of distinct types is two. Mass means the share of raters, and the decision variable is a single reward vector intended to represent the population.

I would hand the authors Continuous Two-Type Mean-Reward Recovery. Its instance is two alternatives \(a,b\), an exact representative comparison rate \(q=p_\mu(a\succ b)\), and an error threshold \(C>0\). The hidden society is any distribution \(\mu\) with binary-rational masses over at most two reward-vector types, satisfying

\[ q=\sum_t\mu_t\frac{e^{r_t(a)}}{e^{r_t(a)}+e^{r_t(b)}}. \]

The algorithm sees \(q\), not the types or their masses. It must either return \(\hat r\) such that, for every compatible society,

\[ \min_{\alpha\in\mathbb R}\sum_{x\in\{a,b\}} \left(\bar r_\mu(x)-\hat r(x)-\alpha\right)^2\le C, \qquad \bar r_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t r_t(x), \]

or certify that no such estimate exists. At \(q=1/3\), Theorem 2.2 supplies the obstruction: two equal-mass, two-type societies yield the same comparison rate but can have arbitrarily separated mean reward differences as \(C\) grows. No estimate based on that rate can meet a uniform finite error bound.

This is a strong population mirror of the paper’s mean-reward impossibility result: replacing two named annotators by masses \(1/2,1/2\) leaves the aggregate comparison model intact, and many raters sharing those two types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The obstruction transfers directly, so in the programme’s shorthand it is Class B-like. More precisely, it is an information or identification impossibility, not NP-hardness. It covers Theorem 2.2; it does not claim to mirror the weighted-MLE clone-robustness theorem or the case study. It also suggests follow-up questions: does a uniform reward bound restore finite-error recovery, and do more alternatives or richer observed comparisons identify the mean?

The soft spot is that this mirror adds little new computation. The paper already averages over a finite annotator population, and the comparison rates depend on that population through its type proportions. The proposed problem makes those proportions the explicit population object, but its anchor is still an identification result—not a complexity classification. Also, the paper’s construction uses reward gaps that grow with \(C\); under a fixed reward bound, or a fixed Lipschitz constant on a bounded context domain, this particular impossibility needs a new argument.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2.2 is the proponent’s only explicit anchor, and its direct population mirror mostly changes notation. Its witness already has two reward types, each with mass \(1/2\). Replicate each type any number of times: the population size grows, but every pairwise rate and the impossibility are unchanged. More generally, the paper’s data are representative pairwise rates, \(p^*(x\succ y)=\mathbb E_i[p_i(x\succ y)]\). The estimator sees those aggregate rates, not a list of annotators whose multiplicity it must process. Turning that expectation into a measure over types adds no computational parameter or new algorithmic task.

The proposed two-alternative recovery problem makes this especially clear. At \(q=1/3\), the paper’s theorem already supplies the obstruction for every finite error threshold \(C\). That establishes non-identifiability before computation; it does not reveal a complexity landscape for continuous societies. A more substantial version could bound reward gaps and ask for the largest range of mean rewards compatible with observed rates, perhaps as the number of alternatives or types varies. That is a plausible follow-up, but the bound and the resulting inverse problem are additional modeling choices. The paper’s theorem alone does not make their computational study a high-multiplicity relaxation of a problem it analyzes.

The best alternative anchor is Theorem 4.1 on clone robustness. But that theorem adds an alternative, not voter mass. The annotator population enters through pairwise rates, and the guarantee is already stated in terms of representative data and applies to those rates. A computational question about how much rater mass must change to alter the learned reward would need an intervention model and costs absent from the paper.

So the negative case is strongest against the claimed mirror of Theorem 2.2: it is an information-theoretic result over an aggregate model that already behaves like a type distribution, not a computational problem whose population axis can usefully be continuized. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A bounded-reward ambiguity problem could be a sensible new question, and I can’t rule out that the authors would recognize it as theirs.
