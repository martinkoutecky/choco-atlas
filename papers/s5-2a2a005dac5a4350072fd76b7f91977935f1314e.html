What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors are Theorems 3, 4, and 5; the paper's other results likewise concern equilibrium properties, welfare, or asymptotic implementation rather than computational complexity.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest setting for a population mirror is a large petition, polling, or shareholder-voting population. A type could be a sender’s private signal, \(g\) or \(b\), together with the preferences and information structure relevant to the game. Mass is the fraction of each signal type; conditional on state \(\theta\), the masses are \(\rho_\theta\) and \(1-\rho_\theta\). The action is the mass of each type approving, and the receiver chooses a policy from the aggregate approval. This is a convincing high-multiplicity regime: many senders, very few types.

But I can’t honestly give this paper a qualifying ChoCo anchor. Its numbered results concern equilibrium properties and asymptotic implementation, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. For example, Theorem 3 (proved here) says that with positive probability on the disagreement state, symmetric equilibria cannot aggregate information: errors stay bounded away from zero, and informative equilibria have bounded \(Nx\) and receiver cutoff. Theorem 4 (proved here) gives conditions for information transmission to persist or fail as the population grows. Theorem 5 (proved here) constructs feasible mediator mechanisms that asymptotically implement allocations on a Pareto frontier. These are substantial results, but none asserts a computational complexity result.

There is also a real obstacle to turning the promising setting into a faithful continuous version of those results. In the usual atomless limit, an individual sender cannot change the approval mass or the receiver’s decision. Under an exact law of large numbers, truthful reports reveal the state through the signal masses. That removes the pivotal incentives central to the finite-\(N\) analysis and to Theorem 3. Preserving those incentives would require changing the model, for example by giving strategic groups positive mass.

So the population-continuum angle is plausible as an analytic extension, but I do not see an honest, anchor-based continuous computational question in this paper. Its weakest point as a ChoCo candidate is that the large-population behavior it studies depends on individual pivotality, precisely what the straightforward continuum limit erases.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper proves no computational result. Theorem 3 bounds equilibrium learning and pivotality; Theorem 4 characterizes when informative equilibria persist; Theorem 5 establishes asymptotic feasibility of mediated outcomes. These are analytical results about equilibria and limits, not complexity classifications or algorithmic problems. A computational question could be built around them, but it would be a new agenda, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 3 is the best candidate for a stronger mirror because petitions and shareholder votes can have many participants with only a few signal types. Yet the atomless version removes the pivotal incentives behind the theorem. Conditional on each state, a continuum has exactly the corresponding signal proportions, so informative reports can identify the state from their aggregate. An individual sender has zero mass and cannot change the receiver’s decision; the incentive constraints based on being pivotal disappear. In the paper’s equilibria, \(x_N\) is of order \(1/N\) and the cutoff stays bounded. Rescaling to preserve those quantities gives a sparse, finite-count limit with Poisson-style randomness, not a continuous population distribution. Giving strategic blocs positive mass restores pivotality, but changes the agents who matter to the result.

Theorem 4 inherits that problem: its persistence threshold comes from the limiting behavior of \(Nx_N\) and a finite approval cutoff. A population continuum collapses the approval share when \(x_N\) is of order \(1/N\); preserving the threshold again requires a sparse-count limit. And the threshold is an analytical characterization over scalar parameters and integer cutoffs, not a computational task whose complexity the paper establishes.

Theorem 5 also loses its central mechanism in the continuum. Truthful signal proportions reveal the state exactly, while an individual report cannot affect the recommendation, making sender incentive constraints vacuous. The frontier then reduces to state-contingent recommendations subject to receiver obedience—a finite set of payoff inequalities. Its delicate balancing of two pivotal events disappears. One could study finite-\(N\) optimal mechanisms computationally: for an explicit \(N\), an anonymous mechanism has \(N+1\) tally outcomes and can be optimized with a linear program. A succinct-input complexity question might be interesting, but it would concern that newly specified finite-\(N\) problem, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 5.

I cannot make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous model exists in any scenario. The population setting is unusually plausible here, and new computational questions about finite-\(N\) equilibria or mechanisms could be worth pursuing. The narrower negative case is stronger: all three proposed anchors either lose their pivotal structure under genuine population continuity or reduce to tractable analytical conditions, and none supplies the computational result ChoCo is meant to continuize.
