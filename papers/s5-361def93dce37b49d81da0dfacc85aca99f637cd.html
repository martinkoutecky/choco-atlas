The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is only a possible model, not a case anchored in this paper’s results. The paper has no named computational result: no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserts that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

Theorem 1.2, proved here, says that for at least three alternatives, transitivity, IIA, and WNI force a constitution to be a dictator. Theorem 1.3, also proved here, characterizes constitutions satisfying transitivity and IIA by a partition of the alternatives and the behavior within each block. Both are axiomatic characterizations, not complexity results. The quantitative Arrow result mentioned in the conclusion is described as follow-up work, and it concerns probability of paradox and closeness to certain functions, not computational complexity.

A tempting continuous model would take types to be strict rankings \(t\in S_k\), with mass \(\mu_t\) equal to the fraction of society holding each ranking, and ask about pairwise aggregation from those masses. But making the constitution depend only on \(\mu\) imposes anonymity: the paper’s constitution acts on labeled profiles, and its dictatorship and pivotal-voter arguments depend on voter identity. The proposed model would therefore add an axiom and pose an axiomatic continuum question, rather than mirror a named computational problem from the paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to continuize. Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 classify constitutions by axioms; Theorem 2.4 is a structural lemma in that proof. None states an algorithmic problem or gives a complexity result. The quantitative paradox result is described as follow-up work, and it is an analytic stability statement, not a computational classification. That leaves no paper result for ChoCo’s complexity programme to mirror.

The strongest apparent candidate is to represent society by a mass vector \(\mu\) over rankings and ask for a constitution on that vector. But this identifies labeled profiles with the same ranking counts, while the paper permits constitutions whose behavior depends on voter identity: its pivotal-voter argument and dictatorship characterization turn on exactly that distinction. Including identity in each type preserves the paper’s object only by giving each voter a separate type of mass \(1/n\). That is not a high-multiplicity regime, and its continuous limit loses the individual whose influence the theorem tracks.

One could instead study axioms for functionals on distributions, or the complexity of checking axioms given a circuit encoding of a constitution. Those may be worthwhile new questions, but they change the subject: the former is an axiomatic continuum question, and the latter needs a computational representation and problem that this paper does not supply. The conclusion’s probability over random profiles is also not a continuous population model; replacing it with a distribution over types would still need to address the lost identity dependence. So the proponent’s mass-vector proposal does not survive in a stronger form. The negative case is unusually strong because the paper lacks a qualifying computational anchor, though that does not rule out a separate computational project inspired by its ideas.
