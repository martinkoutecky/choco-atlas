The continuous mirror question

Given a finite applicant type set \(T\), binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), strict rankings over \(o_1,o_2,\bot\), and each organization’s rational piecewise-polyhedral choice map \(C_j(q)\leq q\) satisfying \(q\leq q'\Rightarrow C_j(q)\leq C_j(q')\), find an exactly stable allocation \(x_{t,a}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\), or determine that none exists. Require applicant and organization individual rationality, and rule out every nonzero blocking cohort \(z\) whose members prefer \(o_j\) to their current assignment and satisfy \(z\leq C_j(x_{\cdot,o_j}+z)\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic applicant population divided among finitely many preference and organization-relevant types, with each organization choosing masses of member types. Applicants retain unit demand; allocations may divide each type’s mass, and stability excludes any preferred recruit cohort that the target organization would choose alongside its current members.

The objection that survived

The paper motivates organizational complementarity partly through members’ connections, which may depend on identities or pairwise relationships that a choice map over type masses does not preserve.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its pick-side recruitment application; it leaves Theorem 2 on TU stable-outcome existence and Proposition 1 on the equivalence of stability and setwise stability aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do these aggregate choice assumptions guarantee a stable allocation for every instance?
  • Can one find or rule out a stable allocation exactly in time polynomial in \(|T|\), the input bit length, and the number of polyhedral pieces?
  • Which relationship-based complementarities can be represented with a fixed, small type set?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper (its proof is in Appendix 7.1): in the finite NTU matching model, the alternate Deferred Acceptance algorithm finds a stable outcome when contracts are same-side complementary and cross-side substitutable for all agents. This is an algorithmic result, but the paper gives no polynomial-time classification or hardness theorem. So the case here is for mirroring its stable-outcome computation, not for claiming that a known hardness result disappears.

A plausible regime is a large recruitment drive by two rival organizations, drawing from a shared pool of applicants. Let a type specify an applicant’s ranking of the two organizations and the characteristics relevant to each organization’s choice. There may be many applicants \(N\), but only \(\tau\) recurring types, with \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\). That is a natural high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s pick-side application: each applicant joins at most one organization, while each organization may value combinations of member types.

Here is a precise mirror: Continuous Pick-Side Stable Allocation. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational binary-encoded masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a strict ranking for each type over \(o_1,o_2,\bot\), where \(\bot\) means unmatched. Each organization \(o_j\) has a choice map \(C_j(q)\), where \(q\in[0,1]^T\) is the vector of offered member masses and \(C_j(q)\leq q\) is the vector it chooses. Encode each map by finitely many rational polyhedral pieces, and require \(q\leq q'\Rightarrow C_j(q)\leq C_j(q')\) coordinatewise. This lifts the paper’s same-side complementarity condition to member masses.

A solution is an exact allocation \(x_{t,a}\geq 0\), for \(a\in\{o_1,o_2,\bot\}\), with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). It must be individually rational for applicants and organizations: no assigned applicant type prefers being unmatched, and \(C_j(x_{\cdot,o_j})=x_{\cdot,o_j}\) for each organization. It must also have no blocking cohort. Formally, a block by \(o_j\) is a nonzero vector \(z\geq 0\) such that each \(z_t\) comes from mass currently assigned elsewhere that strictly prefers \(o_j\), and
\[ z\leq C_j(x_{\cdot,o_j}+z) \]
coordinatewise. The right-hand side asks whether the organization would choose all the proposed recruits from its current roster plus that cohort. The problem is to find a stable allocation—or establish that none exists. Fractional type assignments are part of the population mirror: they let the model express that a share of a large cohort joins each side.

I think authors working on the paper’s pick-side model would recognize this question: it keeps their stability and blocking concepts, the applicants’ choice between two sides, and the organizations’ complementary preferences over members. It changes the population from named applicants to type masses, with mass-level organization choices as the necessary co-modification. A natural direction to investigate is Class A when \(\tau\) and the polyhedral choice representation are manageable: choice and blocking checks then live in a finite-dimensional rational space, and the alternate DA algorithm’s monotonic structure suggests a route to an exact method. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1; in the finite proof, termination follows from eliminating contracts, and the continuous version needs a new argument for exact convergence.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s NTU stable-outcome result and its pick-side application. It does not attempt to mirror the TU existence theorem or the setwise-stability proposition. It raises further questions: Does the continuous model always admit a stable allocation under these assumptions? Can one compute one in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the encoding length \(L\), and the number of polyhedral pieces? How does the solution relate to discrete elections or recruitment pools with \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and can it yield useful additive approximations there?

The weakest point is that the paper’s theorem is about finite contracts and says nothing about the runtime of its algorithm. Continuous choice maps also need a defensible representation, and the mass-level blocking condition requires its own existence and algorithmic analysis. The mirror is strongest as a clear computational question suggested by a named algorithmic theorem; the paper itself supplies no hardness anchor for the programme’s hardness-dissolution question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has one anchor, Theorem 1: the alternate DA algorithm finds a stable outcome. That is a real constructive result. The trouble is the bridge from its market to the proposed population model. In the paper, an organization chooses among sets of contracts with named agents. Its choice can depend on which people appear together: the paper itself motivates complementarity through members’ connections. Two applicants with the same ranking and résumé may therefore be different types for the problem’s purposes. Pooling them loses information that can change both choices and blocking coalitions. Making each person’s relevant relationships part of their type preserves the model, but can make the number of types grow with the population.

A stronger mirror could restrict attention to anonymous recruitment: organizations value only the masses of a fixed set of applicant types, with choices represented by rational polyhedral functions. That is coherent, and large recruitment cohorts make it plausible. But the restriction supplies the central computational input that Theorem 1 leaves unrestricted. The paper neither requires anonymous choice nor provides a succinct representation for it. Its finite algorithm relies on calls to each agent’s choice function and terminates because contracts are removed; it gives no result about computing choices from a compressed description. The mirror’s complexity would consequently depend on newly chosen representations of aggregate preferences and blocking, rather than on a complexity landscape already present in the paper.

This does not make the anonymous model uninteresting. It does make it a new, restricted matching problem whose authorship and relevance would need to be established separately. The proposed polyhedral maps, mass-level blocks, and exact computation questions are sensible ways to define that problem; they are not consequences of the paper’s assumptions. The TU existence theorem and the setwise-stability proposition do not supply a stronger computational anchor.

So the case against a direct, general mirror is substantial: preserving the paper’s identity-sensitive complementarities defeats type compression, while making compression work adds assumptions that carry the computational content. The case against any worthwhile mirror in any scenario is weak. Anonymous cohort recruitment is plausible, and Theorem 1 makes stable-outcome computation a recognizable question. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile restricted mirror; I can only argue that the proponent has proposed a promising new model, rather than shown that this paper’s result transfers to it.
