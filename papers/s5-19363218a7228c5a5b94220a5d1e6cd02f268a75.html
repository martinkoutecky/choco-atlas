The continuous mirror question

Given \(p\ge1\), a finite ordered type set \(T\subset\mathbb{Q}\), and rational masses \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), compute the replication-consistent lower-median facility \(F_{\mathrm{med}}(\mu)=\inf\{y:\mu((-\infty,y])\ge\tfrac12\}\) and certify \(\Phi_p(\mu,F_{\mathrm{med}}(\mu))\le 2^{1-\frac1p}\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\Phi_p(\mu,y)\), where \(\Phi_p(\mu,y)=\left(\sum_{x\in T}\mu_x|x-y|^p\right)^{1/p}\), with admissibility inherited from strategyproof finite replicas.

The model it lives in

Finite-support high-multiplicity facility location on the line: types are ideal locations \(x\in T\) with masses \(\mu_x\), the decision is \(y\in\mathbb{R}\), the objective is the normalized \(L_p\) mass cost \(\Phi_p\), and the median rule is certified through finite anonymous strategyproof replicas.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity facility-location model, with incentive constraints interpreted through finite replicas or positive-mass type deviations. A naive atomless model is not acceptable: a single zero-mass agent cannot affect the facility location, so strategyproofness becomes vacuous.

Let a type be a complete location-and-cost description \(x\in\mathbb R\), with individual cost \(c_x(y)=|x-y|\). A society is a finitely supported distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of residents of type \(x\). The facility is placed at \(y\in\mathbb R\), and normalized social cost is

\[ \Phi_p(\mu,y)=\left(\int |x-y|^p\,d\mu(x)\right)^{1/p}. \]

The optimum is \(\mathrm{OPT}_p(\mu)=\min_y\Phi_p(\mu,y)\). Normalizing by population size changes the paper’s social cost only by the factor \(N^{-1/p}\), so approximation ratios are unchanged.

The intended regime is a large public-service population—say \(N\) residents but only \(\tau\ll N\) recurring ideal-location types: residential districts, workplaces, commuting communities, or demographic cohorts with identical cost functions. Rational masses \(\mu_x=n_x/N\) are exactly high-multiplicity finite profiles.

I would define population-strategyproofness as follows. If a mass \(\varepsilon\) of type \(x\) changes its report to \(x'\), the distribution becomes \(\mu'=\mu-\varepsilon\delta_x+\varepsilon\delta_{x'}\), and a deterministic rule \(F\) must satisfy

\[ |x-F(\mu)|\le |x-F(\mu')|. \]

Equivalently, one may define the rule as the limit of anonymous strategyproof mechanisms on all finite replicas. This keeps the paper’s strategic content while making the population itself continuous.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that for every profile of \(n\) agents and every \(p\ge1\), locating the facility at a reported median gives social cost at most \(2^{1-\frac1p}\) times the optimal \(L_p\) social cost. The \(p=\infty\) parenthetical refers to an earlier result, but the finite-\(p\) theorem is proved here.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Deterministic \(L_p\)-Facility Mechanism Design. An instance consists of \(p\), a finite rational type universe \(T\), and a target ratio \(\alpha\). The solver must produce an anonymous rule \(F\) on all rational distributions \(\mu\) over \(T\), satisfying population-strategyproofness and

\[ \Phi_p(\mu,F(\mu))\le \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}_p(\mu) \]

for every \(\mu\). A solution is the rule together with its approximation guarantee; operationally, given \(\mu\), it outputs the facility location.

The continuous median rule is

\[ F_{\mathrm{med}}(\mu)=\inf\{y:\mu((-\infty,y])\ge\tfrac12\}. \]

It is computable by sorting the \(\tau\) types, and Theorem 1’s pairing argument extends directly from empirical profiles to distributions. Thus this mirror is plausibly Class A: the continuous problem has a simple exact rule, while the optimum is a one-dimensional convex minimization problem.

The paper’s Theorem 2 says that no deterministic strategyproof mechanism can achieve a ratio below \(2^{1-\frac1p}\). I would not use it as a primary anchor for the high-multiplicity model, because its proof obtains the lower bound from a two-agent profile. Under a strict \(N\to\infty\) interpretation, that obstruction might disappear. Under the positive-mass/cohort interpretation above, however, the same two-location contradiction should transfer, suggesting that the median ratio remains optimal.

A second, particularly good anchor is Theorem 3, proved in Section 4.1. It considers randomized mechanisms that, for an \(n\)-agent profile, place the facility at \(OPT\) with probability \(p_{OPT}^n\), at reported locations \(x_j\) with probabilities \({p_j^n}'\), and at order statistics \(x_{[j]}\) with probabilities \({p_j^n}''\), where all probabilities are nonnegative, sum to one, and the mechanism is strategyproof. The theorem proves that for every finite integer \(p>2\), the approximation ratio of every such mechanism is at least \(2^{1-\frac1p}\) as \(n\) tends to infinity.

Its continuous mirror is High-Multiplicity Randomized \(L_p\)-Facility Design. An instance consists of integer \(p>2\), a finite type universe \(T\), and a population distribution \(\mu\). For every replica size \(N\) with \(N\mu_x\in\mathbb Z\), form the corresponding \(N\)-agent profile. An admissible rule is a sequence of exactly the Theorem 3 mechanisms, with coefficients independent of the reported locations, whose output distributions converge as \(N\to\infty\). The question is whether some such limiting rule can have worst-case ratio strictly below \(2^{1-\frac1p}\), or equivalently whether a positive limiting probability on the population optimum can survive strategyproofness while improving on the median bound.

The expected answer is negative, and the proof should be essentially the paper’s asymptotic argument in mass language. The paper’s four-point profiles become distributions with fixed masses at \(-a\), \(0\), \(1\), and \(1+a\); the half-at-\(0\), half-at-\(1\) profile gives the limiting lower-bound witness. This is not a hardness transfer—the paper contains no NP-hardness result—but it is a tractable asymptotic impossibility problem. It also generates useful questions: whether the \(p=2\) and \(p=\infty\) randomized improvements survive the limit, whether the order of \(\limsup_N\) and worst-case minimization matters, and whether the negative result extends beyond the restricted mixture class.

The mirror covers the paper’s deterministic median guarantee and its large-population randomized lower bound. It does not claim to continuize every result: Theorem 4 is specifically about two agents and is a weaker high-multiplicity fit, while the discussion of trees, cycles, and general cost functions would require separate mirrors.

The weakest point is unavoidable: literal individual strategyproofness on an atomless population collapses, because one individual has zero influence. The proposed finite-replica or positive-mass-deviation semantics is therefore a deliberate co-modification. It is defensible because it preserves the original location preferences, facility decision, \(L_p\) objective, and finite strategyproofness, and because Theorem 3 already studies the \(n\to\infty\) regime. But I would state that convention explicitly rather than pretending it is automatic.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has found a plausible high-multiplicity public-service model, but that model is a new problem rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.

The decisive issue is that the paper contains no named computational result in the sense relevant to ChoCo. Theorems 1–4 give approximation guarantees and mechanism-design impossibility results; none studies the complexity of a finite-input computational problem. Given a distribution \(\mu\), computing the proposed median mechanism is just computing a quantile, while computing \(\mathrm{OPT}_p(\mu)\) is one-dimensional convex minimization. The proponent’s task of “producing a rule on all rational distributions” is a synthesis problem over an unspecified function space, not a finite-input complexity problem. Thus there is no computational landscape here for continuization to expose.

The normalized objective does have a perfectly legitimate measure formulation:

\[ N^{-1/p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^N |x_i-y|^p\right)^{1/p} = \left(\int |x-y|^p\,d\mu_N(x)\right)^{1/p}. \]

Theorem 1 can consequently be extended from empirical measures to probability measures, and the median becomes a quantile. That is mathematically sensible, but it is an analytic restatement of the same inequality, not a computational result of the kind the programme is designed to classify.

More seriously, the paper’s strategic content does not survive the population limit. In the paper, strategyproofness is unilateral: one agent changes one report. In a continuum, one agent has measure zero, so changing her report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. At every continuity point of \(F\),

\[ F\!\left(\mu-\frac1N\delta_x+\frac1N\delta_{x'}\right)\longrightarrow F(\mu), \]

and the incentive constraint degenerates into equality. The rule is then “strategyproof” for the wrong reason: no individual can influence the outcome.

The proposed replacement, in which a positive mass \(\varepsilon\) changes its report, is not a harmless limiting convention. It is a coalition or block-manipulation notion. A deviation of mass \(\varepsilon\) corresponds to \(\varepsilon N\) agents in a finite replica, whereas the paper requires one agent. A finite-replica definition does not repair this: it gives a sequence of discrete mechanisms whose unilateral constraints disappear in the limit, except at rounding and median-tie boundaries. A direct mass-deviation definition instead studies a different incentive concept.

This defeats the proposed reading of Theorem 1. The median guarantee itself extends, but the theorem’s strategic claim either becomes vacuous or is replaced by coalitional strategyproofness. The latter could be an interesting new facility-location problem, but it is not the continuous version of the theorem proved in the paper.

Theorem 2 does not rescue the case. Its contradiction depends on one of two agents misreporting and thereby moving the facility to a precise location. Replicating those two agents into two large cohorts removes that power from every individual. Allowing an entire cohort to deviate restores a nontrivial constraint only by making the cohort a strategic super-agent. Theorem 4 has exactly the same defect: its essential object is a mechanism for two individual reports, not a population mechanism.

Theorem 3 is an even weaker anchor than claimed. Its mechanism class is indexed by the population size and by individual slots \(j\): probabilities \(p_j^n\) are attached to reported locations and order statistics. A distribution \(\mu\) forgets those labels. The same \(\mu\) can be represented with \(N\) agents or \(rN\) replicated agents, but the theorem permits different coefficients \(p_j^N\) and \(p_j^{rN}\). Without an additional replication-consistency axiom, there is no rule \(F(\mu)\) at all.

Nor does the proof automatically become a mass argument. The witness profiles use support

\[ \{-a_j,0,1,1+a_j\}, \]

where \(a_j\) depends on both \(j\) and \(n\), and the proof derives its bound from separate unilateral deviations for all slots \(j=1,\ldots,k\). After aggregation, those labelled deviations are not deviations of a positive-mass type. If one permits \(N\)-dependent type spaces or an unbounded continuum of locations, one obtains a new asymptotic model, not a fixed high-multiplicity society. If one instead insists on a fixed type universe and a rule determined by \(\mu\), the paper’s \(p_j^n\)-based lower-bound argument no longer transfers.

Thus the statement that Theorem 3 “should be essentially the paper’s asymptotic argument in mass language” is precisely what has not been established. Under individual strategyproofness the lower-bound mechanism disappears; under mass strategyproofness the incentive inequalities must be reproved for a different problem; under sequence-of-replicas semantics the object is a family of discrete mechanisms rather than a continuous rule.

There is a worthwhile research direction nearby—coalition-strategyproof facility location for large demographic cohorts—but it would be a new continuous mechanism-design paper. For this paper, the normalized objective has a natural measure notation, but the strategic notion degenerates and the numbered results do not supply a computational anchor. I would therefore reject it as a worthwhile ChoCo continuous mirror, while acknowledging that the broader facility-location model itself is not nonsensical.
