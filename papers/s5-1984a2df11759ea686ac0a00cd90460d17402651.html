The continuous mirror question

Given a finite school set \(C\), a finite support \(X\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_x\) summing to one, and for each type an explicit finite list of positive-probability uncertainty events \(j\) with feasible set \(B_{xj}\) and assigned school \(\alpha_{xj}\), compute each type’s relation set \(P_x\) and, for every ordered pair \(a,b\in C\), compute \(M_{ab}=\sum_{x\in X}\mu_x\mathbf{1}\{(a,b)\in P_x\}\). Here \(P_x\) is the transitive closure of the relations \((\alpha_{xj},c)\) for \(c\in B_{xj}\setminus\{\alpha_{xj}\}\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete TEPS-relevant types: each type carries mass \(\mu_x\), a submitted ranking and intrinsic priority, and its uncertainty trace. For each type, the event outcomes define a directed school-relation graph; transitive closure yields \(P_x\), and mass aggregation yields \(M\).

The objection that survived

The mirror takes an explicit uncertainty trace as input, while the paper does not bound the cost of constructing that trace from a compact population and uncertainty model; the proponent acknowledges this limitation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 1’s TEPS relation inference and its aggregation across types; leaves trace generation, Theorem 1’s asymptotic-stability result, preference estimation, and policy counterfactuals outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the uncertainty traces and cutoffs be computed or approximated from a compact rational description of the population and uncertainty laws, with complexity stated in \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • In plausible large school-assignment markets, can common rankings, priorities, and uncertainty patterns produce moderate support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow positive case around Proposition 1, but a qualification matters: the paper has no named result classifying a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Proposition 1 is a proved algorithmic correctness result, not a complexity theorem. If only complexity-class results count as anchors, this paper has no eligible anchor.

Proposition 1, proved here in Appendix A.4, says: fix a student’s submitted ROL \(R\) and observed priorities \(t\). If TEPS simulates a subset of uncertainty realizations \(\Omega'\), its output \(P\) is exactly \(P_{\mathrm{ST}}(\Omega')\): the preference relations inferable from stability on those realizations and transitivity. Thus TEPS recovers all, and only, the relations justified by those assumptions.

The lead mirror I would offer is Exact Mass-TEPS. Its instance has a fixed set \(C\) of schools and \(\tau\) applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_x\) summing to one. A type groups applicants with the same relevant preference ranking, submitted ROL, intrinsic priority, and priority-uncertainty law. For each type, an explicit uncertainty trace lists positive-probability events \(j\), each giving a feasible set \(B_{xj}\) and the school \(\alpha_{xj}\) assigned under its ROL. This is the post-simulation stage of the paper’s TEPS procedure; the trace can be produced by running DA on the continuous population.

The task is to compute each type’s TEPS relation \(P_x\), then the identified mass for every ordered pair of schools:
\[ M_{ab}=\sum_x \mu_x\,\mathbf{1}\{(a,b)\in P_x\}. \]
For each event, add the direct relations \((\alpha_{xj},c)\) for every \(c\in B_{xj}\setminus\{\alpha_{xj}\}\); \(P_x\) is the transitive closure of those relations. The output is the type-level relation sets and the matrix \(M\). Here \(M_{ab}\) is the population share for whom TEPS identifies \(a\) above \(b\), not an estimate of the share who truly hold that preference when it is unidentified.

A plausible regime is a large centralized school assignment pooling many applicants over a fixed menu of schools and coarse priority classes, where common preference and application templates recur. The relevant setting is \(N\gg\tau\), with each type’s mass representing a fraction of the cohort. That is not a claim about every school-choice market, or about the paper’s Staten Island sample; exact rankings and priority laws can make the support large. For the TEPS ordinal-inference task, grouping by a shared ranking is enough. A mirror invoking the paper’s payoff-based equilibrium theorem would need to refine types to include shared cardinal utilities too.

I expect this explicit-trace problem to be tractable: form the relation graph for each type and compute its transitive closure, then sum the rational masses. Its running time is polynomial in the number of schools, types, trace events, and input bit length. This mirrors Proposition 1’s exactness claim, but the paper does not analyze the full end-to-end complexity of constructing the traces. That leaves natural follow-up questions: can cutoffs and uncertainty traces be computed exactly from a compact rational description of the population and score laws, and how does that cost depend on \(m\) and \(\tau\)?

The authors could plausibly recognize Mass-TEPS as a computational version of their preference-inference problem: Proposition 1 already defines what counts as the correct answer, and the mass output is the natural population-level summary. The scope is just that TEPS inference stage, not the paper’s econometric estimation or its counterfactual policy analyses.

The weakest point is that the paper already formulates a continuum economy and proves its analytic limit result: Theorem 1, proved here, states that under Marginal Full Support any regular robust equilibrium is asymptotically stable. So the population continuization itself is already present. The proposed mirror asks for a computational analysis of a step the paper carries out; moreover, with the uncertainty trace supplied, the closure computation is straightforward. This is a credible but modest positive case, not a hardness-dissolution story, and it cannot support one of the programme’s preferred hardness anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered complexity result here: no classification, hardness theorem, approximation result, or algorithmic running-time bound. Proposition 1 is the nearest anchor. It proves that TEPS returns exactly the preference relations implied by stability and transitivity for the simulated uncertainty set. That is a correctness statement, and it gives the proponent a real result to discuss, but no complexity landscape to continuize.

For Exact Mass-TEPS, once the uncertainty trace is supplied, each type’s answer is just the transitive closure of a relation graph, followed by weighted sums. This is polynomial in the explicit trace size, number of types, and number of schools. The mass aggregation adds no computational difficulty to Proposition 1’s individual inference. A stronger end-to-end version would have to compute those traces from the population and uncertainty laws. Yet the trace can have many distinct outcomes, and continuous score laws need not yield a finite event list. The paper’s proposition gives no complexity result for computing the traces. A bound polynomial in an already-expanded trace would leave the main computational cost outside the claim; a compact-input version would be a new problem.

There is also a tradeoff in making that aggregate output matter to the paper’s downstream aims. Grouping by submitted ROL, intrinsic priority, and uncertainty law preserves TEPS inference, but a matrix of pairwise identified masses loses joint preference information used in utility estimation and counterfactuals. Preserving those analyses requires retaining the relevant utility and covariate variation in the types. The paper assumes an atomless distribution of ex-post types, so exact types can have vanishing mass; that representation offers little high-multiplicity compression. This is a limitation of the estimation mirror, not of the ordinal TEPS task itself.

Theorem 1 does not close the computational question: it is an analytic asymptotic-stability result, and the paper’s own continuum economy supports the claim that school choice is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. Existing high-multiplicity work is likewise supporting evidence, not a collision. The negative case is narrower: the sole proposed anchor yields either a routine weighted graph-closure task when traces are explicit, or a substantially different end-to-end computation when they are implicit. I would reject this anchor as a strong ChoCo complexity mirror, while conceding that the end-to-end algorithmic question could be worthwhile on its own.
