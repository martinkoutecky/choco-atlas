The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many user types \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational contact intensities \(\lambda_{st}\), mutual-complementarity GAPs \(\mathbf Q^+\), fixed \(\mathcal A\)-seed masses \(a_t\), and a \(\mathcal B\)-seed mass budget \(\kappa\), consider finite populations with type proportions tending to \(\mu\) and independent directed contact probabilities \(\lambda_{st}/N\). Under the paper’s Com-IC adoption and reconsideration rules, choose masses \(x_t,y_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le a_t\), \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t-a_t\), and \(\sum_t(x_t+y_t)=\kappa\), to maximize the limiting expected fraction \(F_A(x,y)-F_A(0,0)\) of \(\mathcal A\)-adopters; determine the complexity of exact optimization and of finding an additive-\(\varepsilon\) approximation.

The model it lives in

A finite set of exchangeable user cohorts with mass \(\mu_t\), contact kernel \(\lambda_{st}\), and the paper’s Com-IC automaton. The decisions \(x_t,y_t\) allocate \(\mathcal B\)-seed mass among already \(\mathcal A\)-seeded and other users in each cohort, and the objective is the increase in limiting expected \(\mathcal A\)-adoption.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s CIM hardness question in a typed, large-population regime; it leaves the other Com-IC results, approximation analyses, experiments, and parameter-learning results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions does the finite-population expected adoption fraction converge to \(F_A(x,y)\) for every feasible seed-mass allocation?
  • What are the exact and additive-approximation complexities in \(|\mathcal T|\), input bit length, and \(\varepsilon\); does fractional seeding preserve any hardness?
  • Can reverse-reachable-set methods give useful guarantees for mass allocations when \(q_{\mathcal B|\mathcal A}=1\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Complementary Influence Maximization for a large typed population. In a plausible regime, a very large platform has \(\tau\) user cohorts—say, language and activity groups—with the same per-capita contact rates to each cohort and the same adoption behavior. The number of users \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\), and each cohort contains many users. A type records everything the diffusion model uses about a user: their contact rates and any relevant seed status. This is a narrower setting than an arbitrary social graph, but influence maximization over cohort-level exposure is still recognizably the authors’ problem.

Here is a precise version. The input has a finite type set \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational contact rates \(\lambda_{st}\), mutual-complementarity GAPs \(\mathbf Q^+\), a fixed \(\mathcal A\)-seed mass \(a_t\) for each type, and a budget \(\kappa\) for \(\mathcal B\)-seeds. Interpret \(\lambda_{st}\) as the per-capita contact intensity from type \(s\) to type \(t\). Equivalently, in a finite population approximation of size \(N\), an individual in \(s\) contacts an individual in \(t\) with probability \(\lambda_{st}/N\), and the original Com-IC automaton governs adoption and reconsideration.

The decision is a pair of mass vectors \(x,y\). Here \(x_t\) is \(\mathcal B\)-seed mass chosen from the already \(\mathcal A\)-seeded mass, and \(y_t\) is \(\mathcal B\)-seed mass chosen from the rest. They satisfy
\[ 0\le x_t\le a_t,\qquad 0\le y_t\le \mu_t-a_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in\mathcal T}(x_t+y_t)=\kappa. \]
Let \(F_A(x,y)\) be the limiting expected fraction of the population that adopts \(\mathcal A\) under the resulting mean-field Com-IC diffusion. The problem asks for feasible \(x,y\) maximizing
\[ F_A(x,y)-F_A(0,0), \]
or, computationally, an allocation within a specified additive tolerance of the optimum. Thus it retains the paper’s fixed \(\mathcal A\)-seeds, chosen \(\mathcal B\)-seeds, and expected boost in \(\mathcal A\)-adoption. It changes the seed decision from selecting named users to allocating seed mass across indistinguishable cohorts.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that CIM under Com-IC is NP-hard. The reduction is from ordinary influence maximization under IC: for each vertex of the IC instance it creates a dummy copy, makes all dummy copies the fixed \(\mathcal A\)-seeds, sets \((q_{\mathcal A|\emptyset},q_{\mathcal A|\mathcal B},q_{\mathcal B|\emptyset},q_{\mathcal B|\mathcal A})=(0,1,1,1)\), and uses the original seed budget. Choosing the dummy copies corresponding to an IC seed set \(T\) produces \(\mathcal A\)-spread equal to \(\sigma_{IC}(T)+n\); the proof also shows that an optimal \(\mathcal B\)-seed set can be taken to contain only dummy copies. So the theorem’s hardness is driven by which parts of the interaction structure are targeted, not by a complicated adoption automaton.

For the continuous problem, I would expect exact optimization to be hard, tentatively Class B: the type-contact structure can retain the combinatorial targeting choices, even when every type represents many users. But Theorem 1 does not prove that claim. Its reduction chooses individual graph vertices, whereas this mirror permits fractional seeding within a type. A prover would need to show that this relaxation still preserves hardness, or find an instance where fractional mass changes the answer. Approximation is a separate question; the anchor gives no inapproximability result.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem in a large-platform regime: the decision still allocates limited \(\mathcal B\)-marketing to maximize its complementary effect on \(\mathcal A\)-adoption. The main co-modification is a per-capita contact kernel so that the typed population has a defined large-population diffusion limit. The question is about the population; the stochastic cascade and integral adoption decisions of individual users remain in the model.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s CIM hardness claim, not the paper’s results about arbitrary graphs, all parameter regimes, or its empirical algorithms. It also raises the bridge question the programme wants: as cohort sizes grow, does the finite CIM optimum converge to this mass-allocation optimum, and under what conditions does hardness survive fractional seeding? A further question is whether reverse-reachable-set methods can give useful guarantees for mass allocations under \(q_{\mathcal B|\mathcal A}=1\), the regime where the paper proves its CIM RR-set result.

My weakest point is that the paper’s inputs are individual social graphs, and Theorem 1’s reduction encodes their topology directly. Replacing that graph by a finite contact kernel loses local network structure, while allowing fractional seeds changes the feasible solutions. The mirror is therefore convincing for large, cohort-structured populations, but it should not be presented as preserving the paper’s hardness result until that discrete-to-continuous bridge is proved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 1’s hardness lives in the *particular graph*, while the proposed mirror replaces that graph with a cohort contact kernel. In the reduction, selecting dummy nodes corresponds to selecting vertices whose locations in the IC network determine the spread. For an arbitrary fixed graph, two users with the same average contact rates can still have different neighborhoods and different roles in cascades. A complete type would have to preserve those distinctions; its support could then grow with the population, leaving no useful compression into \(\tau\) cohort masses.

The proposed model avoids that problem by changing the instance regime: users in a cohort have exchangeable contacts, with pairwise probability \(\lambda_{st}/N\). That is a coherent model, but it does not carry Theorem 1’s reduction. With a seed budget of \(\Theta(N)\), the decision is a positive-mass allocation across cohorts, rather than choosing a small set of influential vertices. With a sublinear budget in this sparse regime, the chosen seeds have vanishing population mass, and their impact can depend on individual cascade paths that cohort averages do not retain. So the bridge from the theorem’s hardness to this mass-allocation problem is genuinely open.

A stronger repair would retain network heterogeneity through a continuum kernel or a structured graph sequence. That could preserve community-level exposure and give a computational question about allocating seed mass. But it still restricts the input networks to those admitting that representation, and it does not recover arbitrary-graph seed placement. This is a real limitation on what the mirror says about the paper’s result. It is not, by itself, a reason the restricted problem is unworthy: the programme explicitly permits a meaningful population regime and co-modifications that make it well-defined.

So I cannot honestly defeat the proponent’s anchor on the required standard. The proposed cohort version is plausibly a computational CIM question the authors would recognize, and the fact that Theorem 1’s hardness may fail to transfer is a question for the mirror to investigate, not an objection to asking it. The negative case is strongest against presenting it as a continuous counterpart that preserves the paper’s hardness claim; it is weak against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
