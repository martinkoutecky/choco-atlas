What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers central source assignment and its submodular-cost tractability; it leaves the cooperative-game properties, Shapley-value implementation result, and source-specific organization impossibility result aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do natural replenishment models provide exactly evaluable aggregate cost functions with the proposed concavity and submodularity properties?
  • Can the continuous source-assignment problem be solved efficiently when those added structure assumptions are weakened?

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no numbered result that directly classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally hard. Its theorems mainly concern cost functions, cooperative-game properties, and strategic implementation. The closest computational anchor is Lemma 1, proved in the paper: for every submodular CRI situation \(\Gamma\) and coalition \(S\subseteq N\), the replenishment cost
\[ r^\Gamma_S(X)=r_m(X)+r_w(X^\Gamma_S\setminus X) \]
is submodular on \(X^\Gamma_S\). The authors then cite Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver to infer that optimal replenishment policies can be found by strongly polynomial submodular minimization. That algorithmic consequence appears in the discussion following the lemma, rather than in a numbered complexity statement, so Lemma 1 is a near-anchor under your strict rule, not an unambiguous qualifying anchor.

If that consequence is accepted as the anchor’s computational content, the strongest positive mirror I see is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s central source-assignment problem. Imagine thousands of garages on a purchasing platform, with many sharing exactly the same relevant procurement profile: product basket, per-capita demand, fulfillment region, and supplier terms. Those complete profiles are the types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of garages of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The scenario is convincing only if the number of garages is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of such profiles. The paper already lets the organization choose, for each buyer-product pair, between manufacturer and intermediary, so choosing source by type-product class is recognizably its problem.

One precise version is Continuous-type CRI Source Assignment. The input is the rational mass vector \(\mu\), the product set \(E_t\) and per-capita demand of each type, and rationally evaluable aggregate per-period cost functions for the two sources. For each \(t\) and \(\ell\in E_t\), let \(x_{t\ell}\in[0,\mu_t]\) be the mass of type \(t\) whose demand for product \(\ell\) is sourced directly. The objective is to find \(x\) minimizing
\[ R_m(x)+R_w(\mu^E-x), \]
where \(R_m\) and \(R_w\) are the costs of jointly replenishing the corresponding masses from manufacturers and intermediaries. Ask for a minimum-cost policy and its cost. To make the computational question well specified, the input must give exact rational evaluation oracles for these functions, with evaluation time and output bit length included in \(L\).

The mirror’s tractable regime would retain the paper’s diminishing-cost structure: the mass-cost extension is concave in each sourcing coordinate, and its restriction to whole type-product classes gives a submodular set function. Then a minimum has a type-uniform policy: with other coordinates fixed, concavity lets each coordinate be moved to an endpoint without increasing cost. Submodular minimization over the \(\sum_t |E_t|\) type-product classes gives a natural Class A expectation, with complexity in \(\tau\), the product dimension, \(L\), and the cost-oracle runtime—not the raw garage count. For a finite CRI instance with identical buyers grouped by type, \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), giving the intended high-multiplicity bridge.

This covers only the paper’s central optimization of replenishment sources. It does not mirror Theorems 4 or 5: those establish strategic implementation and its failure in an alternative organization, rather than computational complexity results. Further questions include whether the tractability survives realistic non-submodular costs—truck capacities and shortages are examples the paper mentions—and whether the result can extend beyond the submodular regime.

The weakest point is the added mass-cost model. The paper defines costs on finite sets of buyer-product pairs; it does not specify how those costs extend to fractions of a type, or prove that the extension remains submodular. The type-uniform conclusion also needs those added assumptions. So this is a credible mirror for a deliberately structured, standardized procurement regime, but the paper itself does not supply the continuous model or establish its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper has no numbered computational result to mirror. Lemma 1 proves submodularity of a finite replenishment cost function; the claim that this permits a strongly polynomial algorithm appears in the surrounding discussion. The remaining numbered results concern cost games, allocation rules, and participation incentives. So the proponent’s anchor depends on treating an algorithmic consequence of Lemma 1 as the result itself.

Even granting that generous reading, Lemma 1 concerns a finite ground set \(X^\Gamma_S\), with one element for each buyer-product pair. Its cost functions are defined on subsets of those elements. The paper gives no rule for evaluating costs at fractional masses. A mirror with type masses \(\mu_t\) therefore needs aggregate functions \(R_m\) and \(R_w\) that the paper neither specifies nor constrains. An oracle for those new functions would also need its own evaluation complexity and encoding in \(L\).

The strongest scenario is a large platform of garages with repeated, complete procurement profiles: the same product basket, demand, supplier access, and cost parameters. A source-assignment problem over the resulting mass distribution is plausible. But the proposed tractable version adds coordinate-wise concavity and a tractable mass-cost oracle. Under those assumptions, each sourcing coordinate can be moved to an endpoint without increasing cost. With symmetric submodular costs, an optimum can likewise be chosen uniformly for each type-product block. The resulting problem is a finite submodular minimization over those blocks; it does not require the proposed fractional source assignments. Allowing meaningful partial-mass assignments instead requires additional cost structure, and Lemma 1 does not establish that the problem remains tractable.

That is the best case against the anchor: the paper’s computational foothold is a finite set-function fact, and the stronger continuous model needs substantive assumptions that determine its answer. It does not defeat every plausible mirror. A well-specified replenishment model whose costs naturally depend on aggregate demand could still be a worthwhile high-multiplicity problem, and the paper’s source-assignment setting makes that scenario credible. I would call the negative case weak on the universal claim.
