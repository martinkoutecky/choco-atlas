The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no eligible named anchor: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic result. Its reported prediction accuracies and feature rankings are empirical findings, not such anchors. So there is no honest anchor-specific continuous problem to state or classify as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

There is a plausible population extension for a follow-up: on a photo platform with many users and recurring aesthetic profiles, let each type be a complete pairwise preference-response model, and let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s population share. Given a distribution \(Q\) of photo pairs and their feature differences \(z_{AB}\), fit a shared logistic predictor \(w\) by minimizing the population-weighted cross-entropy
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbb{E}_{(A,B)\sim Q}\!\left[ -p_t(A,B)\log \sigma(w^\top z_{AB}) -(1-p_t(A,B))\log\!\left(1-\sigma(w^\top z_{AB})\right) \right]. \]
That is recognizably related to the paper’s group preference model. But it is an extension, not a mirror of a named result in this paper; the paper does not analyze its computational complexity. The missing anchor is the case’s decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the gap the proponent admits: there is no named computational result here to mirror. The paper reports predictive accuracies and feature weights from experiments; it does not state a complexity result about a computational problem. Logistic regression is the method used to produce those measurements, not a problem whose complexity the paper studies. So the proponent has raised no eligible anchor.

Their population-weighted cross-entropy is a coherent follow-up for a platform with many users sharing a finite set of complete preference-response types. That scenario is plausible, and repeated types would make the high-multiplicity regime sensible. But the formula introduces a new shared training objective, with a pair distribution \(Q\) and type-level response probabilities \(p_t\). The paper instead trains on observed 4AFC comparisons, averages raters’ judgments for its group data, discards ambiguous group comparisons, and fits separate models for individual raters. Its reported accuracy depends on that finite dataset and its evaluation procedure; the paper gives no population-level computational claim for the formula to continue.

A better adjacent question might use the same kind of user population to study how changing preferences or exposure changes a collective photo ranking. That could be a sensible ChoCo problem, but it would introduce a social-choice rule and an intervention objective absent from this paper. If the aim remains fitting the proposed shared predictor, the task is finite-support, weighted logistic regression. Neither version mirrors one of the paper’s results.

That is a strong case against treating this paper as a source of a continuous mirror. It does not establish the universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile continuous work related to the paper. A large platform with recurring aesthetic types is plausible; the objection is that the worthwhile computational question would be a new problem, not a continuous mirror of a result this paper contains.
