The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern whether a social choice function can be implemented, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. I therefore can’t honestly state a continuous problem per anchor or predict its complexity.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: under the paper’s normal hard-evidence setup, an SCF is fully implementable by a direct revelation mechanism in mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, for every profile of bounded utilities, if and only if it is measurable with respect to the evidence structure. Here, measurability means that states with identical evidence endowments for every agent must have the same prescribed outcome. That is a substantive implementation theorem, but it is not a computational result under the anchor requirement.

A possible population story—many stakeholders grouped by shared preferences and evidence endowments—doesn’t repair the gap. The paper’s results depend on incentives and transfers among named agents; moving to a continuum would require a new model of those incentives and a new implementation result. That may be a research direction, but presenting it as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the strongest candidate, and the proponent is right that it proves an implementation characterization, not a computational classification. The best population version would take a society of types \(t=(u_t,E_t(\cdot),c_t)\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and ask when a social choice function remains fully implementable by a direct mechanism in a continuum of stakeholders. A setting with many firms or advocacy groups sharing the same preferences and evidence access is plausible.

That version still needs a new strategic model before it becomes a mirror. The paper’s instance is a finite evidence profile over a common state; its measurability condition asks whether two states give at least one agent different evidence endowments. It does not depend on population masses. Its mechanism also singles out agent 1 to determine the outcome and uses person-by-person cross-checks and penalties. In a nonatomic society, any one person has zero mass: retaining those named roles leaves the population unaggregated, while replacing them with aggregate type reports requires new incentive and equilibrium conditions. Neither step mirrors a result the paper establishes.

Nor does Theorem 1 leave a complexity classification to continuize. With an explicit finite evidence table, checking measurability amounts to comparing state pairs and evidence endowments. A harder algorithmic question—such as minimizing transfers or finding a compact implementation—would add an objective the theorem does not study. The paper’s other implementation and contracting characterizations do not supply a named complexity result either.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor: the proposed population story cannot supply the missing complexity theorem. It is weak as the literal universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mechanism-design problem could arise from this setting. A continuum implementation theory might be worth developing, but on the evidence here it would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
