The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\), a finite catalogue \(\mathcal T\) of complete binary-XOS types \(v_t(S)=\max_{r\in R_t}|S\cap A_{t,r}|\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita item supplies \(q_j\), and \(k\ge2\), compute a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\), with \(W(x)\ge(1/2-1/k)W^*(\mu,q)\), where \(W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\) and \(W^*\) is the optimum. Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

The model it lives in

A configuration LP over integral bundle configurations \(S\subseteq[m]\), with type mass \(\mu_t\), item supply \(q_j\), and objective \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). For explicitly listed clause families, pricing reduces to \(\max_{r\in R_t}\sum_{j\in A_{t,r}}\max\{1-p_j,0\}\), enabling column generation and exact optimization.

The objection that survived

The mirror drops the paper's private sequential communication model: once the type catalogue is centrally available, Protocol 4's messages, order, and residual-item argument no longer operate, while scalable supplies require explicit multi-unit valuation choices.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the binary-XOS welfare-allocation content of Theorem E.2 through a configuration LP, but leaves aside the simultaneous-versus-interactive communication separation, the Theorem 5.1 decision lower bound, and most truthful-mechanism conclusions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite multi-unit realization and its rounding or discretization be related formally to Protocol 4 with an additive or multiplicative guarantee?
  • Can a private simultaneous-communication model for repeated types preserve any nontrivial analogue of Theorem E.2 rather than collapsing to centralized optimization?
  • Can a population-based formulation retain a meaningful analogue of Theorem 5.1 without hiding the entire obstruction inside one type description?

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest positive case is a high-multiplicity combinatorial-auction mirror. The paper already studies many bidders, so the population reinterpretation is not artificial: take a large market with \(N\) advertisers or procurement units, but only \(\tau\ll N\) complete valuation types. A type \(t\) is a binary XOS valuation \(v_t\), including its entire clause family, budgets, and other relevant data. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the market with that valuation. Item \(j\) has per-capita supply \(q_j\), meaning \(Nq_j\) interchangeable copies in an \(N\)-agent realization.

The action is not a fractional bundle. Every individual receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq[m]\); only the aggregate assignment is fractional. Formally, let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(S\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\). Welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). Thus the continuous question is a genuine population limit, with the forced co-modification of per-capita item supply, not an outcome-space relaxation.

My lead anchor is Theorem E.2, proved in this paper. It says that Protocol 4 gives a \((1/2-1/k)\)-approximation in expectation for the \(n\)-party binary-XOS allocation problem. The protocol processes players sequentially; each of the first \(n-1\) players computes a \((k,1/2)\)-sketch on the remaining items, randomly selects one of its \(k\) clauses, and receives the currently remaining items that clause values at \(1\); the last player receives what remains. The expected welfare is at least \((1/2-1/k)\) times optimal.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\[ \mathrm{BXOS\text{-}MassAllocation}_{\infty}(k): \]

given \(m\), rational type masses \(\mu\), rational per-capita supplies \(q\), and binary-XOS valuations \(v_t\), output a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\) whose welfare is at least \((1/2-1/k)\) times the optimum \(W^*(\mu,q)\). The exact version asks for \(W^*(\mu,q)\) itself.

This is a problem the authors should recognise immediately: it retains their binary-XOS valuations, integral bundles, item scarcity, welfare objective, and multi-player allocation structure. Only the named players are replaced by many repeated agents of finitely many types. The regime is plausible for standardized advertising campaigns, procurement departments, or platform users drawn from a small catalogue of demand templates.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A for explicitly represented clause families. Its configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables, but pricing for type \(t\) is polynomial:

\[ \max_{S\subseteq[m]}\left(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\right) = \max_{r\in R_t}\sum_{j\in A_{t,r}}\max\{1-p_j,0\}. \]

Thus column generation or separation yields an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the total clause-list size, and the encoding length. A basic solution has polynomial support, so it can be handed back as masses assigned to integral bundles. This is precisely the sort of continuous-optimization route the programme is intended to expose.

A second, more ambitious anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved here and restated as Theorem H.1. For every constant \(\epsilon>0\), the paper constructs a distribution over binary XOS valuations such that no simultaneous randomized protocol using less than \(e^{2Cm/9}\) communication can obtain an \(\alpha\)-approximation for the two-party BXOS decision problem with probability greater than \(1/2+2e^{-Cm/9}\), where \(\alpha=3/4-1/108+\epsilon\) and \(C=2\epsilon^2\). Equivalently, this gives the headline exponential lower bound in Theorem 1.2.

Its continuous counterpart is \(\mathrm{BXOS\text{-}MassDecision}_{\infty}\): two agencies privately represent large homogeneous subpopulations, each with a private binary-XOS type or type distribution. They simultaneously send one message to an auctioneer. Given a threshold \(X\), the auctioneer must answer YES when \(W^*(\mu,q)\ge X\), NO when \(W^*(\mu,q)<\alpha X\), and may behave arbitrarily in between. The question is whether a simultaneous protocol can achieve this with communication polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass-description length.

I would expect the private-simultaneous version to retain a Class B-style hardness phenomenon: the hidden information in Theorem 5.1 lives in the item structure and the exponentially many XOS clauses, not in the number of individually named bidders. Replacing each private bidder by a large homogeneous cohort should not itself reveal the hidden clause. The precise issue is whether the fractional mass assignment creates enough additional flexibility to destroy the paper’s gap.

That is also the weakest point. Theorem 5.1 is fundamentally a communication lower bound, not a population-multiplicity lower bound. If all type data are centralized and explicitly listed, the continuous configuration problem may be solved by the pricing argument above, and the communication separation disappears. Consequently, the secure positive case rests on Theorem E.2 and the welfare-allocation mirror; Theorem 5.1 supplies a valuable further boundary question rather than an automatic hardness transfer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors confuse the paper’s computational object—private communication among named bidders—with a population-allocation problem. That objection decisively defeats the proposed reading of Theorem 5.1, but it only weakens, rather than eliminates, the Theorem E.2 mirror.

Theorem E.2 is a sequential protocol theorem. Its guarantee depends on processing individually named bidders in an order, repeatedly recomputing a sketch on the residual set of the paper’s \(m\) single-copy items, and randomly selecting one clause for each bidder. A distribution \(\mu\) over valuation types contains neither the bidder order nor the finite-item inventory. With one copy of each item, an atomless population is degenerate: only vanishing mass can receive anything. The proposed per-capita supplies \(q_j\) repair that degeneracy, but they also introduce a multi-unit market that is not the paper’s auction. One must additionally decide whether repeated copies are perfect substitutes, whether a bundle can contain several copies, and how the XOS valuation extends to those copies. These choices are not consequences of Theorem E.2.

The proponent’s configuration LP is therefore a legitimate new typed-market problem, but its connection to the theorem is indirect. Once all clause families are centrally available, the sequential communication protocol, its randomness, and its residual-item argument have disappeared. The pricing identity

\[ \max_{S\subseteq[m]}\left(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\right) = \max_{r\in R_t}\sum_{j\in A_{t,r}}\max\{1-p_j,0\} \]

then solves a generic high-multiplicity welfare-allocation LP; it does not continuize the paper’s simultaneous-versus-interactive phenomenon. A faithful private version would retain the communication model, but then repeated agents of the same complete type can be compressed to one message, while sending messages individually makes communication scale with the population. Either way, the population mass is not the source of the theorem’s computational content.

Theorem 5.1 is more clearly non-transferable. Its hard instance has only two communicating parties. The information is hidden in a specially planted clause among exponentially many clauses inside each party’s XOS valuation. Replicating either party into a large homogeneous cohort changes none of that. If each cohort is represented by one complete XOS type, the hidden bit remains internal to the type; if the input is centralized, the planted clauses are simply part of the supplied valuation data and the simultaneous-communication obstruction vanishes.

Turning each clause into a population type does not repair this. The paper’s welfare is based on

\[ v(S)=\max_r |S\cap A_r|, \]

whereas a population of clause-types produces an aggregate sum or average. That replaces XOS valuation by a different welfare model and destroys the planted-clause construction. Reintroducing private distributions merely restores a communication problem already present with two bidders; it does not make population multiplicity computationally relevant.

There is also no useful complexity transfer from the stated lower bound under explicit encoding. The construction uses \(\ell=e^{4Cm/9}\) clauses. Its communication lower bound \(e^{2Cm/9}\) is roughly \(\sqrt{\ell}\), hence polynomial in the explicit input length \(L=\Theta(\ell m)\). With a succinct or oracle representation, the paper does not provide the corresponding pricing or representation model needed for a continuous optimization theorem.

Thus Theorem 5.1 should be rejected as a continuous-population anchor. The attack on Theorem E.2 is substantial: the proposed LP is a new multi-unit, centrally represented market rather than a population version of the paper’s protocol. But it is not fatal. Standardized repeated buyers, scalable supplies, and integral bundles assigned to fractional masses form a coherent high-multiplicity model, and its configuration-LP question is genuinely computational. The honest negative conclusion is therefore not that no worthwhile mirror exists: the decision lower bound has no population content, while the welfare-allocation mirror anchored by Theorem E.2 survives as a plausible, if substantially re-modelled, Class A problem.
