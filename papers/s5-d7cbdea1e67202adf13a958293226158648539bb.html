The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Propositions 1–4 compare the numbers or proportions of manipulable profiles for plurality and SNTV rules; Lemmas 1–5 and Propositions 5–6 characterize non-manipulable domains; Propositions 7–10 concern group manipulability. These are proved mathematical results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The cited Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is background, not a computational result of this paper.

The strongest nearby result is Proposition 9, proved here: for each \(k=2,\ldots,m-1\), there exists an anonymous tie-breaking rule under which the plurality \(k\)th rule is group manipulable at every profile with \(n\ge2\). A population version is plausible: types are complete rankings \(t\), mass \(\mu_t\) is their population share, and plurality scores are \(s_\mu(a)=\sum_{t:\,\mathrm{top}(t)=a}\mu_t\). One could ask for a minimum-mass transfer \(x_{t,t'}\) of ballots that changes the plurality \(k\)th winner and benefits every type whose members participate. A large electorate with relatively few ranking types is a credible regime for that model.

But this would be a new optimization question, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper. Proposition 9 concerns finite-profile group manipulation and an existing tie-breaking rule; it gives no complexity claim for finding a manipulation or minimizing coalition mass. The paper’s other results likewise depend on finite one-voter changes, connected domains of profiles, and IC/AIC counts. So I cannot honestly assign the requested tractability or hardness-transfer expectation to an anchor here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper does not state a computational problem whose complexity a continuous version could classify. Its propositions compare manipulability, characterize domains, or establish existence; none asks for an algorithm or gives a complexity result. That matters under ChoCo’s stated scope. A high multiplicity election with many voters sharing rankings is entirely sensible, but that fact alone does not supply the missing computational anchor.

Proposition 9 is the closest candidate, and a minimum-mass coalition that changes the plurality \(k\)th winner while benefiting every member is a plausible question. But it pulls the result in two different directions. Keep the paper’s profile-dependent anonymous tie-breaking, and a coalition can alter rankings while preserving top-choice masses; the tie-break can then change the winner with arbitrarily little mass. The proposed minimum can collapse to an unattained infimum of zero, reflecting the tie-break construction rather than a meaningful population threshold. Fix an exogenous tie-break to avoid that, and the mass-coalition problem becomes coherent, but it no longer mirrors Proposition 9’s key claim, which depends on the existence of a specially chosen tie-breaking rule. It may be a worthwhile project in its own right; the paper does not give a computational result to carry over to it.

The individual-manipulability results fit even less well. Their Nitzan–Kelly index counts finite profiles under the specified IC or AIC sampling law. A continuum of societies has no corresponding canonical measure, and an absolutely continuous prior would assign zero probability to exact score ties. Likewise, the domain results use adjacency by a one-voter change. In a nonatomic population, one voter has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate outcome; individual manipulability becomes vacuous. Replacing individuals with positive-mass coalitions avoids that problem, but changes the question.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope argument: this paper offers no named computational result for a continuous mirror to target, and its nearest candidate yields either a tie-break artifact or a new coalition-optimization problem. It is not a proof that no useful continuous study could grow from the paper. The claim that *no scenario* could be worthwhile is weaker, especially because plurality elections do have natural high-multiplicity regimes.
