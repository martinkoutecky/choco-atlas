The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), an explicitly supported rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings \(\mathcal L(C)\), target \(p\), and rational \(\beta\in[0,1]\), decide whether there is a mass-transfer vector \(x_{\rho,\sigma}\ge0\) with \(\sum_\sigma x_{\rho,\sigma}\le\mu_\rho\) and \(\sum_{\rho,\sigma}x_{\rho,\sigma}\le\beta\) such that the extended \(E_1\) rule makes \(p\) a winner; in the search version, output such an \(x\).

The model it lives in

Ranking types \(\rho\in\mathcal L(C)\) carry masses \(\mu_\rho\); bribery transfers mass \(x_{\rho,\sigma}\) between types at unit cost, producing \(\mu'\). Under the extended \(E_1\) rule, \(p\) wins iff the set of satisfying rankings placing \(p\) first has positive \(\mu'\)-mass.

The objection that survived

Because \(p\) wins whenever \(\mu(S_p)>0\), every positive budget suffices once a satisfying ranking is known, so the mass objective has no nontrivial optimization or robustness content.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only constructive bribery from Theorem 3.1(3), including its conditional search-versus-decision separation; it leaves the other manipulation, bribery, control, density, and reduction results untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a prescribed positive target mass or a nondegenerate aggregate scoring variant retain the conditional search separation?
  • Can type-dependent prices or approximation requirements add meaningful mass optimization without changing the authors' bribery problem beyond recognition?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible, narrow positive case. My lead mirror is continuous constructive bribery, anchored on Theorem 3.1(3).

Theorem 3.1(3), proved in this paper in §4.1.3, states that if \(P\neq NP\cap coNP\), then there exists an election system \(E\), whose winner problem is in \(P\), such that \(E\)-constructive-bribery decision is in \(P\), while \(E\)-constructive-bribery search is not polynomial-time computable. The decision problem asks whether, given \((C,V,p,b)\), at most \(b\) voters can have their preferences reset so that \(p\) becomes a winner; the search problem must actually output such a bribe. The paper’s proof reuses its constructed system \(E_1\), whose candidate names encode a formula from a Borodin–Demers set and whose ballots encode satisfying assignments. The auxiliary Borodin–Demers theorem is cited as Theorem 3.3 [BD76]. Corollary 3.2 further says that, under the same hypothesis, search does not polynomial-time Turing reduce to decision.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\[ \textsc{Continuous-}E_1\textsc{-Bribery}_{\infty}. \]

An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings \(\mathcal L(C)\), a designated candidate \(p\in C\), and a rational budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\). A ranking \(\rho\in\mathcal L(C)\) is a voter type; its complete type description includes its preference order and the common unit bribery price. The mass \(\mu_\rho\) is the fraction of society with that type.

A bribe is a mass-transfer vector \(x=(x_{\rho,\sigma})\), where \(x_{\rho,\sigma}\) is the mass converted from type \(\rho\) to type \(\sigma\). It must satisfy

\[ x_{\rho,\sigma}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{\sigma}x_{\rho,\sigma}\le \mu_\rho,\qquad \sum_{\rho,\sigma}x_{\rho,\sigma}\le\beta. \]

The resulting society is

\[ \mu'_\rho = \mu_\rho-\sum_{\sigma}x_{\rho,\sigma} +\sum_{\sigma}x_{\sigma,\rho}. \]

The objective is to make \(p\) a co-winner using at most \(\beta\) mass. The decision version asks whether such an \(x\) exists; the search version must output an explicit sparse rational transfer vector, or report that none exists.

The continuous extension of \(E_1\) is natural at the population level. Let \(\operatorname{puzzle}(C)\) be the formula encoded by the candidate names. If it is not in the Borodin–Demers set \(B\), there are no winners. Otherwise, each ranking \(\rho\) encodes a Boolean assignment through the same bit-extraction rule used in the paper. Candidate \(c\) is a winner precisely when the set of rankings that both encode a satisfying assignment and place \(c\) first has positive mass. For a finite empirical distribution this is exactly the original rule: positive mass means that at least one voter has that ballot.

The high-multiplicity regime is a large bloc of otherwise indistinguishable voters: for example, a campaign has access to a massive mailing list or membership group whose members share one ballot type, and can persuade a fraction of them to adopt another ranking. The hard instances can have \(\mu_{\rho_0}=1\), so arbitrarily many named voters are represented by one type. The number of occupied types is constant or very small compared with the population size; the combinatorics live in the candidate agenda and in the ranking, not in individualized voters.

I would expect this mirror to be Class B-like: the paper’s hardness survives because it is not caused by population multiplicity. Given a formula \(F\in B\), construct the candidate set encoding \(F\), put all initial mass on one arbitrary ranking, and give the briber positive budget. Any successful continuous bribe must place positive mass on a ranking whose encoded assignment satisfies \(F\) and whose top candidate is \(p\). Reading that ranking recovers a satisfying assignment. If the initial ranking already makes \(p\) win, it itself supplies the assignment. Thus a polynomial-time continuous search algorithm would contradict the same Borodin–Demers search condition used in the paper.

The continuous decision problem remains easy: test whether \(\operatorname{puzzle}(C)\in B\), and, when \(\beta>0\), use the fact that \(F\in B\) guarantees some satisfying assignment. Checking the winner of a supplied distribution is also polynomial-time. So the mirror preserves the paper’s central phenomenon: easy recognition of manipulability but hard production of the manipulation.

This covers only Theorem 3.1(3), not the paper’s entire table. It generates natural follow-up questions: whether the separation persists with type-dependent prices, whether approximate mass transfers help, and whether any analogous separation exists for standard scoring rules rather than the paper’s constructed election system.

The weakest point is precisely that \(E_1\) is artificial, and its positive-mass winner condition is deliberately discontinuous. A referee could regard this as a technically faithful lifting of the paper’s coding construction rather than a socially compelling election model. The case nevertheless survives as a legitimate ChoCo mirror because the population itself is genuinely continuous, bribery is a mass action rather than an outcome-space relaxation, and the paper’s hard information is encoded in candidates and rankings rather than in the identities or multiplicities of voters.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror does not make bribery a genuinely continuous problem. It preserves the paper’s search witness, but loses the population-level quantity that would make continuization valuable.

Let \(S_p\) be the rankings that encode a satisfying assignment and put \(p\) first. The proposed winner condition is essentially

\[ p\text{ wins} \iff \mu(S_p)>0. \]

Start with all mass on an arbitrary ranking and suppose \(F\in B\). Once a satisfying ranking \(\sigma\in S_p\) is known, transferring any amount \(0<\varepsilon\leq\beta\) to \(\sigma\) suffices. Hence the minimum bribed mass has infimum \(0\), usually with no minimizer. The budget question collapses to \(\beta=0\) versus \(\beta>0\). A continuum of voters is present only formally: an arbitrarily small sliver of mass changes the winner exactly as one discrete voter did.

This is not merely a different answer to the paper’s problem. It is a degeneracy of the continuous object. There is no meaningful cost landscape, robustness margin, approximation question, or mass trade-off. The output is still just one satisfying ranking; the transfer vector is a wrapper around that witness. Corollary 3.2 adds no independent continuous content, since the same ranking extracted from a successful transfer yields the Borodin–Demers assignment.

The obvious repair is to require a fixed positive threshold \(\delta\) of satisfying mass, or to replace E1 by an aggregate scoring rule. That removes the zero-cost pathology, but it also creates a new election system and a new bribery problem. Theorem 3.1(3) says nothing about that thresholded model. One can deliberately redesign E1 so that transferring \(\delta\) mass to a satisfying ranking remains search-hard, but then the result is a hand-built encoding of Borodin–Demers search, not a continuous consequence of the paper’s bribery theorem. The mass optimization remains trivial: the only hard step is finding the coded ranking.

The proponent is right that voter identities are not an objection: E1 is anonymous, and one can place a continuum of otherwise identical voters in a single type. That concession is also revealing. The paper’s hard instance works with one manipulable ballot, and its continuous version works with one occupied source type and one target type. High multiplicity supplies scale but no mathematical role. The difficult information is entirely in the candidate names and the ballot code, while the population variable merely records how much of a type is moved.

Thus the proposed construction is a formally valid lifting of Theorem 3.1(3), and I would not claim an airtight negative. But as a ChoCo mirror it is weak: the faithful version degenerates into support reachability, while every nondegenerate repair is a newly engineered problem whose continuous part is trivial. I would not treat this anchor as evidence of a worthwhile population-continuization programme without a further restriction requiring the winning condition and objective to depend nontrivially on aggregate mass.
