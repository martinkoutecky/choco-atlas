The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant cohorts \(\mathcal H\) and programs \(\mathcal D\), rational cohort masses \(\mu_h\) and program capacities \(\kappa_d\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and an input bound \(q\), suppose each cohort has an unknown strict ranking \(\succ_h\) of programs. In each query, an attacker chooses a strict priority order over cohorts for every program; a mass deferred-acceptance procedure returns the aggregate assignment matrix \(x\), with each cohort retaining its highest-ranked offers up to mass \(\mu_h\) and rejected mass continuing to its next proposal. Decide whether an adaptive strategy can identify every \(\succ_h\) exactly for every hidden preference profile within \(q\) queries, and determine the computational complexity in \(|\mathcal H|\), \(|\mathcal D|\), and the bit length of the rational inputs.

The model it lives in

A continuum of applicants partitioned into \(\tau\) cohorts sharing complete rankings, with mass \(\mu_h\) per cohort and scaled program capacities \(\kappa_d\). Programs are attacker-controlled proposers with chosen priorities over cohorts; the oracle returns fractional aggregate flows \(x_{h,d}\), representing integral placements of individuals. The task is exact adaptive identification of the hidden cohort rankings, including determining when aggregate outputs cannot identify them.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's centralized attack with the dishonest side proposing. It leaves the paper's other centralized results, decentralized attacks, and experiments without continuous counterparts.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can identifiability be recognized efficiently from the masses and capacities?
  • What are the minimum adaptive query count and matching lower bounds on identifiable instances?
  • When does a discrete high-multiplicity instance have the same attack complexity as its mass version?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is a cohort-level privacy attack for a large placement market. Imagine thousands of applicants choosing among a fixed set of residency programs or job tracks. Applicants fall into \(\tau\) preference cohorts, where every person in a cohort has the same complete ranking and other matching-relevant traits. Each cohort has rational mass \(\mu_h\), with \(\sum_h \mu_h=1\). Program capacities scale with the market: program \(d\) has capacity \(\kappa_d\), with \(\sum_d\kappa_d=1\). The intended regime is \(N\gg \tau+|\mathcal D|\), not a seven-person committee treated as a limit.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: when centralized Propose&Reject is used with the dishonest side proposing, there is a privacy attack that learns the entire honest preference matrix \(\Pi_H\) in \(n^2\) matching iterations. The proof first learns every honest agent’s top choice, then proceeds by induction: to reveal an agent’s next choice, the attacker places that agent last in the lists of already identified choices and first in the other attackers’ lists, forcing the desired pair to appear in the output.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Mass Privacy Recovery. There is a finite set \(\mathcal H\) of opaque applicant cohorts and a finite set \(\mathcal D\) of programs. The attacker knows the cohort masses \(\mu_h\), program capacities \(\kappa_d\), and the cohort labels, but not each cohort’s strict ranking \(\succ_h\) of programs. In each run, the attacker chooses a priority order over cohorts for each program. The matching system runs hospital-proposing deferred acceptance in mass: programs propose capacity mass to cohorts in priority order; each cohort holds the best offers under its hidden ranking, up to its mass; rejected mass continues to its next proposal. The attacker sees the resulting aggregate matching matrix \(x\), where \(x_{h,d}\) is the mass of cohort \(h\) assigned to program \(d\).

Given \((\mathcal H,\mathcal D,\mu,\kappa,q)\), ask whether there is an adaptive strategy that, from at most \(q\) such matching outputs, identifies every \(\succ_h\) exactly for every hidden preference profile consistent with the instance. A solution is the reporting strategy and a decoder; the computational question is to decide this for \(q\), with running time and query count measured separately in \(|\mathcal H|\), \(|\mathcal D|\), and the bit length of the rational masses and capacities. The problem should also report when some rankings are unidentifiable from aggregate outputs at all.

I expect a polynomial-query characterization on identifiable instances, perhaps using rational mass flows to adapt the paper’s position-by-position attack. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 1: the discrete proof targets named individuals, while the mass version sees cohort flows. Useful follow-up questions are whether identifiability can be recognized efficiently, what query lower bounds hold, and when a discrete high-multiplicity instance has the same attack complexity as its mass version. This makes a query-complexity problem out of the paper’s interaction-round result rather than claiming a standard P-versus-NP result.

The co-modification is deliberate: applicants still receive integral placements, while cohort averages and program capacities are fractional. The continuous object is the applicant population, with capacities scaling alongside it; fractional assignment records are how the matching of that population is represented. This is not continuity of outcomes in place of population continuity.

The weakest point is also the main modeling risk. The paper’s theorem recovers each person’s ranking over individual counterpart identities. This mirror recovers a shared cohort ranking over program identities from aggregate assignment flows, and aggregation may erase information that individual match announcements reveal. It also uses a capacitated placement model rather than the paper’s formal balanced one-to-one model. I still think the question is recognizable to these authors: their motivating application is residency placement, and the mirror preserves their central attack—repeatedly changing colluding-side reports to learn honest-side preferences—while putting a genuinely large, repeated market into the model. It covers Theorem 1’s centralized attack only; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s decentralized proposal-leakage results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the right anchor, and it is a genuine computational result: it gives an adaptive attack that recovers preferences within \(n^2\) matching runs. The strongest objection is that its mechanism depends on named agents at both ends. The attacker changes each dishonest agent’s ranking over named honest agents, and the observed matching identifies named pairs. The induction then uses those identities to isolate the next-ranked partner of a particular honest agent.

In a mass model, preserving those identities risks making the supposed cohorts distinguishable in exactly the way the privacy task uses. Quotienting them into cohorts instead changes the oracle: the attacker sees aggregate flows, ranks cohorts, and asks for shared rankings over programs. That may be a good problem, but Theorem 1’s proof supplies no attack or query bound for it. The proponent’s model therefore has to establish its own computational question from scratch.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. Publicly identifiable applicant cohorts with shared rankings, scaled program capacities, and aggregate matching outputs form a coherent continuous-population model. Asking for the minimum adaptive query count, or whether hidden cohort rankings are identifiable at all, is a computational active-learning problem. The fractional flows and capacities are part of the population model, and the paper’s residency and placement settings make it plausible that the authors would recognize the question.

So the negative case is weak. It shows that the discrete theorem does not carry over automatically; it does not show that a worthwhile mirror cannot be posed. I cannot honestly rule out the proponent’s cohort-level question, and it appears to survive the strongest objection.
