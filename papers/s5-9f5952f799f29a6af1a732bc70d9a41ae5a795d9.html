This is plausible for a large assembly whose rules assign the same quota to many comparable member units. For a replication with \(N_t/N\to\mu_t\), let \(\beta_t^{(N)}(q)\) be the aggregate normalized Banzhaf power of type \(t\)’s members at threshold \(q\). The continuous problem would be: given rational type masses \(\mu\), weights \(a\), and threshold interval \([0.5,0.87]\), find a threshold \(q^\star\) minimizing \(\sum_t(\Beta_t(q)-w_t)^2\); a solution is a minimizing threshold and its discrepancy. Under standard regularity conditions, the expected answer is Class A: at the 50% threshold, the high-multiplicity Banzhaf limit is proportional to voting weights, so the objective reaches zero there. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no eligible anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational result; its main finding is a calculation on the 191-member IMF. That leaves no named result to mirror under the stated rule.

The threshold-design question is still a plausible candidate, but its Banzhaf objective does not carry directly to a population distribution. Banzhaf power counts whether adding one particular player flips a coalition. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass, so that pivotal effect vanishes; at first order, random coalitions reduce to their expected type masses. The resulting decisiveness curve collapses around the \(50\%\) threshold, and individual Banzhaf indices are zero, making their normalized values undefined.

The strongest repair is to specify a sequence of replicated games, with \(N_t/N\to\mu_t\), and define type-level power through the limit of aggregate Banzhaf indices. That is coherent, but its informative part comes from finite-population fluctuations: near \(50\%\), one would need a threshold such as \(q_N=1/2+z/\sqrt N\); elsewhere, rare-coalition probabilities matter. The limit may be worth studying, but it is an asymptotic voting-power problem whose scale and random-coalition model must be supplied, rather than a computational result already established by this paper.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, and it has a real weakness: the paper’s near-oceanic example already treats large-population voting limits, which supports the plausibility of a high-multiplicity regime. I can reject the proponent’s proposed mirror as unanchored and under-specified, but I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
