Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed α, is the best worst-case ratio over all finite-support rational distributions finite?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is the total-cost clause of Theorem 10, proved in this paper. It says that with private locations, no anonymous strategyproof mechanism for \(\Gamma^\alpha\) has approximation ratio below \(\frac{(\alpha+1)(n-2)}{2}\). This is an approximation-impossibility result in mechanism design, not a conventional NP-hardness or runtime theorem; the paper does not give a standard complexity-class result.

A natural continuous mirror is Total-Cost Facility Placement with Mass Strategyproofness. A society is a finite-support rational distribution
\[ \mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}w_j\delta_{(x_j,b_j)} \]
over types \((x,b)\in[0,1]\times\{1,2\}\), where \(x\) is location and \(b\) is the publicly known preferred facility. The weights sum to \(1\). For facility locations \(y=(y_1,y_2)\), a type’s cost is
\[ c_b(x;y)=\min\{|x-y_b|,\alpha|x-y_{3-b}|\}, \]
and the social objective is the mean cost
\[ SC_\mu(y)=\int c_b(x;y)\,d\mu(x,b). \]
The benchmark is \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\min_{y\in[0,1]^2}SC_\mu(y)\).

A mechanism takes the reported distribution of locations and public preferences and returns two facility locations. Require it to be group-strategyproof against location misreports by any positive-mass coalition: no such coalition can change its reported locations so that every member strictly lowers their true cost. The question is whether a deterministic rule can guarantee one finite ratio \(R\) on all these distributions, or, more sharply, what the best worst-case ratio \(R^*(\alpha)\) is. A solution is a single rule for the whole input class, not just a facility placement for one society. The support list and its rational weights give a finite input representation.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many families at a few residential clusters choose between two public-school systems. Families at the same cluster with the same preference are one type, so the population size can be much larger than \(\tau\). In the theorem’s witness, the locations occupy only three points and the location-preference support has at most four types. Repeating each cohort many times makes the population large while keeping \(\tau\) fixed; small endpoint cohorts can still have rational mass \(1/n\).

The lower bound transfers directly. Given a mass-based rule, restrict it to distributions formed by \(n\) equal mass units. This induces an anonymous mechanism for the \(n\)-agent problem. A discrete agent’s location misreport corresponds to a coalition of mass \(1/n\) changing its report, so group strategyproofness of the mass rule implies strategyproofness of the induced rule. Dividing the paper’s total cost by \(n\) leaves its approximation ratio unchanged. Thus, on the theorem’s profiles, the continuous rule must have ratio at least \(\frac{(\alpha+1)(n-2)}{2}\). As \(n\) grows while the number of types stays bounded, no finite uniform ratio can hold over all rational mass distributions. The expected direction is hardness transfer: the obstruction already lives in a constant number of location-preference types, rather than in the number of distinct types.

The weakest point is the incentive notion. Individual truthfulness is vacuous for zero-mass agents in a continuum, so the mirror uses mass-coalition strategyproofness. That is a defensible co-modification—the paper itself studies group strategyproofness—but it is stronger than the theorem’s stated assumption. Also, the result establishes an impossibility boundary, not a useful finite-ratio algorithm. Follow-up questions include how guarantees change when only coalitions above a minimum mass can coordinate, and what tight bounds are possible when reports have a minimum mass granularity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat Theorem 10’s mirror on the modelling grounds in the brief. A rule that sees only the reported distribution is anonymous, and on a society with \(n\) equal-mass agents, one agent’s location misreport is a positive-mass deviation. So mass group-strategyproofness implies strategyproofness on those instances. The paper’s lower bound therefore carries over: the theorem’s constant-support witnesses can have arbitrarily fine rational masses, while the approximation lower bound grows with \(n\). Neither anonymity, the meaning of a type, nor the use of mean cost breaks the transfer.

The strongest case against is about the value of the resulting question for ChoCo. In the lower-bound family, endpoint agents of mass \(1/n\) force the mechanism’s behavior, while the bulk of the population sits at the middle location. As \(n\) grows, those endpoint masses vanish; the limiting society can put both facilities at the middle location and have zero cost. The growing bound therefore exposes sensitivity to arbitrarily rare types and a collapsing optimum. A project could instead ask for guarantees under a lower bound on type masses or other regularity conditions, but that would no longer test the full regime used by this mirror.

That concern is limited: the programme explicitly allows arbitrary positive rational masses, and rare groups are a plausible part of its population model. The paper also studies strategyproof mechanisms, so the mirror is recognizable as the same problem. Calling it outside ChoCo’s scope because it is mechanism synthesis rather than a pricing LP is not decisive: the paper’s named approximation bounds are computational mechanism-design results. On the brief’s criteria, the anti-case is weak. The anchor survives, and I don’t see an honest universal argument that no worthwhile mirror exists.
