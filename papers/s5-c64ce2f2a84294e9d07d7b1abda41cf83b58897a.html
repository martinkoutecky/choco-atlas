The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets the required anchor standard: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. It gives no theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here: with an odd number of agents, the only equilibrium is the one in which the median agent proposes their own peak and everyone else votes. Theorem 1 establishes the corresponding equilibrium’s existence; Proposition 2 rules out equilibria with exactly two proposers for odd \(N\); Theorem 3 extends uniqueness to even \(N\) by adding an artificial voter. These are equilibrium-characterization results, not computational results.

A possible extension would take a distribution \(\mu\) over ideal points, let each type choose a proposer or voter role, and ask whether the resulting continuum PoV game has a unique equilibrium selecting the \(\mu\)-median. But that would be an analytic continuum-agent game, not a computational mirror of a named result in this paper. There is also a substantive obstacle: in a nonatomic population, one agent’s role choice has negligible effect on the vote, so the pivotal incentives driving the finite-agent theorems may disappear. Treating whole type groups as strategic blocs could restore influence, but would change the game.

So the paper offers an interesting possible limit-game extension, but no eligible anchor from which to state the required continuous computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the paper’s results: none is a computational result. Theorem 2 is the best anchor, with Theorem 1 and Proposition 2 establishing pieces of its equilibrium characterization. Theorem 3 adds an artificial voter to restore uniqueness for even \(N\). These are all claims about equilibria of a finite strategic game; the paper states no complexity classification or algorithmic problem. The election section applies the procedure to named candidates, but adds no computational result either.

A stronger proposed mirror would take a distribution \(\mu\) over ideal points and ask whether the continuum PoV game has a unique equilibrium, or how to compute one from a rational, finite-support input. The first question is an analytic equilibrium question of the kind the programme explicitly distinguishes from computational continuization. The second needs a model in which the finite game’s pivotal incentives survive. In an atomless population, one person’s move from voter to proposer changes neither the voter mass nor the distribution of proposals; that person’s vote cannot pivot, and their proposal has zero probability of being selected. The individual role deviations driving the finite-agent results disappear.

One could restore strategic force by making whole type groups act as blocs, or by retaining designated proposer atoms alongside a continuum electorate. Those may support interesting new computational questions, but they change who can deviate and how PoV works. The same issue applies to Theorem 3: an artificial voter with finite voting power must remain an atom, while one with zero mass cannot restore parity. The election variant has an additional obstacle: candidates are named individuals, so a continuum version would need to redefine the candidate pool as well as the electorate.

I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could ever grow from PoV. A hybrid model or a study of how finite equilibria behave as populations grow could be valuable. But neither is a continuous computational mirror of a result this paper proves. That is where the negative case is strongest, and where it remains limited.
