What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the IC spread objective and its submodularity; it leaves the paper's graph-specific pruning algorithm, simulation bound, and experiments without a continuous analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the limiting spread function have the diminishing-returns property needed for a continuous greedy approximation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is narrow: type-continuous IC influence maximization, anchored on Theorem 2. The paper cites this theorem from Kempe, Kleinberg, and Tardos (2003); it does not prove it here. Its full claim is that under the independent cascade model, the expected spread \(\sigma(S)\)—the expected number of activated vertices from seed set \(S\)—is nonnegative, monotone, and submodular. In particular, for \(S\subseteq T\) and \(v\notin T\), adding \(v\) to \(S\) gives at least as much expected spread as adding it to \(T\). This is the structural fact behind the paper’s greedy seed-selection objective.

The regime would be a very large online platform with users grouped into a modest number \(\tau\) of propagation types—for example, community and activity profiles. A type fully specifies an agent’s seeding cost and transmission rates to every other type. There are \(N\) agents, with \(N\gg\tau\), and agents of each type are exchangeable under the model. To give that population a well-defined limit, co-modify the network model: each type-\(t\) agent has an independent chance \(K_{tu}/N\) of activating any particular type-\(u\) agent. Here \(K\) is a nonnegative rational interaction-rate matrix. A campaign with budget proportional to population size seeds mass \(\nu_t\) from each type, rather than choosing a fixed number of named users.

Call the resulting problem Type-Continuous IC Influence Maximization. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, the interaction rates \(K_{tu}\), a seed-mass budget \(\beta\), and an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon\). Choose \(\nu_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t\nu_t\le\beta\) to maximize final influenced mass. One precise objective is the mean-field cascade limit: initialize \(x^{(0)}=a^{(0)}=\nu\), where \(x\) is total activated mass and \(a\) is the newly activated mass in a round, and iterate

\[ a_u^{(r+1)} = (\mu_u-x_u^{(r)}) \left(1-\exp\left[-\sum_t K_{tu}a_t^{(r)}\right]\right), \qquad x_u^{(r+1)}=x_u^{(r)}+a_u^{(r+1)}. \]

Set \(\Phi_K(\nu)=\sum_u\lim_{r\to\infty}x_u^{(r)}\). The computational question is: given this encoded instance, return a rational \(\hat\nu\) such that \(\Phi_K(\hat\nu)\ge(1-1/e)\max_{\nu}\Phi_K(\nu)-\varepsilon\), with running time measured in \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\).

I expect the approximation version to be Class A. The finite-population objective is an expected IC spread function, so Theorem 2 gives a credible route to diminishing returns in the type-mass limit; continuous submodular optimization could then provide the approximation guarantee. That is a research direction, not a result already established by Theorem 2: one must prove the relevant diminishing-returns property for the limit and show that evaluating or approximating \(\Phi_K\) is efficient. Exact optimization may still be hard as \(\tau\) grows.

The paper’s prose says ordinary influence maximization is NP-hard, citing Kempe et al., but it gives no numbered theorem for that claim, so I do not use it as an anchor. I also do not claim a mirror of Theorem 5’s simulation bound or of the pruning algorithm. This mirror covers the IC spread objective and the structural theorem the greedy method uses.

I think the authors could recognize this as their problem in a specific high-multiplicity regime: the campaign still chooses whom to seed, propagation is still IC, and the objective is still expected spread. The soft spot is substantial, though: replacing a fixed realized graph by type-level random interaction rates removes the individual topology, hubs, and local structure that the paper’s algorithm exploits. An author focused specifically on placement in an arbitrary realized network could reject that as too changed. The case survives only as a mirror of the underlying optimization problem in an exchangeable, type-compressed network regime, not as a continuization of their proposed algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a weak anchor for a continuous mirror of this paper. It is a structural property of IC spread, cited from prior work; it says nothing about the complexity of finding seeds. The paper’s distinctive contribution is computational: pruning reachability searches and reusing simulations on a supplied graph. Its sample bound in Theorem 5 concerns that algorithm’s simulations, not the complexity of a continuous-population optimization problem.

There is also a real modeling gap. In a fixed graph, a seed’s marginal influence depends on which vertices it can reach and how those reachable sets overlap with other seeds’ sets. Those relationships are part of what makes the algorithm’s work graph-specific. If type means a complete description for this problem, then in a generic fixed network it must include enough of that position and structure to distinguish most vertices. The support can then grow with the population, leaving no useful type-level compression. Grouping vertices by transmission rates alone does not preserve the reachability information that drives the objective.

The proponent’s best formulation addresses this by imposing an exchangeable block-network model, with a finite interaction matrix and a seed budget proportional to population. That is coherent, and a complete block graph with IC edge probabilities could make it especially natural. But it changes the input from the paper’s arbitrary realized graph to a highly structured network model. In that setting, the mean-field spread function is the limit of finite IC spread functions; Theorem 2 supplies a route to its diminishing-returns property. The new computational question would be how to optimize type-level seed densities for that model. It might be worth studying, but it does not carry over the paper’s graph-specific algorithmic problem.

The fixed-\(k\) regime also exposes the modeling choice: seed mass tends to zero as the population grows, so normalized spread can degenerate or depend on giant-component effects that a positive-mass initialization does not represent. Scaling the budget with population avoids that issue, but selects a different campaign regime. That co-modification is allowed by the programme, so it is not a decisive objection by itself.

My negative case is therefore strongest as a challenge to the proposed anchor, not as proof that no useful mirror exists. A finite-type IC model with population-scaled seeding is plausible enough that I cannot honestly rule it out in every scenario. The case against is that it appears to be a new mean-field influence-optimization problem, while the paper’s computational contribution depends on topology that a useful high-multiplicity compression tends to remove.
