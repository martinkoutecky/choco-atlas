The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a ChoCo mirror is strongest at the programme’s entry gate: this paper proves no named computational result. The proponent raised no qualifying anchors, and the obvious candidates do not supply any. Theorem 3.1 proves a coincidence of solution concepts for PS games; Proposition 4.2 classifies the PS simple games; Proposition 4.3 characterizes the reasonable queueing games. They are structural results, not claims about complexity or algorithms. Turning one into an algorithmic question would add a computational problem the paper itself does not pose.

The best rescue is the queueing application. Large cohorts with the same waiting cost are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a continuous model could represent them by a distribution of waiting-cost types. That is evidence the regime makes sense, not an objection to it. But the paper’s result concerns equality of two solution concepts on finite coalitional games. In a continuum, adding one agent does not change coalition mass, so ordinary marginal contributions vanish; the finite Shapley formula over player orderings no longer directly applies. A density-based Shapley analogue, and a prenucleolus defined over continuum coalitions, would need new definitions. Studying their coincidence could be worthwhile cooperative game theory, but it would be a new structural question, not a computational mirror of a result here.

The simple-game application faces a related problem. A continuum version of voting power might assign each infinitesimal player zero individual power; rescaling it or using a different power measure would be an additional modelling choice. And a computational task such as recognizing PS structure or computing solution values would depend on an input representation the paper never specifies. With an explicit coalition table, recognition is a direct scan; with a compact game description, the complexity depends on that description. Neither choice turns the paper’s characterizations into a grounded complexity result.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope, but it cannot establish that no continuous extension of these games is worth studying anywhere. A continuous queueing game may be a sensible research object. The narrower conclusion is that pursuing it would require specifying and studying a new continuum solution theory, rather than charting the computational landscape of a problem this paper already establishes.
