The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition stating a computational result. Its results are experimental. The preliminary section says that computing Chamberlin–Courant and Monroe winners is NP-hard, citing earlier work ([18, 11] and [18]); it also describes a Greedy-Monroe approximation algorithm from [21]. Those are useful context, but they are not named computational results of this paper, so none qualifies as an anchor under your criteria.

The strongest candidate for a mirror, if the paper’s cited Chamberlin–Courant hardness claim is allowed as context rather than as an anchor, is continuous Chamberlin–Courant winner selection. Let \(C\) be a finite candidate set, \(k\) the committee size, and \(T\) the ranking types present in a large electorate. Give each type \(t\) rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). For Borda score \(\beta(c,t)\), find a size-\(k\) committee \(W\subseteq C\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max_{c\in W}\beta(c,t). \]
A solution is a maximizing committee. Here a type is a complete ranking, and its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated rankings induced by a stable candidate slate and a relatively small number of ideological clusters. That is a direct high-multiplicity version of a rule the paper studies, and it computes the committee underlying the paper’s representational comparison.

I would expect hardness to transfer: a finite election with \(n_t\) voters of each ranking type maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the continuous objective is the discrete Chamberlin–Courant objective divided by \(n\). The mirror could prompt follow-up questions about whether restrictions on the spatial model or on the support size \(|T|\) yield tractable cases. Its weakest point is also the reason this remains only a candidate: the paper does not present the NP-hardness as one of its own numbered results, and the proposed mirror’s computational anchor is therefore missing.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no paper-owned computational result to mirror. Its numbered sections report experiments; the NP-hardness of Chamberlin–Courant and Monroe is cited as background to explain the solvers, not established here. The paper makes no complexity, approximation, or algorithmic claim of its own. Under the proposed anchor criterion, that is decisive.

The Chamberlin–Courant mass-transfer question is well-defined, and I would not reject it because hardness might transfer or because it is a high-multiplicity version. But it asks about a cited optimization problem, not a result of this paper. It does not capture the paper’s main contribution: comparing representation outcomes under particular spatial election experiments.

A more faithful mirror would hold a candidate slate fixed, give voters a continuous distribution over positions, and measure outcomes such as average misrepresentation, \(\int \min_{c\in W} d(x,c)\,d\mu(x)\). Ranking-type masses suffice to determine winners under ordinal rules, but they discard the positions needed to compute that distance. Keeping positions can preserve the metric, but turns the problem into geometric integration over a distribution; reproducing the paper’s reported averages also means accounting for its random candidate slates and tie-breaking. That may support a useful new study, but it is not a continuous version of a computational result asserted in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about *this paper*: its CC mirror is an imported follow-up problem, not an anchor. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could grow from the paper. A high-multiplicity CC study or a continuous spatial evaluation could be valuable; the paper itself simply gives no computational result for that study to mirror.
