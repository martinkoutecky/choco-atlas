What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers approximate computation of the logit random-utility equilibrium characterized by Theorem 5; it leaves Theorem 1, the queueing interpretation, and the matching-function comparison aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a computational question about the random-utility equilibrium and the paper’s deferred-acceptance algorithm. The mirror is narrow: it does not claim that this paper first made the population continuous. It asks for the bit complexity of computing an equilibrium the paper already defines and gives a convergent algorithm for.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved here. Under Assumption 1, Algorithm 1 converges to a limit matching \(\mu\). The theorem recovers passenger and taxi waiting times from the positive parts of the unique solutions to the corresponding constrained-choice problems with capacity \(\mu\); the resulting \((\mu,\tau^\alpha,\tau^\gamma)\) is an aggregate stable matching with money burning. This is an algorithmic result, but it gives convergence, not a bound on the number of rounds or the bit cost of computing an approximate equilibrium.

Logit Money-Burning Equilibrium Approximation. An instance consists of finite passenger and taxi type sets \(X,Y\); positive rational masses \(n_x,m_y\); rational systematic utilities \(\alpha_{xy},\gamma_{xy}\); and a rational accuracy target \(0<\varepsilon<1\). Assume unit-scale i.i.d. Gumbel taste shocks, the paper’s logit example. For nonnegative waiting times, define
\[ D^P_{xy}(\tau^\alpha) = \frac{n_x e^{\alpha_{xy}-\tau^\alpha_{xy}}} {1+\sum_{y'\in Y}e^{\alpha_{xy'}-\tau^\alpha_{xy'}}}, \qquad D^T_{xy}(\tau^\gamma) = \frac{m_y e^{\gamma_{xy}-\tau^\gamma_{xy}}} {1+\sum_{x'\in X}e^{\gamma_{x'y}-\tau^\gamma_{x'y}}}. \]
The task is to output nonnegative rational matching masses \(\tilde\mu_{xy}\) and waiting times \(\tilde\tau^\alpha_{xy},\tilde\tau^\gamma_{xy}\) such that, for every \(xy\),
\[ |\tilde\mu_{xy}-D^P_{xy}(\tilde\tau^\alpha)|\le\varepsilon,\qquad |\tilde\mu_{xy}-D^T_{xy}(\tilde\tau^\gamma)|\le\varepsilon,\qquad \min\{\tilde\tau^\alpha_{xy},\tilde\tau^\gamma_{xy}\}\le\varepsilon. \]
Ask whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(|X|+|Y|\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), with certified evaluation of the exponential terms. The variables are match masses and waiting times; the goal is to find an approximate equilibrium, not to maximize a welfare objective.

A plausible regime is a large ride-hailing or service market with many requests and vehicles but a moderate number of recurring observable categories: for example, requests grouped by location and party size, and vehicles by service class. Mass records the share of each category, while people in a category draw tastes from the same specified law, as in the paper’s model. The number of agents can grow enormously while the number of categories stays modest. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the market the authors study.

I expect the approximation problem to be Class A, though Theorem 5 alone does not establish that. Its finite type-indexed equilibrium and convergent constrained-choice procedure make polynomial-time approximation a natural target for monotone or convex optimization methods. The open computational questions include a polynomial iteration bound for Algorithm 1, bit complexity for general random-utility distributions with an effective input representation, and how accurately the continuum equilibrium approximates a finite high-multiplicity market.

The mirror covers equilibrium computation in the random-utility section, especially the limit characterized by Theorem 5. It does not mirror every result in the paper: the deterministic finite-agent correspondence in Theorem 1, the stationary queueing interpretation, and the econometric matching-function comparison are outside this question.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already puts a continuum population at the center of its model, and Theorem 5 already gives an algorithm for that population. The proposed mirror therefore contributes no new continuous model. Its case rests on a narrower gap the theorem leaves open: the computational complexity of finding a certified approximate solution from finitely encoded type masses and utilities. A referee might regard that as a useful computational continuation of the paper rather than a genuinely new continuization mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 5 as a continuization mirror is that its central object is already continuous. Sections 3–4 define an equilibrium for a continuum of agents, indexed by type masses, and Theorem 5 gives a deferred acceptance algorithm that converges to it. The proposed logit question uses those same masses, utilities, demands, and equilibrium conditions. Adding rational input encodings and a bit complexity bound would be a computational follow-up to the paper, but it would not make a finite society continuous. That is a model collision, rather than an outside answer that happens to be known.

The gap is real: convergence does not bound the number of rounds or establish bit complexity. But the paper already presents the algorithmic problem of finding this continuous equilibrium. Its logit formulas even specify a concrete numerical target. The broader random utility version has a separate obstacle: arbitrary distributions \(P_x,Q_y\) lack a finite input representation, so complexity claims there need additional assumptions. Restricting to logit resolves that issue while narrowing the question to computation for the paper’s existing continuous model.

The better alternatives do not restore a continuization gap. For deterministic types, the paper aggregates finite individual matchings in Theorem 1, and its random utility section moves to a continuum; a finite-sample approximation question would study how finite random markets approach that already-defined limit. An intervention problem such as manipulation or control could be a worthwhile new ChoCo problem, but it would introduce a new computational task rather than mirror a computational result this paper proves.

This is a strong reason to exclude the proposed anchor from a programme specifically about what changes when a population is continuized. It is not a strong case that the computation is unworthy of study. The high-multiplicity market regime is credible, and a polynomial-time approximation result for the logit equilibrium could be useful. If the programme counts computational classification of an already-continuous model as a mirror, this anchor survives; I cannot honestly rule out that interpretation.
