The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational complexity result to anchor a mirror on. Its only numbered result is Theorem 2.1, proved here: when true labels are deterministic, minimizing \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\parallel P)\) is equivalent to the paper’s minimax conditional entropy problem. That is an equivalence between objectives, not a result that the underlying problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another computational class. The coordinate ascent algorithm is described as reaching a stationary point; the paper gives no named guarantee about its complexity or global optimality.

There is a plausible population-level object behind the paper. Imagine a large labeling service handling millions of recurring tasks, with workers grouped into a moderate number of cohorts that share the same confusion behavior, and items grouped into recurring task and difficulty types. Worker-type and item-type masses would be fractions of their respective populations. The variables would be \(q_b(c)\), the fraction of items of type \(b\) whose true label is \(c\), and \(p_{ab}(k\mid c)\), the probability that a worker of type \(a\) assigns label \(k\) to an item of type \(b\) with true label \(c\). One could ask for a global optimum of the paper’s minimax conditional-entropy objective, with worker and item confusion constraints aggregated by type and weighted by those masses. This retains noisy-label inference and the worker-ability/item-difficulty structure, so it is a plausible continuous-population research question.

That candidate is not an anchor-derived mirror: it is a new optimization question, and the paper establishes no expected complexity class for it. The weakest point is that the paper gives each item its own latent label distribution and difficulty parameters. Grouping items therefore needs a convincing repeated-task regime; it would not describe generic crowdsourcing with highly individualized items.

So the honest positive case is that the paper’s model suggests a sensible continuum research direction. Under the requested anchor rule, however, there are zero qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor prover question or tractability-versus-hardness verdict to report.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no anchor, so there is no in-paper computational result to defend. The only numbered result, Theorem 2.1, equates two objective formulations; it gives no complexity or global-optimization guarantee. Section 6 says coordinate ascent reaches a stationary point of a nonconvex problem. The empirical comparisons add no computational guarantee. Under the requested anchor test, the paper therefore has no qualifying mirror question.

The proposed joint worker-and-item continuum has a deeper problem: item identity is central to the task. The paper estimates a label distribution \(Q(Y_j)\) and difficulty parameters for each item \(j\). Replacing items with cohorts and estimating only \(q_b(c)\) gives the class prevalence within a cohort, not the labels of its individual items. If a type includes the item’s true label, its mass is unknown—the object being inferred. Assuming that label in advance removes the inference problem.

A stronger version would keep items individually indexed and continuize only the worker population. Large, known cohorts of workers with identical modeled behavior could be represented by rational masses, while the model still estimates each item’s label. That is plausible, and I cannot rule out its value. But the paper makes worker abilities latent and individual-specific; imposing known shared worker types is a restriction, while inferring an unknown ability mixture changes the input and the problem. In either version, the paper supplies no complexity result to extend. With known cohorts, the immediate operation is to aggregate repeated-worker counts and reweight the existing objective; the paper gives no reason to expect that this yields a new tractability result.

So the strongest negative case is that this is a poor *anchor-driven* target for ChoCo: the paper proves no computational claim whose continuous fate the programme can chart, and the proponent’s joint-cohort model loses item-level inference. The universal claim that no worthwhile worker-only mirror exists is weaker. A known-cohort, worker-continuum model is sensible; rejecting it as unworthy would go beyond what this paper establishes.
