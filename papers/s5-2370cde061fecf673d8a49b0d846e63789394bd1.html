The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\u005c) of valuation types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\u005c) summing to \(1\u005c), and a succinct normalized, monotone, \(L\u005c)-Lipschitz valuation \(v_t\u005c) for each type, find a partition of \([0,1]\u005c) into \(p\u005c) pieces using at most \((p-1)|T|\u005c) cuts such that \(|v_t(A_a)-v_t(A_b)|<1/2\u005c) for every supported type and every pair of pieces, or return a witness that an input valuation violates its stated requirements; here \(p\ge3\u005c) is prime.

The model it lives in

A population distribution \(\mu\u005c) over complete valuation types, where all members of type \(t\u005c) share \(v_t\u005c); choose a partition of the divisible resource that meets the paper’s equal-value tolerance for every positive-mass type. This is a direct cohort interpretation of its consensus-division target.

The objection that survived

Every supported type must meet the same fairness constraint regardless of its mass, so the masses can be deleted and the mirror is polynomially equivalent to ordinary finite-list consensus division. The proponent acknowledges that masses are unused but does not establish a population-dependent computational gain.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the consensus-division targets of Theorems 1.2 and 1.3. It leaves aside the chromatic-number proof and the \(\mathsf{PPA}_p\u005c) membership and limitation results for Kneser search.

Open questions for a prover

  • For \(p\ge3\u005c) and tolerance \(1/2\u005c), does the type-indexed search problem have a polynomial-time algorithm or a hardness result?
  • What is the complexity of the two-piece version at tolerance \(1\u005c), the target of Theorem 1.2?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s consensus-division search. The society consists of many agents who value a divisible resource, with agents grouped by their complete valuation type.

Formally, an instance gives a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and, for each \(t\in T\), a succinctly represented normalized, monotone, \(L\)-Lipschitz valuation \(v_t\). Given a prime \(p\) and tolerance \(\varepsilon\), \(\textsc{HM-ConDiv}^{p}_{<\varepsilon}\) asks for a partition of \([0,1]\) into \(p\) pieces using at most \((p-1)|T|\) cuts, such that
\[ |v_t(A_a)-v_t(A_b)|<\varepsilon \]
for every supported type \(t\) and every pair of pieces. As in the paper’s definition, an instance may also be answered with a witness that a supplied valuation violates its stated requirements. This is a search problem: any qualifying partition is a solution.

A plausible regime is a large city or institution dividing a shared resource among millions of people whose valuations fall into a small number of standardized profiles. Here \(N\), the number of people, is much larger than \(\tau=|T|\); each type’s mass records its share of society, and each type’s valuation describes its members completely. Requiring fairness for every type is a natural way to protect all cohorts. The masses don’t change those constraints once a type has positive mass, but they give the population its continuous, high-multiplicity description. The cut bound scales with the number of distinct types.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1.3, proved in this paper; its general form is Theorem 4.10. It states that for every prime \(p\ge 3\), there is a polynomial-time reduction from \(\KneserP^p\) with subset queries to \(\ConDiv{\tfrac12}{p}\) on normalized monotone functions. In the standard \(K^p(n,k)\) case, the source coloring uses
\[ \left\lfloor\frac{n-p(k-1)-1}{p-1}\right\rfloor \]
colors, one fewer than the chromatic-number bound. The source asks for \(p\) pairwise disjoint \(k\)-sets of the same color, or a violation of the supplied subset-query circuit. The target asks for a \(p\)-way partition with at most \((p-1)m\) cuts and value differences strictly below \(1/2\) for every one of its \(m\) valuation functions. In the mirror, set \(m=\tau\): the functions are valuation types, and their masses describe how common those types are. This makes \(\textsc{HM-ConDiv}^{p}_{<1/2}\) my strongest proposed question.

A second anchor is Theorem 1.2, also proved in the paper, with general form Theorem 4.8. It gives a polynomial-time reduction from the \(p=2\) Kneser problem with subset queries to \(\ConHalvi{1}\) on normalized monotone functions. For \(K(n,k)\), the coloring has \(n-2k+1\) colors; the search is for a monochromatic disjoint pair or a subset-query violation. The target is a partition into two pieces using at most \(m\) cuts, with value difference strictly below \(1\) for each of the \(m\) functions, again allowing a witness of an invalid function. Its mirror is \(\textsc{HM-ConHalving}_{<1}\), the \(p=2,\varepsilon=1\) case of the same type-population model. Theorem 1.3 is the stronger anchor because it gives the more demanding \(1/2\) tolerance; Theorem 1.2 supplies the notable two-piece case.

These theorems establish reductions, not hardness or polynomial-time algorithms for the mirror. They show that the authors already treat computational division with succinct monotone valuations as a problem worth studying, and a population of many agents sharing a small number of such valuations is a plausible instance regime for that problem. Existing consensus-division work supports that setting; its continuous resource is part of the task, while this mirror also makes the society a distribution over types.

My tentative expectation for both questions is Class C, at low confidence: the succinct valuation and cut search may remain computationally difficult even when the number of agents is compressed to the number of types. The paper’s reductions give no hardness result for the source Kneser search with subset queries, so they do not prove that expectation or establish a Class B transfer. They also leave open whether either mirror has a polynomial algorithm.

The soft spot is that population mass is largely bookkeeping here: equal-value constraints apply to each type regardless of its mass, so duplicate agents can be removed. That makes the mirror close to the paper’s existing finite-list consensus-division problem, and the paper itself does not analyze a weighted society. I think it remains a credible high-multiplicity instance regime because identical agents really are indistinguishable for the question being asked, but it is a modest continuization rather than evidence that population continuity changes the complexity.

The scope is the paper’s reductions to approximate consensus division, specifically Theorems 1.2 and 1.3. It does not cover the chromatic-number proofs or the \(\PPAp\)-membership results for Kneser search. Natural follow-ups are whether the type-compressed problem admits algorithms polynomial in \(\tau\), whether Theorem 1.2 can be strengthened to the ordinary Kneser search without subset queries, and whether either reduction can be paired with a hardness result for its source problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that the proposed population is only a relabeling of the paper’s existing list of valuation functions. In a type-level version of consensus division, each supported type must value every piece nearly equally. Two agents with the same valuation impose the same constraint, so replacing \(N\) agents by \(\tau\) types changes neither the feasible partitions nor the search problem. The masses \(\mu_t\) can be discarded. Conversely, any ordinary instance with \(\tau\) valuation functions becomes such a population instance by assigning those functions any positive rational masses. The two formulations are polynomially equivalent.

That applies to Theorem 1.3 and its \(p\)-way division target: the \(m\) constraints become \(\tau\) type constraints, but the theorem still concerns exactly the same consensus-division problem. The theorem is a genuine computational result, and its \(1/2\) tolerance makes it the stronger anchor. But it gives no reason to think population continuity has introduced a new computational question. The paper already treats the valuation functions as a finite list; calling them types and attaching unused masses adds no population-dependent structure.

Theorem 1.2 has the same problem. Its two-piece target is the \(\varepsilon=1\) version of that same finite-list search. It does not become a distinct high-multiplicity problem when the list is called a support: duplicate valuations still add no constraint, and their masses still do no work. The weaker tolerance does not rescue the mirror, especially since the \(p\ge3\) anchor already covers a stronger approximation.

A mass-sensitive reformulation could give the masses a role—for example, by equalizing population-average value across pieces. But that changes the guarantee: a small type could be treated unfairly, unlike in the paper’s per-function consensus requirement. The aggregate version also reduces to a single valuation function, so it is not a population-compressed form of either theorem’s target. It might be a worthwhile fair-division question in its own right, but these results do not make it their computational question.

So my negative case is that neither anchor supplies a *population* continuization: the paper’s computational target already depends on the distinct valuation functions, and high multiplicity merely removes duplicate constraints. This is a meaningful objection to spending ChoCo’s effort on the proposed mirror. It does not establish the universal claim that no useful population model could be built around consensus division; that claim is too strong. A richer objective in which population mass affects the task might be sensible, but it would need its own motivation and computational results, rather than inheriting the case for these two anchors.
