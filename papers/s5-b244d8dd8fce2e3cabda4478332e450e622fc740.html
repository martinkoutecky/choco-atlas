The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: every threshold-pooling game is balanced. Together with the cited Bondareva–Shapley equivalence in Theorem 2, this gives Corollary 2, also in this paper: every threshold-pooling game has a non-empty core. That is a substantial structural result about the underlying cost-allocation problem. But none of these results classifies a problem by computational complexity or gives an algorithm with a running-time bound. Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result, so there is no fully compliant continuous mirror to submit.

If Theorem 3 is allowed as a structural anchor, the most plausible mirror is a Threshold-Pooling Continuum Core problem. Let the types be complete operating profiles \(t=(\lambda_t,C_t,h_t,\rho_t,d_t)\): failure rate, storage capacity, holding cost, repair rate and downtime cost. The society is a rational distribution \(p\) over a small support \(T\). A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be many comparable maintenance depots or fleet-operating units, sharing a small number \(\tau=|T|\) of such profiles, with centralized pooling and cost allocation.

For a subpopulation \(\nu\le p\), define its threshold-pooling cost \(\bar c(\nu)\) as the long-run average cost per population scale in the limit of finite clone systems: the system has \(q\nu_t\) units of each type \(t\), uses the paper’s threshold-pooling operation, and its cost is divided by \(q\), as \(q\) grows through values making all counts integral. The proposed problem is: given the type distribution and operating parameters, does there exist a type-level cost allocation \(x=(x_t)_{t\in T}\) such that
\[ \sum_{t\in T}p_t x_t=\bar c(p) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\nu_t x_t\le \bar c(\nu) \ \text{for every rational }\nu\text{ with }0\le\nu\le p? \]
A solution is such an allocation. This asks the same substantive question as the paper’s core result—whether no subpopulation has an incentive to leave the pool—while making the population, rather than the list of named organizations, the continuous object.

The expected positive outcome is a non-empty continuum core, perhaps obtained by taking limits of the finite threshold-pooling cores. The corresponding algorithmic questions would be whether such an allocation can be computed and how to check the coalition constraints. These are new questions generated by the result, not results established in the paper. The proposed mirror covers the threshold-pooling core result; it does not mirror the full-pooling example or the MDP formulation as a computational-complexity result.

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 3 concerns a finite set of sovereign DoDs, while the mirror treats repeated depot-like units as coalition members. That may preserve the pooling and cost-sharing question, but it changes who can form a coalition. Also, the limit defining \(\bar c\) needs its own existence and convergence analysis. So this is a credible research direction adjacent to the paper’s main theorem, but not a clean, anchored continuous-complexity mirror of a named result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this as a ChoCo mirror is that its anchor is outside the programme’s computational target. Theorem 3 proves balancedness of a finite threshold-pooling game; Corollary 2 turns that into core non-emptiness using Theorem 2. None states the complexity of a computational problem, gives an algorithm with a running-time bound, or establishes a reduction. The paper’s MDP formulation and mention of policy iteration do not supply such a result either. So the proposed “Threshold-Pooling Continuum Core” is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The best version of the proposed population model is still coherent. Imagine many autonomous maintenance depots that can independently join or leave a pool, with repeated operating profiles. A type could include the full profile \((\lambda,C,h,\mu,d)\); scaling capacities, demand rates, repair rates, and OEM capacity with the number of depots gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime. For each finite clone system, Theorem 3 supplies a nonempty core. Averaging allocations across identical clones and taking limits could yield a type-level continuum core, assuming the relevant cost limits behave well. That defeats objections based only on the paper’s original players being a small number of sovereign DoDs.

But the proposed core-existence question is structural, not computational. Turning it into “compute a cost allocation” would add a new task: the paper does not give a finite representation of \(\bar c(\nu)\), a convergence guarantee for its defining limit, or an algorithm for finding a limiting allocation. Those could support a worthwhile project in continuum MDPs and cooperative games, but they do not make Theorem 3 a computational result or establish a complexity mirror of it.

So the negative case is strong on programme fit: reject this as an anchored continuous-complexity mirror of the paper. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous research direction exists here. The replicated-depot model is plausible, and I cannot honestly rule out valuable work on its limits or algorithms.
