The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and additive goods utilities \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}_+^m\), with one unit of each good per unit population, compute the unique optimal utility profile \(U\) and a supporting allocation \(z_t\) and price vector \(p\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t=\mathbf{1}\), \(U_t=u_t\cdot z_t\), \(\sum_a p_a=1\), and \(z_t\in\arg\max\{u_t\cdot y:p\cdot y\leq 1\}\); equivalently, solve \(\max\sum_t\mu_t\log(u_t\cdot z_t)\) over feasible allocations.

The model it lives in

A weighted high-multiplicity Fisher market: complete additive utility types \(t\in T\) carry masses \(\mu_t\), each type receives a divisible bundle \(z_t\), aggregate supply satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t=\mathbf{1}\), and the unique utility profile maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(u_t\cdot z_t)\), with supporting prices \(p\) satisfying \(\sum_ap_a=1\).

The objection that survived

The opponent's strongest point is that the weighted Fisher formulation is standard and may add little computational novelty; that bounds the contribution but does not invalidate a high-multiplicity mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the goods Competitive rule and its weighted Nash-product computation. It does not establish a computational mirror for Theorem 1's bads profile-count bounds, nor for the paper's axiomatic, multiplicity, and discontinuity results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact weighted optimum and a supporting price vector be computed with polynomial bit complexity in \(m\), \(|T|\), and the input length of \(\mu\) and \(u\)?
  • Does the analogous weighted population problem for competitive bads admit a canonical-selection or output-sensitive algorithm, and how do its profile bounds depend on \(|T|\) rather than \(n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a population-level Fisher-market mirror, with the bads version as the lead. There is one important qualification: this paper contains no named theorem proving NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), FPT, or a comparable complexity classification. Thus, under a strict computational-anchor rule, it has no fully qualifying anchor. The case below relies on accepting named optimization characterizations and explicit output-size bounds as computational anchors.

The natural regime is a large federation of departments or facilities sharing recurring goods or chores. There may be hundreds of thousands of departments, but only \(\tau\) distinct additive utility or disutility vectors: departments with the same vector are indistinguishable for the problem. Let \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of departments of type \(t\), and let \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}_+^m\) be its complete utility or disutility vector. Goods or bads remain divisible exactly as in the paper; what becomes continuous is the population. The per-capita supply of every item is normalized to one, so feasibility is \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t=\mathbf 1\). This is the appropriate rescaling of the paper’s equal-income model when the finite population is replaced by a unit-mass population.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states, for bads, that: (i) the set \(F^c(\mathcal Q)\) of competitive utility profiles is finite; (ii) in general, its size can be as high as \(2^{\min\{n,m\}}-1\) when \(n\neq m\), and \(2^{n-1}-1\) when \(n=m\); (iii) with two agents, the upper bound is \(2m-1\); and (iv) with two bads, the upper bound is \(2n-1\). The theorem is proved in Section 6.6, rather than merely cited.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Competitive-Bads Enumeration. Its input is \((T,\mu,u)\), with \(m\) bads, rational positive masses summing to one, and additive disutilities. A feasible solution consists of type-average bundles \(z_t\), prices \(p\), and type disutilities \(U_t=u_t\cdot z_t\), satisfying

\[ \sum_t\mu_tz_t=\mathbf 1,\qquad \sum_a p_a=1, \]

\[ z_t\in\arg\min\{u_t\cdot y:p\cdot y\ge 1\}, \]

and \(p_a=0\) whenever some positive-mass type is indifferent to bad \(a\), exactly as in Definition 4 of the paper. The task is to output the set of distinct type-level profiles \(U=(U_t)_{t\in T}\), with a price-and-allocation witness for each profile; equivalently, one may ask whether at least \(K\) distinct profiles exist.

This is not merely replacing fractional outcomes by fractional outcomes: the new object is the distribution of agents, while the item set and competitive rule are unchanged. If \(n_t=n\mu_t\) finite agents have the same disutility vector, aggregating their allocations preserves all relevant disutilities and market conditions. The meaningful question is whether Theorem 1’s population-dependent bounds become type-dependent bounds such as \(2^{\min\{\tau,m\}}-1\), \(2m-1\) for \(\tau=2\), and \(2\tau-1\) for \(m=2\). The expected result is tractability for fixed \(\tau\) or fixed \(m\), with an output-sensitive algorithm based on active support forests and linear systems. In the unrestricted \((\tau,m)\) setting, the exponential number of possible profiles is an explicit output barrier. I would treat this as a Class A/Class C boundary question, not as hardness transfer: the paper supplies no discrete hardness result, and the multiplicity being removed is precisely the number of repeated agents.

The second anchor is Proposition 1(i), whose goods statement is cited in the paper as the Eisenberg–Gale theorem rather than proved there: for every goods problem, the Competitive utility profile \(F^c(\mathcal Q)\) is the unique maximizer of the Nash product \(\mathcal N(U)=\prod_iU_i\) over the feasible utility set \(\Psi(\mathcal Q)\). The paper uses this to explain why competitive division of goods is easy to compute by convex optimization.

The continuous problem is Continuous Competitive-Goods Computation. Given \((T,\mu,u)\), \(m\) divisible goods, and per-capita supply one of each, find \(p\), type bundles \(z_t\), and utilities \(U_t=u_t\cdot z_t\) such that

\[ \sum_t\mu_tz_t=\mathbf 1,\qquad \sum_ap_a=1, \]

\[ z_t\in\arg\max\{u_t\cdot y:p\cdot y\le 1\}. \]

Equivalently, compute the unique optimizer of

\[ \max \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log U_t, \]

over feasible type-level utilities. This is exactly the weighted high-multiplicity form of the paper’s Nash-product problem: if \(n_t=n\mu_t\), the finite objective is \(n\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t\), which has the same optimizer. A solution is an exact competitive allocation or, in the standard computational formulation, an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal allocation and price vector with market-clearing and KKT residual at most \(\varepsilon\).

I would expect this anchor to be Class A. The type distribution makes the weighted convex program explicit, and the relevant dimension is \(m+\tau\), not the number of named departments. It also generates sensible follow-up questions: polynomial dependence on the encoding length of \(\mu\), approximation when rational masses are rounded to a finite population, and whether the bads enumeration problem admits an analogous separation or convex-optimization formulation.

The weakest point is decisive: Proposition 1 is an optimization characterization, and Theorem 1 is a structural counting theorem. Neither is a formal complexity result. A strict referee could therefore say that the paper has no admissible computational anchor, only economic and geometric statements around computation. The bads mirror also requires proving that type aggregation preserves the claimed profile-count bounds in the presence of demand ties and zero disutilities. So this is a credible continuous-population research programme—especially for high-multiplicity competitive division—but not a fully compliant ChoCo case if “anchor” is limited to explicit complexity classifications.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s case fails the programme’s strictest gate: this paper has no named computational result. Theorem 1 is an output-size bound, not a complexity classification or algorithm; Proposition 1(i) is an optimization characterization, and the paper explicitly treats its computation as the already-known Eisenberg–Gale result. Thus neither is a qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s stated standard.

Theorem 1 is also a poor population mirror even under a looser standard. Its exponential parameter \(n\) measures heterogeneous agents, not multiplicity. The lower-bound construction uses \(n-1\) agents with different single-minded disutility vectors, each attached to a different bad. Repeating those agents does not recreate the \(2^{n-1}-1\) phenomenon: the number of distinct types remains essentially \(n\). In a genuine high-multiplicity regime with fixed \(\tau\), the relevant bound would instead involve \(\tau\), and the proposed \(2^{\min\{\tau,m\}}-1\) statement is not a consequence of Theorem 1. It would be a new weighted-market theorem.

The proposed enumeration problem is therefore post hoc. The paper does not ask to enumerate competitive profiles, nor does Theorem 1 provide an enumeration algorithm, a decision problem, or a hardness result. An exponential number of possible outputs is only an output barrier; it does not establish an interesting computational landscape. The stronger repair—compute one competitive bads allocation, or a canonical selection such as the Nash-product selection—would be a sensible weighted equilibrium problem, but it would be a new problem inspired by the paper, not a computational mirror of the theorem. It may well deserve study for fixed \(\tau\), but that concession defeats the proponent’s claim that this anchor has already supplied the case.

The goods anchor is even less distinctive. With rational masses, the programme’s problem is exactly the weighted Fisher-market formulation

\[ \max \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log U_t \]

subject to the aggregate feasibility constraints. If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), this is the same objective as the finite clone market, up to multiplication by \(n\). Identical agents can be symmetrized, so the continuous model adds no new equilibrium concept or computational obstruction: it merely replaces a list of repeated rows by weights. The paper’s own cited literature already supplies the relevant convex-programming computation for the finite market.

That does not constitute the forbidden “already continuous” objection: existing high-multiplicity and Fisher-market work should be recorded as supporting evidence, not treated as a collision. But it does show why Proposition 1(i) is a weak ChoCo anchor. Its continuous version is a routine weighted restatement of an already tractable finite optimization problem, with no population-dependent phenomenon to classify. Passing to an actually continuous distribution of utility vectors would not rescue it: one would need a finite representation of the density and an efficient integration oracle, at which point the problem becomes either an analytic market model or returns to finite-support weighted Fisher markets.

There is no honest identity or multiplicity objection to the paper as a whole. Repeated departments, facilities, or recurring chore teams are plausible types, and additive utilities aggregate cleanly. The negative case is therefore narrower than “no continuous mirror exists in any scenario.” What it establishes is that the two anchors offered do not support one: one is a structural count about heterogeneity, the other is a standard convex market computation, and neither is a named computational result of the paper. The bads problem may still yield a worthwhile new mirror after being reformulated as a weighted equilibrium-search problem, so a universal negative verdict would be overstated.
