The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a common axis, a finite support \(T\) of single-peaked ranking types \(\rho_t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a nonincreasing score vector \(w\), and a nonincreasing OWA vector \(\alpha\), find a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\operatorname{OWA}_{\alpha}\bigl((w_{\operatorname{rank}_{\rho_t}(c)})_{c\in W}\bigr)\).

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a finite-support distribution over complete single-peaked ranking types on a common candidate axis. The decision is an integral size-\(k\) committee; its objective is the \(\mu\)-weighted sum of each type’s OWA utility. PAV is the approval-interval special case.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers OWA-based winner determination, including the Chamberlin–Courant and PAV cases. It leaves the egalitarian variants, Monroe’s rule, Dodgson and Kemeny questions, and the other profile restrictions discussed in the paper outside this formulation.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is PAV. Its voter model already has exactly the right additive structure: identical ballots contribute identical utility, and the paper’s algorithm depends on the ballot sets being intervals on a common candidate axis.

Take \(m\) candidates and a committee size \(k\). A type \(t\) is an approval set \(A_t\), and the society is a finite-support distribution \(\{(A_t,\mu_t)\}_{t\in T}\), where each \(\mu_t\) is a nonnegative binary rational and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Promise that the approval sets are intervals under some common axis. Given this instance, find a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{\ell=1}^{|W\cap A_t|}\alpha_\ell\), for a specified nonincreasing rational PAV weight vector \(\alpha\). The solution is the committee \(W\); for ordinary PAV, \(\alpha_\ell=1/\ell\). Mass is the fraction of members holding that ballot. When \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), multiplying the objective by \(n\) gives the usual discrete PAV objective.

The anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper: \(\alpha\)-PAV winner determination is polynomial-time solvable for single-peaked approval preferences. Ordinary harmonic-weight PAV is a special case. This is my lead. The paper’s interval structure gives the relevant tractability: its committee variables have a totally unimodular constraint matrix, and grouping identical ballots replaces voter rows with type rows. Rational masses weight the objective; they do not disturb that matrix. I therefore expect this continuous problem to be tractable in \(m\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\). The auxiliary satisfaction variables record how many approved committee members a type receives; the actual decision remains an integral committee.

A second, broader mirror rests on Theorem 13, also proved here: OWA-based rules with nonincreasing score and OWA vectors can be solved in polynomial time for single-peaked preferences. A type is now a complete weak ranking \(\rho_t\) of the candidates, with mass \(\mu_t\); the support is promised to be single-peaked on one common axis. Given a size-\(k\) committee, a score vector \(s\), and an OWA vector \(\beta\), define \(u_t(W)\) as the OWA of the scores \(s_{\operatorname{rank}_{\rho_t}(c)}\) for \(c\in W\). The continuous problem is to find \(W\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t u_t(W)\). With rationally encoded weights, I again expect tractability in \(m,\tau,L\): the masses change objective coefficients, while the paper’s total-unimodularity argument concerns the constraint matrix. This anchor covers richer ranking-based committee objectives; Theorem 10’s Chamberlin–Courant result is a special case and adds little as a separate anchor.

The regime is a large membership organization choosing a fixed-size slate, or a recommendation platform selecting a fixed-size catalogue. Candidates lie along a shared policy or product dimension. Many members have the same approval interval—or the same single-peaked ranking—and millions of members may therefore be represented by a much smaller number of types. This is a plausible high-multiplicity instance regime, not a claim that every election has few types. The committee remains discrete; the population is what becomes continuous. The authors should recognize these as their winner-determination objectives with the voters aggregated by type and their integer counts generalized to rational shares.

The paper’s numbered anchors are algorithmic, rather than hardness results. Its introduction mentions general PAV hardness by citation, but that is not a numbered hardness theorem proved here, and I would not present it as one. So these mirrors establish a tractable continuous regime; they do not show that continuum mass dissolves a discrete hardness result. They also generate a useful next question: how far can the single-peaked or interval promise be relaxed before the continuous problem loses this tractability?

My weakest point is that, for rational proportions arising from voter counts, the mirror is a weighted high-multiplicity version of a problem the paper already solves. The extension to arbitrary rational masses is mathematically clean, but it may look routine, and it does not expose a new continuum-specific algorithmic phenomenon. Still, the society itself is genuinely the distribution over ballot types, and the mirror asks the same committee question with the natural per-capita objective. That makes it a credible, if deliberately narrow, continuous analogue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that both continuous questions collapse to a routine weighted version of the paper’s existing LPs. For PAV, merge identical approval ballots into types and give each type’s utility variables objective coefficients \(\mu_t\alpha_\ell\). The constraints and their total unimodularity are unchanged. For approval intervals on an \(m\)-candidate axis, there are only \(O(m^2)\) possible types, so the mirror adds no new pricing problem or structural challenge. Theorem 7’s proof already supplies the algorithm; arbitrary rational masses make it a short corollary.

The same objection applies to Theorem 13. For each single-peaked ranking type, weight its OWA contribution by \(\mu_t\). The committee stays integral, the TU argument stays intact, and the resulting algorithm is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). If the support is large, it is part of the input; if it is moderate, repeated voters have been compressed. Chamberlin–Courant is covered as a special case. The likely contribution is a high-multiplicity reformulation and a formal encoding result, rather than new algorithmic machinery.

That is a case for low novelty, though, not a sound case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The programme explicitly treats high multiplicity as a meaningful regime, and accepts a continuous question even when its answer is already known or straightforward. Nor does this paper lack computational results: Theorems 7 and 13 are direct anchors. A large organization or recommendation platform with many members sharing interval approvals or single-peaked rankings is a plausible setting; the objectives aggregate those members’ utilities without needing their identities. The support being finite and the committee discrete are both allowed by the programme’s model.

So I cannot honestly defeat either anchor under the stated criteria. The negative case is weak at its central point: these may be routine continuous lifts of the paper’s results, but routine and already tractable do not make them invalid or unworthy mirrors.
