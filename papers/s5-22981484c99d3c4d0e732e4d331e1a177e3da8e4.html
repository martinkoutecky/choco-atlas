The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper has a natural high-multiplicity regime: it fixes \(M\) groups while every group size \(n_\lambda\) grows without bound, and voters within a group are exchangeable. A continuum version could treat each group’s plus and minus votes as fractional masses, with the de Finetti measure describing uncertainty over those group-level masses. That is a plausible population-continuization, and the paper’s two-tier voting-weight application gives it a recognizable computational destination.

But there is no valid computational anchor in the paper. Theorem 11 and Theorem 13, and Corollary 17, are proved here; they establish limit laws and the asymptotic order of expected voting margins. For example, Theorem 13 gives normal limits under fast contraction, a Gaussian convolution at critical speed, and convergence to the underlying measure under slow contraction. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The article mentions optimal voting weights as an application, but does not state a named computational result about choosing them.

So I cannot honestly write one continuous problem per anchor or predict a complexity class for those anchors: there are none. A precise optimal-weight problem could be proposed as follow-on work, but it would not mirror a computational result of this paper. The soft spot in the positive case is therefore central: the population regime is convincing, while the paper’s actual contribution is asymptotic probability, not computational social choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 11, Theorem 13, and Corollary 17 are limit theorems: they describe the distributions and expected sizes of voting margins as group sizes grow. A continuous version asking what those quantities converge to would ask the paper’s existing analytic question again. The programme’s gate is computational complexity, and these results define no algorithmic problem, finite input, or optimization objective.

The population regime itself is unusually convincing: \(M\) stays fixed, every \(n_\lambda\) grows, and voters within a group are exchangeable. For two vote types, a group’s composition can be written as masses \(p_{\lambda,+}\) and \(p_{\lambda,-}\). But those masses alone lose the distinctions the paper studies. As the de Finetti measures concentrate at zero, the unscaled compositions approach an even split; the fast, critical, and slow regimes survive through fluctuations, their scaling, and the probability law over group compositions. Keeping those features means retaining the paper’s probabilistic asymptotic model. That may be a continuous analysis, but it does not turn one of its theorems into a computational question.

The best possible rescue is the paper’s application to optimal voting weights. One could specify a finite-support rational distribution over group states, choose a voting-weight vector, and optimize a stated measure of influence or error. That could be a sensible new computational problem. But the paper defines no such optimization problem and proves no result about its complexity; it says only that its models can be used to study optimal weights. Formulating and classifying that problem would be a follow-on research direction, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s remit: the paper’s growing, exchangeable populations pass the high-multiplicity plausibility test, but none of its results supplies the computational anchor the programme is meant to continuize. The broader claim that *no* worthwhile computational follow-on exists would be too strong; the optimal-weights application leaves room for one. That possibility does not rescue a mirror of this paper’s actual results.
