What the mirror covers

The candidate concerns Gaussian accuracy evaluation corresponding to Sections 5–7; it leaves the assessment procedure, strategic properties, and implementation without computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that the paper already uses a distribution over competence as its population model. Imagine a large recurring referendum electorate with many voters but relatively few issue-specific competence classes. In the paper’s reduced form, a type is a competence level \(p_j\); its mass \(\mu_j\) is that class’s share of society. Each voter casts a sincere binary signal with correctness probability \(p_j\), and a fixed competence-dependent weight \(w(p_j)\) determines how that signal enters the collective decision. The society is \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{p_j}\), with rational masses and \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of voters. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. It also matches the paper’s reduced-form analysis more closely than a proposed model of the full peer-authored assessment procedure would.

A concrete follow-on problem would be ESCM\(_\infty\) Gaussian Accuracy Evaluation. Given that finite-support society, a fixed weighting rule \(w\), population scale \(n\), and precision \(\epsilon\), compute an \(\epsilon\)-additive approximation to the rule’s Gaussian-approximation probability of choosing the true state, and its gain over equal weighting. In particular, compute the moments
\[ \mu_w=\sum_j\mu_j w(p_j)(2p_j-1) \]
and
\[ \sigma_w^2 =\sum_j\mu_j w(p_j)^2\bigl(1-(2p_j-1)^2\bigr) +\operatorname{Var}_{\mu}\!\bigl(w(p)(2p-1)\bigr), \]
then evaluate \(\Phi(\sqrt n\,\mu_w/\sigma_w)\). The action is the collective binary decision induced by the weighted signals; the objective is its approximate probability of being correct. With fixed rule specifications and finite support, I would expect this evaluation problem to be tractable by direct computation in \(\tau\) and the requested precision. It covers the reduced-form accuracy analysis in Sections 5–7, not the psychometric validity, strategic manipulation, or implementation of the assessment process.

The paper supplies analytic motivation for that question. Proposition 4 proves the moment scaling and central limit approximation for bounded competence-dependent weights under Assumption 3. Corollary 5 gives the resulting Gaussian approximation \(\Phi(\sqrt n\,\mu_T/\sigma_T)\), determined by the induced moments. Proposition 6 proves that, when weights are bounded, non-decreasing, non-constant on the competence support, and normalized to have mean one, they weakly improve mean signed signal over equal weighting, with strict improvement when competence has positive variance and equality in the degenerate case. These are proved here, but they are analytical results, not computational-complexity results.

That distinction is decisive under the requested anchor rule: the paper contains no qualifying named computational anchor. Propositions 4 and 6 and Corollary 5 do not classify a computational problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise. The closest statement is Remark 9, which says that the mechanism’s main stages are computationally tractable in population size under standard implementations, with review, assessment, and aggregation scaling linearly in \(n\). It is a prose remark, not one of the permitted Theorem/Lemma/Corollary/Proposition anchors, and it gives no complexity landscape in \(\tau\), input bit length, or other parameters. I therefore would not present it as an anchor.

My lead candidate is the accuracy-evaluation problem above, and I expect it to be tractable. Its strength is that a distribution over voter types is already native to the paper’s analysis. Its weakness is that it turns an analytic formula into a computation that appears quite direct; it does not reveal a discrete hard problem whose hardness might dissolve under continuization. More ambitious questions—such as optimizing the admissible weight map for Gaussian accuracy or computing exact finite-population accuracy—would be natural extensions, but the paper does not establish results that anchor them. So this is a plausible computational follow-on, not a fully compliant positive case resting on one of the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 4 proves a central limit approximation, Corollary 5 gives its Gaussian accuracy formula, and Proposition 6 proves a covariance inequality for mean signal. They are analytic results, not complexity claims or algorithmic classifications. Remark 9’s statement that implementation scales linearly in \(n\) is a prose observation, not a result about complexity in support size, input length, or other parameters.

The proponent’s finite-support society is sensible: a large electorate divided into competence classes is a credible high-multiplicity regime. But for the proposed task, given \(\mu_j\), \(p_j\), and a fixed, efficiently evaluable \(w\), the moments are just sums over the \(\tau\) types, followed by evaluation of a standard normal CDF. Proposition 4 and Corollary 5 already supply the formula; Proposition 6 supplies a direct covariance argument. There is no substantive optimization or search problem here. Calling the evaluation of those expressions a computational mirror would make almost any explicit analytic formula qualify.

A stronger candidate would be exact finite-population accuracy for high-multiplicity competence classes, or the minimum mass of votes that must be flipped to change the decision. The first could become an interesting algorithmic problem about weighted sums of Bernoulli variables; the second, with fixed weights and type-level flip costs, is a threshold optimization over types. But neither is established by a computational result in this paper. Exact finite-population accuracy also retains the finite-\(n\) sampling noise behind the CLT approximation; in a literal atomless population with independent idiosyncratic signals, that noise averages away. These are plausible new projects, not mirrors of the paper’s named results. The paper’s own future-work discussion of finite-sample performance and coalition robustness motivates them, but does not turn them into anchors.

So the negative case is strong under the requested anchor standard: the proposed accuracy evaluation is well-posed but too direct, and the paper offers no computational result to support a richer mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim about every adjacent research direction. The competence-class regime itself is plausible, and a finite-sample algorithmic study might be worthwhile; I would reject that as a mirror of this paper’s results, not claim that it could never merit the programme’s time.
