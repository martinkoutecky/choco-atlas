The continuous mirror question

Given a baseline society \(\mu\) of unit mass over permitted complete or truncated rankings, a fixed target STV structure \(S=(f,\sigma)\), a seat count \(k\), and the paper’s tie-breaking and fractional Weighted Inclusive Gregory transfer rule, choose added ranking mass \(x\geq 0\) to minimize \(\\|x\\|_1\), subject to \(\mu+x\) producing exactly \(S\). The total population is \(1+\\|x\\|_1\), with quota \(Q=(1+\\|x\\|_1)/(k+1)\). Compute the infimum and an attaining or arbitrarily close feasible campaign, explicitly listing its positive-mass ballot types.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over ballot types, with mass additions \(x\) over permitted rankings and unit cost per added mass. The paper’s STV transfers run on \(\mu+x\), using the population-scaled quota \(Q=(1+\\|x\\|_1)/(k+1)\); the objective is minimum added mass subject to reaching the specified structure.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers optimal additions for a fixed order and round sequence, plus the guaranteed candidate-removal preprocessing of Theorem 3.2. It leaves aside the search across all structures, coalition results, strategic voting, and the empirical bootstrap analysis.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the minimum campaign mass attained, and can the optimal mass vector be represented with polynomially many ballot types?
  • Can the fixed-structure optimum be computed in time polynomial in the number of candidates, support size, and input bit length despite quota-dependent fractional transfers?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a defensible positive case for a mirror of the paper’s campaigning algorithm, with a deliberately limited scope. The paper’s related-work section mentions NP-completeness results proved elsewhere, but it gives no numbered in-paper hardness theorem to anchor here. Its strongest usable anchors are therefore algorithmic. The supplied TeX leaves the theorem references unresolved; from the section order, the two anchors are Theorem 3.1, thm: poly_efforts_B, and Theorem 3.2, thm: remove_irrelevant_candidates. Both are proved here.

The natural regime is a large RCV election in which many voters share a relatively small number of ballot types: for example, a city election with hundreds of thousands of voters and recurring full or truncated rankings shaped by party slates and endorsements. A type is a complete or truncated ranking; its mass is the fraction of the initial electorate with that ballot. The input society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) rankings, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of voters. Campaign action is new voter mass assigned across possible rankings, and its cost is the total mass added—matching the paper’s unit cost per added vote. The paper itself allows arbitrary ranked additions, so this does not change its action model.

My lead is Fixed-Structure Mass Campaigning for STV, anchored on Theorem 3.1. The theorem says: given a budget \(B\), if a target structure is reachable, an optimal strategic addition to reach that specified order-and-sequence pair can be computed in \(O(mn)\) time, where \(n\) is the number of candidates and \(m\) is the number of unique ballots. This result is proved in the paper. It concerns reaching a fixed structure; it does not claim that searching over every possible structure is polynomial.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Fix the candidates, number of seats \(k\), tie-breaking order, a baseline distribution \(\mu\) over all permitted ballot types, and an admissible target structure \(S=(f,\sigma)\), where \(f\) is the final candidate order and \(\sigma\) records each round’s win or elimination. Choose a nonnegative mass vector \(x\) over all permitted rankings, including rankings outside \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\). Run the paper’s fractional Weighted Inclusive Gregory transfer rule on \(\mu+x\), with total electorate mass \(1+\|x\|_1\) and the continuum quota \(Q=(1+\|x\|_1)/(k+1)\), the per-capita form of the Droop quota. Find the infimum of \(\|x\|_1\) among additions producing exactly \(S\), and return an attaining campaign distribution when one exists, or arbitrarily close feasible campaigns when the infimum is not attained. The output should list the ballot types receiving positive mass, so an algorithm cannot hide an exponential-size campaign behind an implicit vector.

I expect this fixed-structure problem to be Class A, given the paper’s sequential slack-allocation method: the continuous version can allocate mass directly instead of choosing individual voters. That expectation needs proof. The quota changes with total turnout, and Gregory surplus transfers depend on proportions of vote paths, so the constraints are not simply an LP. A result polynomial in the number of candidates, input support \(\tau\), and rational bit length \(L\) would have to handle those transfers and give a compact campaign description. This mirror does not claim tractability for the paper’s broader search over all orders and sequences; that search may retain substantial candidate-driven combinatorics.

A second, distinct mirror follows Theorem 3.2, thm: remove_irrelevant_candidates, also proved here. It states that with \(B\) added undescribed ranked-choice votes, the election can be shortened by removing candidates guaranteed to be eliminated first regardless of those additions; the procedure takes \(O(mn^4)\) time. The corresponding continuous problem takes a distribution \(\mu\), a fixed added mass \(b\), and the STV rule. It must return a safely removable bottom group \(R\) such that, for every mass allocation \(x\) over ballot types with \(\|x\|_1=b\), the candidates in \(R\) are eliminated before any round win. It also returns the contracted profile obtained by deleting \(R\) from each ballot, preserving the order and sequence among the remaining candidates for every such \(x\). I expect Class A here too: the paper’s support bounds appear to extend to mass inequalities, though the prover must account for the quota scaling with turnout.

These mirrors cover the paper’s exact strategy computation for a specified RCV structure and its exact candidate-removal preprocessing. They do not mirror the paper’s coalition claims or treat bootstrapping as population continuization; bootstrapping is uncertainty over profiles, not a continuous society.

The weakest point is that the source theorems use integer additions and the paper’s quota includes a unit term. The continuous version replaces that term with the per-capita quota and allows fractional campaign mass. Also, the paper’s 801-person poll is not itself a convincing high-multiplicity regime. The case rests on the separate, plausible large-election scenario and on preserving the paper’s own arbitrary-ballot addition model. The authors should recognize the first mirror as the continuous version of their fixed-structure campaigning problem; a useful next question is whether minimizing mass to place a named candidate in the top \(k\), while searching across structures, remains tractable or becomes hard. Another is how the continuous optimum compares with the least integer number of added voters in finite elections.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about the promised payoff, not whether a continuous population makes sense. A large election with many voters sharing a relatively small number of rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s strategies depend on ballot totals, not on voter identities, and its fractional Gregory transfers do not make the population continuous. So neither identity nor “fractional votes already exist” defeats the mirror.

Theorem 3.1 is a real computational result: for a fixed order-and-sequence structure, it finds an optimal integer-voter addition in polynomial time. But the proposed continuous version changes the optimization in a consequential way. With added mass \(x\), the natural quota is \(Q=(1+\|x\|_1)/(k+1)\); Gregory transfers then depend on vote ratios that themselves change with \(x\). The paper’s appendix explicitly shows nonlinear structure constraints. Its sequential slack-filling argument uses integer corrections and a search over integer budgets, so it does not establish a polynomial mass-allocation algorithm. Searching for the cheapest mass campaign to put a candidate in the top \(k\) would add the paper’s full search over structures as well. The proponent’s Class A expectation is therefore unsupported.

That is a serious limitation on the proposed result, but not a reason to reject the question. Minimizing campaign mass for a fixed structure, or for a candidate’s top-\(k\) finish, remains a well-posed continuous problem the authors would likely recognize. Nonlinearity could make it hard, but this programme treats continuum-specific hardness as a result, not a disqualification. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor on the grounds available.

Theorem 3.2 has the same gap between the discrete proof and the Class A prediction. Its safe-removal procedure is proved for a bounded number of added voters; a mass version must account for a quota that scales with turnout and transfers that depend on changing vote totals. Still, the stronger proposed question—identify candidates guaranteed to lose before any win under every campaign of mass \(b\)—is coherent and useful. The paper already reasons about arbitrary ranked additions, and its support comparisons may extend to mass inequalities. This is a preprocessing result rather than a direct strategy optimizer, but that does not make it an unworthy computational mirror.

So the best negative case is that neither theorem’s proof transfers automatically, and the paper offers no basis for calling either continuous version polynomial-time. But that only rebuts the proponent’s algorithmic expectations. It does not show that either continuous question lacks value. I do not see an honest universal objection here; both anchors survive as plausible mirrors, with the continuous complexity still open.
