What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; the paper's finite implementations of marginal vectors, hierarchical outcomes, the average tree solution, and weighted hierarchical outcomes remain outside one.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper (the average tree solution it implements was introduced by Herings et al.). It says that for every finite tree game \((N,v,L)\) whose TU game is zero-monotonic, every subgame-perfect equilibrium of Mechanism B gives each player their average hierarchical outcome,
\[ \mathrm{AHO}(N,v,L)=\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{i\in N}h^i(N,v,L). \]
Mechanism B has players bid to become the first proposer, then uses graph-constrained proposals and acceptance. The theorem establishes an equilibrium implementation result, not a complexity classification.

The other numbered results are similar: Theorem 3.1 implements a marginal vector for a given ordering; Theorem 3.2 implements a hierarchical outcome on a tree; Theorem 4.4 extends the result to weighted hierarchical outcomes. They are proved here, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. The paper also gives no encoding that it then computes with. So I do not think any is an eligible ChoCo anchor.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting nearby: a hub-and-spoke organization with many interchangeable agents at the leaves. If their worths and bargaining roles are identical, the population can grow while the number of types stays small; mass would represent the fraction of leaves of each type. One could study the limiting average hierarchical allocation or ask whether a continuum bargaining mechanism implements it. But the theorem’s mechanism relies on individual bids, a selected individual proposer, and a finite sequence of proposal rounds. In an atomless population, a single agent has zero mass, so the bidding incentives and proposer selection need a new account. That would be a new strategic implementation question, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

So my best honest case is that the paper suggests a potentially sensible population-limit question, especially for repeated tree positions. It supplies no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror, and I cannot state a qualifying continuous problem per anchor or assign it a ChoCo complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.2 is the only serious anchor, and it does not state a computational result. It proves that a particular finite mechanism has SPE payoffs equal to \(\mathrm{AHO}(N,v,L)\). Theorem 3.1, Theorem 3.2, and Theorem 4.4 have the same form: they implement a marginal vector, a hierarchical outcome, or a weighted variant. None specifies an algorithmic input, an encoding, or a complexity question. In the programme’s terms, there is no computational result here for a continuous mirror to classify.

The best stronger proposal would be to study computation of the AHO for high-multiplicity tree games. Repeated identical branches or interchangeable leaves are plausible settings; treating their population by mass is not inherently objectionable. But the paper allows an arbitrary characteristic function \(v:2^N\to\mathbb R\). Its allocations depend on worths of particular connected coalitions and their subtrees. The paper supplies no rule for extending those worths from integer coalitions to fractional masses. A mass-based input could be built—for example, by imposing a symmetric, density-dependent form on \(v\)—but that adds assumptions that determine the new problem. A well-posed model could result; it would not be a high-multiplicity relaxation already latent in Theorem 4.2.

The proposed continuum implementation has a more direct mismatch. In Mechanism B, individual players bid against one another, one player wins and proposes, and rejected proposals remove players so the remaining graph splits into subtrees. With an atomless mass of interchangeable leaves, an individual has zero mass, so those bidding, selection, and removal steps need new definitions. A game where blocks of mass bargain or leave partially might be a sensible research question, but its action space and equilibrium concept would have to be designed anew. That is a strategic or analytic continuum-game question, not the computational question the theorem answers.

This is not a claim that no worthwhile nearby model could ever be made. A repeated-branch or continuum bargaining paper might be valuable. The negative case is that the paper gives no natural mass-valued computational problem to mirror: the most plausible route either changes the strategic implementation into a new continuum game or adds a new representation of coalition worths to create an allocation-computation problem. That is the case’s weak point, since those choices could still produce a worthwhile project; they just do not establish a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s named results.
