The continuous mirror question

Given a finite adjacency graph \(G=(B,E)\), rational masses \(\mu_{b,p}\ge 0\) over location-party types \((b,p)\) with \(\sum_{b,p}\mu_{b,p}=1\), \(K\) connected districts, seat counts \(N_1,\ldots,N_K\) summing to \(N\), and a population-balance tolerance, choose a partition of \(B\) into connected districts satisfying the balance constraints to maximize the total number of \(R\) seats. For district \(D_k\), let \(y_{R,k}=\frac{\sum_{b\in D_k}\mu_{b,R}}{\sum_{b\in D_k,p}\mu_{b,p}}\), and let \(n_{R,k}\) be the tie-broken PAV maximizer of \(y_{R,k}H_\ell+(1-y_{R,k})H_{N_k-\ell}\) over \(0\le \ell\le N_k\), where \(H_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}1/i\); output the optimal value \(N^{-1}\sum_k n_{R,k}\) and a maximizing map.

The model it lives in

Types are location-party pairs \((b,p)\) with solid-coalition rankings, and masses are \(\mu_{b,p}\). Binary variables \(z_{bk}\in\{0,1\}\) assign indivisible geographic blocks to connected districts; district party fractions and PAV seat counts are derived from the mass, and the objective maximizes \(\sum_k n_{R,k}\) subject to connectivity and population balance.

The mirror covers the solid-coalition inter-party PAV/STV map-selection framework and its proportionality and gerrymandering objectives; it leaves the noisy cross-party simulations, intra-party diversity experiments, and empirical plots without separate mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is real but narrow: the paper has no named theorem proving NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), FPT, or a similar complexity classification. Its best computational anchor is instead a named structural result that the paper actually uses inside its map-optimization pipeline.

The lead anchor is Proposition 1 (Seat shares under STV), proved in this paper (although its STV component is identified as a known fact from Tideman and Dummett, and the paper supplies a self-contained proof). It states that, for a district with \(M\) seats and \(V\ge M(M+1)\) voters, with party-\(R\) fraction \(y_R\), at least \(M\) candidates from each party, all voters ranking every same-party candidate above every opposite-party candidate, and ties broken in favour of \(D\), both the number of \(R\) winners under STV and under PAV belong to \(\{\lfloor y_RM\rfloor,\lceil y_RM\rceil\}\). The paper’s Appendix C also proves Lemma C.1, which gives the sharper PAV formula: if \(\lambda_{\mathrm{PAV}}(i)=1/i\), then \(n_R(y_R,\lambda_{\mathrm{PAV}})\) is the unique integer \(\ell\) satisfying \(y_R(M+1)-1\le \ell<y_R(M+1)\).

My mirror would be Continuous PAV Gerrymandering.

An instance contains a finite geographic adjacency graph \(G=(B,E)\) of atomic blocks, a rational population distribution \(\mu=(\mu_{b,R},\mu_{b,D})_{b\in B}\) with \(\sum_{b,p}\mu_{b,p}=1\), a number \(K\) of connected districts, seat counts \(N_1,\ldots,N_K\) summing to \(N\), a population-balance tolerance, and a target party \(R\). A type is \((b,p)\): location \(b\), party \(p\), and the paper’s solid-coalition ballot behaviour. Thus the society has \(\tau=2|B|\) types, while the number of voters represented by those types may be millions.

A feasible solution is a partition \(\Pi=(D_1,\ldots,D_K)\) of the blocks into connected, population-balanced districts. In district \(D_k\), let \(y_{R,k}\) be the \(R\)-mass fraction. The number of \(R\) seats is the integer \(\ell_k\) determined by Lemma C.1, equivalently by maximizing the PAV score \(y_{R,k}H_{\ell_k}+(1-y_{R,k})H_{N_k-\ell_k}\), with ties broken against \(R\). The objective is to maximize \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_k\ell_k\); the fair-redistricting variant minimizes \(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_k\ell_k-\mu_R\right|\). A solution is the map together with its integral seat vector.

This is a genuine population continuization. The seats, committees, candidate identities, district connectivity, and district assignment remain discrete. Only the electorate is represented by mass. A rational distribution \(\mu\) can be converted back into a finite electorate by clearing denominators, while a large finite electorate over repeated block-party types normalizes directly to \(\mu\). The paper’s own use of tract-level party fractions is therefore evidence that this regime is substantively natural, not an artificial relaxation.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. It preserves their graph, contiguity, population balance, multi-member districts, PAV/STV rules, proportionality objective, and partisan-gerrymandering objective. It merely replaces an individually enumerated voter file by the high-multiplicity object that their Proposition 1 already shows is sufficient for party-seat computation. Proposition 1 also gives the corresponding continuous STV mirror: replace PAV by fractional STV on party-solid mass ballots, using the continuum quota, and compute each district’s seat count from its party mass fraction.

For a fixed map, this is Class A: Proposition 1 and Lemma C.1 reduce winner computation to aggregating type masses and evaluating a closed-form integer rule. For the full map-selection problem, I would expect Class B rather than a continuum-specific collapse. The combinatorics live in connected geographic partitioning, not in the number of individually named voters; rational-mass finite instances can reproduce the same block-level data. A fractional spatial relaxation that permits every block’s population to be split across districts might become an LP, but that would be a further relaxation and is not the lead mirror.

The main weakness is also clear: Proposition 1 is not a formal complexity theorem. Under a strict ChoCo anchor rule, this paper may therefore fail the computational-anchor gate despite offering a very plausible continuous problem. There is also a collision risk because the authors already compute with vote shares. The defence is that they use those shares as an empirical shortcut inside a finite-voter study, whereas the proposed problem makes \(\mu\) the formal society object and asks for exact algorithmic results as a function of type count, district parameters, and encoding length.

The natural follow-up questions are whether continuous PAV/STV gerrymandering admits approximation or parameterized algorithms, whether geographic column generation helps, how much finite-population rounding costs, and what happens when the solid-coalition assumption is replaced by a genuine distribution over complete rankings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s case fails the programme’s primary gate: Proposition 1 and Lemma C.1 are not computational-complexity results. They are structural outcome formulas. Proposition 1 already takes \(y_R\) as a fractional parameter, and Lemma C.1 gives a closed-form rule for evaluating one district. Neither states an optimization problem, a complexity classification, or an algorithmic theorem of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. The paper’s actual computational contribution is an empirical SHP/dynamic-programming/IP pipeline, not a named result about exact, approximation, or parameterized complexity.

The proposed “Continuous PAV Gerrymandering” does not repair this. Under the paper’s solid-coalition assumptions, every voter’s relevant information is already just their atomic block and party. If \(n_{b,p}\) is the number of voters of party \(p\) in block \(b\), the proposed distribution is merely

\[ \mu_{b,p}=\frac{n_{b,p}}{\sum_{b',p'}n_{b',p'}}. \]

The paper’s map-generation procedure already operates on block populations and block-level party vote shares; individual voter identities disappear before optimization begins. Normalizing those counts therefore changes notation and encoding, not the society or the computational problem. Clearing denominators recovers the exact finite instance. This is not a new high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s problem; it is the paper’s existing weighted graph-partitioning model written probabilistically.

The STV version is even less of a mirror. For one district, the continuous question is answered immediately by the same floor/ceiling statement in Proposition 1. For several districts, the interesting problem is selecting connected, population-balanced blocks. That is a discrete geographic partitioning problem, and it is not supplied by Proposition 1. Calling it a continuous mirror silently replaces a named local seat-count lemma with a new global optimization problem. Such a problem might be worth studying, but it is not a computational result of this paper being continuized.

A stronger rescue would distribute complete rankings, partisan scores, or random-utility types over locations. That could define a legitimate high-multiplicity redistricting model, but it no longer mirrors Proposition 1. It instead rebuilds Sections 4 and B, whose findings are simulation methodology and empirical plots rather than named computational results. Moreover, the paper’s candidate pools and rankings depend on the map, eligible tracts, candidate subsampling, and random realizations; fixing a type space in advance would require a substantive new model rather than a harmless population replacement.

The honest concession is that population mass is meaningful here: large groups of voters in the same tract and party are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Thus I cannot honestly claim that every continuous formulation would be pointless. The strongest negative case is narrower but decisive for this paper: there is no qualifying computational anchor, and the proposed mirror merely formalizes an aggregate representation the paper already uses. If ChoCo permits entirely new complexity questions motivated by an empirical pipeline, this paper may still support one; under the stated anchor standard, however, it does not.
