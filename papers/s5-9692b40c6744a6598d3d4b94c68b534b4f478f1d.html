The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s sPJR rank-aggregation problem, anchored on the theorem establishing the Flow-adjusting Borda rule. It is a plausible computational problem, but it is not fully admissible under ChoCo’s anchor rule.

Take \(m\) candidates and voter types \(T=\mathcal R\), where each type is a complete ranking. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\); \(\mu(\succ)\) is the fraction of a very large electorate reporting \(\succ\). Agents of one type are identical in their ranking, budget, and contribution. The decision variable is an output ranking \(\rhd\). Write \(A(\rhd)\) for its \({m\choose 2}\) ordered pairwise comparisons.

The continuous problem is:

\[ \textsc{Continuous-sPJR Rank Aggregation} \]

Given \(m\) and rational \(\mu\), output a ranking \(\rhd\) such that, for every subdistribution \(S\leq\mu\),

\[ \left|A(\rhd)\cap \bigcup_{\succ:S(\succ)>0} A(\succ)\right| \geq \left\lfloor |S|{m\choose 2}\right\rfloor . \]

Equivalently, every population group receives at least its proportional share of the pairwise comparisons represented in the output. A solution may also include the paper’s payment certificate witnessing pair-priceability.

This is a natural regime: millions of voters, benchmark evaluators, departmental reviewers, or recommendation users may fall into a relatively small number \(K\) of repeated ranking types, with \(\mu(\succ)=n_\succ/n\). The population is continuous, but rankings and the output remain integral. Thus this is genuinely population continuization, not divisible candidates or probabilistic rankings.

The strongest anchor would be the paper’s Theorem 3, “The Flow-adjusting Borda rule is pair-priceable,” proved in the paper. Its content is that, for every profile \(R\), the ranking \(\rhd=\fb(R)\) admits payments \(\pi\) from ranking types to output pairs satisfying four conditions: a type pays only for pairs it ranks in the same direction; its total payment is at most \({m\choose2}R(\succ)\); every output pair receives total payment at most \(1\); and total payment exceeds \({m\choose2}-1\). The paper then proves that pair-priceability implies sPJR. Operationally, \(\fb\) constructs \(\rhd\) by repeatedly choosing a Borda winner and computing a maximum flow over the ranking types and remaining candidates.

Under the broader interpretation that an algorithmic theorem may serve as an anchor, this gives a credible Class A mirror. With an explicitly listed support of \(K\) ranking types, each round requires Borda-score computation and a maximum-flow computation, so the natural implementation is polynomial in \(m\), \(K\), and the input bit length. Further questions include exact complexity under implicit type spaces, approximation when only sampled access to \(\mu\) is available, and the minimum population mass whose removal makes the output fail sPJR.

The serious weakness is formal: Theorem 3 is not a named complexity result. It asserts a structural fairness property of a rule, not that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. More importantly, the paper already defines a ranking profile \(R:\mathcal R\to[0,1]\) and explicitly interprets \(R(\succ)\) as a share of voters. Hence the proposed \(\mu\)-model is mathematically already the paper’s model. The continuous mirror would largely rename \(R\) as a population distribution.

So the best honest conclusion is conditional: the paper contains an excellent subject-matter candidate for a continuous-population computational study, with \(\fb\) as a natural tractable algorithmic template, but it does not supply a qualifying computational anchor and its central formal model is already continuous in the relevant sense. I would not present this as a strong ChoCo-positive case without relaxing the anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor fails at the programme’s gate before any substantive complexity question arises. Theorem 3 is a structural fairness theorem—“\(\fb\) is pair-priceable”—not a named theorem about worst-case complexity, approximation, parameterized complexity, or even the computational complexity of a decision problem. The appearance of maximum flow makes an implementation plausibly polynomial, but that is an immediate algorithmic observation, not the paper’s computational result.

More decisively, the proposed mirror is already the paper’s model. The paper defines a profile \(R:\mathcal R\to[0,1]\), with \(R(\succ)\) the share of voters reporting ranking \(\succ\), and defines subprofiles \(S\leq R\). Its sPJR condition is already exactly the proposed condition: for every subprofile \(S\), the output must satisfy \(\left|A(\rhd)\cap\bigcup_{\succ:S(\succ)>0}A(\succ)\right|\geq\lfloor |S|{m\choose2}\rfloor\). Renaming \(R\) as \(\mu\) does not continuize the population; it reproduces the continuous-population formulation already used in the paper.

The same is true of the alleged algorithmic contribution. The paper initializes budgets as \(b_1(\succ)=R(\succ){m\choose2}\), constructs the flow network whose ranking vertices are precisely the ranking types, and proves that the resulting payments certify sPJR. A finite election with \(n\) voters and \(n_\succ\) voters of each ranking has \(R(\succ)=n_\succ/n\), and every quantity used by \(\fb\), pair-priceability, and sPJR depends only on this histogram—not on voter identities. Thus the proposed “continuous” problem is not a new high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s problem; it is the paper’s own weighted-profile problem.

A better version using an explicit support of \(K\) ranking types does not repair this. It would yield a reasonable complexity analysis of the already-defined rule: construct the \(K\)-type flow network and run max flow. That may be useful bookkeeping, but it is not a population continuization and supplies no new continuous optimization problem. Conversely, making the distribution implicit or giving only sampling access could generate interesting oracle or succinct-representation questions, but their difficulty would come from the input-access model, not from replacing individuals by mass.

Nor does a richer application rescue the mirror. If benchmark evaluators, departments, or recommendation users are distinguished only by their rankings, they collapse exactly to the paper’s \(R\). If evaluator reliability, prices, identities, or other attributes affect the rule, then the true type is \((\succ,\theta)\), and one has designed a different rank-aggregation problem to which the stated theorem does not apply. If every agent has idiosyncratic data, the repeated-type regime disappears.

The proposed robustness variant—minimum population mass whose removal destroys sPJR—could be a worthwhile follow-up, but it is a new question about the paper’s already-continuous profile, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result. The paper’s universal quantification over subprofiles already contains the relevant mass-removal structure.

This is therefore a stronger objection than “continuity does not help”: the population has already been continuized, and the proposed anchor merely restates it. Rank aggregation may inspire later computational work on robustness or implicit distributions, but under ChoCo’s stated anchor rule this paper offers no surviving continuous mirror.
