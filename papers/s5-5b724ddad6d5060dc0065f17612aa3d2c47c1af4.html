What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 characterizes monotonicity properties, and Theorem 3 relates individual to group strategy-proofness; neither provides a computational result. The other named results also concern mechanism properties, characterization, or existence rather than computational complexity or algorithms.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest theorem-backed case for a continuous computational mirror: the paper has no named result asserting a complexity classification or an algorithmic bound for a computational problem.

The tempting anchors are theoretical mechanism results. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for OSaA choice profiles, the COM is IR monotonic, weakly Maskin monotonic, and dropping monotonic exactly when the choice profile is strongly OS. Theorem 3, also proved here, says that under strong OS the COM is weakly group strategy-proof if and only if it is individually strategy-proof. Neither is a computational result of the kind required.

A plausible population extension would give mass to agents with each preference type over contracts and study a continuum version of the COM. But that would turn the paper’s axiomatic questions into new continuum-mechanism questions; it would not mirror a named computational result from this paper. So there is no qualifying anchor and no continuous problem to state per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the programme’s gate: this paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–5 characterize axioms or give incentive implications for the cumulative offer mechanism (COM); none classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithmic bound. The other analyst’s Theorems 1 and 3 are the closest candidates, but they are axiomatic results.

Theorem 1 says that, for OSaA choice profiles, three monotonicity properties of the COM are equivalent to strong OS. A natural mass-market version could study preference changes affecting positive fractions of each type. That may be a coherent new axiomatic question, but it does not preserve the theorem’s individual preference transformations: in an atomless population, one person’s report changes no mass, so the COM’s aggregate outcome is unchanged and the individual monotonicity tests become vacuous. Making the perturbation affect positive mass avoids that degeneracy by changing the question.

Theorem 3 has the same problem more sharply. Its content is that individual strategy-proofness implies weak group strategy-proofness under strong OS. In an atomless, per-capita model, an individual has no effect on the outcome, making individual strategy-proofness automatic. Positive-mass deviations could still matter, but then the result would compare a vacuous individual condition with a substantive coalition condition. That is not the paper’s reduction.

A large school or labor market with finitely many preference and priority types, and capacities scaled with population, is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. It could motivate computing a mass version of the COM. But that would require an aggregate choice model and a new computational question; the paper’s arbitrary institutional choice functions operate on contracts for named agents and establish no computational result about the mechanism. The setting is a sensible place to ask further questions, yet it does not supply a worthwhile continuous computational mirror of this paper’s results.

The universal claim is weakest if “mirror” includes any adjacent project: a mass COM could be interesting in its own right. For ChoCo’s stated computational programme, though, the negative verdict holds. This paper offers axiomatic questions that can be reworked for continuous populations, but no computational finding for the continuization programme to carry forward.
