The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 5.5

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 5.5 — survived: The opponent challenges the scaling and ChoCo fit, but the proposed type-mass learning auction remains a recognizable analogue of this algorithmic result.
  • Theorem 5.1 — survived: The opponent raises the same scaling concerns, while the proponent states a concrete type-average version of the theorem's error-condition question.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finitely many user types, finite request sets with rational laws \(\nu_t\), realizable unknown linear values \(v_t(w)=\beta_t^\top\phi(w)\in[0,1]\), and per-round capacity \(q\) for a unit mass of users, design and analyze an online auction that learns from reports \(\mathbf{1}[U\ge c]\) with \(c\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\). Does it achieve sublinear per-capita welfare and revenue regret against the full-information capacity-\(q\) auction and type-average individual rationality, and for what finite-population scaling does its implementation retain sublinear single-user reporting regret?

The model it lives in

A fluid online auction over a distribution of complete user types, with type-level request and value models, mass allocations, capacity scaled per capita, and pairwise-feedback learning; finite-population approximants carry the individual reporting guarantee.

The objection that survived

In an atomless population, one user's report has no effect on the shared estimate, while the paper's guarantee concerns a single agent; the proposed finite-population bridge does not specify how population size, horizon, and capacity must scale to preserve that guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the mechanism guarantees in Theorems 5.1 and 5.5 for type-pooled learning, including welfare and revenue regret. It leaves the toxicity experiments, broader ethical discussion, and results outside the feedback-auction mechanism unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What relation among population size, horizon, type masses, and capacity preserves the single-user reporting guarantee?
  • Do the welfare, revenue, and type-average participation bounds follow under type-pooled learning and capacity-scaled allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a narrow, algorithmic mirror of the paper’s feedback auction. The paper has no named result saying that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in a parameterized complexity class. Its named results concern what an online mechanism can guarantee. Those are still useful anchors for an algorithms question, but they do not establish a complexity classification.

A plausible regime is a large digital-goods or data marketplace with many users but relatively few stable user classes. For example, users might share the same request distribution and value model, or the same content-sensitivity profile. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of users in class \(t\), with \(\tau\) classes and \(N\gg\tau\) users. A type must include everything relevant to the mechanism: its request distribution, value function, and any type-specific costs or payment parameters.

To keep the amount of service meaningful as the population grows, I would make the allowed co-modification explicit: each period the platform can serve a fraction \(q\) of requests, or \(qN\) users in a finite-\(N\) approximation. The paper itself allocates one good per period, so this is a scalable multi-unit version of its auction. Each user still receives a made-to-order good and can report whether its realized utility exceeds a random comparison value.

The most concrete anchor is Theorem 5.5, proved in the paper (with its proof in Appendix B.2). It says that, when expected utility follows a realizable linear model, the mechanism using uniformly sampled comparison prices, linear regression, and exploration rate \(\eta_t=t^{-1/3}(n\log t)^{(1+2\epsilon)/3}\) satisfies Theorem 5.1’s conditions. It is therefore asymptotically individually rational and incentive compatible, with no regret in welfare and revenue relative to a second-price auction. The theorem invokes Theorem 5.1 for those guarantees; the finite-regret rates come from that more general result, rather than being a separate bound stated in Theorem 5.5.

I would mirror it with a Fluid Pairwise-Feedback Auction problem. An instance gives a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types; for each type \(t\), an explicit finite request set \(W_t\) with rational request probabilities \(\nu_t\); a rational feature map \(\phi\); a capacity fraction \(q\); and a horizon \(H\). Each type has an unknown value function \(v_t(w)=\beta_t^\top\phi(w)\in[0,1]\). The mechanism observes requests and pairwise reports \(r=\mathbf 1[U\ge c]\), where \(c\) is sampled uniformly from \([0,1]\), but does not observe \(\beta_t\). A solution is an online rule for exploration, allocation, comparison queries, and payments. It must allocate at most fraction \(q\) each period and use only observed history and current requests. The target is a mechanism whose per-capita welfare and revenue regret against the full-information, capacity-\(q\) second-price benchmark are \(o(H)\), whose truthful participation is asymptotically individually rational, and whose single-user reporting regret is \(o(H)\) in its finite-\(N\) implementation. The benchmark serves the \(q\) fraction with highest expected values and charges the marginal losing value, splitting ties proportionally.

For this problem, I expect Class A in the stated finite-support, fixed-dimensional linear regime: allocation reduces to ranking explicit values, and estimation uses linear regression. The open part is whether the paper’s incentive and regret proof survives type pooling and the fluid limit, and how the exploration rate should depend on type masses. The paper’s \(n\)-dependent schedule cannot simply be copied: the mirror must determine whether the relevant scale is the number of users, the number of types, or the smallest positive mass \(\min_t\mu_t\).

A useful supporting anchor is Theorem 5.1, also proved in the paper (Appendix B.1). It assumes estimates bounded to \([0,1]\), expected estimation error decreasing with the number of samples, and \(\sum_{t=1}^H\Delta_t=o(\sum_{t=1}^H\mathbb E[\eta_t])=o(H)\). Under those conditions, the mechanism is asymptotically individually rational and truthful, and welfare and revenue maximizing relative to the second-price benchmark. It also gives \(\widetilde O(H^{2/3})\) welfare and revenue regret for a learner with a \(\widetilde O(H^{-1/2})\) slow learning rate, and \(\widetilde O(H^{1/2})\) regret for a learner with a \(\widetilde O(H^{-1})\) fast rate.

Its continuous counterpart is the same Fluid Pairwise-Feedback Auction with a general learner \(L\) in place of regression. Define \(\Delta_h\) as the expected largest value-estimation error over the supported types and their requests, measured from exploration reports. The question is whether the condition \(\sum_{h\le H}\Delta_h=o(\sum_{h\le H}\mathbb E[\eta_h])=o(H)\) is sufficient for the fluid mechanism’s type-average individual rationality, single-user reporting guarantee, and per-capita welfare and revenue guarantees—and what finite-horizon bounds follow. This is the broader question; Theorem 5.5’s linear case is the strongest and most concrete anchor.

The paper makes this mirror plausible: its digital-goods and annotation markets naturally admit large cohorts with repeated value profiles, and its linear utility model already gives a compact description of those profiles. Its random reports and uncertain outputs are part of the learning problem; they do not make the population itself continuous. The mirror would cover the online allocation and payment guarantees, not the paper’s experiment or broader ethical discussion.

The weakest point is truthfulness. In a strictly nonatomic population, one user’s report has zero effect on the aggregate estimate, so unilateral truthfulness can become vacuous. A meaningful mirror must state the guarantee for finite-\(N\) implementations and show how it behaves as \(N\) grows. If instead the question is whether a positive mass of users can coordinate on false reports, Theorem 5.1 does not answer it: its deviation bound concerns a single agent. Scaling service capacity with the population is a second adaptation, but a reasonable one for a digital marketplace whose intended service level is per capita. The welfare, revenue, and pairwise-learning questions remain substantive even if the incentive guarantee needs a separate finite-population bridge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about fit with ChoCo’s computational question. Theorem 5.5 and Theorem 5.1 are real algorithmic results, but neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem or analyzes the cost of finding a solution. Theorem 5.5 gives a learning schedule under a realizable linear model and derives mechanism guarantees from it. Theorem 5.1 assumes a bound on estimation error and turns it into truthfulness, participation, welfare, and revenue regret bounds. A continuous version would ask whether those statistical and incentive guarantees survive a population limit. That could be worthwhile online mechanism design, but it does not supply the complexity question—such as pricing for an exponential-variable LP—that motivates ChoCo’s continuization programme.

There is also a scaling problem behind both anchors. In the paper, \(n\) is the number of named bidders competing for one allocation each round; \(H\) is the repeated time axis. With the same one-item supply and a nonatomic population, a winner has zero mass, so type proportions do not describe who receives the good or whose utility and payments enter the guarantees. Scaling supply to \(qN\) allocations per round is a plausible co-modification, and it is within the programme’s scope. But it creates a capacity auction with a different allocation and pricing benchmark. The positive case proposes such a model, while also acknowledging that the paper’s second-price result does not establish its guarantees.

Pooling by type does not directly resolve the difficulty. If users of a type share an estimator, one user’s influence on it disappears as the cohort grows, so the single-user truthfulness condition can become vacuous. A guarantee against a positive mass of coordinated misreports would be more substantive, but the paper’s deviation bound covers one agent. If instead the mechanism keeps a separate estimator for every user, the independent histories that Theorem 5.1 relies on remain individual: under its random exploration scheme, a particular user’s exploration probability shrinks with population size. A meaningful limit therefore needs a specified relationship among population size, horizon, and capacity, plus a new analysis. That may be a good project, but it is not a direct continuous counterpart of either theorem.

Theorem 5.5 is the sharper anchor, and the same objection applies to its best version. A large annotation workforce with a few stable sensitivity profiles is a credible high-multiplicity setting; the paper’s toxicity example makes that scenario especially plausible. One could ask for a fluid, capacity-scaled feedback auction over those profiles. That would preserve a recognizable application and may be a sensible research question. Yet the central task would still be to prove learning and incentive guarantees for a dynamic auction. The theorem’s computational work is linear regression and choosing the highest estimated value; it presents no hard optimization or complexity bottleneck for continuous-optimization machinery to unlock.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about ChoCo’s fit, not as proof that no useful continuous model exists. I cannot honestly rule out the annotation-cohort mirror: its multiplicity is plausible, and authors might recognize a scaled auction as their problem. The case against is that both anchors point toward a new population-scale online mechanism-design theory, while the computational complexity question that would make this a ChoCo contribution remains unspecified.
