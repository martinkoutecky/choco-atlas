The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying computational anchor under your rule. Propositions 3.1, 3.3, 4.7, 4.10 and 4.13 give structural statements about Bloc winners and their stability; Section 5 reports simulation frequencies. None asserts a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. I can’t honestly make an anchor-backed case or give one continuous problem per anchor.

The strongest positive direction I see is a follow-on to the paper’s IAC simulations. Consider a large electorate with a fixed left-to-right candidate axis and many voters, but only \(2^{m-1}\) possible single-peaked ranking types. A type is a ranking; its mass is the fraction of voters with that ranking. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime when \(m\) is small and the electorate is large.

One precise candidate is IAC-Limit Bloc Local-Instability Probability. For each type \(t\), let \(\mu_t\) be its population share, and let
\[ b_\mu(c)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[c\text{ is among the top }k\text{ in }t] \]
be candidate \(c\)’s Bloc score. Draw \(\mu\) uniformly from the simplex over the single-peaked types—that is, \(\mu\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(1,\ldots,1)\), the limiting distribution of normalized profiles under IAC. The problem is: given \(m\) and \(k\), compute the exact probability that the size-\(k\) Bloc winning set is not locally stable. For a losing candidate \(d\) and winner set \(W\), the relevant mass is
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[d\succ_t w\text{ for every }w\in W]. \]
The set is locally stable when this mass is below \(1/2\) for every loser. A solution is the exact probability of a violation; ties between candidate scores occur with probability zero under this prior.

This is recognizably connected to the paper: it studies the same Bloc outcomes, single-peaked rankings, and stability criteria, and turns the finite-\(N\) IAC simulations into an exact population-limit question. For fixed \(m\), the probability reduces to volumes of finitely many rational polytopes. With \(m\) variable, its complexity is open; the paper supplies no basis for claiming an efficient algorithm or a hardness result.

The weak point is that this is a computational follow-on to the simulations, not a mirror of a named computational result. The formal propositions classify properties of finite profiles, while the paper does not frame any of them as computational problems. So the positive case is plausible as a research direction, but it does not satisfy the requested anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

On the requested anchor standard, the positive case has a decisive gap: it names no computational result in the paper. The propositions characterize which Bloc committees can occur and when they satisfy stability conditions; the simulations estimate frequencies under three preference models. None classifies an algorithmic problem. The proponent acknowledges this, so their proposal is a follow-on, not a continuous mirror of a computational result.

The faithful population translation is easy to state. Replace each ranking count \(x_t\) by its mass \(\mu_t\). Bloc scores become \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[c\text{ is in the top }k]\), and pairwise and local-stability tests are also linear sums of type masses. The propositions then become comparisons among linear forms on the type simplex. With the natural treatment of ties, their arguments carry over directly. This is a sensible population regime—large electorates can have relatively few ballot types—but the translation does not expose a computational question the paper posed.

The strongest proposal is IAC-Limit Bloc Local-Instability Probability. It is mathematically coherent: partition the Dirichlet simplex according to Bloc winners and stability, then compute the relevant polytope volumes. But this asks for an exact probability over a prior on societies, rather than an algorithmic question about a given continuous society. For fixed \(m\), the dimension is fixed; with variable \(m\), the volume calculation lives in dimension \(2^{m-1}-1\). That may be difficult and could be worthwhile as a separate study of random voting or polytope volumes. It does not supply the missing computational anchor, and its difficulty would come from integrating over a growing space of ranking types.

A closer computational variant might ask how much voter mass must change before a Bloc committee loses local stability. That is a coherent robustness question, but it is newly added: the paper neither studies robustness distances nor poses an optimization problem over profiles. It might yield a useful result, yet its connection to this paper is thematic rather than a continuization of a named computational result.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed *anchor-backed* case: there is no computational anchor to preserve, and the direct mass versions of the paper’s results are linear restatements. The universal claim is weaker. The electorate is a credible high-multiplicity object, and the exact probability proposal—or a carefully chosen robustness problem—could merit independent attention. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror; the material supports saying that this paper does not itself make a compelling case for one.
