The most plausible prospective mirror would continuize the stakeholders: in a large property or infrastructure portfolio, let each type be a stakeholder profile with the same criteria weights and project assessments, and let its mass be its share of the decision body. The decision would assign each project to continue, postpone, or terminate under a shared budget, using aggregate preferences in a goal-programming objective. That could preserve the paper’s portfolio-optimization stage. Its weakest point is substantial: the paper’s coalition analysis is about agreements among five distinct, named roles, and replacing them with interchangeable mass may change the central negotiation question. This is a possible follow-up model, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. It applies AHP and Goal Programming to one five-stakeholder, five-project case, then reports portfolio choices and coalition rankings. It gives no theorem about an algorithm, complexity, or a family of computational instances. The proponent is right to offer no anchor; under the requested standard, that is decisive.

The best prospective mirror is a large infrastructure portfolio with many small investors or affected residents. Give each person a complete type describing their role, criteria weights, project assessments, and bargaining position; use the mass of each type to represent its share of the decision body. That could be a sensible model, and it is stronger than simply treating the paper’s five stakeholders as a crowd. But it would define a new population decision problem rather than mirror a computational result this paper establishes.

The paper’s coalition stage makes that gap especially clear. Its players are five named, role-specific decision makers, and the analysis considers which subsets cooperate and what outcomes they can secure. A mass version would need a formal account of positive-mass coalitions, participation, and payoffs. The paper introduces a characteristic function but does not specify coalition values or a payoff-allocation rule. Its reported rankings therefore do not provide the formal coalition problem that a continuous analysis could take as input.

I would not claim that no plausible high-multiplicity version could ever be worth studying: the investor or resident scenario could motivate one. The negative case is weaker at that universal claim. What the paper supports is narrower: it supplies no computational result for ChoCo to mirror, and the strongest population-scale proposal is a follow-up model whose central coalition questions remain to be formalized.
