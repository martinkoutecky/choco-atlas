  • Theorem 3 — survived: The opponent challenges the continuous rule space's finite representation; the \(q\)-tagged frontier remains a recognizable extension of the paper's mechanism-design question.

The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(M\), strict-ranking types \(S\), and rational \(q\in(0,1]\), determine the exact frontier \(\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)=\inf\{D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F):\epsilon_q(F)\le\epsilon\}\) over rules \(F:\Delta(S)\to\Delta(M)\), and characterize its attaining rules. Here \(\epsilon_q(F)\) is the supremum of a type-\(t\) resident's expected utility gain from reporting \(t'\), when the rule's input changes from \((1-q)\nu+qe_t\) to \((1-q)\nu+qe_{t'}\), over \(\nu\in\Delta(S)\), \(t,t'\in S\), and utilities \(u\in U_t\); \(D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F)\) is the worst-case Plurality deficit over \(\Delta(S)\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(S)\) over complete ranking types. A randomized mechanism maps \(\mu\) to lottery probabilities over \(M\); one tagged resident has mass \(q\), and a deviation transfers that mass from type \(t\) to \(t'\). Minimize worst-case Plurality deficit subject to an upper bound on the resident's maximum expected gain, and characterize the full frontier.

The objection that survived

The rule \(F\) must be specified over the entire simplex, so the paper's finite-profile LP and breakpoint argument do not directly provide a finite encoding for this frontier.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the manipulability–deficit frontier for Plurality over a fixed set of strict-ranking types with a \(q\)-mass tagged resident. It leaves the paper's results for general desiderata, weak preferences, matching and assignment domains, and finite-market examples aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(q\)-tagged frontier admit a finite representation or separation method for each rational \(q\)?
  • How does the frontier behave as \(q\to0\), including whether an appropriately scaled manipulability bound has a limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s manipulability–deficit frontier. The paper contains no named P, NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result, so I would not present it as a case where discrete hardness dissolves. Its strongest anchor is instead:

Theorem 3, proved in this paper. For any fixed problem, there is a finite sequence of supporting manipulability bounds from \(0\) to \(\bar\epsilon\), the least bound that permits d-maximization. Between adjacent bounds \(\epsilon_{k-1}\) and \(\epsilon_k\), every optimal mechanism is a hybrid of mechanisms optimal at those endpoints: if \(\beta=(\epsilon-\epsilon_{k-1})/(\epsilon_k-\epsilon_{k-1})\), then
\[ \mathrm{Opt}(\epsilon)=(1-\beta)\mathrm{Opt}(\epsilon_{k-1})+\beta\mathrm{Opt}(\epsilon_k), \qquad \delta(\epsilon)=(1-\beta)\delta(\epsilon_{k-1})+\beta\delta(\epsilon_k). \]
The theorem is proved here using the paper’s linear-program formulation of optimal mechanism design. It gives both a finite description of the frontier and a way to recover intermediate optimal rules by mixing endpoint rules.

Here is a continuous-population problem that keeps the paper’s strategic and social questions. Let \(M\) be a set of alternatives and \(S=\{t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\}\) a fixed set of allowed strict rankings. A city choosing among three transit plans is a plausible regime: it may have millions of residents but only a few distinct rankings, so \(N\gg\tau\). A type is a resident’s ranking, and \(\mu\in\Delta(S)\) gives each ranking’s fraction of the population. A randomized rule is \(F:\Delta(S)\to\Delta(M)\), mapping the reported type distribution to a lottery over alternatives.

To retain the paper’s individual deviation test, give one tagged resident population mass \(q\), where \(q=1/N\) corresponds to one resident in a population of \(N\). If the other residents have distribution \(\nu\), the truthful and misreported inputs are \((1-q)\nu+qe_t\) and \((1-q)\nu+qe_{t'}\). Define
\[ \epsilon_q(F)= \sup_{\nu\in\Delta(S),\,t,t'\in S,\,u\in U_t} \sum_{a\in M}u(a) \left[ F_a\big((1-q)\nu+qe_{t'}\big) - F_a\big((1-q)\nu+qe_t\big) \right], \]
where \(U_t\) is the paper’s set of utilities in \([0,1]\) representing ranking \(t\). For Plurality, write \(p_a(\mu)\) for the mass ranking \(a\) first and define the worst-case deficit
\[ D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F)= \sup_{\mu\in\Delta(S)} \left[ \max_{a\in M}p_a(\mu) - \sum_{a\in M}F_a(\mu)p_a(\mu) \right]. \]
The named problem is the \(q\)-Tagged Continuum Mechanism Frontier: given \(M,S\), rational \(q\), and Plurality as the desideratum, determine the exact function
\[ \mathcal D_q(\epsilon)= \inf\{D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F):\epsilon_q(F)\le\epsilon\} \]
and characterize rules attaining it. A solution must describe the full frontier, not just one point. At \(q=1/N\), restricting \(\nu\) to empirical distributions recovers the anonymous finite-population incentive test; allowing all \(\nu\in\Delta(S)\) is the high-multiplicity relaxation. Studying \(q\to0\) asks how that frontier behaves as the market grows.

I expect the fixed-\(\epsilon\) optimization to have a plausible Class A route for fixed \(m\) and \(\tau\): the rule’s lottery probabilities, incentive gains, and Plurality values are linear quantities, so an exact continuous-optimization or separation formulation may exist. For the *entire exact frontier*, my provisional prediction is Class C unless a finite-breakpoint or separation theorem can be proved. The finite population has finitely many profiles; the continuous domain has infinitely many \(\nu\). The paper’s finite-support conclusion therefore does not carry over automatically. This is an open prediction, not a hardness claim.

The anchor is especially plausible because the paper itself studies frontiers as the number of agents grows and conjectures near-ideal behavior in large markets. This mirror covers that manipulability–deficit tradeoff for a fixed scoring desideratum; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s general results about all ordinal mechanisms or its matching-domain extensions. It also generates concrete follow-up questions: does \(\mathcal D_q\) have finitely many affine pieces for each \(q\), and does it converge as \(q\to0\)?

The weak point is the incentive model. In a truly atomless society, a single resident has zero mass and cannot affect \(F(\mu)\), so unilateral manipulability vanishes. The tagged mass \(q\) preserves the paper’s finite individual deviation and makes the large-market limit meaningful, but the authors could reasonably see the limit as a strategyproofness-in-the-large question rather than their exact finite-setting question. The paper explicitly distinguishes those questions. The case survives because its own large-market frontier conjecture makes this a natural continuation of the authors’ agenda, but the connection depends on accepting the \(q\)-parameterized bridge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the right anchor to challenge, but its computational content is narrower than the proposed mirror suggests. The paper’s finite frontier follows from a finite LP: its variables are lottery probabilities at each of finitely many preference profiles, and its piecewise-linear result uses the finite set of LP constraints and vertices. The theorem does not give a complexity classification for a population-level intervention problem.

The city example is sensible as a high-multiplicity setting: millions of residents may share a small set of rankings. And the paper already makes mechanisms anonymous without worsening their signatures, so aggregating reports by type is a fair strengthening of the proposed case. But that does not yet yield a continuous computational mirror. At fixed \(N\), a rule on anonymous profiles is a rule on a finite grid of count vectors. That is a compressed finite problem. Extending the rule to every \(\mu\in\Delta(S)\) introduces values at uncountably many profiles and an optimization over functions \(F\). The incentive constraints compare \(F(\mu)\) with \(F(\mu+q(e_{t'}-e_t))\); they do not supply a finite representation of \(F\) or the full frontier. The paper’s finite-basis argument therefore does not carry over. A new finite-representation theorem might make this a valuable project, but it would be the central new result needed to make the proposed exact-frontier question computationally tractable.

The tagged-resident construction sharpens that problem. With \(q=1/N\), the resident has the right finite-market influence, but the proposal is a continuum of other residents plus a positive-mass atom. Restrict \(\nu\) to empirical distributions and the model returns to the finite grid. Allow arbitrary \(\nu\), and the new, off-grid policy values become part of the mechanism design problem. As \(q\to0\), uniformly Lipschitz rules have incentive gain \(O(q)\), so the unscaled frontier collapses toward zero manipulability. Without a uniform regularity condition, discontinuities near ties can preserve large gains and make the limiting frontier unstable. Rescaling gain by \(q\) or imposing regularity could define useful new questions, but neither is simply the paper’s exact finite-setting frontier.

So my negative case is that this is not yet a direct ChoCo computational mirror: it either compresses a discrete high-multiplicity problem or becomes an infinite-dimensional rule-synthesis problem without a finite encoding. I would not claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. The city scenario clears the high-multiplicity test, and the mirror could survive if a finite-representation result were found. That is the honest weakness in the case against it.
