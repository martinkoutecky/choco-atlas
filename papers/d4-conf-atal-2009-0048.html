The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: a continuous, type-aggregated version of the paper’s single-task bargaining game.

Anchor: Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Under the assumptions in Section 4, the bargaining process \(B(G,\varepsilon,m)\) has a stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium (SSPE), and every SSPE has agreement in the first round. If each weight has a common integer denominator \(W\), an SSPE can be computed in time \(\operatorname{poly}(n,\log W)\). This is an algorithmic result, not a hardness result. The paper’s Partition-based discussion in Section 7.3 is not a numbered hardness theorem, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A candidate mirror is Mass Weighted-Voting Bargaining Equilibrium. Its instance has a finite set \(T\) of resource types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-unit resource endowments \(a_t\), and a threshold \(\theta\). Normalize total resources so that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t=1\). A coalition is specified by masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\); it wins if \(\sum_t a_t x_t\ge\theta\). Thus the society is the distribution \(\mu\), and coalition formation selects portions of its type masses.

To retain the paper’s bargaining question, the protocol has one delegate for each represented type, with proposal turns ordered by decreasing \(a_t\). At its turn, a delegate proposes recruited masses \(x_t\) and a transfer \(q_t\) per recruited unit of mass; the delegate receives the remaining task value. The proposal must meet the resource threshold and the total-payment budget. Recruited agents of type \(t\) accept when \(q_t\) meets their reservation and continuation value. If a proposal is rejected, the next delegate proposes. The delegates’ roles are part of the protocol; the population itself remains the continuum of resource holders.

The problem is: given this instance, compute an exact stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium, including each delegate’s proposal and each type’s acceptance strategy, and determine whether agreement occurs in the first round. This is the underlying equilibrium-computation question from Theorem 1; the proposed offers and responses are the solution, not an encoding.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. With finitely many types, each proposal stage can be expressed as a continuous resource-selection problem with linear budget and threshold constraints. Backward induction over the ordered delegates should therefore use linear programs over \(\tau=|T|\) mass variables, with rational data. The conjectured running time is polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and the number of delegate turns. Whether every stationary equilibrium remains delay-free is a separate part of the mirror to prove.

The regime is a large resource pool with few recurring endowment classes: for example, many interchangeable workers or resource holders grouped by capacity, negotiating through cohort delegates. Here population size could be in the thousands or millions while the number of resource types remains small. Fractions of a cohort are meaningful allocations, just as the paper’s total contributed resource is the quantity the system designer wants to control.

The scope is Theorem 1’s equilibrium computation and first-round agreement for a single task. I would not claim that this covers the paper’s multiple-coalition results or its price-of-democracy bounds.

The weakest point is the bargaining protocol. In the paper, every individual may get a proposal turn, and that individual’s position in the ordering affects their continuation payoff. Grouping identical resource holders therefore does not automatically preserve the game: including proposal role in each complete type could make the support grow with the population. The type-delegate protocol gives a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, but it is a co-modification of the bargaining model rather than a direct limit of the stated process. A referee could reasonably ask whether the authors regard type-level delegates as an acceptable version of their question. The positive case stands or falls mainly on that recognition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the proponent’s only computational anchor, and it is a genuine one: the paper gives a polynomial-time algorithm for computing an SSPE. The difficulty is that the game’s types cannot be just resource levels. An agent’s place in the proposer order changes their continuation payoff and bargaining power. The proof uses precisely those later proposals to determine what earlier proposers must offer. Two agents with the same weight but different turns are therefore not interchangeable for this problem.

The proposed delegate model removes that distinction by replacing all agents of a resource type with one proposer. That changes the bargaining institution: it removes repeated turns and gives a delegate bargaining power on behalf of a mass, while the paper gives each proposer their own payoff and turn. Calling the recruited units of mass “agents” does not repair this; the model still needs a rule explaining why a cohort acts through one delegate and whose utility that delegate maximizes. The result may be a sensible new bargaining game, but the paper’s Theorem 1 does not supply that rule.

A closer mirror would preserve the individual proposer slots and aggregate only agents who have no such slot. That works as a restricted hybrid when the number of turns is fixed and small. But the paper allows \(m\) to grow up to \(n\); preserving the game then requires preserving up to \(m\) strategically distinct roles. If instead the number of delegates is tied to the number of resource types, the model again changes who gets to propose. So the proposed compression either keeps the feature that makes the population discrete or replaces a central feature of the game.

That is the negative case, but it has a real limit: a large workforce represented by a small negotiating committee could make the delegate model a worthwhile problem, and the authors might recognize it as a natural extension. The case against depends on the authors regarding that committee structure as a new assumption rather than a continuous version of their bargaining process. I cannot rule out that scenario from the paper alone; the anchor is vulnerable, but the universal negative is not airtight.
