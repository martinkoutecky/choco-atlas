The strongest positive-side observation is only a research prompt, not a valid ChoCo case: Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper, says that on any \(M\)-closed class of finite TU games, Pareto optimality, equal treatment, and marginality uniquely characterize the Shapley value. The proof’s stepwise enlargement of equivalence sets might inspire a limit question about many interchangeable agents.

Turning that inspiration into a continuous question about a game \(v(x)\) over coalition masses would continuize the axiomatic question, which is outside ChoCo’s scope. And in a general TU game, compressing many agents into types would need to preserve their marginal contributions across coalitions—structure this paper does not provide.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 3.4 and its applications characterize a solution by axioms; they make no claim about an algorithm, running time, reduction, or computational complexity. Proposition 3.6 and Theorem 3.9 establish when the characterization applies to particular classes of games. Those are all axiomatic results, which the programme explicitly places outside its scope.

The best version of the proposed mirror would start with many interchangeable players and represent a coalition by its mass of each type. One could then ask how to compute Shapley allocations in that model. But that supplies a new computational problem, not a continuous version of a problem this paper studies. The paper gives no encoding of a game by type masses: a general TU game assigns values to all coalitions, and a distribution over player types alone does not specify those values. Choosing a mass-based worth function and an allocation algorithm would require new modeling choices and new computational results.

The proof’s stepwise enlargement of equivalence sets does not rescue the anchor. It is a proof technique for uniqueness, not an algorithmic task the paper formulates. Continuizing that technique would continue the axiomatic inquiry; adding computation would change the inquiry.

So the proponent’s only candidate anchor fails, and the stronger high-multiplicity version fails as a *mirror* for the same reason.
