The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor for a computational continuous mirror in this paper. Its numbered results are Propositions 1–4, all proved in the paper: they characterize minority payoffs as the winning-coalition threshold changes in the main Baron–Ferejohn model, two bargaining variants, and the Shapley-value model. They are equilibrium comparative-statics results, not claims about an algorithm, computational complexity, or parameterized tractability. The empirical findings are not stated as numbered computational results either.

The model’s group shares offer a possible starting point for a population-continuum model. But the strategic players here are ethnic groups acting as unitary blocs; their members are not the agents making proposals or votes. Replacing those blocs with an atomless population would change the game, and asking for equilibrium payoffs would remain an analytic question rather than the computational mirror this programme seeks.

So I can’t honestly give a named continuous problem per anchor, designate a lead, or assign the proposed problems to the tractability trichotomy. A problem about computing equilibria from rational group shares could be formulated, but it would be a new question without a qualifying result in this paper to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 gives a usable anchor, even though it is not itself a complexity theorem. It characterizes minority payoff as the winning threshold \(w\) changes. The natural computational question is whether that payoff curve and its winning coalitions can be computed efficiently for a high-multiplicity version of the same bargaining problem.

Take a society with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), where each type records the relevant bargaining attributes, such as minority status and continuation payoff. In each round, draw a marked proposer whose type is \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t\). The proposer may recruit any fraction \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type, and a proposal passes when the recruited mass reaches \(w\). Given \(\mu\), \(w\), and discount factor \(\delta\), ask for an exact or additive-\(\epsilon\) algorithm to compute stationary equilibrium payoffs and coalition composition, with running time in the support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\).

This makes the population itself the continuous object. It is not just a continuously varying democracy index or a fractional outcome: many interchangeable members have the same type, and a leader can recruit a fraction of them. The paper already motivates \(w\) as the amount of popular support a leader needs and makes recognition proportional to population share. A legislature with many interchangeable members and type-dependent continuation payoffs is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for that model. Reporting minority welfare per member is also compatible with the paper, which says that normalizing payoffs by group size does not change its comparative statics.

The model’s coalition step has a clear optimization structure: for fixed continuation payoffs, choosing the cheapest mass that reaches \(w\) is a linear program over type masses. The equilibrium couples those choices across proposer types, so the full algorithmic question remains open in this formulation. It may reduce to tractable optimization, or the equilibrium coupling may produce hardness; either answer would classify a computational version of the payoff question in Proposition 1. The paper’s symmetric majority/minority case is a special, low-support instance, not a reason to assume the general problem is already settled.

The main vulnerability is the change from cohesive ethnic groups as unitary players to many individual members who can be recruited fractionally. That is a real modeling choice, not a literal limit of the paper’s game. But it is a plausible version of its winning-coalition question, and the paper’s own interpretation of \(w\) as required popular support gives the authors a reason to recognize it. So the absence of a numbered complexity theorem weakens the anchor; it does not establish that no worthwhile computational mirror exists.
