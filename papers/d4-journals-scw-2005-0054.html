The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, so I can’t make a positive case anchored to one of its results.

Theorem 1, proved here, says there is no finite-type characterization of Pareto representability in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Theorem 2, also proved here, says that for \(k>2\), every characterization of Pareto representability in \(\mathbb{R}^k\) has type at least \(2k+2\). Both concern the size of local characterizations in ordered tuples of alternatives. Neither classifies an algorithm for a computational problem.

The paper notes that a finite-type characterization can be checked in polynomial time, but the absence of such a characterization does not imply computational hardness; the paper explicitly leaves open the possibility of other useful characterizations. Its references to unanimous voting suggest a possible population story, but the named theorems do not study a many-agent regime. Moreover, under unanimity, positive mass itself does not affect the relation: only whether a type is present matters.

So there is no eligible anchor here, and consequently no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. Any such problem would be a new question suggested by the paper’s motivation, not a mirror supported by its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest possible mirror I see comes from the paper’s own committee interpretation. Let \(\mu\) be a continuous society over voter types, each type specifying a complete ranking of the alternatives, and define \(x\succ_\mu y\) when almost every voter type ranks \(x\) above \(y\). One could ask whether this society’s unanimous relation is representable in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), or in \(\mathbb{R}^k\) for fixed \(k>2\), with complexity measured in \(m,\tau,L\), and \(k\). That is a recognizable population version of the property in Theorems 1 and 2: the paper itself connects Pareto representation to unanimous committees and says that efficiently testing the property would be useful.

But this is only a weak countercase. The theorems establish limits on bounded-size characterizations, not computational complexity results. And under exact unanimity, \(\mu\)’s positive masses do no work: the induced relation depends only on which types have positive mass. Adding a mass-cost campaign to change that relation would make mass matter, but it would add a new optimization problem that the paper does not study.

So the proponent raised no computational anchor, and I cannot supply one without stretching what the paper proves. There is a plausible continuous-population question in its orbit, but I would not call it a strong mirror of this paper’s results.
