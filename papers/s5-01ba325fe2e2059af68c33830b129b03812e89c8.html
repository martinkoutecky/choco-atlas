The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition claiming a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its results are empirical. For example, Section 6 reports that the tested SMI outcomes were stable and maximum-cardinality, while SM asymmetric stability ranged from \(100\%\) in the 8-agent, \(3\times3\) case to \(46\%\) in the 14-agent, \(5\times5\) case. Those findings are not named complexity results, and the paper gives no formal guarantee that its MARL method converges.

There is a plausible *setting* for a future mirror: a large hiring market where agents share a finite set of types defined by side, location, and utility profile, and mass represents each type’s share of the market. But turning that into a computational problem about decentralized learning or stable outcomes would require adding a formal question the paper does not answer. I therefore can’t honestly supply a prover-ready problem per anchor or classify an anchor as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper gives us no named computational result to continuize. Its central finding is empirical: on small sampled markets, SARSA often produces stable or nearly stable outcomes, with measured fairness and instability. It states no complexity claim and gives no formal convergence or approximation guarantee. So there is no theorem here whose high-multiplicity version would yield a direct complexity question. The proponent’s proposed hiring market is a plausible setting, but it is a setting for a follow-on study, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

The best version of that follow-on would use a fixed set of employer and worker types, defined by location and utilities toward the opposite types, with many agents in each category. That avoids the mistaken objection that repeated types themselves distort the model. It also keeps a meaningful population to study. But it changes what is being studied: the paper’s agents learn from noisy, individual encounters and choose among named counterparts while navigating a grid. With type-level interactions, one must specify a population learning process and define success—for example, convergence probability, finite-horizon reward, or the mass of blocking interactions. The paper does not select among these computational questions or supply a formal target for one. Studying equilibrium behavior would be a new analytic model; studying algorithms for the chosen target would require substantial new problem design.

The reported stability and fairness measures do have natural mass versions: blocking-pair density, per-capita rank cost, or a type-level matching flow. But those measure outcomes; they do not turn the paper’s experimental claim into an algorithmic result. A stable allocation over types would omit the learned search process, while retaining that process leaves the state, policy, and success criterion to be newly formalized.

That is a strong case against calling this paper a continuization candidate on the strength of its results. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A large hiring market with fixed, repeated types could plausibly support a meaningful study of decentralized population learning. I cannot honestly defeat that scenario under the programme’s scope. The negative case is strongest—and limited to—saying that such a study would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s stated results.
