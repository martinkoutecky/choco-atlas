The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives with rational feature vectors \(z_i\), a common rational parameter matrix \(B\), and \(\tau\) agent-feature types \(x_t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where rankings for type \(t\) follow the Plackett–Luce model, compute the probabilistic Borda winner distribution \(\psi\) to additive error \(\epsilon\), where \(\psi_i = \frac{2}{m(m-1)}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{\exp(x_t^\top Bz_i)}{\exp(x_t^\top Bz_i)+\exp(x_t^\top Bz_j)}\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support society of agent-feature types \(x_t\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\), with alternatives described by features \(z_i\) and shared preference parameter \(B\). The decision is the population’s probabilistic Borda winner distribution, computed from the mass-weighted pairwise probabilities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s probabilistic Borda distribution computation. It leaves Theorem 1, Corollary 1, the elicitation framework, posterior approximation, MPC, and the experiments outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s probabilistic Borda aggregation result. Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that for any alternative \(a_i\), its probabilistic Borda winner probability is proportional to the sum, over key-group agents \(j\), of their probabilities of ranking \(a_i\) above each other alternative: \(\Pr_{\text{Borda}}(a_i)\propto\sum_{j=1}^{n_1}\sum_{i'\ne i}p_j^{a_i\succ a_{i'}}\). The paper connects this identity to computation by observing that these pairwise probabilities are easy to compute under the Plackett–Luce model. This is the closest named tractability anchor in the paper; it does not state a standard complexity bound such as membership in P, and the paper has no named hardness result.

A plausible regime is a large workforce choosing among benefit plans, or a large user community choosing among services. Alternatives have feature vectors \(z_i\); people have relevant covariates \(x\); and many people share the same covariate profile. A type is that complete profile for the preferences being modeled, with the shared parameter \(B\) mapping features to utilities. If there are \(n\) people but only \(\tau\) such profiles, type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), its fraction of the population. The case rests on a genuine many-agent regime with moderate \(\tau\), not on a small committee.

Lead problem — Continuous Probabilistic Borda Distribution. An instance gives \(m\ge2\) alternatives with feature vectors \(z_i\), a finite list of \(\tau\) types with feature vectors \(x_t\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational parameter matrix \(B\). For each type and pair of alternatives, define \(p_t(i\succ j)=\frac{\exp(x_t^\top Bz_i)}{\exp(x_t^\top Bz_i)+\exp(x_t^\top Bz_j)}\). The task is to output the population’s probabilistic Borda winner distribution \(\psi\), where \(\psi_i=\frac{2}{m(m-1)}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\sum_{j\ne i}p_t(i\succ j)\). A solution is the probability vector \(\psi\), exactly or to a specified additive error \(\epsilon\). The normalization follows because every complete ranking awards \(m(m-1)/2\) total Borda points. This is Theorem 2’s calculation with the finite agent sum replaced by a type-mass-weighted sum.

I expect this problem to be tractable: compute the pairwise probabilities and sum them, using work polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the feature dimensions, the input bit lengths, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) for additive approximation. The paper’s theorem supplies the pairwise-probability identity; the mass-weighted extension is direct. It generates a further question about replacing the finite type support with a continuous feature distribution: what representation or integration assumptions preserve an efficient algorithm? A related, separate question is the complexity of optimizing elicitation queries over type masses under a budget.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s winner-distribution computation, not the paper’s full cost-effective elicitation framework, posterior approximation, or MPC criterion. That boundary is deliberate: the theorem is about aggregation from the preference model, and replacing a sum over agents with an expectation over population types preserves that underlying question.

The weak point is that this is a modest mirror. Theorem 2 is an exact computation identity, not a hard problem whose complexity might change in the continuum; with finite support, the continuous version is essentially a weighted sum. The original authors could reasonably see it as a high-multiplicity form of their result rather than a new computational challenge. The case is strongest as a clear, plausible population mirror—not as evidence of a dramatic change in complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the anchor’s computational content. Theorem 2 gives an identity for the probabilistic Borda distribution; it does not classify the complexity of a search or optimization problem. With a finite type support, its proposed mirror is the same calculation with a weighted sum:
\[ \psi_i=\frac{2}{m(m-1)}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\sum_{j\ne i}p_t(i\succ j). \]
Compute each pairwise probability and add it. If several agents share a feature vector, grouping them by that vector already gives the same weighted sum. The continuum changes the input representation, but creates no new algorithmic obstacle or question about query selection.

A stronger population model could put mass directly on ranking types and calculate the population’s Borda scores. Or it could retain the paper’s elicitation process: query exchangeable regular-group types, update the posterior over \(B\), and choose questions under a budget. The first is still direct score aggregation. The second is a more substantial research problem, but the paper gives no named complexity result for query selection to mirror; its proposed procedure is greedy. A genuinely continuous feature distribution could raise integration questions, but their computational content would depend on how that distribution is represented, rather than follow from Theorem 2.

That makes the mirror modest, but modesty is not enough to reject it under the programme’s standard. The paper does not already treat the population as a continuous society, and a large group choosing among benefit plans or services with repeated covariate profiles is plausible. Replacing the sum over people with type masses is a natural model the authors could recognize. I cannot honestly defeat that anchor: the negative case is weak, and the proponent’s finite-support mirror survives, even if it offers little complexity theory beyond a weighted-sum computation.
