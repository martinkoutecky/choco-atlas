The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that a continuous-population *setting* is plausible, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational result to mirror.

A natural high-multiplicity scenario would be a large sensor network with many devices sharing a small number of complete types: each type specifies its signal likelihoods and its communication role. Mass would mean the fraction of devices of each type. A type-level contact structure could represent repeated network blocks, and the learning rule would use type-level beliefs and the paper’s min and filtering updates. That is a conceivable continuous-agent model, though preserving the paper’s connectivity and Byzantine-resilience conditions in it would need careful work.

The closest anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that if the graph is strongly \((2f+1)\)-robust with respect to every pair’s source set, LFRHE lets every regular agent learn the true state almost surely despite any \(f\)-local Byzantine adversaries; each false hypothesis is rejected at an asymptotic rate at least the *minimum* KL divergence among regular source agents. This is a substantive algorithmic guarantee, but it is a convergence and learning-rate theorem—not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified by complexity. The paper’s polynomial-time claim about checking the robustness condition appears in prose and points to an external proposition; it is not a numbered computational result proved here.

The other named results do not fill that gap: Theorems 1–4 and the corollaries establish consistency, rates, or identifiability conditions, not computational complexity. So there is no eligible anchor, and therefore no prover-ready continuous computational problem per the requested standard. A continuum limit of the learning dynamics would be a plausible analytic extension, but that would continuize the population to study learning behavior—not chart the computational complexity of the authors’ problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 5 is the strongest candidate: it proves that LFRHE learns despite Byzantine agents under a strong robustness condition. But its conclusion is about almost-sure convergence and rejection rates, not the complexity of a decision, optimization, or search problem. The paper’s claim that the robustness condition can be checked in polynomial time is in the prose and points to an external proposition. Turning that condition into a continuous network-design problem would add a computational objective the paper does not study.

The sensor-network scenario is plausible as a setting, but it does not by itself supply a mirror. The theorem depends on the communication graph’s specific edges, source sets, and local adversary counts; its guarantee applies to every regular agent. A type description that preserves those details will generally distinguish agents by their network roles, leaving little multiplicity to aggregate. Collapsing repeated roles into type masses can lose the reachability and local robustness information the theorem needs. A dense-network or graphon model could restore an aggregate description, perhaps with adversaries scaled by a population fraction, but that would be a new network model and a new analysis. It would make a plausible analytic limit of the learning dynamics, not a computational question already posed by this paper.

The other apparent anchors do not fix the gap. Theorem 1’s rate is controlled by the best informative agent for each false hypothesis, and Theorem 4 concerns identifiability of source components. These are meaningful analytic guarantees, but their extrema and connectivity conditions are not naturally optimized over type masses: changing the amount of a positive-mass source type need not change the rate, while removing it can destroy identifiability. A mass-allocation problem could be invented, but it would be a new intervention problem rather than a mirror of either theorem. Theorems 2 and 3 and the corollaries likewise concern rates, concentration, or network conditions, not computational complexity.

I would therefore reject Theorem 5 as a ChoCo anchor and find no replacement among the other named results. The universal claim is not airtight: a researcher could define a continuous, fraction-adversarial network model and study its limits or design problems. That may be worthwhile elsewhere. The negative case is strongest on the narrower point that such work would not continue this paper’s computational results by making the population continuous; it would require supplying the computational problem and much of the model anew.
