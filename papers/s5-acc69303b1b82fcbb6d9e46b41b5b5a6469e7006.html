The anchor — other

Theorem 4

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4 — survived: Its explicit cycle criterion yields a recognition test, and replacing ranking counts with type masses preserves that test.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates and a finite support of \(\tau\) strict-ranking types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and with no pairwise majority ties, does the induced majority tournament contain a directed cycle?

The model it lives in

Represent each ranking as a type carrying mass \(\mu_t\). Compute pairwise majority comparisons from the weighted rankings and decide whether the resulting tournament contains a cycle; the decision has no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's cycle criterion for strict rankings. It leaves aside the paper's results on Sen's condition, its precursors, and the discussion of indifference.

Open questions for a prover

  • Given a specified ranking-change cost, what minimum mass transfer makes a majority cycle appear or disappear, and what is the complexity of that problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results says that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The strongest positive case is therefore a borderline one. Theorem 4 gives an exact characterization of the computational predicate “does pairwise majority generate a cycle?” The authors prove their stated version here; they describe it as a version of Miller’s result [3]. If the anchor rule requires a named complexity result, rather than a theorem characterizing a computable predicate, then this paper has no qualifying anchor.

The plausible mirror is Continuous Majority-Cycle Recognition. A type is a complete strict ranking of the candidates. An instance gives \(m\) candidates and a support of \(\tau\) rankings, each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with the masses summing to \(1\). Restrict each ranking to a triple \(x_1,x_2,x_3\), and let \(a_{hij}\) be the total mass ranking that triple as \(x_h\succ x_i\succ x_j\). Restrict the input to societies with no pairwise majority ties. The question is whether the resulting majority tournament contains a cycle; a yes-solution is a cycle, and a no-solution certifies that the tournament is transitive.

Theorem 4’s content, in this notation, is: majority voting generates a cycle if and only if there is a triple for which either
\[ a_{123}>a_{321},\quad a_{312}>a_{213},\quad a_{231}>a_{132}, \]
or all three inequalities are reversed, and, defining
\[ \Delta=|a_{123}-a_{321}|+|a_{231}-a_{132}|+|a_{312}-a_{213}|, \]
each of the three absolute differences is strictly less than \(\Delta/2\). This is the paper’s Theorem 4, proved here as a version of Miller’s result. The condition is homogeneous in the \(a\)’s, so replacing discrete counts by normalized masses preserves it.

The regime is a large electorate with many voters sharing ballots: for example, millions of voters but relatively few distinct rankings, so \(\tau\ll N\). Since this problem uses only preferences and their effects on pairwise majority, voters with the same ranking are indistinguishable for it. The authors should recognize the mirror as their majority-cycle question applied to a distribution of rankings.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. Given the explicit support, one can compute all pairwise majority comparisons and check for a three-candidate cycle in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). That algorithmic conclusion is a consequence of the mirror and the theorem’s characterization; it is not a complexity result claimed by the paper. Natural follow-ups include allowing indifference and asking how much mass must be changed to create or eliminate a cycle.

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s strict-ranking cycle predicate, not the paper’s full discussion of Sen’s condition and its precursors. Its weakest point is also its limit: the article’s contribution is a structural theorem about majority decisions, not a computational study. Cycle recognition is an easy problem, and the mass version does not expose a complexity boundary. So this is a plausible, narrow population mirror—but not a strong example of the programme’s computational-complexity gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 and Theorems 2–3 are possibility results: they show that a condition on a profile is sufficient to prevent cycles. Theorem 4 is the closest thing to a computational anchor, but it characterizes exactly when a cycle occurs; it does not study an algorithmic problem or its complexity. Turning that predicate into a decision problem is possible, but it creates the computational framing rather than continuizing one the paper supplies.

The proposed majority-cycle mirror is nevertheless coherent. Rankings make sensible types for a large electorate, and the inequalities in Theorem 4 carry over to rational masses. The objection is not that the model is unnatural or that the result would be uninteresting because it is tractable. It is that the paper’s contribution is a structural characterization, while the mirror offers no corresponding computational contribution to continue: it repackages that characterization as a recognition task.

A stronger candidate would ask how much mass must be changed to create or remove a cycle. But that requires choosing what changes are allowed and how they are priced—for example, a distance between rankings. The paper specifies neither interventions nor costs. Such a problem could be worthwhile, but it would be a new robustness or bribery study prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a result it establishes.

The negative case is therefore strongest as a judgment about the programme’s scope: Theorem 4 supports a narrow, legitimate high-multiplicity recognition question, but not a computational result of the kind the programme is organized to chart. It is not airtight. If any well-posed computational question derived from a theorem counts as an anchor, the cycle-recognition mirror survives.
