What the mirror covers

The proposed model generalizes the PMMS feasibility question to replicated valuation types and per-capita supplies. It does not turn Theorem 1 into a computational result, and leaves the finite proof and Section 3's approximation barriers as finite statements.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a high-multiplicity PMMS feasibility problem. There is one qualification up front: the paper has no named complexity result establishing a problem in P, NP-hard, or a parameterized class. Its strongest usable anchor is Theorem 1, a named *nonexistence* result. So this is a credible mirror of the paper’s allocation question, but not a complexity result the paper itself supplies.

Theorem 1, proved in this note, says that the following additive fair-division instance has no PMMS allocation: three agents, nine indivisible goods \(a_1,a_2,a_3,b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4,c,d\), and valuation vectors
\[ u_1=(143,133,137,22,20,21,29,38,2),\quad u_2=(159,140,133,15,8,1,1,35,2),\quad u_3=(135,133,134,13,11,20,28,40,2), \]
in that goods order. The theorem rules out every allocation of those goods, not merely a particular algorithm’s output.

A natural regime is a large allocation of nine standardized, indivisible categories of goods to residents in three stable valuation groups. Everyone in a group has the same complete valuation vector; the group masses are each \(1/3\), while the population size \(N\) is much larger than the three types. There are \(N/3\) copies of each category, so each category has per-capita supply \(1/3\). A resident still receives an integral bundle; only the fraction of each type receiving each bundle can be fractional. This is a reasonable high-multiplicity version of the authors’ problem, though the exact valuation profile is deliberately stylized.

I would call the mirror problem Continuous PMMS Feasibility. An instance gives a finite set of goods \(G\), additive valuation types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\). Take each person’s bundle to be a subset \(B\subseteq G\), with at most one copy of each category per person. A solution is a measurable allocation, represented by masses \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni g}x_{t,B}=q_g. \]
It must also satisfy PMMS between every pair of positive-mass type–bundle cells: for cells \((t,B)\) and \((s,D)\), each agent’s value for their own bundle must be at least that agent’s maximin share of the pair’s combined goods. Treat the pair’s union as a multiset of distinct copies, even if both bundles contain the same category. The specific mirror of Theorem 1 uses the three vectors above, \(\mu_t=1/3\), and \(q_g=1/3\) for each of the nine categories. The question is whether this continuous instance has a PMMS allocation, with \(x\) as the solution certificate.

This asks whether the paper’s obstruction persists when each valuation type has many indistinguishable agents and goods are supplied at scale. A solution with rational masses would yield PMMS allocations in arbitrarily large exact replications, by choosing a population size that clears the denominators. Conversely, allocations in arbitrarily large replications have a convergent subsequence of bundle-mass vectors, giving a continuous allocation. Thus the mirror tests whether this nonexistence is a small-instance phenomenon or survives high multiplicity.

For the unrestricted family, my tentative expectation is Class C: once a set of assigned bundles is fixed, feasibility reduces to rational mass and supply constraints, but selecting a support of bundles whose pairs are all PMMS-compatible appears to retain a combinatorial core. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1; the paper proves no hardness for it. The exact three-type, nine-good restriction is finite and can be settled by enumeration, so it cannot by itself establish a scalable complexity classification. Further questions include whether the scaled witness is feasible, how feasibility changes with type masses and supplies, and whether the general problem admits an LP or pricing formulation.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 1 is a single finite impossibility example, not a hardness result, and it says nothing about the continuous instance’s answer. Mixing assignments across identical agents may restore PMMS. Still, the mirror preserves the authors’ central objects—additive valuations, indivisible personal bundles, and the pairwise maximin-share test—while making the population and supplies continuous. That is the strongest positive case I can make without claiming the theorem proves more than it does.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational result. It proves that one particular finite allocation instance has no PMMS allocation. The paper does not classify the complexity of finding one, deciding whether one exists, or approximating one. Its exhaustive search verifies a fixed witness; it is not an algorithmic result. That leaves no stated complexity theorem for a continuous counterpart to carry forward—the central gap ChoCo is designed to study.

The best proposed mirror is still a sensible model: many agents in each valuation type, with supplies of each good category scaled with the population. The proposed equivalence between rational mass allocations and arbitrarily large exact replications helps justify that model. But it does not make the paper’s theorem a computational question. For the specific three-type, nine-category witness, feasibility is just one fixed finite-instance question. To get a complexity problem, one must generalize to variable numbers of goods or valuation types and ask about a new family of clone-market instances. That could be worthwhile research, but its value would come from the new problem, not from a computational result in this paper.

So the case against is strong on anchoring and weak on ruling out the broader research direction. The high-multiplicity PMMS problem may be worth studying, and I cannot honestly argue that no plausible scenario makes it so. What the paper supplies is a compelling finite obstruction, not a demonstrated complexity landscape; the proponent’s mirror turns that obstruction into a reasonable new question, but does not show that this paper has a worthwhile continuous computational mirror.
