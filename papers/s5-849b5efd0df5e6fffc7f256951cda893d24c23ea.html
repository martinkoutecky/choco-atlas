The continuous mirror question

Given rational unit-mass distributions \(\mu_B\) and \(\mu_S\) over finitely many buyer and seller types, where each type specifies a valuation, feedback-noise law, and UCB or LCB parameter, run the paper’s decentralized bidding protocol in a continuum Average auction. Each type carries a measure over agents’ private feedback histories; the auction clears by bid and ask mass quantiles, splits ties proportionally, and sets the price to the average marginal bid and ask. Does its expected aggregate welfare regret relative to the oracle allocation satisfy \(T W^* - \mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^T W_t] \le P(\tau_B,\tau_S,\alpha_{\max},1/\Delta)\log T + O(1)\), with no dependence on a finite number of agents representing those masses, or is there a counterexample?

The model it lives in

A continuum repeated Average auction with unit mass on each side and finite supports of valuation types. Agents retain independent private learning histories within each type; the evolving history measures determine bid and ask quantiles, while the objective is expected aggregate welfare regret against the true-valuation oracle.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible population mirror here. The best setting is a recurring energy balancing market: many sites trade the same standardized unit over a stationary window, and fall into a modest number of contract or equipment classes with the same valuation, feedback noise law, and bidding parameters. The number of sites can be very large while the number of such classes stays small. Each site still learns from its own noisy trade history; mass records how much of each class is present. The paper itself motivates energy markets and decentralized protocols for large markets, so its authors could recognize this as their setting scaled to a continuous population.

The paper has named algorithmic learning results, but no named computational-complexity result of the requested kind: it proves no problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The following are the closest useful anchors, and I would present them as regret results rather than dress them up as complexity classifications.

Lead mirror — Theorem 1, proved in this paper. The theorem bounds expected social welfare regret for the finite Average mechanism when buyers use UCB bids and sellers use LCB bids, with \(\alpha_{\min}\ge4\). In the paper’s notation, the bound is
\[ \begin{aligned} R_{\mathrm{SW}}(T)\le{}& \sum_{i\le K^*}\sum_{i'>K^*} \frac{(\sqrt{\alpha_{\max}}+2)^2}{B_i-B_{i'}}\log T\\ &+\sum_{j\le K^*}\sum_{j'>K^*} \frac{(\sqrt{\alpha_{\max}}+2)^2}{S_{j'}-S_j}\log T\\ &+\sum_{j'>K^*}\sum_{i'>K^*} \frac{(\sqrt{\alpha_{\max}}+2)^2}{S_{j'}-B_{i'}}\log T +\frac{MN b_{\max}\pi^2}{6}. \end{aligned} \]
Here \(K^*\) is the oracle number of trades, and \(b_{\max}\) is the paper’s value bound. For fixed \(M,N\), this is logarithmic in \(T\); the bound’s dependence on population size is explicit.

A concrete continuous problem is Continuum Average-Auction Social Regret. Its instance has rational unit-mass distributions \(\mu_B,\mu_S\) over finitely many buyer and seller types. A buyer type specifies a fixed valuation, observation-noise law, and UCB parameter; a seller type specifies the corresponding seller data and LCB parameter. Each agent follows the decentralized protocol using only its own participation history and noisy observations. At each round the auctioneer trades the largest mass supported by the bid and ask quantiles, splitting ties proportionally, and sets the price to the average of the marginal bids. Define \(W^*\) as the oracle welfare from true valuations and \(W_t\) as the welfare at round \(t\), counting the value retained by unmatched sellers. The question is whether the UCB/LCB protocol achieves
\[ T W^*-\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^T W_t\right] \le P(\tau_B,\tau_S,\alpha_{\max},1/\Delta)\log T+O(1), \]
where \(\tau_B,\tau_S\) are support sizes and \(\Delta>0\) separates valuations from the oracle price, with no dependence on how many agents represent those fixed masses. A solution is a protocol and proof of the bound, or a counterexample to it. I expect a logarithmic guarantee to be plausible, but Theorem 1 does not establish it: its finite bound depends on \(M,N\), so a population-independent type-based bound would be new.

Supporting mirror — Corollary 26, proved here from the paper’s bandit lower-bound argument. For every bidding policy, for any seller \(j\) there is a system with \(\mathbb E[R_{s,j}(T)]\ge \frac{1}{36}\sqrt{T}\); the same statement holds for any buyer \(i\). The argument uses a simplified system with fixed price and no shortage, reducing the agent’s decision to a two-armed bandit.

Its continuous counterpart is Continuum Fixed-Price Participation Regret. Take unit mass of buyers and sellers facing a fixed public price and unlimited supply. Agents have noisy private feedback, may only use their own histories, and are evaluated by average individual regret against the policy that knows their true valuation. Ask whether every such decentralized policy has worst-case average regret at least \(c\sqrt T\) for some constant \(c>0\). The solution is a policy or a minimax lower-bound proof. I expect the lower bound to persist: replicating identical types does not pool their private observations. This is a statistical regret lower bound, not computational hardness, and it persists from the single-agent problem rather than arising from the continuum.

The scope is therefore the Average mechanism’s social welfare learning guarantee and the individual learning lower bound. It does not cover equilibrium behavior, incentive compatibility, or other auction mechanisms. The paper’s experiments and large-market motivation reinforce the regime, but the named results above are the anchors.

The weakest point is that a valuation type is not a complete description of an agent’s current bid: independent noisy histories create a distribution of internal states within each type. The continuum model must track that state distribution to compute bid quantiles. It may not have a compact representation, and the paper’s \(MN\)-dependent bound gives no direct population-independent guarantee. That makes this a plausible continuous analogue of the authors’ learning problem, but a weaker fit for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme than a problem whose core reduces to a pricing or separation oracle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is a mismatch with ChoCo’s computational target. The named results are regret guarantees for decentralized bandit learning. They do not classify the complexity of an input problem such as bribery, control, or winner robustness. The proposed mirrors ask whether regret bounds persist in a population limit. That is a sensible question in learning theory, but it is a mean-field learning analysis rather than the computational complexity landscape ChoCo is set up to chart.

Theorem 1 is the stronger anchor, and the energy-market scenario makes its population regime plausible. The difficulty is that a valuation class alone cannot determine an agent’s bid: agents in that class receive independent noisy observations and acquire different private histories. A faithful continuum model therefore has to track a changing distribution over learning states for each valuation class. The resulting question is whether the measure-valued dynamics preserve a regret guarantee. That may be useful work, but it does not supply a computational problem whose complexity the result classifies. Computing each round’s allocation from finite bid support reduces to finding quantiles; propagating the learning-state distribution is a dynamic mean-field analysis. With a finite feedback alphabet, one could represent the state distribution by a finite-horizon recurrence. With the paper’s general sub-Gaussian feedback, the observation law also needs an explicit computational representation.

Corollary 26 is a weaker anchor. Its simplified market has a fixed price and unlimited supply, so each agent’s participation decision reduces to a two-armed bandit. A unit-mass version averages copies of that individual learning problem; it introduces no shared allocation constraint or population-level decision. A richer model with endogenous prices and scarce supply could couple agents, but that becomes the mean-field dynamics underlying the Theorem 1 mirror, rather than a continuous version of this lower-bound argument.

I would not claim that these population limits are incoherent or that the paper’s authors would reject them. The paper motivates large markets and decentralized protocols, so a continuum regret analysis could be worthwhile in bandit research. The negative case is strong on ChoCo fit and weak as a universal claim that no scenario merits study. The proponent has identified a credible continuous-population research question; it simply does not appear to be a mirror of the computational-complexity programme described here.
