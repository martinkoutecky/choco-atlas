What the mirror covers

The possible mirror covers Theorem 1's statistical lower bound; it does not cover a computational complexity result for the worker-clustering model or its experiments.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is for a plausible high-multiplicity crowdsourcing model, but I can’t attach it to a qualifying computational result from this paper.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here. Under Assumption 1, it lower-bounds the minimax error rate by
\[ \inf_{\hat G}\sup_{G\in[K]^n}\mathbb E[\mathcal L(\hat G,G)] \ge \frac{1}{n\log K}\left(R(\rho,\pi)-\frac{\log 2}{n}\right), \]
where
\[ R(\rho,\pi)=H(\rho)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{g,g'=1}^{K} \rho_g\rho_{g'}\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi^j_{g*}\Vert\pi^j_{g'*}). \]
This is a statistical lower bound on recovering task labels from noisy annotations. It does not establish a complexity classification or an algorithmic result. Lemma 2 is Fano’s inequality cited from prior work, and the experiments report empirical comparisons rather than named complexity results. Under the requested anchor rule, then, the paper has no qualifying named computational result.

The population regime itself is credible: a large annotation service may process millions of routine tasks using many workers who fall into a small number \(\tau\) of behavioral types. A worker type would include the confusion matrix and any task-selection or activity behavior relevant to the model; its mass is the fraction of workers of that type. Tasks could likewise be represented by masses over the \(K\) latent classes. This fits the paper’s worker-clustering motivation, provided task difficulty and worker behavior really are captured by those types.

A nearby continuous problem can be stated precisely. Given rational task masses \(\rho_g\), worker-type masses \(\nu_t\), confusion matrices \(\Pi_t\), and an annotation design giving each task \(b\) annotations drawn from the worker population, find a classifier \(\delta\) mapping each observed annotation signature \(z\) to a class so as to minimize
\[ \sum_{g=1}^{K}\rho_g\sum_z P(z\mid g)\, \mathbf 1[\delta(z)\ne g]. \]
A solution is the classifier minimizing this population misclassification mass. This preserves the paper’s underlying question—recovering task truths from noisy worker labels—and the continuous masses give a high-multiplicity interpretation. But it is a new population decision problem, not a continuous version of a computational result established in the paper. Theorem 1 supplies a lower-bound technique, not a complexity result for this problem. Also, with a fixed annotation budget, its displayed lower bound shrinks with \(n\), so it does not by itself provide a useful nonzero bound in the task-population limit.

That is the weak point I would foreground: the scenario is sensible, but the paper’s results are statistical rather than computational in the sense this programme is meant to continue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only plausible anchor, and it is an information-theoretic bound, not a computational result. Its infimum ranges over all estimators, with no restriction on running time, and it proves no complexity classification or algorithmic guarantee. Lemma 2 is a cited form of Fano’s inequality; the worker-clustering experiments and the choice of \(L\) add no computational theorem. So the paper gives ChoCo no named computational result to continue.

A sensible population model is easy to imagine: give workers masses by confusion-matrix type, and include annotation behavior in the type when needed. If \(\nu\) is the distribution over worker types, the sum over workers in \(R(\rho,\pi)\) could become \(m\) times an expectation over \(\nu\). That would be a reasonable continuous statistical version of the bound. But it remains a lower bound on estimation error. It does not ask how hard it is to compute an estimator, or provide an algorithm whose complexity could be charted.

The proponent’s classifier over annotation signatures is a better-posed continuous decision problem, but it changes the question: it assumes a known worker-type distribution and a fixed annotation design, then optimizes population misclassification risk. The paper instead studies recovering task truths from observed labels and derives a minimax lower bound. A richer model could preserve worker types, task assignment, and repeated annotations, but any computational problem built on it would be a new research direction rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s criterion: no anchor survives as a computational result from this paper. It is weaker as a claim that no continuous crowdsourcing theory could be worthwhile. A statistical continuum treatment may be useful; it simply is not the computational-complexity mirror ChoCo is tasked with finding.
