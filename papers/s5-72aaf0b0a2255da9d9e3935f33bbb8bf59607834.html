The anchor — hardness

Theorem 4.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4.1 — survived: The opponent challenges whether fractional mass allocations preserve the gap, but the mass version remains a concrete, recognizable allocation question.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), partitioned into \(k\) equal-mass groups, a population scale \(H\gg\tau\), and small-good classes \(g\) with mass supply \(\lambda_g\) and additive per-copy values \(v_t(g)\) satisfying \(\sum_g H\lambda_g v_t(g)\le 1+\varepsilon\) for every type, choose masses \(y_{t,z,r}\) over types, integral small-good bundles \(z\), and large-good status \(r\in\{0,1\}\). Require type-mass conservation, aggregate use at most \(\lambda_g\) of each small-good class, and group-specific large-good supply \(2/(3k)\) valued at \(1/\varepsilon\) by eligible types. Distinguish whether there is a feasible allocation with mass \(2/3\) receiving large goods and every person without one receiving utility at least \(3(1-\varepsilon)/H\), or whether every such allocation has \(H\sum_{t,z} y_{t,z,0}\sum_g v_t(g)z_g\le 65/81+3\varepsilon\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population described by type masses \(\mu_t\), with per-capita supplies of small-good classes and group-specific large goods. Each infinitesimal person receives an integral bundle; the mass allocation may divide a type across bundles and large-good status, and the objective is the paper’s promised allocation gap.

The objection that survived

A type’s mass can split between large-good and non-large-good status, letting small-good supply go to the non-large portion and breaking the theorem’s binary waste accounting; replication therefore does not establish that the promised gap survives.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 4.1’s allocation distinction, the shared scaffold for the paper’s Nash-welfare and budgeted-allocation hardness results; it does not cover their objective-specific approximation thresholds or the separate GAP result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s reduction be adapted to prove the stated gap when each type’s mass can split across large-good status and bundles?
  • What is the complexity of the mass-allocation problem when support size \(\tau\) is fixed or modest?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest anchor is Theorem 4.1 (Meta Theorem), proved in this paper by a reduction from Unique Games. For every rational \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), assuming the Unique Games Conjecture, it is NP-hard to distinguish instances with \(n\) agents partitioned into \(k\) equal groups, each group having \(2/3\) as many group-specific large goods as agents, valued at \(1/\varepsilon\) by that group and zero by others, while each agent values all the other goods at no more than \(1+\varepsilon\) in total:

  • In the YES case, an allocation gives \(2n/3\) agents a large good they value at \(1/\varepsilon\), and every other agent utility at least \(3(1-\varepsilon)/n\).
  • In the NO case, every allocation giving \(2n/3\) agents a large good they value at \(1/\varepsilon\) leaves the agents without large goods with total utility at most \(65/81+3\varepsilon\).

This is the paper’s proved hardness result for the allocation gap that it then uses for Nash welfare and budgeted allocation.

The mirror I would put to a prover is the Mass Meta-Allocation Gap. It models a large allocation programme with many applicants but relatively few complete valuation types: for example, a public-benefit or in-kind allocation system with recurring cohorts that share preferences and eligibility, and inventories that grow with the population. A type includes everything the problem uses about an agent, including additive values and eligibility for group-specific goods. Let the population have mass \(1\), with rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), partitioned into \(k\) equal-mass groups. There is a baseline population scale \(H\); the intended regime has \(H\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of positive-mass types.

Each small-good class \(g\) has rational per-capita supply \(\lambda_g\) and type-specific per-copy value \(v_t(g)\). Require \(\sum_g H\lambda_g v_t(g)\le 1+\varepsilon\) for every type, matching the paper’s bound on the value of all non-large goods. Group \(j\) has large-good supply \(2/(3k)\) per capita, valued at \(1/\varepsilon\) by its eligible types. A mass allocation is described by \(y_{t,z,r}\): the mass of type \(t\) receiving an integral bundle \(z\) of small goods and status \(r\in\{0,1\}\), where \(r=1\) means receiving one eligible large good. The masses assigned to each type sum to \(\mu_t\); aggregate small-good use is at most \(\lambda_g\) for every \(g\), and each group’s large-good supply is fully allocated. People receive whole goods and bundles; only the population and aggregate supplies are represented by mass.

The question is to distinguish two promised cases. In the YES case, there is a feasible mass allocation with \(2/3\) of the population receiving large goods and every person without one receiving utility at least \(3(1-\varepsilon)/H\). In the NO case, every feasible allocation giving that mass a large good has
\[ H\sum_{t,z} y_{t,z,0}\sum_g v_t(g)z_g \le \frac{65}{81}+3\varepsilon. \]
The output is the YES/NO classification, with a mass allocation serving as a witness in the YES case. The factor \(H\) makes this statistic equal to total utility in a corresponding \(H\)-person instance. I would restrict to, say, rational \(0<\varepsilon<1/100\).

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B) for the unrestricted type-input problem. The reduction’s hard structure is in the Unique Games graph and its label coordinates, and the soundness analysis already handles \([0,1]\)-valued low-influence functions. There is also a natural high-multiplicity lift: replicate each \((a,x)\) agent \(h\) times, replicate the large goods accordingly, and make \(h\) copies of each small good, each worth \(1/h\) of its original value. This keeps the allocation gap and type-level preferences while making the population-to-type ratio \(h\). That is a strong reason to think mass mixing will not by itself remove the gap, though adapting the reduction to the stated mass-allocation problem remains work for the prover.

This mirror is close to the authors’ question: it retains their additive utilities, large-versus-small goods structure, group-specific eligibility, and the allocation gap they use for welfare hardness. It continuizes the population, not the individual bundles. Its scope is Theorem 4.1’s allocation distinction, the shared engine for the paper’s Nash-welfare and budgeted-allocation results. It does not claim the same approximation thresholds for those objectives, and it does not cover the GAP result, whose argument additionally depends on capacities and a different scaling of large-good values.

The weakest point is that the reduction’s types and good classes are highly structured and grow with the Unique Games instance. Replication gives many people per type, but does not show that the paper’s hardness persists with a fixed small support \(\tau\), or that the tuple-indexed goods are a realistic catalogue. The useful follow-up questions are whether the fractional-mass soundness can be proved for this exact configuration model, and what happens when \(\tau\) is fixed or small.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 is a real computational result, and a large allocation system with repeated valuation types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. So the negative case cannot start by denying either the result or the population interpretation.

The strongest objection is to the proposed lift’s connection to the theorem’s proof. In the finite instance, each agent has a binary status: they receive a large good or they do not. A small good is wasted on the non-large-good population exactly when all four agents who value it are large-good recipients. In the proposed mass model, a type can be split between those statuses, so its large-good indicator becomes a fraction in \([0,1]\). With the stated \(2/3\) supply, for example, every type can have \(2/3\) of its mass receive a large good and \(1/3\) receive none. Then each small-good tuple has non-large mass among its valuers, and its copies can be assigned to that mass while keeping each person’s bundle integral. The theorem’s binary waste accounting does not directly describe this allocation.

The proposed replication illustrates the change: it makes \(h\) copies of each small good and gives each copy \(1/h\) of the original value. A third of the \(h\) clones of a type can receive those copies, with about three copies per such clone for that good. Thus the lift makes goods finer as it makes the population larger. Requiring each type to have one uniform large-good status would restore the binary structure, but that treats a preference type as an indivisible cohort. Preferences do not require identical people to receive identical bundles. The other obvious restrictions—equal treatment or limits on how many goods a person can receive—would add allocation rules absent from the paper.

This weakens the claim that the reduction itself supplies a strong high-multiplicity lift. The mass problem is better viewed as a new fractional allocation problem, closely related to a configuration LP. That problem may still be worth studying; the failure of the proof to carry over is not, by itself, a reason to reject the question.

That qualification is decisive for the broader negative case. The paper has named complexity results, and repeated valuation types are credible in allocation settings. The proponent’s exact hardness-transfer story needs more work, but I cannot honestly turn that into “no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario.” The case against this particular anchor is meaningful; the universal case against the paper is weak.
