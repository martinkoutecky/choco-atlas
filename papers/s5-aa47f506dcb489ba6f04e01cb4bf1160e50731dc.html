The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper has no named computational complexity result of the kind the anchor requirement calls for: none of its theorems asserts a running time or a hardness classification. The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: in the no-commitment game, every stationary equilibrium produces the same stable matching in every period, and every stable matching can be produced in every period by some stationary equilibrium. That is a game-theoretic characterization, not a complexity result, so it cannot serve as an eligible anchor. It does, however, give a clear structural target for a continuous computational question.

A plausible regime is a large market for standardized entry-level jobs: many applicants and single-job positions, with applicants sharing the same preferences over job classes and positions sharing the same preferences over applicant classes. A type records the agent’s utility vector, discount factor, and commitment status; within a type, agents are interchangeable for everything the model uses. The number of agents can be very large while the number of supported types, \(\tau\), remains modest. The continuum describes the population; each person still gets either one whole job or none.

Here is the problem I would put to a prover: No-Commitment Continuous Stationary-Equilibrium Search. The input is two finite sets of types, with rational masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\) summing to one on each side, rational utility data, and strict rankings over counterpart types and the unmatched option. The commitment regime is no commitment. A solution is a rational matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\), satisfying
\[ \sum_j x_{ij}\le p_i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij}\le q_j. \]
The unmatched masses are the respective remainders.

Require individual rationality: \(x_{ij}>0\) only when both types find the pairing acceptable. For a mutually acceptable pair \(i,j\), define the mass of type \(i\) willing to move to \(j\) by
\[ D_i(j;x)=\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik} +\mathbf{1}[j\succ_i\varnothing]\left(p_i-\sum_k x_{ik}\right), \]
and define \(D_j(i;x)\) symmetrically. Stability requires that there be no pair with both \(D_i(j;x)>0\) and \(D_j(i;x)>0\). The solution must also give a measurable one-to-one implementation of \(x\), with stationary offers and responses that make it an equilibrium. Such an implementation can be specified by partitioning each type’s population into the required pairwise masses and pairing the corresponding pieces. Agents maximize their discounted utilities as in the paper; the search objective is to find an equilibrium, not to maximize aggregate welfare.

I would expect this problem to be Class A: the no-commitment structure reduces the equilibrium target to stable matching, and a type-level deferred-acceptance or flow-based method looks like a plausible route to an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). That is a research expectation, not a result established by this paper. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s no-commitment case only. It generates further questions about the discrete-to-continuous approximation in \(N\) and \(\tau\), and about whether the commitment cases in Theorems 2–5 admit similarly compact mass-level equilibrium certificates.

The weakest point is the move from preferences over named partners to preferences over interchangeable partner types. It is credible for the cohort market described above, but it selects a particular regime of the authors’ model; their paper gives no complexity result whose hardness or tractability could be tested under that move. Theorem 1 makes the proposed equilibrium question recognizable, while the computational contribution would have to be developed from scratch.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is also the paper-level one: there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium outcomes: in the no-commitment game, stationary equilibria produce stable matchings, and every stable matching can be supported. It gives no algorithm, complexity bound, optimization problem, or hardness result. Theorem 2 and Theorems 3–5 likewise establish equilibrium support and patience conditions. So the proposed search problem is not a continuous version of a computational problem the paper studies; it is a new problem built from one of its structural findings.

The proponent’s large cohort market is plausible, and a type-level mass assignment is a sensible object. But getting from the paper to that object changes the model: it replaces preferences over named partners with preferences over partner types, and replaces individual blocking pairs with pairs that can form a positive-mass block. That may be a good continuum matching model, and an assignment could support an equilibrium in it. Still, the resulting task computes a static stable mass assignment and uses it to construct an equilibrium. The paper’s own proof says no-commitment dynamics reduce to the static model; the new task therefore does not expose a computational question about the dynamics studied here.

I would not claim that no useful research question can be built from this paper. The type-level stable-assignment problem could be worthwhile on its own, and the proposed hiring regime is not defeated by lack of multiplicity. The negative case is strong if the question is whether this paper offers a direct computational mirror for ChoCo. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous research can grow out of it.
