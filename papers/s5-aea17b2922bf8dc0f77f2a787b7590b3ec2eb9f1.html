What the mirror covers

The mirror covers pairwise-utilitarian aggregation, the public-finance maximal-outcome example, and conceptually the affine-utilitarian characterization; it leaves the domain impossibilities, rationalizability results, SSB representation, and vNM appendix without computational mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: the paper has a very natural population-continuous mirror, but it does not contain a qualifying numbered computational anchor. Thus the mirror is substantively credible but formally weak under ChoCo’s anchor rule.

The best mirror is Continuous Pairwise-Utilitarian Maximal Lottery. Let \(U\) be the finite set of alternatives, with \(|U|=m\), and let \(T\) be the set of complete ranking types. A type \(t\) is a complete ranking together with the paper’s pairwise-comparison extension to lotteries, represented by the skew-symmetric matrix
\[ \phi_t(a,b)\in\{-1,0,1\}. \]
The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\). Its aggregate pairwise preference matrix is
\[ \Phi_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\phi_t(a,b). \]

The decision variable is a lottery or divisible allocation \(p\in\Delta(U)\). Given a rational linear tie-breaking vector \(r\), the problem is
\[ \max_{p\in\Delta(U)} r^\top p \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{a\in U}p_a\Phi_\mu(a,b)\ge 0 \qquad\text{for every }b\in U. \]
The constraints say that \(p\) is maximal under the aggregate pairwise-utilitarian preference: no alternative lottery \(q\) defeats it. Without \(r\), the problem simply asks for any maximal outcome.

A convincing regime is a large participatory-budgeting or public-finance population: millions of residents, delegates, or party members, but only a small number \(K\) of recurring preference profiles. For example, the paper’s four delegate groups with shares \(0.25\), \(0.20\), \(0.45\), and \(0.10\) can be interpreted as four large constituencies rather than exactly 100 named delegates. Each person within a constituency has the same complete ranking and therefore the same pairwise-comparison preferences. Here \(n\gg K\), and the mass \(\mu_t\), not the identities of individual delegates, is the honest input.

This is recognizably the authors’ problem. Their public-finance example already aggregates groups of identical delegates using pairwise majority margins and selects a maximal convex combination of proposals. The mirror changes the population from a finite tuple of delegates to a distribution over preference types; it does not need to alter the paper’s outcome space. In particular, the lottery \(p\) is inherited from the paper, while \(\mu\) is the newly continuized object.

The closest named anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper. It states that, when \(|U|\ge 5\) and \(D\) is a rich domain, an anonymous social welfare function is Arrovian if and only if it is the utilitarian social welfare function and \(D\subseteq R^{PC}\). In other words, anonymous Arrovian aggregation is possible exactly on the pairwise-comparison domain, and aggregation is performed by summing the agents’ pairwise-comparison SSB matrices. The matrix \(\Phi_\mu\) above is precisely the high-multiplicity version of that sum.

Corollary 1 is the strongest possible anchor, but it is not formally a computational anchor: it does not state membership in \(P\), an NP-hardness result, or any other complexity classification. The paper does say, immediately afterward, that pairwise utilitarianism is computationally tractable because comparisons use matrix-vector multiplication and maximal outcomes can be found by linear programming. However, that assertion is unnumbered. Theorem 2, also proved here, is similarly structural: it says that every Arrovian SWF on a rich pairwise-comparison domain is affine utilitarian with positive weights, but it is not a complexity theorem.

The continuous problem above is nevertheless plainly in Class A under an explicit-support representation. Computing \(\Phi_\mu\) takes polynomial time in \(K\), \(m\), and the input bit length, and the maximal-lottery problem is a rational linear program with \(m\) variables and \(m\) essential maximality constraints. Further questions would become more genuinely computational if \(\mu\) were supplied implicitly, if one asked for the minimum mass transfer needed to make a target alternative maximal, or if one studied continuous winner robustness under changes to the population distribution.

The weakest point is decisive: this is close to a normalized version of the paper’s existing finite-group example, and its tractability is already stated informally in the paper. A referee could reasonably say that the proposed problem is a sensible high-multiplicity reformulation but not a new computational mirror anchored in a named complexity result. Therefore, the strongest positive conclusion is: a natural continuous-population model exists and is plausibly tractable, but this paper does not support a fully compliant ChoCo case because it contains no numbered computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is formal but decisive for ChoCo: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Corollary 1 and Theorem 2 are axiomatic characterizations of social welfare functions and preference domains. They state neither a computational problem nor a complexity, approximation, or algorithmic result. The sentence after Corollary 1 saying that pairwise utilitarianism is tractable is an informal observation, not a numbered computational theorem.

The proposed mirror of Corollary 1 also does not create a new computational object. For pairwise-comparison types, replacing the finite sum by a population distribution gives

\[ \Phi_\mu=\sum_t \mu_t\phi_t, \qquad \phi_\mu(p,q)=p^\top\Phi_\mu q. \]

All outcomes and maximality questions depend only on this finite pairwise-margin matrix, not on the underlying distribution \(\mu\). Thus the proposed LP is a computation on \(m(m-1)/2\) aggregate margins. The detailed continuous society has disappeared into a sufficient statistic. This is not an objection based on existing high-multiplicity work—the four constituency example is indeed a sensible high-multiplicity regime—but it means that the mirror is a normalized reformulation of the paper’s aggregation operator, not a population-computational problem supplied by the paper.

The same dilemma appears if one tries to improve the type model. Retaining the full domain \(R\) gives a distribution over real-valued SSB matrices, for which the paper supplies no finite input representation. Restricting to \(R^{PC}\) gives finitely many comparison matrices for fixed \(U\), after which aggregation again collapses to \(\Phi_\mu\). Making \(\mu\) implicit could create hardness, but then the difficulty lies in evaluating the chosen encoding or oracle, not in continuizing the population.

The tie-breaking vector \(r\) exposes another gap. Corollary 1 characterizes a collective relation and its set of maximal outcomes; it does not select one maximal lottery by maximizing an externally supplied \(r^\top p\). Without \(r\), the task is merely to find a maximal element of the relation already defined in the paper. With \(r\), it is a new selection problem. Theorem 2 has the analogous problem: its positive weights are attached to named agents. To represent them by a population measure, weights must either be discarded under anonymity or added as type attributes, which changes the model.

A stronger rescue would ask for minimum mass transfer, campaigning, or robustness under perturbations of \(\mu\). Those could be good ChoCo problems, but they introduce transition costs, admissible interventions, targets, norms, and tie-breaking conventions absent from the paper. They are new problems inspired by pairwise utilitarianism, not continuous mirrors of a named computational result.

So the paper fails the programme’s strict anchor test: Corollary 1 and Theorem 2 do not support the promised complexity landscape, and the only computational claim is unnumbered and already expressed as an ordinary finite-dimensional LP. I would not claim that no mathematically worthwhile population-continuous model can ever be built from this paper—the constituency model is legitimate, and a mass-transfer extension could be interesting. But that concession is precisely why the universal substantive negative is weak. The defensible negative verdict is narrower: this paper is not a qualifying ChoCo computational mirror candidate under the stated rules.
