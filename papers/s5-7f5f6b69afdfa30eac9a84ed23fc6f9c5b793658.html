The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses μ_t over complete applicant-profile types, per-capita university capacities κ_u, and type assignment fractions \(q_{t,u}\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(\sum_u q_{t,u}\leq 1\) and \(\sum_t μ_t q_{t,u}\leq κ_u\), what pairwise-fairness guarantees does a mass-scaled version of the paper’s nomination heuristic satisfy? In particular, do the type-level priority implications corresponding to Proposition 3 hold, and can the weak- and strong-fairness violations of Proposition 4 persist?

The model it lives in

Applicant types contain the complete rankings, aptitude scores, GPA, and prior exchange semesters used by the paper. The decision variable \(q_{t,u}\) is the fraction of type \(t\) nominated to university \(u\); total nominated mass is \(\sum_{t,u}μ_t q_{t,u}\), and capacities are per-capita. Pairwise fairness is stated over type-level assignment fractions, with the precise fractional interpretation made explicit.

The objection that survived

The paper’s guarantees compare individual applicants, while the proposed fractions \(q_{t,u}\) do not by themselves preserve those pointwise comparisons; a type-level fairness condition must be specified.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the heuristic’s pairwise fairness guarantees and failures in a mass-based nomination setting. It leaves the Pareto-efficiency propositions and the paper’s finite-instance experimental comparisons outside the question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which type-level fairness condition best preserves the paper’s individual priority guarantees when assignments are fractional by type?
  • Does the mass-scaled nomination heuristic retain Proposition 3’s guarantee, and do the fairness failures in Proposition 4 persist?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named complexity anchor in this paper. Proposition 1 says weak pairwise fairness implies Pareto efficiency; Proposition 2 says the strong-fair solution space is contained in the weak-fair one, so the maximum number of nominations under strong fairness is no larger. Proposition 3 says the heuristic guarantees fairness cases (a)–(d), and Proposition 4 says it can violate weak and strong pairwise fairness. These are numbered results, but none classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally hard.

The closest candidate is the unnumbered claim in §3.4 that the general nomination problem is NP-hard, described through the multiple knapsack problem and unsplittable flows. It does not meet your numbered-anchor requirement. Nor do the sub-second MIP runtimes establish a polynomial-time result. So I can’t give the requested anchor-by-anchor complexity case.

A plausible continuous counterpart to investigate would be nomination at a large exchange-program clearinghouse. A type \(t\) would contain a student’s full application profile: ranked universities, aptitude scores, GPA, and prior exchange semesters. Let \(\mu_t\) be the cohort’s fraction of that type, and let \(q_{t,u}\in[0,1]\) be the fraction nominated to university \(u\). With per-capita capacity \(\kappa_u\), impose \(\sum_u q_{t,u}\leq 1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_tq_{t,u}\leq\kappa_u\), then maximize \(\sum_{t,u}\mu_tq_{t,u}\). Large cohorts with standardized application bands could have many students but far fewer distinct profiles. This is a credible population mirror of the paper’s assignment model, and the resulting linear program suggests tractability.

Its weak point is fairness: translating the paper’s individual pairwise guarantees into constraints on type-level fractions may change what those guarantees mean. In any event, that is a plausible mirror of the model, not a mirror anchored to a qualifying named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no eligible anchor: they identify no numbered result about computational complexity. Propositions 1–4 concern fairness properties and the heuristic; the sub-second runtimes are experiments. Those can motivate continuous questions, but they do not supply a named computational result for this mirror to extend.

The best candidate is the paper’s unnumbered NP-hardness claim in §3.4. For the base model as written in equations (1), (4)–(6), though, each student can take at most one place and each university has a capacity. That is maximum-weight capacitated bipartite matching, solvable as a flow problem; the associated linear-programming relaxation is integral. So the proposed mass model is sensible, but it does not reveal a tractability gain from continuization: the corresponding discrete base problem already has that structure. Pairwise-fairness constraints may change the complexity, but the paper proves no complexity result for them.

Fairness offers the strongest possible upgrade. One could formulate constraints on type-level assignment fractions, but those would be fractional versions of the paper’s individual, pairwise guarantees. If the authors accept the fractional interpretation, that is a plausible new model; if they require the original pointwise guarantee, the fractions alone do not certify it. Either way, this would pose a new question rather than mirror a computational theorem in the paper.

I would therefore reject this paper as a result-anchored ChoCo mirror. The negative case is weaker against the broader claim that some continuous model could be worthwhile: a large exchange programme with repeated, complete application profiles and capacities that scale with cohort size is plausible, and the resulting allocation problem is well-defined. I cannot honestly rule out every such scenario; the strongest case against rests on the absence of a named computational anchor and the routine flow structure of the closest candidate.
