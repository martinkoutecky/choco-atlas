The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it has no named computational result to anchor one. Proposition 1, proved in the paper, establishes that sufficiently differentiated candidates have a unique, interior, convergent pure-strategy Nash equilibrium for any admissible voter distribution. Proposition 2, also proved here, characterizes that equilibrium at the marginal means when the issue distributions are independent. Both are analytic equilibrium results; neither gives a complexity classification or an algorithm.

A natural high-multiplicity scenario would be a large electorate grouped into classes with identical bliss points and issue weights, with each class’s mass given as a fraction of voters. One could ask whether a pure-strategy equilibrium exists and compute candidate platforms. But a finite-support version changes the paper’s smooth-distribution assumptions, and neither proposition establishes a result for it. That would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper.

There is a further obstacle: the paper already models voters as a unit-mass population distributed over bliss points. Making the population continuous would restate the paper’s model, rather than supply the missing continuous mirror. A computational analysis of how to find its equilibria might be worthwhile, but this paper provides no named computational finding for that analysis to extend.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper already starts with a continuous population. It defines voters as a unit mass distributed according to \(F\), and computes vote shares by integrating over that mass. A finite-support version, with rational masses for classes of voters, could support a worthwhile computational study. But it would be a new finite-input formulation of an already continuous-population game, not a continuous mirror of this paper.

Neither Proposition 1 nor Proposition 2 supplies the required computational anchor. Proposition 1 proves existence and uniqueness for sufficiently large differentiation; its threshold \(\hat d(F)\) is existential, and the paper gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or finite encoding for \(F\). Proposition 2 characterizes equilibrium locations by marginal means under independence. Computing those means for a chosen input representation might be a useful exercise, but the proposition itself says nothing about computational complexity. The numerical example and the probability-of-winning extension do not add such a result.

The strongest proposed mirror—give bliss-point classes rational masses and ask for an equilibrium—makes sense as a new question. Yet making the society a distribution over voter types is already built into the paper. To ask for worst-case complexity, one would also have to choose a finite encoding, such as finite support or a parametric family; that choice creates a computational problem the paper does not formulate or analyze. It may be useful work on a continuous game, but it does not fill the programme’s continuization gap. My case is strongest for this paper specifically: its population is already continuous, and its named results are analytic rather than computational.
