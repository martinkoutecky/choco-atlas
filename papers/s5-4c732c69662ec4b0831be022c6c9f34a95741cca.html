The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive mirror is a weighted version of the paper’s general approval-ratio problem. Its best anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. For every \(n\)-voter pairwise-intersecting double-interval society, it proves
\[ a(S)\ge \left\lceil 2n+\frac12-\sqrt{3n^2-n+\frac14}\right\rceil, \]
and hence
\[ \frac{a(S)}n\ge 2-\sqrt3+\frac{3+\sqrt3}{6n}-\frac{\sqrt3}{24n^2}. \]
Equivalently, if the approval number is \(a(S)\), then
\[ n\le \left\lfloor 2a(S)-\frac32+\sqrt{3a(S)^2-5a(S)+\frac94}\right\rfloor. \]
This is the paper’s general lower bound, not its double-\(n\)-string bound.

I should be precise about the limitation: Theorem 4.1 is a structural extremal result, not a named complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Theorem 3.6, Theorem 3.7, and Proposition 5.1 are also combinatorial bounds or constructions. So the paper has no named computational result of the kind the anchor rule asks for. Theorem 4.1 is the strongest substantive anchor for a continuous analogue, but it does not satisfy that computational-anchor requirement.

The candidate mirror is Weighted Double-Interval Approval. An instance is a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types. Each type \(t\) has approval set \(A_t=[a_t,b_t]\cup[c_t,d_t]\), with rational endpoints and \(a_t\le b_t<c_t\le d_t\), and a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Assume the approval sets are pairwise intersecting. The task is to return a platform \(p\in\mathbb R\) and the exact maximum approval mass
\[ v=\max_{x\in\mathbb R}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[x\in A_t]. \]
A platform attaining \(v\) is a solution. The natural computational expectation is tractability: a sweep over the interval endpoints finds a maximizing platform in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the endpoint and mass bit lengths.

Here, a type is a complete approval set shared by indistinguishable voters; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the population. A plausible regime is a large workforce or electorate with a moderate number of recurring approval patterns—for instance, groups who find the same two disjoint work shifts acceptable. The population becomes continuous; the paper’s already-continuous political spectrum remains the platform space. That preserves the paper’s central quantity: the largest share approving one platform.

Theorem 4.1 suggests the continuous guarantee \(v\ge 2-\sqrt3\). For rational masses, replace each type by the corresponding number of clones after taking a common denominator, apply Theorem 4.1, and divide by the denominator. This gives the bound, with a finite-size correction depending on that denominator. For real masses, approximation gives the limiting guarantee \(2-\sqrt3\). The finite-\(n\) correction itself has no natural population-size counterpart in the continuous model. Further questions include whether \(1/3\) holds for weighted societies, whether \(2-\sqrt3\) is tight, and how the paper’s modified constructions behave with unequal masses.

The mirror covers only the general approval-guarantee result, not the double-\(n\)-string analysis or the paper’s conjecture as a proved claim. Its weak point is substantial: the computation for a supplied society is simple, and the theorem being mirrored is not computational. So this is a credible high-multiplicity analogue of the paper’s mathematical question, but a weak case for the programme’s computational-complexity gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the kind of result the paper actually proves. Theorem 4.1 is a finite extremal bound on approval ratios, not a complexity result: the paper defines no computational problem whose worst-case complexity or algorithm it classifies. Its named results are bounds, constructions, and conjectures. That leaves no computational result for ChoCo’s continuous-complexity programme to mirror.

A continuous population is nevertheless sensible here. A large workforce with recurring two-shift approval patterns gives a clear type-and-mass model, and approval by a fraction of workers is natural. The problem is that the direct computational task—given those types and masses, find the platform with greatest approval mass—is just a sweep over the interval endpoints. Sorting the \(O(\tau)\) endpoints and tracking the active mass gives the exact answer in polynomial time. The finite problem is equally straightforward. This does not expose a complexity landscape.

The stronger candidate is the weighted version of Theorem 4.1 itself: determine the least possible maximum approval mass across pairwise-intersecting distributions of double intervals. That is a coherent extremal question, and could be worthwhile mathematics. But it is still a theorem about a class of societies, not an algorithmic problem on a supplied society. Turning it into a computational task requires adding, for example, a catalog of allowable types and asking which compatible types should make up the worst society. That adds a type-selection problem the paper does not pose; any combinatorial difficulty would come from that added choice. Likewise, adding costs for changing approval sets would introduce an intervention problem absent from the paper. The proponent’s clone argument does not supply an efficient bridge: a common denominator for binary-rational masses can be exponential in their bit length.

The double-\(n\)-string results do not provide a stronger computational anchor. Their structure depends on each discrete symbol occurring exactly twice and on distances between every pair of symbols. The paper asks for constructions and bounds, not complexity classifications; replacing symbols by masses would require a different objective, leading back to weighted approval coverage.

So the negative case is strong as a judgment about fit with ChoCo: the natural continuous model exists, but the paper’s substantive contribution is extremal geometry, while its direct computational question is easy. The weak point is the universal claim. I can’t honestly rule out a valuable weighted extremal theorem; I can say it would be a mathematical extension, not a compelling continuous-complexity mirror of this paper.
