The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a compliant anchor for a continuous mirror: the paper has no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or another complexity classification.

The closest result is Theorem 5, proved here. It says that, in the generalized variable-population framework, Interpersonal Extensive Domain, Interpersonal Ratio IIA, Weak Pareto, Fixed-Population Anonymity, and Extensive Social Preference hold together if and only if the social welfare function satisfies Classical Utilitarianism. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are also impossibility or characterization results, not complexity results.

The paper’s variable populations do not by themselves supply a continuous-population mirror. Alternatives have finite populations drawn from a countable set of possible individuals; the paper studies how to rank alternatives using interpersonal welfare comparisons. It does not model society as mass distributed over a small set of interchangeable agent types. One could invent a computational task around evaluating or constructing a welfare ordering, but it would not mirror a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies no compliant anchor, and that is the strongest case against a continuous mirror here. Theorem 5 is the nearest candidate, but it characterizes when a social welfare function satisfies Classical Utilitarianism; it does not assert a complexity result for a computational problem. Theorems 1 and 4 characterize welfarism, while Theorems 2 and 3 give impossibility and dictatorship results. None specifies an algorithm, a runtime, or a hardness classification. The paper also gives no finite encoding of a menu of alternatives or of its welfare comparisons from which such a complexity question could be read off.

A population of interchangeable welfare types is still a plausible setting for a *new* project. Given types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), and utilities \(u_t(x)\), one could compute aggregate welfare as \(\sum_t \mu_t u_t(x)\). But for an explicitly represented alternative this is just evaluating a weighted sum. To ask a substantial computational question—such as finding the best alternative—one must additionally specify how alternatives and feasibility constraints are represented. The resulting complexity would come from those additions, not from a computational result in Nebel’s paper.

The paper’s variable populations do not supply that missing problem: each alternative has a finite set of named individuals, and the paper studies interpersonal comparisons among their lives. Recasting those individuals as mass over types may be sensible, but it changes the representation without turning the axiomatization into a complexity theorem. Existing high-multiplicity work would support the plausibility of that regime; it would not create an anchor in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for excluding this paper as a ChoCo mirror. The broader claim that no worthwhile computational project could be built from its ideas is weaker: such a project could be interesting, but it would be a new computational question, not a continuization of a named computational result here.
