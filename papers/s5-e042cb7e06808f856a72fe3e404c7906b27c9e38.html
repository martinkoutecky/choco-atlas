The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(k\ge3\), given a finite set of network types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a feasible rational supply \(\kappa\) of channels per unit of network mass, and type-level bid tables for every channel pattern, choose \(x_{p,S}\ge0\) to maximize total revenue. Here \(p\in T\) is the primary type, \(S\) is a multiset of at most \(k\) secondary types, and each pattern earns the primary bid plus the bids of its secondary slots. Require \(\sum_{p,S}x_{p,S}=\kappa\) and \(\sum_{p,S}a_t(p,S)x_{p,S}\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\), where \(a_t(p,S)\) counts the slots of type \(t\).

The model it lives in

The market is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete network types, with per-capita channel supply \(\kappa\). The decision variables \(x_{p,S}\) assign channel mass to primary and secondary type patterns; type-mass constraints ensure that each unit of network mass is used for at most one role. Maximize the type-level bids earned across all assigned channel mass. For fixed \(k\), explicitly listed patterns give a rational linear program with polynomially many variables in \(\tau\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's \(k\)-DBA allocation problem for fixed \(k\ge3\), when networks share complete bid profiles, and also gives a type-cohort version of Theorem 1. It does not establish that the arbitrary identity-specific instances used in the hardness reduction occur in a small-support regime, nor does it cover the paper's independent-bid approximation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What integrality gap or rounding guarantee relates this mass allocation to finite markets with \(n_t\) networks of each type and \(M\) channels?
  • Can succinct descriptions of context-dependent type bids be optimized efficiently, or do they create a hard pricing problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s allocation problem in a market with many repeated network profiles. The agents are the bidding networks—not the wireless devices inside them. A type records everything the auction uses: traffic and access profile, and the network’s bid for each role and each possible set of co-occupants. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of bidding networks of type \(t\). The regime I have in mind is a large pool of standardized network deployments—say, local or industrial networks in repeated regional access markets—where \(n\) is large, \(\tau\) is modest, and channel opportunities scale with the pool. Networks of a type share the same bid behavior, including how they value sharing with other types. That last condition matters: if their counterpart-specific bids differ, they are different types.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It states that for each fixed \(k\ge 3\), \(k\)-DBA is NP-complete: given bids, allocate channels so that each has one primary network and at most \(k\) secondary networks, each bidder takes part on at most one channel, and the revenue reaches a given threshold. The proof reduces \(k\)-dimensional matching to the exact-\(k\)-secondary version, then reduces that problem to \(k\)-DBA. Thus the optimization problem is NP-hard. This is a worthwhile anchor because it is a genuine hardness result about the allocation question, not just about expressing bids.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Fix \(k\ge 3\). The input is a finite supported type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a rational channel supply \(\kappa\) per unit of network mass, and rational bid tables. A channel pattern is a primary type \(p\) and a multiset \(S\) of at most \(k\) secondary types; repeated types mean distinct networks with the same type. Let \(x_{p,S}\ge 0\) be the mass of channels allocated to that pattern. Write \(a_t(p,S)\) for the number of slots of type \(t\) in the pattern. The feasible allocations satisfy
\[ \sum_{p,S}x_{p,S}=\kappa,\qquad \sum_{p,S}a_t(p,S)x_{p,S}\le \mu_t\quad(t\in T). \]
If \(b^P_p(S)\) is the primary’s bid and \(b^S_t(p,S\setminus\{t\})\) is a secondary network’s bid in that context, maximize
\[ \sum_{p,S}x_{p,S}\left( b^P_p(S)+\sum_{\text{secondary slots }t\in S} b^S_t(p,S\setminus\{t\}) \right). \]
The solution is a maximizing assignment of channel mass to patterns. The constraints preserve the paper’s one-role-per-network rule and its channel structure; the objective preserves its revenue objective and dependence on who shares a channel. At the microscopic level, every channel still has integral roles. The fractional quantities describe the allocation across a large population and its per-capita channel supply.

For fixed \(k\), this mirror is Class A: tractable. With explicit bid tables there are at most \(\sum_{j=0}^{k}O(\tau^{j+1})\) patterns, so the problem is a rational linear program of size polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. This does not claim that the paper’s NP-hard instances become easy unchanged. Its 3-dimensional-matching reduction gives elements distinct roles in the construction; preserving those arbitrary distinctions can require \(\tau\) to grow with \(n\). The continuized result is about the high-multiplicity regime where many networks really do share complete bid profiles.

A useful supporting anchor is Theorem 1, also proved in the paper. Under Assumptions 1 and 2—exactly one secondary per channel, and each network used on at most one channel—the revenue-maximizing allocation is obtained from a maximum-weight matching with exactly \(M\) edges. Each edge’s weight is the better revenue of the two possible primary/secondary orientations. The authors give a polynomial-time solution using a cited modification of the matching algorithm. Its continuous mirror is the one-secondary special case: for each unordered type pair \(\{i,j\}\), let \(x_{ij}\) be channel mass assigned to that pair, and let
\[ r_{ij}=\max\!\left\{ b_i^P(j)+b_j^S(i),\; b_j^P(i)+b_i^S(j) \right\}. \]
For identical types \(i=j\), the pair consumes two distinct networks of that type. Given type masses and channel supply \(\kappa\), maximize \(\sum_{i\le j}r_{ij}x_{ij}\), subject to \(\sum_{i\le j}x_{ij}=\kappa\) and \(\sum_{j\ne i}x_{ij}+2x_{ii}\le\mu_i\). This is a small LP, a fractional matching over type cohorts, and is also Class A.

Both mirrors retain the authors’ central decision—who gets primary and secondary access on each channel—and the paper’s revenue-maximizing objective. The continuum allows mass to be allocated across the same role combinations; it does not replace network allocation with an outcome lottery or an analytic opinion-dynamics question. Theorem 2 is the stronger anchor: it creates the useful question of whether the paper’s discrete hardness survives when many bidders share a small number of complete types. Theorem 1 independently confirms that the same allocation structure has a natural continuous formulation.

The weak point is the regime. A regulator’s channel supply may not grow with the number of bidders, and real networks may have distinct beliefs and bids for particular counterparties. In either case, the proposed high-multiplicity regime may fit poorly, or \(\tau\) may approach \(n\). I would not claim this mirror covers the paper’s full model or its unrestricted hard instances. Its value is narrower: it gives a concrete, author-recognizable continuous version of the allocation problem for repeated, type-homogeneous markets. Further questions include how well its LP solutions round to finite markets, what happens when \(k\) is part of the input, and whether succinct context-bid representations make the pricing problem hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that identity is part of the bidding model. In the dependent-bid sections, a network’s value can depend on *which* networks share a channel with it. In the Theorem 2 reduction, that dependence carries the combinatorics: a bid can distinguish one particular tuple of networks from another. Under ChoCo’s definition of type, a network’s complete type must include its whole pattern of such bids. In these instances, the types are therefore generally all distinct, so \(\tau\) grows with \(n\). Writing each network as mass \(1/n\) does not create a high-multiplicity regime; rescaling the channel variables recovers an instance with individual capacity constraints.

The proposed LP is coherent if the market instead has a few classes of networks whose bids depend only on the types in each channel pattern. But that changes the key feature of the hard instances: arbitrary identity-specific tuple values become anonymous type-composition values. The reduction’s input is an arbitrary hypergraph of compatible triples; a fixed set of exchangeable types cannot express that input without adding enough types to track its distinctions. The case against this mirror is thus not that the symmetric problem is easy. It is that the paper gives little reason to expect a few repeatable, complete bid profiles to describe the markets that motivate its dependent-bid model. Real sharing values can depend on locations, transmission patterns, and beliefs about particular counterpart networks.

Theorem 1 has the same tension. A type-level fractional matching is a sensible model for cohorts with identical pairwise bids. But with distinct pairwise bids, the complete type records a network’s relationships to the other bidders, again tending to make \(\tau\) grow with \(n\). Aggregating those bidders loses matching constraints: for example, a fractional matching can take half of each edge in a high-value triangle, even though no integral matching can take two of those edges. The proposed mirror becomes faithful only in the cohort regime, where the pairwise values repeat; that regime is plausible as a designed market, but it is not established by the paper’s identity-dependent matching model.

Scaling channels per network does not by itself settle this problem. A fixed spectrum supply in one region does not grow with the number of bidders. Pooling many regions could make supply scale, but if regions clear independently, the resulting mass allocation describes an average across separate auctions. The programme allows per-capita supply as a co-modification, so this is not a formal objection; it does make the proposed regime less directly grounded in the paper’s setting.

That is the strongest negative case, and it has a clear limit. The paper’s independent-bid model is more amenable to repeated types, and a deliberately standardized market with a few network classes and type-level co-sharing bids is coherent. In that setting, the proposed mirrors survive as legitimate high-multiplicity questions. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario. The negative case is a relevance objection: the paper’s most distinctive results use bidder-specific context, while the mirror’s useful continuous form requires that context to repeat by type. Whether that gap is enough to make the mirror not worth ChoCo’s time remains uncertain.
