The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous population of network operator units, with a question about stable coalition formation. That is more faithful to the paper’s main result than making only the users continuous: the paper’s players are NOs, and its one-BS-per-NO assumption lets each operator unit be described by the BS and customer base it brings.

The anchor is Proposition 2 (Nash-stability), proved in this paper: “Any final partition \(\Pi_f\) resulting from Algorithm 1 is Nash-stable.” The paper defines this to mean that every NO weakly prefers its current coalition to joining any other coalition in the partition or acting alone. This is an algorithmic guarantee, not a polynomial-time result. The paper notes that its search can take up to the \(N\)th Bell number of steps. It cites the NP-completeness of finding a Nash-stable partition from elsewhere, in reference [25]; I do not treat that citation as a hardness result proved by this paper.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be a dense shared-coverage deployment with many independently managed local cellular or micro-operator units. Think hundreds or thousands of units, each with one BS and its own customer cohort, but only a small number \(\tau\) of recurring technical and commercial profiles. The paper’s own experiments use five NOs, so they do not instantiate this regime. The case rests on the broader setting its model already permits: operators can share users and BSs, and, in the simplified model, an NO and its BS are interchangeable.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Atomless Green-NO Coalition Stability. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), \(|T|=\tau\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A type is a complete operator-unit description: BS capacity and power parameters, energy price, customer load and revenue profile, QoS mix, and coalition cost. A coalition with composition \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{\tau}\) has value \(v(x)\), the aggregate net profit after optimally allocating its fluid user demand across its BS mass, including QoS penalties and coordination costs. This fluid allocation is the co-modification needed to scale the paper’s user-to-BS optimization with operator mass.

To extend the paper’s Shapley-based allocation, use a continuous Shapley, or Aumann–Shapley, allocation. For a piecewise-linear \(v\), define type \(t\)’s aggregate share by
\[ A_t(x)=x_t\int_0^1 \partial_t v(\lambda x)\,d\lambda \]
where the derivative is taken wherever it exists along the ray. Then \(u_t(x)=A_t(x)/x_t\) is the per-unit payoff when \(x_t>0\); define the singleton payoff \(u_t(0)\) by the limit for a type-\(t\) coalition of vanishing mass.

A candidate solution is a finite list of coalition compositions \(x^1,\ldots,x^q\) summing to \(\mu\). It is stable if, for every type \(t\) present in coalition \(j\),
\[ u_t(x^j)\ge \max\bigl(\{u_t(x^k):1\le k\le q\}\cup\{u_t(0)\}\bigr). \]
That is the nonatomic unilateral-deviation version of the paper’s Nash-stability condition: an infinitesimal operator unit compares its current coalition with each existing coalition and with going alone. The computational question is to decide whether such a finite-support stable partition exists and, if so, output its coalition compositions and verify the inequalities.

I expect the general problem to be continuum-specifically hard, or at least to require an existence theorem absent from the finite case. The finite algorithm’s termination argument uses a finite set of partitions; a continuum of possible mass splits removes that argument. The discrete NP-completeness cited in [25] does not by itself transfer: it concerns partitions of distinct players, while this mirror permits type mass to split. A useful next result would characterize when a stable finite-support partition exists, bound how many coalition blocks are needed, and determine whether finite replicated games converge to this solution concept.

This mirror covers the paper’s guarantee about the stability of the resulting coalition structure and uses its coalition-profit calculation. It does not claim that the paper’s five-NO experiments or its full algorithm transfer unchanged.

The weakest point is the regime and the limit: real mobile operators are few and differentiated, and atomless unilateral stability is weaker than finite-player Nash stability because one infinitesimal operator unit has negligible effect on coalition value. The case survives only for deployments with many genuinely independent, repeated local operator units. The paper’s one-BS-per-NO abstraction and roaming model make that scenario recognizable, but the Aumann–Shapley payoff and continuum stability condition would need author and referee buy-in.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor is Proposition 2: any terminal partition produced by Algorithm 1 is Nash-stable. The trouble is that it is a correctness guarantee, not a complexity result. Proposition 1 supplies the finite-state convergence argument; Proposition 2 says what a terminal state guarantees. Neither establishes an efficient algorithm or a complexity boundary that continuization could change. A continuous version could still pose a new question, but it would be a question about a new stability concept, not a relaxation of a computational theorem in this paper.

The proposed operator-mass model makes that change explicit. An NO in the paper is an autonomous organization that chooses whether to join or leave a coalition, and owns an indivisible BS with an on/off decision. Replacing many such organizations by infinitesimal mass means an operator can effectively be split across coalitions; a single operator’s deviation no longer changes a coalition’s value. That is a coherent nonatomic-game model, but it is not the paper’s unilateral deviation by a provider. Nor does Aumann–Shapley allocation follow automatically from the paper’s Shapley-based payoff rule: the choice of how to allocate coalition profit helps determine preferences and hence stability. The proposed mirror would need both a new player model and a substantive new payoff rule.

The strongest scenario for it is a large population of independent, nearly identical local operators. That is conceivable, but the paper’s cellular setting makes it a strained instance regime: BS location, coverage, load, and ownership shape what an operator contributes, while the paper assumes a small set of operators whose coverage overlaps. To keep only a small number of types as the operator population grows, one must make these units increasingly interchangeable. If they are separate local markets, their BSs cannot simply be pooled into one coalition allocation problem; if they are interchangeable units in one market, the atomless stability model is doing substantial conceptual work beyond aggregating repeated agents.

A better alternative is to make the users continuous and keep the NOs finite. This is much more natural: users can be grouped by QoS and revenue profile, with mass allocated among the coalition’s BSs. But it does not rescue Proposition 2 as a continuous result. The strategic players remain the same finite NOs, so stability is still over a finite set of partitions. The allocation model may become a useful mixed-integer optimization problem with fluid demand, though the BS on/off decisions remain binary. That could be worthwhile work, but it is a different computational question; the paper’s named stability guarantee does not become more informative through it.

So the negative case is strongest against the proposed operator-continuum mirror: it has no complexity theorem to mirror, and its natural repair changes the strategic unit and stability notion. I would not claim the broader “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” conclusion is established. A continuous-user optimization study is admissible under the programme’s scope and could be useful in its own right; it simply does not preserve the paper’s central named result.
