The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no qualifying anchor: it prints no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result, and it makes no complexity classification. Section 4 describes an algorithm; Sections 7–8 report experiments and applications. The closest anchor-shaped result is the unnumbered claim in Section 6, proved in the paper: for a fixed profile of approval ballots, the set of parliaments obtained by assigning each ballot to one party it approves is convex; its extreme points correspond to assigning ballots by party orderings. I cannot honestly cite that as a numbered complexity result.

The strongest positive case is a conditional mirror of that Section 6 result. Consider a nationwide election with many voters but relatively few distinct approval ballots. Let each type be a nonempty set \(S\) of approved parties, and let \(\mu_S\) be its fraction of the population. For each \(p\in S\), let \(x_{S,p}\) be the mass of type \(S\) assigned to party \(p\). A parliament has party shares \(q_p=\sum_{S\ni p}x_{S,p}\), where \(x_{S,p}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_{p\in S}x_{S,p}=\mu_S\).

Call the resulting question Continuous Representative-Parliament Membership: given rational masses \((\mu_S)\) and a proposed rational share vector \(q\), is there an allocation \(x\) satisfying those constraints and producing \(q\)? A solution is the allocation itself. This captures the paper’s central question—whether a proposed parliament is compatible with voters’ approvals—while making the population the continuous object. The input has \(\tau\) supported ballot types, even though the universe of possible types has size \(2^m-1\).

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: voters in a large electorate can share the same approval ballot, and the paper’s assignment rule already uses only those ballots and their counts. Its authors should recognize the mirror. The expected answer is tractable: membership is a fractional bipartite-flow feasibility problem between ballot types and the parties each type approves, solvable in polynomial time in the support size, number of parties, and input bit length. The mirror covers the assignment space in Section 6, and its allocation model also accommodates the greedy assignment in Section 4. It does not cover the paper’s claims about governability or the behavioral conclusions of its simulations.

The weak point is substantial: this is a new computational formulation around an unnumbered geometric result, not a complexity result the paper actually proves. The continuous assignment region is naturally convex, and the discrete paper already aggregates voters by repeated ballot type. So the mirror is credible as a high-multiplicity model, but this paper offers little of the discrete-hardness-versus-continuous-tractability contrast that makes a strong ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper offers no computational complexity result to mirror. Section 6 gives a geometric claim about the set of representative parliaments; Section 4 describes a greedy assignment rule. Neither states a complexity problem or classifies its difficulty. The proposed “Continuous Representative-Parliament Membership” is therefore a new computational question built around the paper’s geometry, not a continuous version of a computational result the paper establishes.

There is a sharper wrinkle: Section 6’s proof already uses a simplex for each ballot type, allowing the type’s ballots to be split fractionally among approved parties. That is exactly the mass allocation in the proposed mirror. For indivisible voters, the attainable set need not be convex: one voter who approves \(a\) and \(b\) can be assigned to either party, but not half to each. So the paper’s convexity claim either already reasons with divisible mass or conflates the finite attainable set with its convex hull. In either reading, a continuous membership problem would formalize the paper’s existing geometric argument rather than uncover a missing computational result.

The stronger proposed mirror is sensible. Large electorates with repeated approval ballots are a plausible high-multiplicity regime; ballot type is a complete description for this assignment task; and the mass allocation preserves which parties each type approves. One could also ask for the most majoritarian feasible parliament under additional constraints. I cannot defeat those formulations on modeling grounds, and their being easy to solve is not by itself an objection.

So the defensible negative case is narrow: under a remit that asks for continuous mirrors of computational results, this paper supplies no qualifying anchor. The broader claim that *no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario* is weak. The flow formulation survives as a credible, author-recognizable computational extension of the paper’s central geometric result, even though it is not a mirror of a complexity result the paper actually proves.
