The anchor — hardness

Theorem 3.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 3.1 — survived: The fixed-\(k\) objection limits transfer, but the finite-replication question preserves buyer-level MIWR and is a credible high-multiplicity extension.
  • Theorem 4.1 — survived: The same fixed-\(k\) gap applies, while the opponent acknowledges that a repeated budgeted-additive market is a plausible question.

The continuous mirror question

Fix a finite catalog \(T\) of complete valuation types \(v_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(b_j\). For admissible replication sizes \(N\) with \(N\mu_t,Nb_j\in\mathbb Z\), there are \(N\mu_t\) buyers of type \(t\) and \(Nb_j\) indivisible units of good \(j\); each buyer receives a whole bundle \(S\subseteq[m]\). For a given \(\rho\in(0,1]\), does a uniform polynomial-time family of randomized MIWR mechanisms exist, with each finite-\(N\) range independent of reports, whose aggregate type-bundle masses converge to feasible \(x\) and whose limiting expected welfare is at least \(\rho\,\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,b)\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,b)=\max_x\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\), subject to \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\)? Determine which \(\rho\) are achievable, or prove a complexity barrier.

The model it lives in

A finite catalog of buyer types carries masses \(\mu_t\), and each good has per-capita supply \(b_j\). Variables \(x_{t,S}\) describe the mass of type \(t\) receiving the indivisible whole bundle \(S\); welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). Finite replications use report-independent MIWR or MIR ranges over integral buyer allocations, with VCG-compatible incentives.

The objection that survived

Both anchors fix \(k\), while the mirror lets the buyer population \(N\) grow, so their \(1/k+\epsilon\) barrier supplies no hardness conclusion in the limit.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1 for regular valuation types and Theorem 4.1 for budgeted-additive types. It leaves the paper's shattering lemmas and its fixed-bidder complexity results outside the high-multiplicity regime.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the item-level shattering argument yield a hardness barrier when the number \(N\) of repeated buyers grows and the type support stays bounded?
  • Can finite-\(N\) truthful MIWR or MIR families attain any fixed positive limiting approximation ratio under proportional supplies?
  • What input-size measure and uniformity conditions give a meaningful polynomial-time bound for these mechanism families?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity combinatorial auction: many buyers share a small catalog of complete valuation types, while the supply of each good scales with the population. I would lead with Theorem 3.1. The paper proves it here: for any regular valuation class \(\mathcal C\) whose two-player social-welfare maximization problem is APX-hard, any fixed \(k\geq1\), and any constant \(\epsilon>0\), no polynomial-time randomized MIWR algorithm for \(k\)-player combinatorial auctions achieves expected approximation ratio \(1/k+\epsilon\), unless \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{P}/\mathrm{poly}\). MIWR allows an allocation to be canceled with some probability, so this is a hardness result for a broad mechanism class.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved in the paper, with its proof omitted as parallel to the earlier argument. For budgeted-additive valuations, no polynomial-time randomized MIR mechanism for \(k\)-bidder combinatorial auctions achieves a \(1/k+\epsilon\) approximation to optimal social welfare, unless \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{P}/\mathrm{poly}\). This is narrower in valuation domain and mechanism class, but valuable in its own right: the paper notes that the underlying budgeted-additive welfare problem admits an FPTAS. I would keep it as a second question because it tests whether the mechanism barrier persists even where welfare optimization alone is comparatively easy.

Here is the continuous market I would use for both. There are \(m\) good classes and a finite catalog \(T\) of complete buyer types. Each type \(t\) has a normalized monotone valuation \(v_t:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\); in the second question, \(v_t(S)=\min\{B_t,\sum_{j\in S}a_{tj}\}\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and good \(j\) has per-capita supply \(b_j\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\). For a finite replication by \(N\), there are \(N\mu_t\) buyers of type \(t\) and \(Nb_j\) indivisible copies of good \(j\), for denominators where these are integers. Each buyer receives an integral bundle.

The continuous allocation records how mass is assigned to whole bundles: \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\subseteq[m]\). It satisfies
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\leq b_j \quad\text{for each }j. \]
Per-capita welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). Thus fractional \(x_{t,S}\) means that different members of a type receive different integral bundles; it does not give any one buyer a fractional bundle.

The questions I would hand to a prover are:

  • Lead — High-Multiplicity Regular MIWR Welfare. Given the valuation catalog, rational type masses and per-capita supplies, does there exist a uniform polynomial-time family of randomized MIWR mechanisms whose finite-replication outcomes converge to a feasible mass allocation \(x\), with limiting expected welfare at least \(\epsilon\) times the continuous optimum, for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\)? The range at each finite \(N\) must be fixed independently of reported valuations, and the mechanism must retain the paper’s MIWR meaning, with VCG-compatible payments. The intended regime is \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau=|T|\), and each represented type has many copies. A solution is a uniform mechanism family and its limiting allocation guarantee, or an impossibility proof under a stated complexity assumption. The \(1/N+\epsilon\) threshold in the paper motivates the positive constant \(\epsilon\) in the limit.
  • High-Multiplicity Budgeted-Additive MIR Welfare. Use the same instance and output, restricted to budgeted-additive types and finite-replication randomized MIR mechanisms. Ask whether such a uniform polynomial-time family can attain limiting ratio \(\epsilon\) for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\). A solution must give the MIR ranges, selection algorithm and welfare guarantee, or rule out such a family. This is a separate mirror of Theorem 4.1, not a claim that its FPTAS result for welfare optimization also solves the mechanism problem.

I expect Class B for both: the named proofs use shattering of allocation ranges over subsets of the \(m\) items, and the supply scaling leaves those item-level choices in place. That is a reason to investigate hardness transfer, not a transfer theorem. In particular, the continuous welfare benchmark may have tractable formulations or approximation algorithms while the fixed-range incentive constraint remains the source of difficulty.

The weak point is substantial. In the paper, \(k\) is the number of strategic bidders and is fixed; it is not the number of valuation types in a large population. Its hard profiles need not have \(N\gg\tau\), and may rely on bidders with distinct valuations. Also, standard VCG truthfulness is defined for finite agents: in an atomless limit, one person’s report has zero mass. The finite-replication definition preserves that strategic question, but the paper does not show that its hardness survives when every type is repeated many times. The next questions are whether the shattering reductions can be realized in that regime and whether a useful limit of the finite mechanisms exists. So this is a concrete, plausible research lead anchored in the paper’s mechanism results, but the anchors do not establish its complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that their computational object is tied to a fixed number \(k\) of strategic bidders. The paper varies the item set; it does not take the bidder population to a limit. That mismatch matters for both the theorem and its incentive constraints.

Theorem 3.1 is a genuine complexity result, but it rules out better-than-\(1/k\) approximation for fixed \(k\). If \(k\) stays fixed, there is no large population to aggregate. If \(N\) repeated buyers replace those bidders, the proposed mirror changes \(k\) into \(N\), outside the theorem’s stated regime. Treating each valuation type as one mass player keeps the number of players fixed, but changes the strategic unit from an individual buyer to a whole bloc.

One can repair the proposal by asking for finite-\(N\) MIWR mechanisms on compressed type counts, with supply scaling with \(N\), whose outcomes approach a mass allocation. That is well-defined, but its MIR or MIWR condition still concerns each finite buyer’s unilateral report and the mechanism’s range over individual assignments. The aggregate vector \(x_{t,S}\) records how much type mass gets each bundle; it does not, by itself, encode those individual incentive constraints. In an atomless limit, one person’s report has zero effect on aggregate mass. Requiring exact truthfulness at every finite \(N\) preserves the strategic problem, but leaves a sequence of discrete mechanism problems rather than a continuum mechanism problem with the paper’s original guarantee.

Theorem 4.1 has the same obstacle. Repeated budgeted-additive buyers with proportional supply are a plausible market, and the budgeted-additive domain makes a reasonable second question. But the theorem’s \(1/k\) barrier is again for a fixed bidder count. Its FPTAS for welfare optimization does not dispose of the mechanism question; equally, the theorem does not establish a barrier for a large repeated population. A finite-replication version would need its own incentive model and complexity analysis.

I would not use outcome continuity, the welfare FPTAS, or existing high-multiplicity work as objections. The last supports the plausibility of the regime. Nor can I rule out that authors would find the repaired market problem worthwhile. The negative case is therefore limited: it defeats the claim that these two theorems already provide a well-aligned continuous mirror, but it does not defeat the stronger high-multiplicity mechanism-design question. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is weak on this paper.
