  • Theorem 5.1 — survived: The opponent shows that identity-level information and the six-agent promise do not transfer directly, but the type-mass prediction-validation question remains a recognizable analogue.

The continuous mirror question

Given τ resident types and τ hospital types, each with mass \(1/\tau\), strict rankings over the opposite-side types, a predicted bijection π, and an error bound η, does there exist a stable flow \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with every row and column summing to \(1/\tau\), such that every edge with \(x_{ij}>0\) is within rank displacement η of the predicted partner on both sides? Stability means no type pair has positive mass on both sides assigned to types each ranks below the other. In the rank-query version, rankings are exposed by rank queries and instances may promise that at most \(6/\tau\) mass leaves the predicted flow.

The model it lives in

A society consists of resident and hospital type distributions; members of each type share the same rankings and predicted partner type. The decision variable is a mass flow \(x_{ij}\) with prescribed type masses, and the question is whether a stable flow exists whose used edges all stay within the prediction error bound.

The objection that survived

The theorem’s promise that at most six agents change does not carry over directly: with \(M\) copies of each type, six agents have mass \(6/(M\tau)\), while the proposed \(6/\tau\) promise allows six whole type masses.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.1’s prediction-validation lower bound. It does not cover the WDA and PDA correctness or proposal bounds, the other lower-bound claims, or the empirical results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this stable-flow feasibility question be decided in time polynomial in τ and the input bit length?
  • Does fractional flow eliminate the set-disjointness query lower bound, and what scaling of the near-perfect promise is meaningful as the number of copies per type grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s lower bound on validating a predicted stable matching.

Theorem 5.1, proved in the paper using a reduction from set disjointness, says that given a predicted matching \(\hat{\mu}\) and error bound \(\eta\), any algorithm deciding whether its maximum error is at most \(\eta\) needs \(\Omega((n-2\eta)^2)\) queries to preference lists in the worst case. This remains true even when \(\hat{\mu}\) can be made stable by changing the matches of at most six agents. The set-disjointness lower bound it builds on is cited from earlier work; Theorem 5.1 itself is proved here.

Here is a continuous version of that decision problem. There are \(\tau\) resident types and \(\tau\) hospital types, each with mass \(1/\tau\). A type specifies a strict ranking of the opposite-side types and a predicted partner type; members of the same type share those descriptions. The predicted matching is a bijection \(\pi\) between types. A candidate continuous matching is a flow \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) from resident type \(i\) to hospital type \(j\), with every row and column summing to \(1/\tau\).

Call \(x\) stable if no pair of types both has positive mass assigned to partners they rank below each other. Define its maximum prediction error as the largest rank displacement, on either side, over any pair \((i,j)\) with \(x_{ij}>0\), measured against the predicted partner \(\pi(i)\) or \(\pi^{-1}(j)\). The problem is: given the type rankings, \(\pi\), and \(\eta\), does there exist a stable flow \(x\) whose maximum prediction error is at most \(\eta\)? For the query-complexity version, expose each type’s ranking through the same kind of rank-query oracle used for the paper’s theorem. The near-perfect promise is that some stable flow can be obtained from the predicted flow by moving at most \(6/\tau\) total endpoint mass.

This is a population mirror rather than a new outcome-space or noise model: the society is the distribution over resident and hospital types, and \(x\) records how much population mass is matched across types. It is plausible in large repeated matching markets where many applicants and programs share the same relevant rankings and prediction templates. If there are \(M\) copies of each type, the market has \(N=M\tau\) agents per side while the represented society has only \(\tau\) types. That is the regime where a type-level query bound matters. The authors should recognize the question: it retains their object (stable matching), their prediction error, and their central efficiency issue, while replacing individual agents with mass.

I expect a hardness-transfer, Class B-style result at the support-size scale: the disjointness bits in Theorem 5.1 live in cross-type rankings, so repeating each type many times should not remove them. The natural target would be a lower bound on the order of \(\Omega((\tau-2\eta)^2)\) type-level queries, not the original \(\Omega(N^2)\) bound in total population. The paper’s theorem does not establish this transfer: its exact bound is an oracle-query lower bound, not NP-hardness, and the continuous flow permits fractional assignments that may evade the discrete gadget. Whether the reduction survives that fractionalization is the key research question.

This mirror covers Theorem 5.1’s prediction-validation lower bound, not the paper’s WDA/PDA correctness results or empirical claims. Its weakest point is precisely the flow model: if fractional splitting makes near-predicted stability much easier, the lower bound may dissolve. Even then, the question remains a good mirror because it asks whether prediction-based stability validation becomes easier when the *population* is represented by types and mass—and identifies the support size as the likely source of any remaining difficulty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.1 is the proponent’s only anchor, and its reduction makes its high-multiplicity translation difficult. The disjointness bits sit in individual preference lists: each hospital’s list encodes a row of \(A\), and each resident’s list encodes a column of \(B\). A type that is complete for stable matching must preserve not only an agent’s own preferences but also how the other side ranks them. With strict rankings over named agents, the gadget’s agents generally cannot be merged without losing those bits. Keep them distinct and the support grows with \(n\); merge them and the reduction’s identity-specific information disappears.

The proposed flow is a sensible model for a different regime, where agents rank counterpart *types* and members of a type are interchangeable. But its rank-error bound just restricts which type-to-type edges may carry mass. That gives a capacitated stable-matching problem on the type graph, rather than Theorem 5.1’s query problem over individual lists. Its \(\Omega(\tau^2)\) type-query bound would match the size of an explicit table of \(\tau\) rankings over \(\tau\) types. Replicating each type \(M\) times raises the population to \(M\tau\), but does not create an \(\Omega((M\tau)^2)\) barrier. The “at most six agents” promise also loses its original scale when agents are aggregated: six identities inside a large type are not six distinguishable units of the type-level model.

So the strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed mirror does not carry over the theorem’s central phenomenon: a constant number of matching changes can coexist with a need to inspect quadratically many identity-level preferences. Preserving that phenomenon seems to require preserving the identities; compressing to types changes the problem being bounded. Still, I cannot honestly turn this into a convincing claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A typed, mass-based stability question may be sensible for large markets with genuinely interchangeable participants. This argument defeats the claimed transfer of Theorem 5.1’s lower bound; the broader universal negative case remains weak.
