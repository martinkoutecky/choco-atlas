What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the majority consistency guarantees in Theorem 29 and Corollary 41. It leaves aside the paper’s other agenda characterizations and results across logics; the paper explicitly leaves computational complexity for future work.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly provide a compliant computational anchor. The paper says in §11 that it does not discuss computational complexity and leaves that work for the future. Its numbered results concern aggregation and consistency, not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise. For example, Theorem 29, proved here, says that *every agenda in additive linear logic (ALL) is safe for majority rule*: majority aggregation of complete, robustly consistent judgments always produces a robustly consistent outcome. Corollary 41, also proved here from earlier results, says the same for majority aggregation when individuals reason in classical logic and collective consistency is assessed in ALL or AR. These are relevant JA results, but they are not computational-complexity anchors.

The strongest plausible continuous question nearby would be: take a large organization with many members judging the same finite agenda; let each type be a complete, classically consistent judgment set, and let \(\mu_J\) be the fraction of members of type \(J\). The continuous majority outcome is
\[ M_\mu=\left\{\varphi:\sum_{J\ni\varphi}\mu_J>\tfrac12\right\}. \]
Given the agenda and an explicit finite support of types with rational masses, ask whether \(M_\mu\) is consistent in ALL. This is a natural population model for the paper’s subject, with many members and comparatively few judgment types. The cited results suggest the answer is always yes.

But that candidate is a direct continuous extension of a consistency theorem, not a mirror of a named computational result. The paper studies no such complexity landscape, and its aggregation rule is fixed rather than an optimization or algorithmic choice. So while there is a plausible continuous-population version of the paper’s JA question, the paper supplies no qualifying anchor from which to make the requested computational case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s eligibility gate: the paper states that it does not study computational complexity, and its numbered results characterize aggregation and consistency rather than the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 29 and Corollary 41 are genuine JA results, but neither supplies a computational result to mirror.

The proposed population model is sensible. For Theorem 29, give each complete, robustly consistent judgment set a rational mass and accept a proposition when its supporters have mass greater than \(1/2\). The theorem’s consistency guarantee carries over to these weighted profiles: in ALL, an inconsistent outcome has a two-proposition witness, and two sets each of mass greater than \(1/2\) must overlap. Corollary 41 admits the same lift for classically consistent individual judgments and collective consistency assessed in ALL or AR. That establishes a continuous JA model; it does not make either result computational.

One could formulate follow-on algorithmic questions, such as deciding whether an agenda is safe or finding a low-cost population change that alters collective consistency. The first would add a complexity analysis to the paper’s agenda characterizations. The second would add an intervention objective and costs that the paper does not specify. Either could become worthwhile research, but neither is a computational result already anchored by this paper.

I cannot make the stronger claim that no scenario supports continuous judgment aggregation: a large organization with repeated judgment types is plausible. The honest negative is narrower: this paper offers a credible continuous-population interpretation, but no established computational result for ChoCo to mirror.
