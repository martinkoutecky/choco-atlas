The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bipartite conflict graph \(H=(U,F)\), rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), integer profits \(p_\theta(u)\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_u\), and a threshold \(q\), decide whether there are masses \(x_{\theta,S} \ge 0\) over independent sets \(S\) of \(H\) such that \(\sum_S x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,S:\,u\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le b_u\), and \(x_{\theta,S}>0\) only if \(\sum_{u\in S}p_\theta(u)\ge q\). Equivalently, maximize the minimum utility guarantee \(q\) while each unit of population mass receives an integral, conflict-free bundle.

The model it lives in

A continuum of depots with finitely many complete valuation types, allocating recurring indivisible job categories. The decision variables \(x_{\theta,S}\) describe the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving integral independent-set bundle \(S\); per-capita supplies constrain category use, and the objective maximizes the minimum utility received.

The objection that survived

Mass mixing may prevent the Clique reduction in Theorem 7 from transferring to the continuous problem; the proponent already identifies this as an open bridge, so it limits the hardness prediction rather than the question's worth.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror takes up Theorem 7's bipartite-graph hardness setting, in a high-volume regime with repeated job categories and a small number of valuation types. It leaves the paper's other graph-class results and its FPTAS results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the bundle LP admit an algorithm polynomial in \(|U|\), the number of supported types \(\tau\), and the input bit length, and what is the complexity of its pricing problem on bipartite graphs?
  • Can a discrete instance from Theorem 7 be encoded so that feasibility of a continuous mass assignment implies a discrete coloring, or does mass mixing make this mirror easier?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think there is a credible mirror in a high-volume allocation setting. Imagine many depots receiving recurring jobs or contract packets. There are \(N\) depots but only \(\tau\) valuation types: depots of a type have the same profit for every job category. The supply of category \(u\) is \(N b_u\) copies, where \(b_u\) is its per-depot supply; the copies remain indivisible. Two categories joined by an edge cannot be assigned together to one depot. This is plausible when categories represent recurring, incompatible jobs, and the paper itself motivates conflicts with scheduling and shipping examples. The relevant regime is \(N\gg\tau\), with the number of job categories and the conflict graph still part of the input.

My lead question is continuous fair division under conflicts. An instance has a finite bipartite conflict graph \(H=(U,F)\), rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), integer profits \(p_\theta(u)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_u\). A bundle \(S\subseteq U\) must be an independent set of \(H\). For a proposed guarantee \(q\), ask whether there are nonnegative masses \(x_{\theta,S}\) such that

\[ \sum_{S}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta \quad\text{for every }\theta,\qquad \sum_{\theta,S:\,u\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le b_u \quad\text{for every }u, \]

and \(x_{\theta,S}>0\) only when \(\sum_{u\in S}p_\theta(u)\ge q\). The output is a feasible mass assignment \(x\), or a certificate that none exists; the optimization version maximizes \(q\). Each individual receives an integral, conflict-free bundle. Only the assignment of bundles across a type’s mass is continuous. The minimum is over the utilities actually received, not type-average utility. Supplies scale with the population, as they must for a meaningful high-multiplicity allocation.

The anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper. For every fixed integer \(k\ge2\), deciding whether a bipartite conflict graph with \(k\) integer-profit functions admits a partial \(k\)-coloring in which every color class has profit at least a given threshold \(q\) is strongly NP-complete. The theorem holds even when all \(k\) profit functions are identical. The paper proves it by reduction from Clique. This is a good anchor because it isolates the difficulty of fair allocation under conflicts even where the graph is bipartite and the agents’ valuations agree.

For the mirror, I lean toward Class B: hardness transfers. The theorem’s hard combinatorics are in the growing item-conflict graph, not in a large or diverse population. The continuous instance can still have a growing graph while having many agents and few valuation types; every agent’s bundle also remains integral. But Theorem 7 does not itself prove hardness for the mass problem: mixing different bundles across a type’s population may defeat its reduction. Establishing whether that happens is part of the question, not something to assume away.

The exponential bundle formulation makes that question concrete. For fixed \(q\), feasibility is an LP over independent-set bundles; its pricing problem must find useful bundles subject to the utility threshold. Whether that can be done efficiently on bipartite graphs, and whether the Clique reduction survives mass mixing even with \(\tau=1\), are immediate follow-up questions. The scope here is Theorem 7’s bipartite hardness result, not a mirror of every graph-class result in the paper.

My weakest point is the gap between the theorem’s \(k\) individually allocated agents and a mass of agents who share types and can receive different bundles. That is a real change in the feasible assignments. The mirror still seems worth asking: it preserves the paper’s agents, profits, indivisible bundles, conflicts, and maximin objective, while changing the population regime explicitly. But a prover would need to settle whether this relaxation erases the hardness or leaves the item-side obstruction intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that Theorem 7 does not establish a high-multiplicity result. It proves hardness for each fixed \(k\), including \(k=2\), as the conflict graph grows. Its Clique reduction assigns items to a fixed number of recipient classes. The depot mirror instead has a growing population, scaled item supplies, and a mass distribution over independent-set bundles. The reduction does not show that a feasible mass distribution can be converted back into the discrete coloring: agents of the same type can split across bundles. So the theorem supplies no hardness transfer or two-way high-multiplicity dictionary for this mirror.

That gap survives the better formulations. One could give each of the \(k\) original valuation profiles a positive mass and scale item supplies, or use one agent type with per-capita supplies. Both are coherent models: each person still gets an integral, conflict-free bundle meeting the guarantee; only the population assignment is fractional. The first preserves the recipient roles, and the second fits Theorem 7’s identical-profit case especially naturally. Neither, however, is ruled out by the reduction. Establishing whether mass mixing changes the complexity is itself a real computational question.

So I can make a case that Theorem 7 is a weak *bridge* to its proposed mirror, but not that the mirror is unworthy. Calling the population scaling a distortion, or arguing that the answer might become easier, would not defeat it under the programme’s criteria. The paper gives a named computational result, and the proposed repeated-depot setting is plausible. The honest negative case is weak: this anchor supports a worthwhile question even though its discrete hardness result does not settle that question.
