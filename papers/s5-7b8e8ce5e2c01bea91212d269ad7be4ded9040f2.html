The case FOR (proponent)

I do not find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named results analyze the behavior of a stochastic process; none asserts that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The main result, Theorem 1 (proved here), says that for each window size \(w\), sufficiently large rings started from a uniformly random labeling reach a frozen configuration with high probability; the final average run length is \(O(w^2)\), and the probability that a random site lies in a run longer than \(\lambda w^2\) is at most \(c^\lambda\), for a constant \(c<1\). Theorem 2 (proved here) bounds the difference between the numbers of unhappy \(x\)- and \(o\)-individuals by \(n/w^4\), with probability greater than \(1-1/w\), up to the stated stopping time. These are genuine results, but not computational-complexity anchors of the kind requested.

The strongest positive foothold is Theorem 1’s large-population regime: \(n\) grows while \(w\) is fixed, there are two labels, and each initially has mass \(1/2\). A possible continuous shadow is a Continuum Run-Tail Limit problem. Take a shift-invariant probability law \(\mu_0\) on bi-infinite \(x/o\) configurations—specifically the fair product law for the paper’s main theorem—and consider the distribution of rooted local environments in large rings evolved by the paper’s swap rule. Ask whether the terminal rooted-environment distributions converge as \(n\to\infty\), and, if they do, determine the limit and its run-length tail. A solution would be the limiting law together with a proof of its tail bound. Theorem 1 supports the tail-bound part of this question, though it does not establish convergence to a particular terminal law.

That is a plausible population-limit analysis, but it is not a strong ChoCo mirror. The complete “type” would have to include a site’s evolving spatial environment: \(x/o\) alone does not determine happiness or swap eligibility. A finite window gives finitely many patterns, but the paper explains why their evolution is not closed at any such finite level: larger patterns keep entering the dependency chain. The rooted-environment formulation therefore has an infinite type space, and its question is about limiting dynamics and run lengths, not the computational complexity of a decision or optimization problem. The paper’s ODE is a tool for analyzing those dynamics, not itself a continuous-population model.

So the honest positive case is narrow: Theorem 1 has a natural large-population aggregate interpretation, and one can pose a precise limit-law question around it. But there are no qualifying named computational anchors, and I would not present this paper as supplying a continuous computational-social-choice problem to classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to continuize. Theorem 1 proves a high probability statement about the outcome of a stochastic process; Theorem 2 bounds an imbalance during that process. Neither studies an algorithmic decision or optimization problem, nor makes a claim about computational complexity. The paper’s differential equations are analytical tools for understanding the process. A continuous-time approximation to state frequencies does not make the population itself continuous.

Theorem 1 is the strongest possible anchor. Its best continuous question is close to the one proposed: find a limiting law for the run lengths or rooted environments in large rings. That is a sensible problem in probability and statistical mechanics; the paper even raises a normalized run-length limit as an open question. But it asks for an asymptotic description of dynamics, not for an algorithmic classification. The paper’s local dependencies also resist a finite type model: tracking finite neighborhoods creates a hierarchy that calls on ever larger neighborhoods. A complete type for the unbounded process would therefore need to encode an entire environment, leaving an infinite-dimensional law rather than a finite-support society with a tractable input description.

The spatial scaling exposes the same difficulty. With fixed \(w\), rescaling the ring to unit length makes each interaction neighborhood and each firewall shrink to zero measure as \(n\) grows. Keeping those microscopic features visible by using rooted environments gives a useful local limit, but it does not produce mass transfers between types in the ChoCo sense. In an atomless population, swapping two individual agents changes zero mass. Defining nontrivial swaps as flows of positive mass would require a new dynamics, whose outcomes are not covered by Theorem 1.

Theorem 2 has a natural limiting statement too: symmetry suggests equal masses of unhappy \(x\) and \(o\) agents in a deterministic continuum approximation. That is an analytical symmetry property, not a computational question. The theorem’s actual bound is an imbalance of at most \(n/w^4\); for fixed \(w\), its normalized bound remains \(1/w^4\) as \(n\) grows. Taking \(w\) to grow changes the regime.

A planner might ask for the least mass to relocate to produce a desired stable configuration. That could be a worthwhile new control problem, but it adds an intervention objective absent from this paper; retaining spatial neighborhoods also requires a new type and flow model. I cannot rule out such an extension being useful. The narrower conclusion is that none of the paper’s results supplies a continuous computational mirror: the run-tail limit is an analytic limit-law problem, and the balance result is an analytic estimate. Neither gives ChoCo a computational question to classify.
