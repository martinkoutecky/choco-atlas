The case FOR (proponent)

The crucial qualification is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. It contains Definition 1 (\(X\)-Recovery), Examples 1–4, and a set of proposed research directions, but no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, an approximation guarantee, or an algorithm. Its claim that shortlisting research is “tractable” is an aspiration, not a proved result. Thus there is no honest theorem-level result to mirror.

The strongest positive case is therefore a prospective mirror of the paper’s central formal framework, anchored non-computationally in Definition 1. That definition says that, given an axiom \(X\) and a voting rule \(r_N\), a shortlisting procedure satisfies \(X\)-recovery if, for every full preference profile \(P_{\mathcal A}^N\), the final decision \(r_N(P_{\shortlist}^N)\) satisfies \(X\), where \(\shortlist=r_L(P_{\mathcal A}^L)\) and the restricted preferences are induced by the full preferences. This is not a qualifying computational anchor, but it gives a precise object to continuize.

My lead problem would be Robust Continuous \(X\)-Recovery Shortlisting. Let \(A\) be a finite set of alternatives and let \(T\) be a finite set of complete voter types. A type is either an approval vector in \(\{0,1\}^{A}\) or a complete ranking of \(A\), together with every other attribute used by the voting and recovery rules. A society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters of type \(t\). For a shortlist \(S\subseteq A\), each type is restricted to \(S\), and \(r_\mu(S)\) denotes the outcome of the paper’s voting rule applied to the resulting type distribution.

Because the paper explicitly allows shortlisters to have incomplete information, the shortlister receives a rational polytope \(\mathcal U\subseteq\Delta(T)\) of societies compatible with its observations. Exact information is the special case \(\mathcal U=\{\mu\}\). The optimization problem is

\[ \min_{S\subseteq A}|S| \]

subject to

\[ r_\mu(S)=r_\mu(A)\qquad\text{for every }\mu\in\mathcal U. \]

The decision version asks whether a shortlist of size at most \(k\) exists. More generally, equality can be replaced by any specified \(X\)-recovery predicate from the paper: preservation of a welfare guarantee, proportionality condition, fairness property, or the outcome that would have been obtained by voting over all alternatives. The output is an ordinary integral shortlist \(S\); neither candidates nor the shortlist are fractional. Only the voter population is continuous.

A plausible regime is a participatory-budgeting or large-platform setting with \(n\) in the hundreds of thousands or millions, \(m\) in the hundreds or thousands, and only \(\tau\ll n\) recurring preference types because citizens use standardized approval forms or belong to stable preference cohorts. For example, a type may be a complete approval pattern over a recurring catalogue of policy proposals. The mass \(\mu_t\) is then the honest quantity: “\(16.8\%\) approve this proposal,” rather than a list of millions of nearly interchangeable voters. If shortlisters are representatives drawn from the same population, their types can similarly be summarized by a distribution \(\lambda\); if they are a small expert committee, they can remain discrete while the voter society is continuized.

This would be recognizable to the authors as their problem rather than a diluted optimization problem. It retains the two-stage structure, the shortlist as an integral set, the final vote over the restricted alternatives, the distinction between shortlisting and multiwinner election, and the paper’s concern with limited knowledge. The only essential change is replacing repeated individual profiles by type masses, exactly as the paper’s population-scale applications invite. The uncertainty polytope \(\mathcal U\) is not an arbitrary embellishment: it formalizes the paper’s repeated emphasis on partial knowledge, biased preference distributions, preference queries, and information beyond the shortlisters’ own preferences.

I would expect the general problem to be Class B rather than an easy consequence of continuity. For a fixed shortlist, many voting and recovery checks become linear or piecewise-linear in \(\mu\), so separation over \(\mathcal U\) may be tractable. But choosing \(S\) remains a combinatorial problem over alternatives. Set-system selection can plausibly be encoded through preference types and recovery constraints, with hardness driven by \(m\), \(k\), or the structure of the candidate space rather than by the number of voters. In that case the continuous formulation would preserve the underlying hardness while eliminating irrelevant dependence on \(n\). Restricted cases—fixed \(k\), bounded \(\tau\), approval voting with a simple recovery condition, or structured uncertainty polytopes—could instead be Class A and admit LP or separation-based algorithms.

The resulting research questions are concrete: when does the inner optimization over \(\mathcal U\) have an efficient separation oracle; which voting rules make recovery tractable for a fixed shortlist; can one obtain approximation guarantees trading shortlist size against recovery loss; and how does an efficient continuous solution round to a finite electorate with additive error? One can also ask whether continuizing the shortlister population changes anything when representatives are sampled from the voter society, and whether strategic shortlisters create a genuinely different continuous game.

The mirror covers the paper’s framework, \(X\)-recovery discussion, cognitive-efficiency tradeoff, and aggregation sections. It does not yet cover the paper’s speculative material on prediction-augmented algorithms, strategic games, or incentive compatibility; those would require separate computational models.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper supplies no named computational theorem to anchor this case, and the robust uncertainty formulation is a natural formalization of the paper’s prose rather than a result it proves. There is also a genuine regime question when shortlisters are a tiny, idiosyncratic expert committee rather than a high-multiplicity population. Nevertheless, the voter side of the problem has a credible high-multiplicity interpretation, and the proposed problem is a faithful continuous-population version of Definition 1 rather than a fractional-outcome reformulation. So the positive conclusion is modest: this paper supports a sensible prospective continuous mirror, but not a theorem-anchored mirror claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive at the anchoring stage: this paper contains no computational result to continuize. It has no theorem, lemma, corollary, algorithm, complexity classification, approximation guarantee, or even a fixed decision problem. Its claim that shortlisting is “tractable” is a research aspiration. Definition 1 is an axiomatic schema, quantified over unspecified \(X\), \(r_L\), and \(r_N\); it does not assert existence, computability, or optimality of anything.

The proposed Robust Continuous \(X\)-Recovery Shortlisting problem therefore is not a continuous mirror of a result in the paper. It adds the objective \(\min |S|\), an uncertainty polytope \(\mathcal U\), and exact outcome preservation. The paper mentions a tradeoff involving shortlist size and partial information, but never defines this optimization problem. In particular, \(\mathcal U\) is not generated by the paper’s shortlisting rule: it is supplied as a new input. The proposal consequently replaces the paper’s question—how should a shortlisting procedure operate under limited information?—with a robust candidate-set optimization problem.

The best repair would be to model a joint distribution over voter types and shortlister types, together with an observation channel producing \(\mathcal U\), and then ask for an efficiently representable rule \(r_L\) satisfying a recovery guarantee for every compatible society. But that exposes the problem rather than solving it. The computational object is now the synthesis or verification of a mechanism over an entire information structure, and its complexity depends on how \(X\), the observation channel, and the rule representation are chosen. Continuizing the voter population is not what makes that problem well-defined; the missing mechanism-design specification is.

Nor does fixing approval voting, a particular recovery axiom, and a finite type space restore the anchoring. That would produce a perfectly legitimate new computational-social-choice project, possibly an interesting one. But it would be a theorem about a newly selected instantiation of the authors’ agenda, not a result of this paper whose discrete high-multiplicity relaxation can be studied. The proponent’s large-population PB example shows that such a regime can be sensible; it does not show that this paper has supplied a worthwhile continuous mirror.

I would not argue that the population interpretation is intrinsically invalid. Large platforms or recurring PB electorates can plausibly have many voters in a small number of preference cohorts. The negative case is therefore not that no high-multiplicity model exists. It is narrower but substantial: the paper is a blue-sky axiomatic manifesto, and the proposed mirror must invent the computational problem, objective, information model, and rule representation that would make the continuization meaningful.

Thus I defeat the proponent’s sole anchor as an anchor. The paper supports a possible future ChoCo problem, but not a paper-anchored continuous mirror. An absolute claim that no worthwhile scenario could ever be developed would be too strong; the credible cohort-based setting makes the universal negative case honestly weak beyond this decisive absence of computational content.
