What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers evaluating Split Cycle and its undefeated set from a population distribution; it leaves the axiomatic characterization and impossibility results without computational continuations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can continuous Split-Cycle evaluation be formalized with running time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of rational \(\mu\)?
  • Does the six-axiom characterization extend from rational distributions to suitably represented real-valued \(\mu\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly speaking, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. It proves axiomatic characterizations and social-choice impossibility results, but no numbered theorem stating NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm P \), parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic formulation. The Arrow, Baigent, and Blau–Deb theorems are impossibility results, not computational hardness results. Thus the positive case must be modest: this paper supplies a very natural problem to continuize, but not an existing complexity result whose fate in the continuum can be classified.

The strongest mirror is a continuous-population computation of Split Cycle, anchored structurally by Lemma 3.7, proved in this paper. That lemma states that \(x\) defeats \(y\) precisely when \( \mathrm{Margin}_P(x,y)>0 \) and \( \mathrm{Margin}_P(x,y)>\mathrm{Split}\#_P(\rho) \) for every majority cycle \( \rho \) of the form \(x\to y\to z_1\to\cdots\to z_n\to x\). In other words, a direct majority win counts as a defeat exactly when its margin exceeds the weakest edge in every relevant cycle.

Define Continuous Split-Cycle Evaluation as follows. The candidates are a finite set \(X\), and the voter types are the complete rankings \(T=L(X)\). An instance gives a rational population distribution \( \mu\in\Delta(T) \), where \( \mu_t \) is the fraction of society with ranking \(t\), together with either a pair \(x,y\in X\) or the request to compute the entire defeat relation. Define the normalized pairwise margin by

\[ M_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t. \]

A solution must answer whether \(x\) defeats \(y\) according to the continuous Split-Cycle relation:

\[ M_\mu(x,y)>0 \]

and, for every majority cycle \( \rho \) containing \(x\) and \(y\),

\[ M_\mu(x,y)>\min_{(a,b)\in\rho}M_\mu(a,b). \]

The associated winner output is the set

\[ W_{\mathrm{SC}}(\mu)=\{x\in X:\text{ no candidate defeats }x\}. \]

There is no optimization objective in the original paper: the decision variable is the defeat relation, or equivalently the undefeated winner set. If a unique winner is required, the paper explicitly leaves that to an external tie-breaking procedure.

This is a genuine population continuization. The agents are, for example, millions of voters in a national election or party primary, grouped into ideological or party-aligned blocs that submit the same complete ranking. The regime might have \(n\) in the millions, \(m\) perhaps between \(5\) and \(20\), and only \( \tau \) distinct observed rankings, with \( \tau\ll n \). The type is exactly the information the paper uses: two voters with the same ranking are indistinguishable for Split Cycle. The mass is not a probability about an individual voter; it is the fraction of the electorate represented by that type.

The finite-to-continuous dictionary is exact. If a finite profile has \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\), let \( \mu_t=n_t/n \). Then

\[ \mathrm{Margin}_P(x,y)=n\,M_\mu(x,y). \]

All margins and splitting numbers are multiplied by the same positive factor, so every Split-Cycle comparison is unchanged. Thus the continuous rule agrees with the finite rule on every rational profile, while allowing the population to be represented directly by masses rather than by \(n\) named voters. This is a “turns out” identity, not a novelty collision: the paper studies finite profiles and integer duplication, not computational questions over a population simplex.

I would expect Continuous Split-Cycle Evaluation to be Class A. Lemma 3.7 reduces the problem to a weighted directed graph. For each pair \(x,y\), compute the maximum bottleneck strength \(B_\mu(y,x)\) of a positive-margin path from \(y\) to \(x\). Then \(x\) defeats \(y\) exactly when

\[ M_\mu(x,y)>0 \quad\text{and}\quad M_\mu(x,y)>B_\mu(y,x). \]

All pairwise margins can be computed from the type masses, and all bottleneck values can be computed by a max–min variant of Floyd–Warshall in polynomial time in \(m\), the support size of \(\mu\), and the encoding length of the masses. This gives the paper’s rule a clean high-multiplicity computational formulation, although the paper itself does not state this complexity result.

The paper’s Theorem 5.1 makes the mirror especially plausible, though it is not a computational anchor. Proved in the paper, it says that Split Cycle is the most resolute variable-election collective choice rule satisfying Anonymity and Neutrality, Availability, Upward Homogeneity, Monotonicity for two-candidate profiles, Neutral Reversal, and Coherent IIA. Every one of these properties has a natural mass-based translation. Proposition 4.1 also transfers directly as the requirement that \(W_{\mathrm{SC}}(\mu)\) is never empty.

The main further question is whether the full characterization in Theorem 5.1 survives over real-valued populations. For rational \( \mu \), finite replication gives a direct transfer. For arbitrary real \( \mu \), the proof needs either a continuity axiom or a separate weighted-realization argument; that extension is not automatic.

The weakest point is decisive: this is not a paper whose discrete computational hardness might dissolve under continuization. Its core object is already anonymous, margin-based, and homogeneous, so the continuous version is close to a compressed reformulation of the same voting rule. The positive claim is therefore only that the paper supplies a convincing, well-posed Class A population-computation problem—Continuous Split-Cycle Evaluation—not that it supplies a strong new complexity separation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 5.1 is an axiomatic characterization; Propositions 4.1 and 4.16 are structural properties; the Arrow, Baigent, and Blau–Deb results are social-choice impossibility theorems, not complexity results. Thus the proposed mirror must manufacture a computational problem rather than continue one in the paper.

The proposed Continuous Split-Cycle Evaluation is mathematically legitimate, but it is only a normalization of the paper’s existing finite computation. For a rational distribution \(\mu\) obtained from a profile \(P\) with \(n\) voters,

\[ M_\mu(x,y)=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Margin}_P(x,y). \]

Every margin and every splitting number is scaled by the same positive factor, so Lemma 3.7 gives exactly the same defeat relation. Replication already establishes this invariance in the paper through Homogeneity. Nothing computationally new happens when integer margins are divided by \(n\).

More seriously, the distribution over rankings is not computationally essential to Split Cycle. The rule depends only on the \(\binom{m}{2}\) entries of the normalized margin matrix. One may compute those entries from the type masses in \(O(\tau m^2)\) time and then apply a bottleneck-path computation to the resulting weighted graph. Equally, one may give the weighted margin graph directly and never mention voter types or a population distribution. Distinct societies with entirely different distributions over rankings are indistinguishable whenever they induce the same margin graph. The proposed “continuous population” has therefore been quotiented away before the interesting computation begins.

This is not an objection that the answer is easy. It is an objection that the proposed task has no population-specific computational content. At most, it is a compressed encoding of the paper’s already anonymized finite profiles, which the paper explicitly treats as counts of ballot types. The continuum contributes binary or rational encoding of multiplicities, but no new optimization object, separation problem, or complexity landscape.

Theorem 5.1 does not strengthen the case. Its axioms can be translated into statements about distributions, but that produces an axiomatic measure-theoretic restatement, not a computational result. For rational distributions, finite replication handles the translation immediately; for arbitrary real distributions, the issue becomes one of representing real inputs, not an algorithmic question arising from the paper. Proposition 4.1 likewise becomes the elementary fact that the undefeated set of the induced margin graph is nonempty.

A better proposal might ask for the minimum mass of voters whose rankings must be changed to make a candidate undefeated, or for the smallest perturbation of \(\mu\) that changes the winner. But these are not mirrors of anything the paper studies. They require importing a conversion model, a cost metric, or a perturbation geometry that the paper does not specify. With a Kendall–\(\tau\) cost, one has created a swap-bribery problem; with an \(L^1\) metric on \(\mu\), one has chosen an arbitrary geometry on the population simplex. Such problems may be excellent ChoCo problems, but they could be posed for any voting rule and cannot be credited to this axiomatic paper. If perturbations are made directly in margin space, the ranking population has disappeared again.

The negative case is therefore strong under the programme’s stated requirement of a named computational result: there is none, and the natural evaluation mirror is a scale-invariant graph computation whose input distribution is inessential. It is not airtight in the broadest modelling sense. Voting plainly admits high-multiplicity societies, and Continuous Split-Cycle Evaluation is a coherent question. If the programme counts any useful high-multiplicity reformulation as sufficient, this anchor survives. But on the stricter standard of a worthwhile continuous computational mirror, the paper supplies no such result; every stronger version requires adding a new intervention problem not present in the paper.
