The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T=T_R\sqcup T_P\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), endowments \(y_t\), a status-quo tax \(\bar\tau\), and lower bound \(\bar x\), let \(M_i=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_t\). Find a feasible accepted policy \((\tau,e,g)\) maximizing \(\frac{1}{M_P}\sum_{t\in T_P}\mu_t\log(y_t+e)+\log g\), subject to \(g+M_Pe\le M_R\tau\), \(y_t-\tau\ge\bar x\) for \(t\in T_R\), \(y_t+e\ge\bar x\) for \(t\in T_P\), \(g\ge\bar x\), and the rich-party acceptance constraint \(\frac{1}{M_R}\sum_{t\in T_R}\mu_t\log(y_t-\tau)+\log g\ge\frac{1}{M_R}\sum_{t\in T_R}\mu_t\log(y_t-\bar\tau)+\log\bar x\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper contains no named result in the computational-complexity sense: nothing classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest named optimization result is Proposition 3, proved in Appendix 9.5. I can use it as a policy-computation anchor, but it does not meet the paper’s strict anchor requirement or establish a complexity claim.

Proposition 3 characterizes the unique last-period proposal by the poor-party incumbent when utility is logarithmic and the consumption lower bounds equal \(\bar x\). Writing \(\Delta=y_R-y_P\) and \(Y=y_R+y_P\), the proposal’s entitlement, tax, and public-good choices are

\[ E_{P,2}= \begin{cases} \frac{\Delta}{2}-\frac{2\bar x(y_R-\bar\tau)}{Y},&\bar\tau<\frac{\Delta}{2},\\ \bar\tau-\bar x,&\frac{\Delta}{2}\leq\bar\tau<\tau_R^D,\\ e_P^D,&\bar\tau\geq\tau_R^D, \end{cases} \qquad \Psi_{P,2}= \begin{cases} y_R-\frac{2\bar x(y_R-\bar\tau)}{Y},&\bar\tau<\frac{\Delta}{2},\\ \tau_P^D,&\bar\tau\geq\frac{\Delta}{2}, \end{cases} \]

and

\[ G_{P,2}= \begin{cases} g^*,&\bar\tau<\frac{\Delta}{2},\\ y_R-\bar\tau,&\frac{\Delta}{2}\leq\bar\tau<\tau_R^D,\\ g^D,&\bar\tau\geq\tau_R^D. \end{cases} \]

The resulting consumptions are \(C_{P,P,2}=y_P+E_{P,2}\) and \(C_{R,P,2}=y_R-\Psi_{P,2}\). Here \(\tau_R^D=(\Delta+\bar x)/2\), \(\tau_P^D=y_R-\bar x\), \(e_P^D=(\Delta-\bar x)/2\), \(g^D=(Y-\bar x)/2\), and, under log utility, \(g^*=Y/2\). This is a closed-form characterization of a proposer’s optimal policy against the respondent’s veto, not a complexity result.

A plausible mirror is a mass-distribution terminal fiscal bargaining problem. Consider a country with many residents but relatively few complete fiscal types: for example, people grouped by party constituency and endowment. The instance gives a finite support \(T=T_R\cup T_P\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, endowments \(y_t\), a status-quo tax \(\bar\tau\), and the common lower bound \(\bar x\). Support size \(\tau\) counts distinct types, not residents. A type includes everything relevant here: constituency, endowment, tax exposure, entitlement eligibility, and preferences.

The poor-party proposer chooses a per-rich-person tax \(\tau\), a per-poor-person entitlement \(e\), and common public-good provision \(g\). With \(M_i=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_t\), feasibility includes \(g+M_Pe\leq M_R\tau\), \(y_t-\tau\geq\bar x\) for rich types, \(y_t+e\geq\bar x\) for poor types, and \(g\geq\bar x\). Define each party’s average current utility under a policy by
\[ \bar U_i=\frac{1}{M_i}\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_t\log(c_t)+\log(g), \]
where \(c_t=y_t-\tau\) for rich types and \(c_t=y_t+e\) for poor types. The proposer maximizes \(\bar U_P\), subject to the rich party receiving at least its status-quo utility, evaluated at \(\bar\tau\) and the rejection value \(g=\bar x\). A solution is a feasible accepted policy maximizing the proposer’s utility.

This keeps the paper’s tax, entitlement, public-good, status-quo, and veto structure. The high-multiplicity scenario is a large population represented by a small number of income and eligibility cells. The change is that each constituency may contain several endowment types, rather than one representative endowment. For the one-period problem, the expected classification is Class A: it has a constant number of policy variables, and with log utility its objective is concave while the acceptance constraint defines a convex feasible set. An \(\epsilon\)-optimal solution should be obtainable by convex optimization in time polynomial in the support size, input bit length, and requested precision. That is a prediction for this generalization, not a theorem of the paper.

The mirror covers only Proposition 3’s terminal-period bargaining choice. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s infinite-horizon Markov equilibrium, numerical method, welfare comparisons, or institutional-reform analysis. Natural next questions are whether the paper’s threshold formulas persist with heterogeneous types, and whether the infinite-horizon equilibrium can be approximated efficiently as support size grows.

The weak point is that the paper already describes two equal-measure groups, so its baseline can itself be read as the population distribution \(\mu=(1/2,1/2)\). A continuous population is therefore not a new object here. The positive case rests on asking for the computational landscape of a support-parametric extension of its bargaining problem. That extension is plausible, but the supplied paper neither studies that landscape nor supplies the required named complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against has one strong anchor problem: Proposition 3 is a closed-form characterization of a two-party proposer’s terminal-period policy, not a computational-complexity result. It gives the policy across the relevant status-quo regions; the paper does not pose a decision problem or classify an algorithm for it. A complexity question built from Proposition 3 would therefore be newly introduced, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The population is already represented by two equal-measure groups. The proposed distribution \(\mu=(1/2,1/2)\) restates that model; splitting either group into identical copies changes nothing. The paper’s parties are representative strategic actors, and their veto and proposal powers do not depend on how many residents are represented.

The strongest distinct version would give each party a distribution of income types and maximize mass-weighted average utility. But that requires a new rule for aggregating heterogeneous constituents’ interests: the paper’s single representative payoff and veto constraint do not say whether acceptance depends on average welfare, a pivotal voter, unanimity, or something else. Keeping the representative-agent model leaves type masses inert; making them matter changes the bargaining game.

A heterogeneous-type equilibrium model could still be worth studying. But its computational question would be an extension of the paper’s economic model, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result. The negative case is strongest on that distinction; it cannot rule out the extension’s value as new research.
