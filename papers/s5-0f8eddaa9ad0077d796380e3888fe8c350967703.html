The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive case that meets your anchor requirement. The paper has no named computational result: it classifies no problem by complexity and gives no algorithmic result for a computational problem.

The closest anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved here. It says that if every agent receives a signal from the truth at least once in every interval of length \(\lfloor 1/\gamma\rfloor\), the system reaches truthful consensus almost surely, with convergence rate \(O(t^{-\gamma/2d})\), where \(d\) is the maximum outdegree. Its supporting results, Lemmas 3.1 and 4.1, prove convergence of the expected mean process and almost-sure disappearance of deviations. These are results about the dynamics’ limiting behavior, not computational complexity results.

One could imagine a follow-on computational problem about choosing network interactions for a large population. But that would add an optimization question the paper does not study. Nor does replacing the \(n\) agents by masses over a finite set of types preserve the paper’s model by itself: its dynamics depend on the changing graph and agent-specific belief updates. A continuum version would need a further interaction model, and the paper provides no named computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 2.1 and Lemmas 3.1 and 4.1 establish convergence properties of a specified stochastic process: under a condition on the supplied graph sequence, beliefs converge to the truth, with a stated rate. That rate is a rate of learning over time, not an algorithm’s running time. The paper defines no computational problem and proves no complexity bound, algorithm, or approximation result.

There is a natural continuous-population version of the learning model. One could describe masses of agents by their priors and communication roles, and represent their changing interactions with a contact kernel or graphon. Asking whether such a society reaches truthful consensus, and at what rate, would be a legitimate analytic question. But it would not turn Theorem 2.1 into a computational result; it would put the paper’s convergence question into a continuum setting, in the analytic territory the programme distinguishes from its focus.

The stronger rescue would be to ask for a computational task, such as choosing whom to connect or allocating truth signals to achieve a target convergence rate. But the paper treats the graph sequence as given and studies the resulting dynamics; it does not optimize over networks or signal allocations. That task would need a new objective, constraints, and input model. Likewise, reducing agents to population masses alone generally loses who is connected to whom and how prior interactions shape the current beliefs. A contact kernel can retain that information, but its representation and computational questions would need to be supplied by a new model.

So the proponent is right that there is no positive anchor in this paper. I would not claim that no scenario could make continuous social learning worthwhile: a continuum consensus study could be. The narrower and stronger case is that this paper offers no computational problem for ChoCo to continuize, and turning its theorem into one requires adding the central problem rather than mirroring a result.
