The continuous mirror question

Given \(N\) requesters with rational funding requirements \(b_j\), a fund \(B\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over complete requester rankings with support size \(\tau\), compute the funded set under the first-tier rule: let \(q_j=\sum_{t:\,r_j\text{ is ranked first}}\mu_t\), order requesters by decreasing \(q_j\) with a fixed tie-break, and include each requester if its requirement fits the remaining fund. Characterize the bit complexity in \(N\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

Use requester rankings as voter types and their rational masses \(\mu_t\) as population shares. The first-choice shares \(q_j\) induce binary funding decisions \(x_j\) through the paper’s budget scan, with the descending priority shown in its worked example.

The objection that survived

Lemma 1’s stated \(O(N\log N)\) bound omits scanning the \(p\) resident ballots; a complete finite-input bound must include that scan, though this does not remove polynomiality.

What the mirror covers

Covers the first-tier funding scan within Lemma 1; leaves the slot scheduling and executor allocation and payment bounds, as well as the separate probabilistic lemmas, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the full bit complexity when the input includes \(\tau\) rankings and binary-rational masses, requirements, and fund?
  • Which priority direction is intended: Algorithm 1 says ascending vote order, while its worked example uses descending order?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the first tier, anchored in Lemma 1, proved in this paper. Lemma 1 says BULINC is computationally efficient: Algorithm 1 runs in \(O(N\log N)\), Algorithm 2 in \(O((\sum_i n_i)^2)\), and Algorithm 3 is bounded by \(O(\ell(n(n_i m_\ell+n_i|W_i^j|)) )\), yielding \(O(n^3)\) when \(n_i\), \(m_\ell\), and \(|W_i^j|\) are functions of \(n\). The paper gives no named hardness result, so this is the available algorithmic anchor.

My lead is Continuous First-Tier BULINC Selection. A city has \(N\) task requesters, with funding requirements \(b_i\), and a government fund \(B\). Its residents are represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over first-choice types: type \(i\) means that requester \(r_i\) is the resident’s top choice. Each \(\mu_i\) is a binary rational share of the population, and the support size is \(\tau\). For this part of BULINC, first choice is a complete type description: Algorithm 1 sorts by votes received, so lower-ranked preferences do not affect its outcome.

The decision variable is \(x_i\in\{0,1\}\), indicating whether \(r_i\) receives funding. Order requesters by decreasing \(\mu_i\), using a fixed tie-break rule. The objective is to lexicographically maximize the selection vector in that order, subject to \(\sum_i b_i x_i\le B\). The solution is the resulting funded set \(F=\{r_i:x_i=1\}\). This is exactly Algorithm 1’s rule: scan requesters in vote order and include each one if its funding requirement fits the remaining budget. The continuous society makes the rule’s input the shares \(\mu_i\), rather than a list of individual ballots.

A plausible regime is a city participatory-budgeting process with many thousands of residents and a much smaller slate of proposed sensing projects. Residents who rank the same project first are interchangeable for this rule; thus \(\tau\le N\), even when the population is much larger. This is an instance-regime claim, not a claim that every crowdsensing market has repeated executor types. If a finite election has \(P\) residents and \(n_i\) of them favor requester \(i\) first, then \(\mu_i=n_i/P\). Sorting by these shares produces exactly the same ordering, and hence the same funded set, as sorting by vote counts.

This mirror should be tractable: support aggregation takes \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations, followed by sorting in \(O(N\log N)\); with binary-rational inputs, the bit complexity is polynomial in \(N,\tau,L\). In the programme’s terms, it is a modest Class A result about a population-continuous version of a rule the paper actually computes. Its scope is only the first-tier funding-selection component of Lemma 1; it does not mirror the interval scheduling or executor allocation and payment stages.

The further question is whether to study the paper’s stated aggregate-utility objective instead of BULINC’s vote-ordered greedy rule. That would be a different problem: requester selection under a budget can retain combinatorial difficulty in the number of requesters, even with a continuous resident population. A separate extension would model task executors by a distribution over complete cost and capability types, then ask how to preserve the paper’s allocation, budget, and incentive properties.

The weak point is that the first-tier mirror may look like replacing vote counts by percentages, while the paper’s formal optimization discussion emphasizes average voter gain. Algorithm 1 implements a support-ordered greedy selection rule, not a general welfare optimizer. I think the mirror remains plausible because it preserves the actual funding decision the authors implement, and the paper explicitly motivates that decision through city-dweller participation. It is a sound but deliberately limited case for continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this is a very small mirror of Lemma 1. Algorithm 1 sorts requesters and scans them against the remaining budget; it exposes no population-sized optimization problem or need for continuous-optimization machinery. And Lemma 1’s stated runtime omits the loop over city dwellers, so it does not actually establish a bound in the population size. A corrected discrete bound would account for reading the ballots; the continuous version would account for reading \(\tau\) type masses instead.

That makes the proponent’s lexicographic formulation a poor match for the paper’s stated welfare objective in Equation (1), which is not what Algorithm 1 implements. Mirroring the objective instead would require clarifying the voters’ gains and would leave the knapsack choice among requesters as the central computation. But this objection does not defeat a mirror of the implemented rule.

In fact, the stronger mirror is straightforward: let \(T\) be the set of complete rankings over requesters, with rational mass \(\mu_t\), and define \(q_j=\sum_{t:\,r_j\text{ is ranked first}}\mu_t\). Sort requesters by \(q_j\), break ties consistently, and apply the paper’s budget scan. This is a credible city-scale population regime, and for finite ballot counts \(n_t\) out of \(P\) voters, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/P\) preserves the funded set exactly. Its running time is polynomial in the input size, including \(\tau\) and the rational bit length.

So the negative case is weak. The mirror may be too elementary to be a high-priority ChoCo result, but “it is just sorting shares” is a complaint about its likely depth, not a reason the continuous-population question is invalid. Lemma 1 supplies the named computational anchor, and the first-tier mirror survives in a more faithful form than the proponent’s first-choice formulation.
