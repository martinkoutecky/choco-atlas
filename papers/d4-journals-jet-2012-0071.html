The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible positive case is a follow-on population version of the paper’s risk-sharing problem, not a mirror of a computational result the paper proves.

The closest anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved here. It says that, for a bounded allocation of a fixed aggregate risk and a family of strictly convex baseline functions \(w\), there exists a \(w\)-comonotone allocation that dominates the initial allocation in concave order; the domination is strict if the initial allocation is not itself \(w\)-comonotone. The paper obtains this existence result through a convex variational problem and duality. But it does not give a complexity result: the theorem concerns a fixed finite number \(p\) of agents, and the paper says that an efficient algorithm for deciding multivariate comonotonicity remains to be found.

A plausible regime for extending that question is a large mutual-insurance pool. There are \(N\) policyholders but only \(\tau\) complete types, with \(N\gg\tau\). A type records everything relevant to the model, such as the initial contingent allocation and its strictly convex cost function. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s population share. For a finite set of states \(s\), with probabilities \(q_s\), the aggregate risk is \(X_s\), and the initial per-capita allocation is \(y^0_{t,s}\), satisfying
\[ \sum_t \mu_t y^0_{t,s}=X_s. \]
A type-level alternative \(z_{t,s}\) must preserve the aggregate in each state, \(\sum_t\mu_t z_{t,s}=X_s\), and make each type’s new risk no worse in concave order than its initial risk.

Call the resulting optimization problem Type-Mass Risk Sharing: given rational \(\mu_t,q_s,X_s,y^0_{t,s}\) and positive-definite rational matrices \(Q_t\), find \(z\) minimizing
\[ \sum_t\mu_t\sum_s q_s\, z_{t,s}^{\mathsf T}Q_tz_{t,s} \]
subject to aggregate feasibility and those type-by-type concave-order constraints. A solution is the new allocation, along with certificates of the concave-order constraints. For finite state supports, each such constraint has a martingale-coupling formulation, so this version has a convex quadratic objective over a polytope. That makes tractability by continuous optimization a credible expectation, at least for additive-accuracy optimization. It keeps the paper’s substantive question—risk sharing under statewise aggregate conservation and Pareto comparisons in concave order—while replacing named agents with masses of indistinguishable policyholders. The authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime.

There is a crucial qualification: this is not an eligible anchor under your stated rule. Theorem 4.5 is an existence and characterization result, not a theorem that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. The paper’s probability space and distributions describe risk across states; by themselves they do not make the population continuous. The proposed type-mass problem adds that population model, and the paper does not prove its extension.

So the affirmative case is a credible, precisely formulable research direction suggested by Theorem 4.5—not an anchored continuous-complexity mirror of a named result. Its weakest point is that both the population extension and the computational question are new work, rather than a continuization of a complexity result already established in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 4.5 proves no computational result. It establishes that a dominating \(w\)-comonotone allocation exists, with strict domination under a stated condition. Theorem 4.6 characterizes efficiency. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or even a finite input encoding. The paper’s note that an efficient test for comonotonicity remains open is a research question, not a computational result to mirror. And its non-atomic probability space describes uncertainty across states; the number \(p\) of agents remains finite.

The proposed Type-Mass Risk Sharing model is coherent, and a large mutual-insurance pool with repeated contract and preference types is a plausible setting. But it adds the population model, finite encoding, and computational task that the paper does not supply. Replacing the quadratic costs with general convex \(w_t\), or asking for an algorithm that constructs the dominating allocation, would make a stronger follow-on project; it still would not turn Theorem 4.5 into a complexity result. It would be a new computational investigation prompted by an existence theorem and the authors’ stated open question.

That defeats the proponent’s case as an anchored mirror. It does not establish that the follow-on is unworthy, or that no population scenario could make it worthwhile. In fact, the type-mass model is the strongest part of the affirmative case. The honest negative is therefore narrower than the universal claim: this paper offers no computational result for ChoCo to continuize, but the material does support a potentially worthwhile new project.
