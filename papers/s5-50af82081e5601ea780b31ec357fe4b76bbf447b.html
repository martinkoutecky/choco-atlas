The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible population mirror here, especially for the paper’s food-bank setting. But under the anchor rule in this task, the paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor: its numbered theorems bound the Price of Anarchy; none classifies an algorithmic problem as hard, tractable, FPT, or similar. I shouldn’t relabel an efficiency theorem as a complexity result.

The closest result to mirror is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every \(p\leq 0\) and every budget rule \(g:\mathbb{R}_+^C\to\mathbb{R}_+^C\), \(PoA_{p,2}(\text{Highest-Bidder-Wins}_g)\to\infty\). The paper’s construction gives center 1 two kinds of beneficiaries, each valuing a different half of the items, while beneficiaries of every other center value all items equally.

A plausible continuous model keeps the \(C\) centers strategic and replaces their beneficiaries with a finite-support population distribution. A type is a pair \((c,v)\): the center representing the beneficiary and their complete normalized additive valuation vector \(v\). Each type has rational mass \(\mu_{c,v}\), with total mass one; write \(\alpha_c=\sum_v\mu_{c,v}\). The centers make the paper’s same bid decisions, and after the auction each center distributes what it receives across its type masses to maximize its generalized \(p\)-mean welfare. Scale supplies and budgets with population so per-capita quantities stay fixed; under the Trading Post budget rule, for example, \(B_c=\alpha_c\). Type \(t=(c,v)\) receiving per-capita allocation \(y_{j,t}\) of item \(j\) has utility \(u_t=\sum_j v_j y_{j,t}\); total welfare is \(W_p=(\sum_t\mu_t u_t^p)^{1/p}\), with the usual geometric-mean interpretation at \(p=0\).

The corresponding question is: for \(p\leq0\), \(C=m\), and valuation matrices with \(\ell=2\), determine whether the worst pure-equilibrium ratio \(\sup_{\mathcal I}\sup_{e\in PNE(\mathcal I)}\operatorname{OPT}_p(\mathcal I)/W_p(e)\) for Highest-Bidder-Wins remains bounded as \(m\) grows, for each admissible mass-based budget rule. A solution would be either a uniform bound or a family of continuous-population equilibria proving divergence. This mirrors Theorem 1’s problem and conclusion, though it is an equilibrium-efficiency question rather than a complexity classification.

The positive case is unusually concrete: the witness uses only three valuation vectors. If each represented cohort is replicated \(K\) times, the mass shares and the bad equilibrium persist; with center affiliation included in the type, the support is at most \(C+1\), while the population can be made much larger by taking \(K\) large. The paper’s center structure, budget dependence on represented population, internal welfare objective, and equilibrium comparison all survive. So the authors could plausibly recognize this as their model with beneficiary counts replaced by masses. This case covers Theorem 1’s Highest-Bidder-Wins result only; the paper’s divisible goods alone are not the continuity argument.

The soft spot is also decisive for ChoCo’s scope: the mirror asks for a Price of Anarchy bound, not a complexity landscape. The A/B/C trichotomy does not apply to it, and a strict ChoCo review could reject it as an analytic game-theoretic continuation. Exact repeated valuation profiles are another assumption, though this particular witness has low support. The natural follow-up would be a formal replication dictionary for the paper’s PoA results and, separately, a new computational question about finding or certifying a worst pure equilibrium.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a strong population mirror. Let a type record both its valuation and its representative center. In its witness, center 1’s population splits between two valuation types, while each other center has a uniform-valuation type. This gives support at most \(C+1\); replicating each cohort preserves the masses and the bad equilibrium. The construction therefore works in a genuine high-multiplicity regime, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their model with beneficiary counts replaced by population shares. I can’t defeat this anchor on population modeling grounds.

The negative case is about ChoCo’s remit. Theorem 1 bounds the Price of Anarchy over pure equilibria; it does not state the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Its continuous version asks for another equilibrium-efficiency bound, not for the worst-case complexity of computing or approximating an outcome. One could pose a new problem about finding equilibria or certifying a Price of Anarchy, but that would need its own input model, encoding, and computational question. It would be a research direction prompted by the paper, rather than a computational result mirrored from it.

So this is a defensible reason to exclude the proposed anchor from ChoCo’s computational programme. It does not support the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: as a game-theoretic population question, this one remains credible. The honest negative case is weak outside that scope boundary.
