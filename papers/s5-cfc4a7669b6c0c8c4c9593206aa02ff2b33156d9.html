What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only the equilibrium timing claims in Theorems 1 and 2; neither supplies a computational result to carry over.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid positive case under the anchor requirement: the paper has no numbered computational result asserting an algorithmic or complexity classification.

Theorem 1, proved here, says that for \(N\ge 6\), every equilibrium of the restricted persistent-voting game has the same outcome as an equilibrium in which everyone votes in the second period. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that in the \(x\)-model for \(x\) sufficiently close to one, no pure-strategy sequential equilibrium with simple voting-stage strategies has all voters choosing the same period. These are equilibrium-structure results, not results that a problem is in \(\mathrm P\), NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

A continuum-agent version could study equilibrium timing when population mass is distributed over preference types and agents choose when to vote. But making that into an algorithmic equilibrium-computation question would add a computational problem the paper does not establish as a result. And because the paper’s voters choose timing before their preferences are realized, a mirror based on fixed masses of realized preference types would need extra care to preserve its information structure. So this is a useful boundary case for the programme, but there is no honest named computational anchor—and thus no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is at the anchor gate: the paper states no computational result. Theorem 1 classifies outcomes of equilibria in a restricted persistent-voting game; Theorem 2 characterizes when equilibria with a particular timing pattern cannot exist. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational problem. “Compute equilibria” could be a worthwhile new question, but it is not a computational result of this paper for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime here: with three candidates, the \(x\)-model has only six preference rankings, and the voters are otherwise symmetric. The obstacle is what the continuum does to the game. The best version of a mirror would preserve the timing of information: voters choose when to vote before their preferences are realized, then vote with private preferences. With an atomless population and independent draws, the law of large numbers makes the realized mass of each preference type deterministic. An individual voter has zero mass, so changing their timing or vote cannot change candidate viability or the winner. The finite game’s pivotal events—one vote changing viability or breaking a near tie—disappear.

That defeats both candidate anchors. A continuum analogue of Theorem 1 would lose the finite-vote mechanism behind its \(N\ge6\) claim; if positive mass can move together, the deviation is a coalition’s, not an individual voter’s Nash deviation. A continuum analogue of Theorem 2 likewise cannot force a voter to move early: their move changes neither the early vote mass nor what later voters learn. Treating a positive-mass group as a strategic player could restore that effect, but it would replace the paper’s individually chosen timing game with a game among blocs.

One could add an equilibrium-computation problem to a new model, or retain finite-size sampling effects while taking a large-population approximation. The first requires extra strategic structure to avoid the atomless game’s indifference; the second retains the finite-population uncertainty that the continuum limit removes. Neither is an anchored computational mirror of these theorems. This is a strong negative verdict under the programme’s anchor requirement, though not proof that every future continuous timing model would be uninteresting.
