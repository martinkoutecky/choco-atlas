The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational additive values \(p_{tj}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{tj}=r\) for each type, and per-capita supplies \(b_j\), what is the greatest threshold \(q\) for which masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) can be assigned to integral bundles \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\), and \(x_{t,S}=0\) whenever \(\sum_{j\in S}p_{tj}<q\)?

The model it lives in

A finite-support population distribution over additive-value types, with each type’s total value normalized to \(r\). Decision variables \(x_{t,S}\) represent the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(S\); resource categories have per-capita supply limits \(b_j\). The objective is to maximize the minimum utility received by any positive mass, equivalently to find the greatest feasible threshold \(q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s \(r\)-limited egalitarian optimization result. It leaves Proposition 2, the conjectured complexity of the limited optimal-fake-utility problem, and the genetic-algorithm experiments unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is threshold feasibility solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does integral-bundle selection make threshold feasibility hard even when type mass is divisible?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s \(r\)-limited egalitarian allocation problem, in a large humanitarian distribution setting. Imagine a disaster zone with hundreds of thousands of households, a small number of standardized need and preference profiles, and repeated categories of supplies such as food, water, and shelter kits. A type is a complete additive value vector over those categories, together with any allocation-relevant characteristics. The regime is plausible when the number of households is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct types.

Here is a precise continuous problem, \(r\)-Limited Mass-Egalitarian MARA. There are \(m\) resource categories, a finite support \(T\) of types, and a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\) for each type, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Type \(t\) has rational additive values \(p_{tj}\ge 0\), normalized so that \(\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{tj}=r\) for every type. Category \(j\) has per-capita supply \(b_j\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\). The supply scales with population size; each individual can receive at most one unit of each category.

For each type \(t\) and bundle \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\), let \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) be the mass of that type receiving that integral bundle. Feasible allocations satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{for each }t, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j \quad\text{for each }j. \]
An individual of type \(t\) receiving \(S\) has utility \(u_t(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_{tj}\). The objective is to maximize the minimum utility actually received by any positive mass of the population:
\[ W(x)=\min\{u_t(S):x_{t,S}>0\}. \]
A prover can be given the decision version directly: given a rational threshold \(q\), is there a feasible \(x\) with \(x_{t,S}=0\) whenever \(u_t(S)<q\)? A solution to the optimization version is a feasible mass assignment \(x\) attaining the greatest possible \(W(x)\). Every recipient still gets an integral bundle; the fractional quantities describe how much mass receives each bundle.

The high-multiplicity bridge is explicit. At scale \(K\), take \(K\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Kb_j\) copies of resource category \(j\), for compatible integer \(K\). Finite assignments correspond to mass allocations whose \(x_{t,S}\) are multiples of \(1/K\). Increasing \(K\) makes finer allocations possible. This is not a claim that the paper’s single copy of each resource is itself a continuum: the co-modification is that resource supplies scale with population, as they would in a repeated, per-household distribution programme.

My anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It states that egalitarian social welfare optimization remains NP-hard when an additional \(r\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\) is input and every additive utility vector must be \(r\)-limited—that is, its entries sum to \(r\). The proposition’s proof is given here, building on the base NP-hardness cited as [17]: its reduction from PARTITION yields an instance with two agents having the same utility vector, so a common \(r\) can be chosen as that vector’s sum. That is the full result I am mirroring. I am not anchoring on the paper’s experiments, Proposition 2’s result about the optimal unrestricted lie, or its conjecture about the limited optimal-fake-utility problem.

I expect the continuous question to be hard for continuum-specific reasons, rather than to inherit the proposition’s hardness directly. The finite reduction uses resource-subset choices, but splitting a type’s mass among bundles can remove the finite allocation obstruction that makes a two-agent PARTITION instance hard. At the same time, the continuum does not make bundles fractional: for a fixed threshold \(q\), feasibility is a configuration LP over integral bundles with \(u_t(S)\ge q\). Its pricing problem includes finding a minimum-priced bundle whose value reaches \(q\), which has the shape of a knapsack-cover problem. That is a reason to suspect hardness, not a proof that this mass problem is hard. The precise prover question is whether threshold feasibility is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or whether integral bundle selection makes it hard even after mass becomes divisible.

I think the authors would recognize this as their egalitarian MARA problem: the objective remains the minimum utility received, preferences remain additive, and the rule that each person receives an integral bundle is preserved. The new objects are the continuous population and per-capita supplies. The mirror covers the \(r\)-limited optimization result only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s strategic-lying experiments.

The weakest point is that Proposition 1’s stated reduction concerns two agents, not a high-multiplicity population, and the per-capita supply model is a substantive co-modification. Mass splitting may dissolve exactly the hardness the proposition establishes. The continuous question remains worth asking because its residual bundle-pricing problem may still be hard, but neither the paper’s proposition nor the pricing observation settles the answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proponent’s anchor is its narrowness. Proposition 1 proves only that normalizing each utility vector to a common sum does not remove NP-hardness: its proof imports a two-agent, identical-preferences PARTITION instance. It does not establish complexity for the paper’s central strategic-lying question; the limited optimal-fake-utility result is only conjectured, and the lying results are experimental.

Keeping the original fixed stock of \(m\) indivisible resources while replicating the population does degenerate: once there are more than \(m\) people, some receive nothing, so the minimum utility is zero. But that does not defeat the better mirror. Scaling supplies with population size gives a plausible repeated-distribution setting, and the resulting max-min problem over integral bundles assigned to type masses is coherent. The supply change is substantial, but the programme explicitly allows it when population scaling requires it. Its threshold-feasibility problem is a legitimate configuration-LP question; saying the finite PARTITION reduction may or may not survive mass splitting would concern the answer, not whether the question is worth asking.

Nor can adjacent work supply an “already done” objection. There is computational work on max-min fair allocations in high-multiplicity settings, which is evidence that this regime makes sense under ChoCo’s criteria ([Fair and efficient allocation with few agent types, few item types, or small value levels](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370222001606)). Stochastic max-min allocation is a related but distinct model ([Kawase and Sumita, 2020](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5580)). And while the paper’s central story of one named agent lying has no direct population mirror—a single individual has zero mass—the proponent does not rely on that story; Proposition 1 is enough as their anchor.

So the honest negative case is weak. Proposition 1 is a thin anchor, and the scaled-supply mirror changes the operating regime. But I cannot use either point to defeat that mirror under the programme’s stated rules. The proponent’s continuous question survives.
