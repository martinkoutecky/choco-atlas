A continuous version of one of those measurements would not mirror a computational result in the sense this programme is charting.

The best alternative is to imagine a large cohort of demonstrators and ask for the mass of each preference type from their demonstrations. But that changes the paper’s task. If each person’s trajectory remains available, grouping identical cases by mass merely batches the original individual inference; the central difficulty remains learning the trajectory-to-preference mapping. If only aggregate reward features are observed, different preference distributions can produce the same aggregate, so the target distribution may not be identifiable. Adding assumptions to recover it—such as a parametric population model or shared interactions among demonstrators—could make a worthwhile problem, but those assumptions supply a new problem rather than a continuous mirror of DWPI. The paper’s continuous preference vectors are part of the parameter space; making those continuous is not continuizing the population.

That is a strong case against spending ChoCo effort on a direct mirror of this article. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile population-level preference-inference problem can be built from its ideas: a carefully specified cohort problem could be sensible. The honest negative verdict is narrower: this paper itself offers no named computational result to mirror, and the obvious population reformulations either preserve individual inference or introduce a different, potentially non-identifiable inverse problem.
