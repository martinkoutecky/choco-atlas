The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) chores and \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), per-capita budgets \(b_t>0\) satisfying \(\sum_t \mu_t b_t=1\), and normalized convex CES disutilities \(d_t(x)=(\sum_j(d_{tj}x_j)^\rho)^{1/\rho}\), find prices \(p\) and each type's optimal per-capita bundle \(x_t\) such that \(1-\varepsilon\leq\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\leq 1/(1-\varepsilon)\) for every chore \(j\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over CES chore-preference and budget types, with per-capita bundles \(x_t\), mass-weighted market clearing, and multiplicative price updates using aggregate excess supply. The equivalent representative-agent market has budget \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\) for each type.

The objection that survived

The continuous instance collapses exactly to a τ-agent instance, so the mirror adds no separate computational input or algorithmic landscape.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Corollary 1 covers the approximate-CE iteration guarantee for convex CES disutilities; Theorem 1's asymptotic convergence for CCH disutilities also transfers by type aggregation. The experiments provide empirical comparisons, not additional guarantees.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bit-complexity guarantee does the iteration bound yield as \(m\), τ, and the rational input precision grow?
  • Can a comparable approximate market-clearing guarantee be obtained for general CCH type distributions without the ℓ_0\) dependence?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a large recurring chore market staffed by many workers whose earning requirements and task preferences fall into a relatively small number of categories. For instance, a public service or care workforce might have millions of workers but only \(\tau\) recurring combinations of contract and task-aversion profile. A type \(t\) is the complete pair \((b_t,d_t)\): its common per-capita earning requirement and disutility function. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_t\) the share of workers of type \(t\). This is plausible when \(n\gg\tau\); it is a property of that workforce regime, not a claim about every chores market.

My proposed mirror is finite-support CES chores equilibrium approximation. An instance has \(m\) chores, positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), type budgets \(b_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t=1\), and a common \(\rho>1\) with type disutilities \(d_t(x)=(\sum_j(d_{tj}x_j)^\rho)^{1/\rho}\), normalized so \(d_t(\mathbf 1)=1\). Each chore has normalized supply \(1\). At prices \(p\), a type’s per-capita bundle \(x_t\) must minimize \(d_t(x)\) subject to \(p\cdot x\ge b_t\). The question is to output prices \(p\) and optimal bundles \(x_t\) such that each chore’s aggregate assigned amount \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\) lies between \(1-\varepsilon\) and \(1/(1-\varepsilon)\). That is the paper’s approximate-CE condition applied to a mass population.

The population change has an exact bridge to the paper’s finite model. A mass \(\mu_t\) of identical agents with per-capita budget \(b_t\) contributes the same aggregate demand as one representative agent with budget \(\mu_t b_t\): by 1-homogeneity, its bundle is \(\mu_t x_t\). Thus the type-level market clearing signal is \(y_j=1-\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\), and the paper’s local update \(p_j^{k+1}=p_j^k(1+\eta y_j^k)\) uses exactly that signal. A discrete market with \(n_t\) members of type \(t\) gives the corresponding normalization by taking each member’s budget to be \(b_t/n\) and scaling their bundle by \(n\). The continuous object here is the population distribution; divisible chores are already part of the paper’s model.

My lead anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for convex CES disutilities, multiplicative tâtonnement with step size \(\eta=1/[12(L+\beta)]\) finds an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate CE within \(\lceil288(L+\beta)(f(p^0)-\underline f)/(\ell_0\varepsilon^2)\rceil\) iterations. In the type model, \(L=(\sigma-1)m^{\sigma-1}d_{\min}^{-\sigma}\), \(\beta=1+d_{\min}^{-1}\), \(d_{\min}=\min_{t,j}d_{tj}\), and \(\sigma=\rho/(\rho-1)\). The potential is \(f(p)=-\sum_jp_j+\sum_t(\mu_tb_t/\sigma)\log(\sum_jd_{tj}^{-\sigma}p_j^\sigma)\), and \(\underline f\) is its minimum over the price simplex. The barrier parameter \(\ell_0\) is the paper’s parameter for the equivalent \(\tau\)-agent instance, whose representative budget for type \(t\) is \(\mu_tb_t\). This is a named algorithmic result, not a P-versus-NP classification.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the paper’s iterative, continuous-optimization sense: each round can compute one demand per type, aggregate the \(\tau\) demands, and update the \(m\) prices. The result does not establish polynomial bit complexity, but it does give a concrete approximate-equilibrium guarantee for the mass-population problem. The authors would likely recognize it: individual optimization, aggregate market clearing, the price simplex, the excess-supply feedback, and the equilibrium target are all preserved. The type aggregation follows directly from the model’s homogeneity rather than simplifying away the market question.

This case covers the paper’s CES approximate-CE rate result, not its experiments or its broader asymptotic convergence theorem for general CCH disutilities. Natural follow-up questions are whether the rate extends to general CCH type distributions, whether the weighted-residual guarantee from Theorem 2 can avoid the \(\ell_0\) dependence when the target is approximate market clearing, and what bit-complexity guarantees hold as \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input precision grow.

The weak point is that the paper does not study a high-multiplicity population: its experiments include small agent counts, and its synthetic instances use 100 agents against many chores. Also, the corollary’s iteration bound depends on numerical parameters, including \(\ell_0\), so it should not be advertised as a polynomial-time complexity result. The positive case survives those limitations because the continuous mirror is a well-defined market with a direct type-mass interpretation, and the paper’s proved algorithmic guarantee transfers through the exact aggregation of identical agents.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The corollary is a genuine algorithmic anchor, but its proposed mirror is exactly a market instance already covered by the paper. For type \(t\), let \(X_t(p;b_t)\) be the demand set at per-capita budget \(b_t\). Since \(d_t\) is 1-homogeneous, scaling the budget scales every demanded bundle: \(X_t(p;\mu_t b_t)=\mu_t X_t(p;b_t)\). Thus a mass \(\mu_t\) of agents with type \((b_t,d_t)\) has exactly the aggregate demand of one agent with disutility \(d_t\) and budget \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\). The paper allows arbitrary positive agent budgets with \(\sum_t B_t=1\), so this representative-agent market is within its stated model.

This correspondence preserves the objects the paper studies. The aggregate excess-supply signal is the same, so the multiplicative price iterates are the same. The potential in the proposed mirror,
\[ f(p)=-\sum_j p_j+\sum_t\frac{\mu_t b_t}{\sigma}\log\!\left(\sum_j d_{tj}^{-\sigma}p_j^\sigma\right), \]
is precisely the paper’s potential with agent budgets \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\). The CES rate theorem and Corollary 1 therefore apply to the mirror by substitution; the relevant \(\ell_0\) is the one for the equivalent market with \(\tau\) representative agents. The broader CCH convergence theorem transfers by the same aggregation.

This is a structural collapse of the proposed population model, not a claim that a distinct continuous problem has a dull answer. Within the paper’s model, mass and per-capita budget enter the equilibrium computation only through their product \(\mu_t b_t\). Prices and per-type bundles can be recovered from the representative-agent equilibrium by dividing its bundle by \(\mu_t\). Since the mirror takes finite-support rational masses as input, converting it to the paper’s \(\tau\)-agent input requires only forming those products. It adds no new computational instance or population parameter to analyze.

The workforce scenario makes the grouping plausible, but does not change this conclusion. Any finite collection of worker categories with shared disutilities and per-capita budgets reduces in the same way, regardless of the story. The experiments offer no stronger mirror: repeating agents of a given type preserves the aggregate demand signal and hence the price trajectory. A runtime gain from grouping repeated records would be input compression to \(\tau\) representatives, not a new population-continuous result. Questions about individual fairness, identity-targeted interventions, or headcount constraints could make mass matter separately, but they would change the problem beyond the CE computation studied here.

So the strongest in-scope mirror—Corollary 1, extended to the general CCH theorem and to the experiments—does not leave a separate continuous computational question for ChoCo. A richer type model might create one, but it would need additional structure absent from this paper.
