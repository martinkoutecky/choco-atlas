The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification.

Theorem 1, proved here, bounds the efficiency loss of a fair optimum: \(\Delta \le n\cdot \mathrm{OBJ2}(M_1^*)\). Theorem 2, also proved here, gives instances with \(|R|=2n\), \(|D|=n\), and \(\Delta=\Omega((n-1)\mathrm{OBJ2}(M_1^*))\). Both are results about the relationship between fairness and efficiency, not about tractability or hardness. The paper says in Section 4 that finding an efficient maximum matching is equivalent to 3-dimensional matching and is NP-hard, citing [1, 9], but that claim is not a numbered result of this paper. Its statement that fair matching is “believed” intractable is not proved. The proposed algorithms are heuristics, with experimental results rather than numbered guarantees.

There is still a plausible continuous follow-up. In a dense urban service area, many drivers in a batch could share a starting zone and prior-distance level, while many requests share pickup and destination zones. Let driver types \(d\) and request types \(r\) have rational masses \(\alpha_d\) and \(\beta_r\). A type records the attributes relevant to the batch: a driver’s current location and accumulated distance, or a request’s pickup and destination. Let \(x_{d,B}\) be the mass of type-\(d\) drivers assigned bundle \(B\), where \(B\) contains zero, one, or two request types. Require \(\sum_B x_{d,B}\le\alpha_d\) for each driver type, and require that the mass of each request type consumed by all bundles is at most its supply \(\beta_r\).

A precise candidate is Continuous Fair 2-to-1 Batch Matching: among feasible assignments, maximize the mass of requests served; among those assignments, minimize the largest post-batch distance \(z\), counting a driver’s accumulated distance and the route cost of their assigned bundle. A solution is the assignment masses \(x_{d,B}\) and the resulting value \(z\). For any fixed \(z\), the eligible bundles give a linear program; checking the finitely many possible route-cost thresholds yields a polynomial-time algorithm in the number of supported types and the input bit length. This preserves the paper’s batch assignment, vehicle capacity, throughput, and Rawlsian maximum-distance objective. Fractional assignment mass represents different drivers in a homogeneous cohort receiving different integral bundles.

That is a credible mirror of the underlying matching problem, but it is not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The main weakness is that exact driver and request types—especially current locations and accumulated distances—may be numerous in real ride-hailing data, so a small support is a scenario assumption that needs defending.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 bound the efficiency loss of a fair matching; they do not classify the complexity of a problem. The paper’s NP-hardness statement is cited from earlier work, its claim about the fairness objective is explicitly conjectural, and its algorithms are heuristics. So there is no anchor-compliant mirror here.

The proposed batch model is the strongest follow-up: it gives drivers and requests types, lets mass be split among bundles that remain integral for each unit, and minimizes the worst post-batch distance after maximizing requests served. That is a coherent continuous population model, and identical drivers and requests are plausible in a service area with repeated locations and histories. I cannot honestly dismiss it as an invalid limit.

The case against it is narrower. It is a new fractional matching problem, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. With capacity two, a supported driver type can be paired with each single request type or request-type pair; the resulting assignment has a polynomial-size linear-program formulation. For exact types, however, driver location and accumulated distance, and request pickup and destination, can make the support nearly as large as the original instance. Coarsening locations or histories may produce larger cohorts, but changes the model unless those coarsened attributes determine the relevant costs. The repeated setting introduces another difficulty: assignments change drivers’ locations and distance histories, so the type distribution must evolve with the matching.

There is also a mismatch in the paper’s quantitative fairness-efficiency results. Under the programme’s unit-mass convention, total travel cost becomes cost per unit mass. The factor \(n\) in Theorem 1 and the growing gap in Theorem 2 do not carry over as stated: average cost is bounded by worst-driver cost. That weakens those theorems as continuous targets, though it does not make a different continuous question meaningless.

So the strongest defensible negative case is that this paper supplies no computational anchor, and the proposed follow-up is either a new fractional matching formulation or requires a support assumption that needs justification. The broader claim—that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario—is weak: the paper’s single-batch problem does admit a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation.
