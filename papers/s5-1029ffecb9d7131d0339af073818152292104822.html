The continuous mirror question

Given a finite library of complete advertiser types and reports, a rational finite-support population distribution \(\mu\), and per-capita space \(\bar W\), what is the best worst-case welfare approximation achievable in polynomial time by feasible truthful direct mechanisms? Specify each mechanism by per-advertiser assignment probabilities \(q_j(r;\mu)\) and transfers for every report \(r=(b,S)\), including reports outside the support of \(\mu\); require at most one ad per advertiser in expectation, aggregate space at most \(\bar W\), and truthful reporting for each individual holding the population distribution fixed.

The model it lives in

A finite set of complete advertiser types carries masses \(\mu_\theta\). A report-contingent rule assigns each individual a lottery over integral ads, with probabilities \(q_j(r;\mu)\), and a per-individual transfer; aggregate expected space is bounded by \(\bar W\), and each individual’s expected clicks and payment satisfy the bid and menu incentive constraints.

The objection that survived

The proposed mass assignment specifies only cohort averages on the support, so it does not determine an individual’s outcome after an off-support bid or menu report; the mirror needs an allocation rule for every individual report.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns the monotonicity approximation frontier of Theorem 1 and the truthful approximation guarantee of Theorem 3. It leaves the GSP price-of-anarchy bounds and the empirical evaluations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does splitting type mass remove the deterministic \(2\)-approximation and randomized \(12/11\)-approximation barriers of Theorem 1?
  • What is the optimal polynomial-time welfare approximation for report-contingent truthful mechanisms over finite-support populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The most credible continuous mirror is a high-volume ad market in which the allocation is made over a batch of impressions. Imagine many independent local businesses competing for a large regional query category during a campaign window. Advertisers use a small number of standard bid tiers and rich-ad templates, with the same predicted click rates and space requirements within each template. The platform aggregates the available ad space across the batch, so both advertiser count and total space grow together. There may be millions of advertiser-campaign units but only \(\tau\) distinct complete types. Their creative labels can differ; what makes them the same type is that every parameter the model uses is the same.

For a finite set \(\Theta\) of such types, let \(\mu_\theta\) be the fraction of the advertiser population of type \(\theta\), with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). A type records its per-click value \(v_\theta\), available menu \(A_\theta\), and, for each ad \(j\), click probability \(\alpha_{\theta j}\) and space \(w_{\theta j}\). Let \(\bar W\) be the space available per unit of population. A feasible mass allocation is \(x_{\theta j}\geq 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{j\in A_\theta}x_{\theta j}\leq\mu_\theta \quad\text{for every }\theta, \qquad \sum_{\theta,j}w_{\theta j}x_{\theta j}\leq\bar W. \]
Here \(x_{\theta j}\) is the mass assigned ad \(j\); each unit of mass still receives at most one integral ad. The welfare benchmark is
\[ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}} =\max_x\sum_{\theta,j}v_\theta\alpha_{\theta j}x_{\theta j}. \]
This continuizes the advertiser population and scales the shared resource with it. The ad formats and individual outcomes remain discrete.

My lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that no monotone deterministic allocation algorithm can achieve approximation ratio better than \(2-\varepsilon\), and no monotone randomized algorithm can achieve better than \(12/11-\varepsilon\), for any \(\varepsilon>0\). Its two-advertiser example gives each advertiser options \((1,1)\) and \((1+\varepsilon,2)\), with total space \(3\): monotonicity prevents an advertiser who omits the smaller option from getting the larger one, which limits welfare. This is a formal approximation lower bound, not an NP-hardness theorem. The paper’s NP-completeness statement for welfare maximization is not a numbered result, so I do not use it as an anchor.

The continuous question I would hand a prover is Mass Rich Ads Monotonicity Frontier: for finite, explicitly represented libraries of complete types and their ad options, determine the best worst-case welfare approximation achievable by polynomial-time direct allocation rules on rational finite-support distributions \(\mu\), subject to the per-capita capacity above. The rule must be feasible and monotone in each advertiser’s bid and menu: for fixed reports of the other advertisers, an advertiser’s expected clicks cannot fall when their bid rises or their reported menu grows. A solution must give an algorithm and guarantee, or a matching impossibility bound, with running time stated in the support size, option count, and input bit length. The question is whether Theorem 1’s numeric barriers survive when mass can be split among integral ad assignments.

My expected direction is Class A, though that is a prediction about the mirror, not a transferred conclusion. The two-person construction does not by itself establish a lower bound for a nonatomic population: mass can be partitioned among formats, and a fractional share of a type can take the smaller ad while another share takes the larger one. Deterministic aggregate mass splits may also reproduce mixtures that required randomization for two named advertisers. The useful research question is whether monotonicity still imposes a substantial approximation loss after that indivisibility disappears, and whether the resulting frontier can be characterized by linear or convex optimization.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. It says that the rule running ALGI with probability \(2/3\) and allocating the maximum-value ad otherwise is monotone in bid and menu, achieves a 3-approximation to social welfare, and has a tight factor of 3. ALGI allocates space using the bang-per-buck order, then gives each advertiser the highest-value ad that fits their allocated space. By Lemma 1, a monotone allocation can be paired with Myerson payments to obtain a truthful mechanism.

Its continuous mirror is Mass Rich Ads Truthful 3-Approximation: given \(\Theta,\mu,\bar W\), compute a feasible mass assignment and per-advertiser transfers that form a truthful, individually rational direct mechanism and achieve welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}/3\), in time polynomial in the support, listed options, and bit length. The mechanism’s per-unit expected clicks must be monotone in bid and menu; transfers can use the corresponding envelope formula \(p=bq-\int_0^b q(z)\,dz\), with the other reports held fixed. I expect Class A here too: the allocation constraints are linear, and the paper already identifies monotonicity as the route from an allocation rule to truthfulness.

Theorem 3’s construction does not transfer verbatim. In a normalized population, allocating one maximum-value ad has vanishing welfare; the continuous mechanism needs a positive-mass feasible allocation playing the relevant role. That makes the result a useful, concrete problem rather than a claim that the paper’s algorithm already solves it.

The authors should recognize both questions: they retain advertisers’ private values and menus, click-through welfare, one ad per advertiser, a shared knapsack constraint, and monotone allocations. The scenario supplies the regime the paper itself does not discuss: many agents with a small number of complete types. It is important that the capacity scale with population; aggregate ad inventory across a batch is the natural co-modification.

My weakest point is that a high-multiplicity population of advertisers is less immediate than a high-multiplicity population of voters. Real auctions often have few bidders for one query, and businesses’ values and menus can differ substantially. The campaign-batch scenario helps, but it is an assumption about an instance regime, not a general property of advertising. Also, Theorem 1’s discrete lower bound is especially fragile under mass splitting, and Theorem 3’s single-ad fallback cannot simply be rescaled. Those limits rule out claiming that either bound carries over unchanged. They do not remove the mirror: the same truthful welfare-allocation problem can be asked over a large, compressed distribution of advertiser types, with the changed bounds and algorithms left for the continuous analysis to determine.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are computational results, so I would not argue that this paper lacks something to mirror. The stronger objection is that the proposed mass allocation does not yet carry over the strategic object those theorems study.

For Theorem 1, \(\mu\) and the mass assignment \(x_{\theta j}\) describe how much of each reported type receives each ad. They do not specify what happens when one advertiser changes their bid or menu. Moving positive mass between types makes a cohort strategic; it is not one advertiser’s deviation. Holding \(\mu\) fixed makes an individual measure zero, and the mass LP says nothing about that person’s allocation after they report a type with zero mass. One can repair this by specifying an individual allocation rule \(q(r;\mu)\) for every report \(r\), including off-support reports, and requiring feasibility and incentive compatibility for that rule. But that is an additional mechanism model, not a property of the mass LP. Theorem 1’s menu-monotonicity barrier therefore does not directly define the proposed “frontier.” Its two-advertiser construction is especially tied to individual menu changes; a continuum version would need a new construction and a new question.

For Theorem 3, the same gap appears in the claimed truthful 3-approximation. The paper’s Myerson payment uses one advertiser’s expected clicks along a bid path, with the other reports fixed. The aggregate variables \(x_{\theta j}\) give only the average allocation for types in the stated support; they provide neither that bid path nor allocations for off-support deviations. Dividing \(x_{\theta j}\) by \(\mu_\theta\) gives an average for an existing cohort, not a complete incentive-compatible mechanism. The maximum-value fallback also needs a population-level definition: allocating one named advertiser does not specify how a positive mass of advertisers should be treated. A scaled fallback may be sensible, but it is a new allocation rule whose feasibility, monotonicity, and payments need their own analysis.

There is also a problem with the strongest real-market story. More impressions usually mean many separate query auctions, each with its own capacity and click rates; pooling their space into one knapsack changes the allocation problem. Advertisers may recur across those auctions, while the paper allows each advertiser only one ad in total. A single large auction avoids that mismatch, but repeated *complete* types must share values, menus, click probabilities, and space requirements. Standard bid tiers help with values; they do not make advertiser-specific menus and click predictions identical. The local-business scenario makes such a regime imaginable, but it remains a deliberately restricted one.

That is the best negative case: the two anchors do not yield a direct continuous mechanism problem from the proposed type-mass LP, and the most natural high-volume market may not have the paper’s single-knapsack structure. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A fully specified atomless model with individual allocation rules and truthful reporting could be coherent, and a sufficiently homogeneous campaign market could supply its regime. My case is weakest exactly there: under the programme’s standard, a plausible high-multiplicity version need not resemble the paper’s typical market, so I cannot honestly rule out every scenario.
