The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a strict ranking \(\succ_r\) of types for each \(r\in T\), and proposed community masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), decide whether \(x\) is stable. It is unstable if there are exit masses \(g_t\le x_t\) and replacement masses \(y_t\le\mu_t-x_t\) with \(0<\sum_t g_t=\sum_t y_t<\sum_t x_t\), such that for every type \(r\) with \(x_r-g_r>0\), there is a nonnegative transport \(q^r_{u,v}\) with row sums \(y_u\), column sums \(g_v\), and \(q^r_{u,v}=0\) unless \(u\succ_r v\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many preference cohorts. Each cohort ranks the cohort types; a proposed community is a submass \(x\). A deviation transfers equal mass \(g\) out and \(y\) in, with type-specific transport couplings testing whether every remaining cohort prefers the replacement mass.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the group-stability part of the paper, because it turns the paper’s central stability question into a question about fractional coalitions.

The anchor is Theorem 3, which the paper cites from Borgs et al. [2016] rather than proving here: given a preference network and a subset \(S\), deciding whether \(S\) is self-approving is coNP-complete, and deciding whether \(S\) is group-stable is coNP-complete. I rely on the group-stability result. It asks whether some nonempty proper subgroup of \(S\) can be replaced by an equally sized group outside \(S\), in a way that every member who stays prefers the replacement group.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large professional or collaboration network whose members fall into a small number of recurring preference cohorts. Members of a cohort share the same complete, problem-relevant ranking of the cohorts they might collaborate with. There may be millions of members but only \(\tau\) supported preference types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the population. The type-level ranking is a co-modification of the model: at continuum scale, the relevant comparison is between masses of cohort types, rather than between named individuals. That keeps the population continuous and preserves the paper’s stability question.

I would give a prover this problem, Fractional Group-Stability Membership. An instance consists of a finite supported type set \(T\), with \(|T|=\tau\); for each \(r\in T\), a strict ranking \(\succ_r\) of the types; rational population masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\); and a rational proposed community \(x\), where \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\), and \(x_t\) is the fraction of that type in the community.

Ask whether \(x\) is stable. It is unstable if there are exit masses \(g_t\le x_t\) and replacement masses \(y_t\le\mu_t-x_t\) such that
\[ 0<\sum_t g_t=\sum_t y_t<\sum_t x_t, \]
and every type \(r\) with \(x_r-g_r>0\) prefers the replacement mass \(y\) to the departing mass \(g\). Define that last comparison explicitly: for each such \(r\), there must be a nonnegative transport \(q^{(r)}_{u,v}\) from \(y\) to \(g\), with row sums \(y_u\), column sums \(g_v\), and \(q^{(r)}_{u,v}=0\) unless \(u\succ_r v\). In other words, every unit of replacement mass can be paired with a unit of departing mass that type \(r\) ranks lower. A solution is a yes/no answer; for “unstable,” \(g,y\) and the transports are a witness.

This is recognisably the paper’s group-stability question: a group leaves, an equally large outside group takes its place, and all remaining members must prefer the replacement. The finite bijection in the paper becomes a mass-preserving transport. I expect this mirror to be FPT in \(\tau\). For each candidate set \(R\) of types that remain in the community, impose \(g_t=x_t\) outside \(R\), and require positive remaining mass for every type in \(R\). The transport conditions are linear constraints, so each fixed \(R\) gives a rational LP feasibility test. Enumerating the \(2^\tau\) choices yields an \(O(2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L))\) algorithm, where \(L\) is the input bit length. That makes it tractable in the high-multiplicity regime with small support; whether the exponential dependence on \(\tau\) can be removed is an open question.

The mirror covers the group-stability branch of Theorem 3, not its self-approval branch or the paper’s full axiom system. The theorem’s coNP-hardness motivates the question, but does not show that hardness dissolves: its reduction may require many distinct types. Establishing the discrete-to-continuous dictionary—including whether fractional deviations change the answer on a discrete instance—is part of the research question.

My weakest point is the cohort-level preference model. The paper ranks individuals, while this mirror ranks masses of preference types. That coarsening needs a convincing setting where members truly are interchangeable for the stability question; otherwise it could look like a different problem. The case survives if the authors and a referee accept a large network whose members rank collaboration cohorts and whose stability test is about exchanging mass between them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine computational anchor, so I would not argue that the paper has no result to mirror. The strongest objection is that its notion of preference resists the type compression the proposed mirror needs.

In this paper, people are also the objects being ranked. For any two distinct individuals \(a\) and \(b\), every member’s strict ranking distinguishes them. That distinction can affect whether one equal-sized group is preferred to another: group preference requires a matching in which every replacement is ranked above its counterpart. Even if \(a\) and \(b\) have identical rankings of everyone else, swapping them between the community and a deviation can change that test. A type that preserves everything group stability uses must therefore retain each person’s position in everyone’s rankings; in a strict preference network, that makes every person distinct. The large population then has no small-support type distribution of the kind the proposed mirror requires.

The proposed LP is coherent for a different input: people are interchangeable within cohorts, and each cohort ranks cohort labels. The transports then compare masses of categories rather than groups of named individuals. That may be a sensible model of coalition exchange, but it removes the identity-specific relations that make the paper’s object a preference network. The issue is not that rankings need ties or that communities need fractional membership; it is that collapsing people into categories discards information used by the original stability test.

The strongest identity-preserving alternative would keep a continuum of individuals and give each one a ranking over that continuum. But a community and a deviation would then be measurable sets of identities, not just vectors of cohort masses. A finite-support distribution over preference types does not by itself encode those sets or the ranks of their members. Restricting the model to finitely many rank classes restores a compact input only by returning to cohort-level preferences.

That is a substantial objection to this particular mirror, but it does not prove the requested universal negative. A setting where people truly care about cohort membership could make the proposed model worthwhile if the authors accept it as their problem. I cannot honestly rule that out; the negative case is strongest when a mirror must preserve the paper’s individual-to-individual preference structure.
