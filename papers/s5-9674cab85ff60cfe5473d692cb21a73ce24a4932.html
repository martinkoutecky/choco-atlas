The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchored case for a continuous mirror under your criteria: the paper has no numbered result classifying a computational problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar.

The nearest result is Theorem 4.1, proved here. For every odd number of teams \(n=2k+1\geq 3\), it gives an explicit single round-robin schedule with guaranteed rest time \(k-1\), games-played difference index \(1\), and rest difference index \(1\). That is a constructive extremal result, but the paper does not state or analyze a complexity result. Proposition 3.1 and Proposition 4.1 give upper bounds; Theorem 3.1 proves an incompatibility among schedule properties. Those are combinatorial claims too.

A possible continuous model would give team types masses \(\mu_t\) and choose a schedule of matchups to optimize normalized rest and fairness measures. But with a continuum of teams, every team would face a continuum of opponents. The paper’s asynchronous ordering and its measures based on intervening games do not carry over directly: allowing mass matchups or continuous rounds changes the scheduling object. Keeping the individual teams preserves the original problem, but does not give it a low-support population model.

So I don’t see a precise continuous problem, anchored in a named computational result, that the authors would recognize as their problem. Theorem 4.1 could motivate a separate study of continuum limits for tournament schedules, but that would go beyond what this paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.1, but it gives an explicit schedule with optimal discrete measures; it does not classify the complexity of finding such a schedule. That leaves no named computational result of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror.

A more careful continuous proposal would scale the odd-team construction as \(n\) grows. Its guaranteed rest time, divided by \(n\), tends to \(1/2\), while its games-played and rest-difference indices, divided by \(n\), tend to \(0\). That can describe an asymptotic limit, but it does not preserve the paper’s asynchronous scheduling object: the indices count intervening games in a single ordered sequence. A continuum of team identities has no next game in that sequence, and a time-based replacement changes what “rest” measures.

There is a more serious obstacle than the literal definitions. In a type-mass model, teams of the same type collapse into one mass, but the theorem’s schedule must ensure that every particular pair of teams meets exactly once. A mass of one type meeting itself does not say which pairs have already played or which team has a bye. Keeping a continuum of individual labels can retain that information, but then the schedule depends on identity-level pairings rather than a finite-support population description. Dividing each round into a continuum of slots can make a measurable model, but it replaces the paper’s finite sequence of games with a new continuous-time scheduling problem.

So I do not see a worthwhile mirror of Theorem 4.1 within the programme’s computational scope. The negative case is strongest against a high-multiplicity, finite-support mirror; it is weaker against the broader claim that continuous-time tournament scheduling could be interesting in its own right. That broader direction would need its own model and computational questions, which this paper does not supply.
