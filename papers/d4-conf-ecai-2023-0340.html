The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass society with finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) retailer types and rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), where each type specifies per-retailer excess supply \(e_\theta\), unmet demand \(d_\theta\), salvage value \(s_\theta\), selling price \(p_\theta\), shortage penalty \(\rho_\theta\), equity weight \(w_\theta\), and pairwise logistics costs \(\kappa_{\theta\phi}\), compute an optimal type-to-type flow \(x_{\theta\phi}\) and a transshipment price \(r_{\theta\phi}\) for each used pair. Require \(\sum_\phi x_{\theta\phi}\leq\mu_\theta e_\theta\), \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta\phi}\leq\mu_\phi d_\phi\), \(x_{\theta\phi}\geq0\), and \(s_\theta\leq r_{\theta\phi}\leq p_\phi+\rho_\phi-\kappa_{\theta\phi}\) on eligible pairs; maximize \(\sum_{\theta,\phi}x_{\theta\phi}[w_\theta(r_{\theta\phi}-s_\theta)+w_\phi(p_\phi+\rho_\phi-r_{\theta\phi}-\kappa_{\theta\phi})]\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of retailer mass with finite type support and homogeneous full logistics profiles. The platform chooses aggregate flows \(x_{\theta\phi}\) and common per-unit prices \(r_{\theta\phi}\) to maximize the paper's weighted transshipment gain under type-level supply, demand, and price bounds. The resulting rational linear optimization problem has \(\tau^2\) type-pair variables.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's allocation and transshipment-pricing computation. It does not transfer Theorems 1 and 2's individual truthfulness and participation guarantees, or establish the experimental fairness and efficiency findings for the continuous model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a finite high-multiplicity implementation approximate the mass allocation while retaining meaningful counterparts of WVT's truthfulness and individual-rationality guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the allocation problem in Theorem 3, proved in this paper. The theorem states that “there exists a strongly polynomial algorithm for computing transshipment allocation and pricing in WVT.” Its proof represents each retailer once as a possible sender and once as a possible receiver, gives those nodes capacities equal to reported excess supply and unmet demand, and reduces the allocation problem to weighted bipartite \(b\)-matching. The edge weight is the weighted gain to the sender and receiver from one unit of transshipment.

I would mirror it as Mass-WVT. Consider a platform with a continuum of retailers normalized to total mass \(1\), given by a finite support of \(\tau\) retailer types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\). A type \(\theta\) completely specifies the retailer’s relevant attributes: order quantity \(Q_\theta\), realized demand \(D_\theta\), purchase cost \(b_\theta\), salvage value \(s_\theta\), selling price \(p_\theta\), shortage penalty \(\rho_\theta\), equity weight \(w_\theta\), and logistics class. The logistics classes determine a common per-unit transport cost \(\kappa_{\theta\phi}\) between every source of type \(\theta\) and destination of type \(\phi\).

Each retailer has per-retailer excess supply \(e_\theta=(Q_\theta-D_\theta)^+\) or unmet demand \(d_\theta=(D_\theta-Q_\theta)^+\). The platform chooses total flow \(x_{\theta\phi}\) from each type to each type and a per-unit transshipment price \(r_{\theta\phi}\). The flow must obey

\[ \sum_\phi x_{\theta\phi}\leq \mu_\theta e_\theta,\qquad \sum_\theta x_{\theta\phi}\leq \mu_\phi d_\phi,\qquad x_{\theta\phi}\geq 0, \]

and a price for an active pair must satisfy

\[ s_\theta\leq r_{\theta\phi}\leq p_\phi+\rho_\phi-\kappa_{\theta\phi}. \]

The question is to compute an optimal flow and prices maximizing the weighted gain over no transshipment:

\[ \sum_{\theta,\phi}x_{\theta\phi} \left[ w_\theta(r_{\theta\phi}-s_\theta) + w_\phi(p_\phi+\rho_\phi-r_{\theta\phi}-\kappa_{\theta\phi}) \right]. \]

A solution is an optimal rational flow and a feasible price for each used type pair. The buying cost \(b_\theta\) is part of the complete type description, though—as in the paper—it does not affect this allocation objective because direct buying revenue is unaffected by transshipment.

The regime I find plausible is a large platform serving many stores or retailer partners that fall into a small number of standardized operating and logistics classes. Members of a class have the same per-store parameters and the same transport cost to each other class; there may be millions of retailers but only dozens or hundreds of supported types. A mass unit represents a fraction of stores, so \(\mu_\theta e_\theta\) is that class’s aggregate supply per unit of normalized population. This is a reasonable setting for repeated store formats and zone-based shipping rates. It is also recognizable as the authors’ allocation question: aggregate retailer pairs by type, and the same gains, supply limits, demand limits, and price constraints apply.

I expect Class A: tractable. The type-level problem retains the paper’s weighted bipartite flow structure, now on \(\tau\) source types and \(\tau\) destination types, with rational capacities derived from \(\mu_\theta\). A polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\) is the natural expectation; the same \(b\)-matching route may give a strongly polynomial algorithm. This case covers Theorem 3’s allocation-and-transshipment-pricing computation. The paper’s Theorems 1 and 2 establish truthfulness and individual rationality, but they are not complexity anchors for this mirror. The supplied paper has no comparable named hardness result to prefer.

The weakest point is the logistics aggregation. If each retailer’s position gives it a distinct pattern of transport costs, then its full type may be unique and \(\tau\) can grow with the number of retailers; the computational compression disappears. This mirror also does not establish that the paper’s individual side-payment rule remains meaningful in a continuum: an infinitesimal retailer’s removal may have no effect on the allocation. That leaves useful follow-up questions—whether finite high-multiplicity instances can implement or approximate the mass allocation while preserving the paper’s incentive and participation guarantees, and how much rounding error results when mass flows are converted back to store-level trades.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the retailer’s relevant type includes its position in the transshipment network. Two sellers with the same inventory, demand, prices, and penalties are not interchangeable if they face different transport costs to potential trading partners. Preserving those costs may require a type to include a whole row and column of the network’s cost matrix; in an ordinary network, that can leave nearly every retailer in a different type. Then \(\tau\) grows with the number of retailers, and the proposed population compression disappears. This is a real limitation of the mirror, especially because the paper’s fairness motivation is precisely that network position changes retailers’ outcomes.

But it does not defeat the stronger version of the mirror. A hub-and-spoke network, or a platform with standardized zones and pairwise rates, can have many retailers with identical full logistics profiles. In that regime, aggregating their capacities into \(\mu_\theta e_\theta\) and \(\mu_\phi d_\phi\) preserves the allocation problem. It is a plausible instance regime, and the authors would likely recognize it as their transshipment allocation question.

There is also a gap if Mass-WVT is meant to preserve the full strategic mechanism. A nonatomic retailer has zero effect on the allocation, so removing it gives no meaningful counterpart to its finite VCG-style side payment. Treating a whole type mass as one reporting agent would instead change the strategic unit from an individual retailer to a cohort. That weakens the case for a continuous mirror of WVT’s incentive guarantees, but Theorem 3’s allocation-and-transshipment-pricing computation can still be asked without claiming those guarantees.

So the negative case identifies a narrow regime and a mechanism-design caveat, but it does not defeat the allocation anchor. The proposed type-level flow problem remains a credible continuous mirror. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists.
