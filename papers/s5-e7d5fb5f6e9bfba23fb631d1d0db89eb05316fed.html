The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous-population problem in Section 7, but this paper has no eligible named computational anchor under the rule in the brief. Theorem 1 says that, under its stated aggregation axioms, an aggregation function must be dictatorial; it is attributed to Dietrich and List and Dokow and Holzman. Theorem 2, proved here, says that a judgment transformation function satisfying universal domain, rational co-domain, unanimity preservation, minimal relevance, and independence/systematicity must be the identity. Both are axiomatic impossibility results, not complexity results about a computational problem. The paper names no result asserting membership in P, hardness, FPT, or a comparable computational classification. Its axiomatic theorems cannot supply the requested anchors.

The strongest candidate for a *new* mirror comes from the unnumbered constrained-minimal-revision construction in Section 7. Call it Continuum Single-Peaked Minimal Revision. Let \(A\) be a set of policy options. A type is a complete ranking \(R\) of \(A\), and the input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) rankings with positive mass. In a large deliberative consultation, \(\mu_R\) is the share of participants with ranking \(R\). A solution chooses a left-to-right axis \(\prec\) maximizing the mass of rankings already single-peaked on that axis, then gives a mass-transfer plan \(x_{R,R'}\). Transfers may go only to rankings \(R'\) that are single-peaked on \(\prec\) and minimize Hamming distance from \(R\), measured by the number of pairwise judgments changed. The resulting post-deliberation distribution is \(\nu_{R'}=\sum_R x_{R,R'}\), with \(\sum_{R'}x_{R,R'}=\mu_R\). The computational question is to find the axis and transfer plan, given the support and binary rational masses.

This fits the paper’s subject more closely than a continuum of outcomes would: the population is the continuous object, while each person still holds a complete, rational judgment set. Large structured consultations could have many participants but comparatively few distinct rankings; if revision costs or other relevant attributes differ, they belong in the type as well. The paper’s examples of ranking judgments and its discussion of deliberative polls make this a recognizable extension of its model.

The weak point is substantial: this candidate mirrors a construction in the prose, not a numbered computational result. The paper’s empirical deliberative-poll groups have only 150–350 participants, and personal reasoning or revision policies might require so many type distinctions that \(\tau\) approaches the population size. The fixed-axis revision step and the search for a best axis also need their own complexity analysis; the paper supplies neither. So the honest positive case is that the paper motivates a plausible new high-multiplicity computational problem—not that it already contains a named result whose discrete complexity can be tested in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper contains no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic impossibility result attributed to Dietrich and List and Dokow and Holzman; Theorem 2 is an axiomatic impossibility result about judgment transformation. Neither states a complexity classification or poses an algorithmic problem. Under the programme’s stated standard, there is no named computational anchor here.

The proposed “Continuum Single-Peaked Minimal Revision” does not supply one. It turns a Section 7 construction into a computational task: choose an axis that maximizes the mass already single-peaked on it, then assign each type a nearest single-peaked ranking under Hamming distance. That task is grounded in the paper’s example, but the paper presents it as one possible deliberation function, not as a computational problem or result. Computing that function for a distribution would be a new project motivated by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of a result it establishes. The distinction matters if the programme is trying to build a discrete-to-continuous dictionary for computational problems.

That is a meaningful scope objection, but it does not defeat the proposed task on its own merits. A large consultation with many participants and relatively few ranking types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The paper itself supplies the best-fit axis construction and suggests Hamming distance; aggregating participants by ranking preserves the relevant information for that task. The reported poll sizes do not rule out other regimes, and the fact that the answer might be tractable or hard is not an objection.

So the negative case is strong only under a narrow reading of “mirror”: there is no computational result in this paper whose continuous counterpart can be assessed. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous computational question can be built from the paper. The proponent’s candidate appears to be a sensible new problem, though not a mirror of a named computational result.
