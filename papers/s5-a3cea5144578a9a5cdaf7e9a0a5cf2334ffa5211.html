The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest positive case under the stated anchor rule: this paper has no named result about computational complexity or an algorithm for a computational problem. Its theorems concern game-theoretic implementation—whether a mechanism exists whose equilibrium outcomes equal a specified set of allocations.

The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that, on a subdomain \(D_1'\) of \(D_1\), the Rational Expectations Equilibrium correspondence is globally implementable. The subdomain retains finite state spaces, non-exclusive information, state-independent endowments, and concave preferences, and imposes an additional condition ensuring equilibrium allocations are interior. That is a substantive existence result, but it does not say the problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Theorem 3, also proved here, says that nonzero interim envy-free allocations are globally implementable in \(D_1\); it has the same limitation. Theorem 1 gives a Bayesian-monotonicity characterization, with its sufficiency direction requiring at least three agents and non-exclusive information, but it is an implementation criterion, not an algorithm.

A natural-sounding extension would take many households from finitely many complete economic and information types, with \(\mu_t\) denoting each type’s population share, and ask whether the REE allocation correspondence can be implemented in the resulting mass economy. But to make that the paper’s problem, one must specify what mechanisms and equilibrium deviations mean for a continuum of agents. Treating each type as a strategic bloc changes the agents; treating individuals as negligible changes how their actions affect outcomes. And even once specified, the question remains about existence of an implementing mechanism unless a finite input representation and computational task are added.

So this is a plausible direction for a new large-economy implementation theory, but it is not a continuous computational mirror anchored by this paper’s named results. Its probability distributions are over states, not a continuous population, and its public-good allocations do not make the society continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper states no computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes full Bayesian implementation through Bayesian monotonicity and other assumptions. Theorem 2 says the REE correspondence is globally implementable on a restricted domain; Theorem 3 says the same for nonzero interim envy-free allocations. The remaining theorems and examples are also about implementation or non-implementation. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a finite computational task with an input size. The paper asks whether a mechanism exists, not how hard it is to find one.

There is a sensible large-economy model one could build: let \(\mu_t\) give the population share of each complete economic type, including its preferences, endowments, information and prior, and specify per-capita resources and allocations by type and state. Repeated household types could make this a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The difficulty is not that such a society makes no sense. It is that the paper’s implementation results do not supply a computational question for it. A continuum version would also have to define the mechanism and equilibrium concept for negligible individuals or strategic type blocs. Those choices may be reasonable, but they determine a new implementation problem.

For Theorem 2, one might ask for an algorithm to compute an REE, or to construct a mechanism implementing the REE correspondence, in an economy with finitely many rationally described types. Computing an equilibrium is a possible research question, but it is not what Theorem 2 establishes. Constructing an implementing mechanism needs a finite representation of mechanisms and strategies. The theorem permits arbitrary continuous concave utilities and mechanisms, and provides no such representation or computational bounds.

Theorem 3 has the same gap. One could ask an algorithm to find an interim envy-free allocation and then implement it. That adds a search objective and a mechanism construction task; the theorem establishes neither. Theorem 1 is the closest candidate for a computational mirror: one could study the complexity of testing Bayesian monotonicity or synthesizing an implementing mechanism for encoded instances. Restricting utilities and mechanisms enough to make that task finite could make it well-posed, but its complexity would be a new result about a newly specified problem. The paper’s characterization itself has no complexity classification to continue.

So I cannot support the literal claim that no worthwhile continuum implementation theory could be built. A large-population version might be worthwhile in economics. The negative case is narrower and stronger: none of the three anchors is a computational result, and making one computational requires adding the task, input representation and mechanism restrictions that the paper leaves open. That looks like a new research programme rather than a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
