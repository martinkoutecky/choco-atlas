The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-institution endowments \(z_t\), and exposure densities \(h_{tu}\), where each type-\(t\) institution owes \(h_{tu}/n\) to each type-\(u\) institution in an \(n\)-institution replication, choose payment rates \(\lambda_t\in[0,1]\) and type-level flows \(x_{tu}=\mu_t\mu_u\lambda_t h_{tu}\). Compute the greatest clearing flow by maximizing \(\sum_{t,u}x_{tu}\) subject to \(\mu_tz_t+\sum_s x_{st}-\sum_u x_{tu}\ge0\) for every \(t\).

The model it lives in

A society with types \(T\) and masses \(\mu_t\), per-institution endowments \(z_t\), and dense exposure densities \(h_{tu}\); choose payment rates \(\lambda_t\in[0,1]\) and flows \(x_{tu}=\mu_t\mu_u\lambda_t h_{tu}\) to maximize \(\sum_{t,u}x_{tu}\) subject to nonnegative type-level equity.

What the mirror covers

Covers greatest-clearing computation with proportional division in the dense, block-homogeneous type regime; it leaves Theorem 4.1’s general formulation and the constrained-awards, constrained-losses, and priority formulations in Theorems 4.3–4.8 unmirrored. The paper attributes the finite proportional LP to Eisenberg and Noe, so that computational answer is already known.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the greatest-clearing solution in finite replications converge to the stated type-mass LP, and can the finite-replication error be bounded?

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest mirror is the greatest-clearing problem for a large, replicated network of small financial institutions. Take \(n\) institutions whose types describe their endowment, proportional repayment rule, and exposure schedule to each counterparty type. Within a type, institutions are interchangeable; an institution of type \(t\) owes a small, equal claim to each institution of type \(u\), with total exposure to that class remaining finite as \(n\) grows. Think of a large system of mutual or cooperative institutions with standardized balance sheets and contracts: perhaps \(10^4\)–\(10^6\) institutions but only tens or hundreds of distinct types. That is a scenario, not a claim about every financial network.

My anchor is Theorem 4.2. It says that, for a finite network with proportional division rules, the greatest clearing payment matrix is the unique solution to the linear program maximizing \(\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\), subject to \(P_{ij}=\lambda_iL_{ij}\), \(0\le\lambda_i\le1\), and \(z+P^\top\mathbf{1}-P\mathbf{1}\ge0\). The paper presents this as a special case of Theorem 4.1 and attributes the result to Eisenberg and Noe (2001); it is not a new proof in this paper. The anchor is algorithmic: the paper does not give a comparable named hardness result.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous-Type Greatest Proportional Clearing. An instance has a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a per-institution endowment \(z_t\ge0\) for each type, and rational exposure densities \(h_{tu}\ge0\). The density \(h_{tu}\) means that a type-\(t\) institution has liability \(h_{tu}/n\) to each type-\(u\) institution in an \(n\)-institution replication; its total liability to that class approaches \(\mu_u h_{tu}\). Let \(x_{tu}\) be the total payment from type \(t\) to type \(u\), normalized by population size, and let \(\lambda_t\) be the fraction of type \(t\)’s liabilities paid. Compute the greatest clearing flow by solving

\[ \begin{aligned} \max\quad &\sum_{t,u\in T}x_{tu}\\ \text{subject to}\quad &x_{tu}=\mu_t\mu_u\lambda_t h_{tu} &&\forall t,u\in T,\\ &0\le\lambda_t\le1 &&\forall t\in T,\\ &\mu_tz_t+\sum_{s\in T}x_{st}-\sum_{u\in T}x_{tu}\ge0 &&\forall t\in T. \end{aligned} \]

The last constraint is the type’s aggregate nonnegative-equity condition. Maximizing total payments selects the greatest clearing flow, just as in the paper’s finite model. This is the same underlying question—what is the greatest mutually consistent set of payments under a specified bankruptcy rule—expressed in masses of institution types. It does not replace the population by a continuum of payment amounts: money is already divisible in the paper, while its network \(N\) remains finite.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The displayed problem is an LP with \(O(\tau^2)\) flow variables and rational coefficients, where \(\tau=|T|\); it is solvable in polynomial bit complexity in \(\tau\) and the input length. The finite-to-continuous bridge is concrete: the type-level variables aggregate payments in replicated finite networks. A useful follow-up is to bound the difference between this solution and the greatest clearing matrix in a finite replication. Another is to ask whether constrained equal awards or priority rules also yield tractable type-level problems, or whether their threshold choices remain computationally hard as \(\tau\) grows. I would treat those as new questions, not as results established by this anchor.

The mirror covers only the greatest-clearing result for proportional division rules in a block-homogeneous, dense network. Its weakest point is that real financial networks often have sparse, idiosyncratic bilateral exposures, and their important institutions may be few and structurally distinct. That limits the regime: it does not undermine the mirror for a large population of institutions with standardized exposures, but the positive case depends on that scenario being one the authors and a referee accept as economically meaningful.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.2 gives a mirror only after imposing a very special network structure. The paper allows an arbitrary liability matrix \(L\): which institution owes which other institution determines whose payments arrive, and can affect who defaults. The proposed type-level LP replaces that graph with block-uniform exposure: every institution of type \(t\) owes the same infinitesimal amount to every institution of type \(u\). If institutions grouped by type instead have different counterparties, aggregate exposure \(x_{tu}\) does not say which institutions receive it, so it cannot determine their asset values or clearing payments. Preserving those distinctions may require a type to encode its counterparties, leaving as many types as institutions.

That objection rules out a broad mirror of the paper’s general network model, but the proponent’s narrower setting directly answers it: a large, well-mixed system with standardized institutions and exposure schedules. In that regime, type-level masses preserve the feedback between payments and assets; the missing self-liabilities vanish in the limit. The resulting LP is a coherent greatest-clearing problem, and the paper’s arbitrary finite-network formulation does not itself rule it out. A continuous treatment of money is not a continuous population, so those are distinct models.

The remaining case against is about payoff: proportional clearing is already an LP in Theorem 4.2, which the paper identifies as a special case of the earlier Eisenberg–Noe result. The proposed mirror appears to be a direct aggregation of that LP, rather than a new complexity boundary. But that makes it a modest contribution, not an invalid question; an already known answer does not defeat a well-posed mirror under the programme’s criteria.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. I can argue that it applies only to a deliberately homogeneous, densely connected regime and offers limited new complexity analysis. I cannot show that no plausible such regime exists. The universal negative case is weak here; the proportional-clearing mirror survives.
