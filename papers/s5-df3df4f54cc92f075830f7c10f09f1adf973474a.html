The continuous mirror question

Given a rational society μ over complete rankings of \(m ≥ 2S-1\) candidates, with \(S>1\), and fixed tie-breaking, decide whether there are a ranking \(b\) and rational λ>0 such that the original winner set \(W(μ,S)\) has size \(S\), its members occupy the top \(S\) positions of \(b\), and after adding mass λ of type \(b\), the new winner set intersects the original in exactly one candidate, excludes the candidates in the top \(S-1\) positions of \(b\), and includes every candidate in the bottom \(S-1\) positions of \(b\). Run weighted STV with quota equal to total mass divided by \(S+1\) and proportional surplus transfers.

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution μ over ballot-ranking types; the decision variables are a ranking \(b\) and added mass λ>0. The updated society is μ+λδ_b, with quota \((1+λ)/(S+1)\); ask whether the prescribed winner-set reversal occurs under weighted STV.

The objection that survived

The discrete construction relies on the floor-plus-one quota and small integer margins, and neither case establishes that the full winner-replacement pattern persists under a mass quota.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no anchor-compliant positive case in this paper: it contains no named result about computational complexity or an algorithmic classification. The closest formal result is Proposition 2, proved here. It says that for every \(S>1\), there is a profile \(P\) and a collection of identical added ballots \(\mathcal B\) such that the original winners rank in the top \(S\) positions on those ballots; after adding them, exactly one original winner remains, none of the top \(S-1\) candidates on the added ballots wins, and every candidate in their bottom \(S-1\) positions does win. That is a structural existence result, not a result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or similar. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor complexity prediction to report.

A plausible *follow-on* continuous question would be Mass-\(\varepsilon\) STV Negative Involvement. An instance gives a rational distribution \(\mu\) over valid ballot types, a seat count \(S\), a candidate \(c\), a rational bound \(\varepsilon>0\), and a fixed tie-breaking rule. Use weighted STV with the continuous Droop quota \(1/(S+1)\) of total mass. Ask whether there is a ballot type \(b\), with \(c\) ranked last, and a rational added mass \(0<\lambda\le\varepsilon\) such that \(c\notin W(\mu,S)\) but \(c\in W(\mu+\lambda\delta_b,S)\). A yes-instance is witnessed by \(b\) and \(\lambda\). The closest scenario is a large STV election where many voters follow a relatively small menu of rankings, so the number of voters greatly exceeds the number of distinct types.

This question has a real connection to the paper’s definitions and Proposition 2, but it is an extension, not a complexity result the paper proves. Its weakest point is that exact STV uses the integer Droop quota \(\lfloor n/(S+1)\rfloor+1\); the continuous quota removes its rounding and one-ballot effects. Some paradoxes may survive through changes in elimination and transfer order, but the paper does not establish that they do. I would leave the complexity class open rather than claim tractability or hardness from these results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is a scope objection: this paper gives no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 1 and 2 establish structural bounds and constructions; the appendix describes a heuristic search on a fixed dataset. The paper never asks for an algorithm or a complexity classification. So the proposed Mass-\(\varepsilon\) question is a new computational problem, not the continuous version of a computational result in the paper.

That objection survives a better formulation. One could ask for the least added population share that makes a losing candidate win:
\[ \rho_c(\mu)=\inf\left\{\lambda>0:\exists b,\ c\text{ is last in }b,\ c\in W\!\left(\frac{\mu+\lambda\delta_b}{1+\lambda},S\right)\right\}. \]
A strict quota convention and fixed tie-breaking would make the model precise. But minimizing this quantity for arbitrary input societies is not a problem the paper formulates: its worst-case propositions construct profiles, and its empirical section searches particular elections. The optimization would need its own motivation and complexity analysis.

That is a limit on the case against, though. The proposed problem fits winner robustness, and a large election with many voters sharing a relatively small set of rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s use of identical added ballots makes the mass perturbation especially natural. I don’t see an honest argument that defeats that stronger follow-on as unworthy in every scenario. The negative case is strong only if “mirror” means a continuous counterpart to a computational result the paper actually establishes; it is weak against the broader claim that this paper motivates a worthwhile continuous computational question.
