What the mirror covers

The proposed extension draws on Theorem 3's group-strategy-proofness statement; it does not turn Theorems 1 or 2, or the application corollaries, into computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive ChoCo case from this paper: it has no named result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 1 proves lattice and rural-hospitals properties; Theorem 2 proves side-optimal equilibrium existence; Theorem 3 proves group-strategy-proofness. These are structural and incentive results, not computational anchors.

The closest positive lead is a population version of the paper’s two-sided matching application: types would encode agents’ full utilities and market roles, masses would represent repeated types, and type-level bundle assignments would clear contract flows at common prices. One could ask whether a buyer-optimal mass-clearing equilibrium resists every positive-mass coalition’s profitable misreport. A large labor market with repeated worker and employer profiles is a plausible setting.

That would be a new structural extension of Theorem 3, not an anchored continuous computational mirror. The extension would need its own existence and incentive proofs, and the paper supplies no utility encoding from which to pose a complexity question. I would label it a research lead, not a ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest possible anchor is Theorem 3, since its buyer-optimal mechanism could be translated to a labor market with repeated worker and employer types. A credible version would give each type a mass, scale capacities per capita, and ask whether a buyer-optimal mass-clearing equilibrium resists profitable misreports by every positive-mass coalition. That is a sensible structural question. It still is not a computational mirror of this paper: Theorem 3 proves group-strategy-proofness for a mechanism defined as a function on utility profiles; it says nothing about the time or resources needed to compute that mechanism.

The same gap remains if the task is sharpened to “compute a buyer-optimal equilibrium.” The paper permits arbitrary continuous utility functions, but supplies no finite encoding for them, no input model, and no precision convention for the real-valued prices. Binary rational masses alone do not make the equilibrium problem a finite computational problem. One could add a representation—say, a restricted parametric utility class or a demand-oracle model—but then the encoding and restrictions define a new algorithmic problem. That may be worthwhile work; it is not a complexity question furnished by a named result here.

Theorem 2 offers a similar route: compute a side-optimal equilibrium for a continuous market. But it is again an existence and optimality theorem, with no stated optimization input or computational objective. Theorem 1 is further from an anchor: once two equilibrium price vectors are given, their coordinatewise maximum and minimum are immediate to form. Its contribution is proving those vectors are equilibria, not giving an algorithm for finding them. A mass version could preserve the structural claim, but would not supply the missing computational formulation.

I would not base the case on the absence of plausible repeated types. A labor market with a small number of complete preference and capacity types is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s two-sided matching application gives a natural setting for a mass-based extension. Nor is the existing continuous-transfer literature a collision: continuous prices do not make the population continuous. Those points support a possible structural extension, but they do not turn this paper’s results into computational anchors.

So the negative case is strong against treating this as a ChoCo mirror: none of the named results classifies the complexity of a computational problem, and the most promising continuization requires adding both a computational task and an input model. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise. The continuum labor-market extension just described could be worth pursuing as a new structural result; on the paper and program as given, though, it is not a continuous computational mirror.
