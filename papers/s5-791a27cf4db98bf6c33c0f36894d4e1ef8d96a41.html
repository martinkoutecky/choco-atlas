The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), ranking types \(T\), rational total type masses \(\mu_t\), rational stage masses \(a_1,\ldots,a_H\), and an observed history of revealed rankings and panel assignments, each stage reveals its ranking before the chair acts. Future rankings are hidden and must fill the remaining stages while matching the remaining masses \(\mu_t\). At each stage \(j\), the chair routes a fraction \(x_j\in[0,a_j]\) of that stage's mass to the left panel and the rest to the right. Does there exist a contingent policy that makes a designated candidate \(c\) a plurality winner of the runoff for every compatible future ranking sequence, when all winners of each panel's first-round election advance?

The model it lives in

A finite-horizon online game over a continuous population: ranking types carry rational mass totals \(\mu_t\), each stage has rational mass \(a_j\), Nature reveals that stage's ranking subject to the remaining type quotas, and the chair chooses a fractional panel split \(x_j\). The objective is to decide whether a contingent policy guarantees the designated candidate's victory in the ties-promote plurality runoff.

The objection that survived

The chair knows the total ranking masses μ, which constrains future sequences beyond what the paper's voter order reveals; the proponent acknowledges this change but does not establish that it leaves the adversarial online problem equivalent.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the constructive and destructive plurality online partition hardness of Theorem 5.2. It leaves the general election-system bounds and plurality online adding and deleting results, including Theorems 4.1–4.8 and 5.1, aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fractional cohort routing remove the coNP-hardness, or do the candidate-side Hitting Set combinatorics still yield hardness?
  • What is the complexity in \(m=|C|\), support size \(\tau\), horizon \(H\), and rational input length \(L\), and what discrete-to-continuous correspondence holds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a continuous version of the paper’s online partition control for plurality. My lead is Cohort-Online-TP-Partition\(_\infty\). It continuizes the population while keeping the paper’s central difficulty: a chair must route voters as their preferences are revealed, and must guarantee the final outcome against every possible future.

A plausible regime is a large online consultation or public vote. Residents arrive in known cohorts, many share the same ranking over a shortlist of candidates, and an administrator assigns each arriving cohort to one of two review panels. The number of people is large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings is comparatively small. The order and mass of the cohorts are known, but their rankings are revealed only on arrival. Time remains a sequence of stages; the population within each stage is continuous.

Here is the problem. An instance gives a finite candidate set \(C\), a finite set \(T\) of ranking types, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and rational cohort masses \(a_1,\ldots,a_H\) summing to one. It also gives the observed history up to the current cohort: the rankings already revealed and how much mass was routed to each panel. The current cohort’s ranking is revealed before the chair acts. Future rankings are hidden; admissible future sequences are those that fill the remaining cohorts and match the remaining type masses \(\mu_t\).

After seeing each cohort’s ranking \(t\), the chair assigns \(x_j\in[0,a_j]\) of its mass to the left panel and the rest to the right. A solution is a finite contingent policy specifying those assignments from the observed history at each stage. The question in the constructive mode is whether there is a policy that makes a designated candidate \(c\) a final plurality winner for every admissible future ranking sequence. Each panel first holds a plurality election; under ties-promote, every panel winner advances. Then all voters cast their votes over the candidates who advanced, and \(c\) must win that runoff. Atomless mass makes fractional routing literal: the chair can send a chosen fraction of an otherwise identical cohort to each panel. A destructive mode can use the paper’s corresponding goal of preventing the specified disfavored segment from winning.

The anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper. It states that, under plurality and the ties-promote rule, both online constructive and online destructive control by partition of voters are coNP-hard. The paper proves this by reduction from the complement of Hitting Set; it does not claim coNP-completeness. The constructive half is my lead mirror, and the destructive mode above mirrors the other half. This is a strong anchor because the voting rule and control action are recognizable, and because the paper’s hardness comes from a natural election system rather than one built to encode a formula.

I expect the continuous problem to be Class B, with real uncertainty. The reduction’s combinatorics involve the candidate set and Hitting Set, so there is reason to suspect that hardness may survive when the population is represented by masses. But the paper’s result does not establish that: splitting a cohort across panels may let the chair achieve outcomes unavailable with whole voters. The central question is whether that fractional freedom removes the hardness or whether the candidate-side combinatorics still force it. In either case, the problem gives a concrete test of where continuization helps.

The weakest point is the online information model. The paper knows the future voter order, but not future votes; this mirror knows the future cohort sizes and the overall type distribution \(\mu\), but not the order of future types. Those aggregate quotas are a reasonable continuous description of society, yet they change what the chair knows. The paper’s authors could recognize the mirror because the decision remains sequential voter partition control with the same two-stage election and the same worst-case guarantee. Still, the continuous version needs an explicit account of why the chair knows \(\mu\).

The main follow-up is to determine the complexity as a function of \(m=|C|\), \(\tau\), \(H\), and the rational input length \(L\), and to establish a discrete-to-continuous dictionary for the two control modes. In particular: does fractional routing dissolve Theorem 5.2’s coNP-hardness, or does hardness transfer because its essential combinatorics live in the candidate agenda?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper clears the basic bar for a mirror: Theorem 5.2 is a named computational result about online constructive and destructive control by partition under plurality. The axiomatic and analytic work nearby is not a collision. So the negative case has to rest on whether the online problem can be continuized while keeping its essential information structure.

That is where the proposed cohort mirror is vulnerable. In this paper, a voter’s place in the known arrival order matters: it determines what the chair has already observed and how many decisions remain. Two voters with the same ranking at different positions are not interchangeable in the problem’s full sense. Treat position as part of the type, and the number of types grows with the horizon. Group voters into cohorts to keep the type space small, and the model changes the decision point: the chair now sees a cohort’s ranking and routes a divisible mass, rather than responding to individual votes. Giving the chair the final distribution \(\mu\) also constrains future vote sequences and supplies aggregate information absent from the paper’s model.

A more careful version could make time explicit: give each of \(H\) stages a rational mass, let Nature reveal a ranking at each stage subject to remaining type quotas, and let the chair route a fraction of that stage’s mass. This is a coherent online mass-control problem, and both halves of Theorem 5.2 could anchor it. But it is a dynamic game over a scheduled stream, not a static society distribution by itself. If the quotas are hidden to preserve the paper’s information structure, the instance is no longer a single given \(\mu\); it needs a separate model of uncertainty over possible societies. If the horizon is instead atomless, an individual decision has zero mass, so meaningful control again requires macroscopic stages.

These objections apply equally to the constructive and destructive mirrors. They make the proponent’s particular formulation less faithful than it first appears, but they do not eliminate the staged mass model. The programme’s scope permits co-modifying the model when population continuity requires it, and the authors could reasonably recognize this version as their problem. I therefore cannot honestly defeat the anchor: the strongest negative case is that the paper’s online chronology makes its continuous mirror less direct and less obviously suited to the programme’s aggregate optimization tools. That is a reason for caution, not a persuasive case that no worthwhile mirror exists.
