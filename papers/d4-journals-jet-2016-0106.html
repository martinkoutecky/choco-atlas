What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; the paper's results concern existence and structure of universal type spaces.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 2, proved here: for a regular class of preference representations, there exists a universal preference-type space, and every preference-type space in the class has a unique preference-type morphism into it. That is a substantial existence and structure result, but it is not a computational result: it gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or hard problem.

So I cannot give a valid anchor under your criteria. The paper contains no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 2 does not become such an anchor just because its construction uses measurable spaces and hierarchies.

A large game with many agents sharing complete preference and belief types could be a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But Theorem 2 concerns which hierarchies a universal space contains and how type spaces map into it; it does not optimize over a society, aggregate mass over agents, or ask what intervention changes an outcome. To formulate such a task, I would have to add a computational problem the authors do not study. The paper’s use of “type” and the measurable structure therefore offer a conceptual connection, not an honest continuous-population mirror of one of its computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the closest candidate, but it does not state a computational result. It proves that a universal preference-type space exists and that each preference-type space maps uniquely into it. Its “continuity” concerns the functionals representing preferences, not a continuum population. The theorem gives no finite input representation, algorithm, complexity parameter, or computational task to mirror. On the criteria here, the absence of a named computational result is decisive.

The best attempted rescue would be to study the complexity of constructing the universal space, computing the unique morphism, or deciding whether two types induce the same preference. But those questions need a new finite encoding and restrictions on the paper’s arbitrary measurable spaces and preference classes. A finite-depth or finite-type version might be a worthwhile project, but it would be a new computational theory of the construction, not the population-continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

Nor does aggregating many players by preference type supply the missing link. The paper studies each player’s hierarchy of preferences over acts contingent on nature and other players’ types; it does not impose anonymity or make a population distribution the object of study. A large anonymous game might make such aggregation sensible, but adding its payoffs, solution concept, and computational problem would create a different target. So the case against a worthwhile mirror is unusually strong here: there is no computational result to anchor one, and the most plausible replacements require supplying the very computational problem the paper does not address.
