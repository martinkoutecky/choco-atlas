The continuous mirror question

Given finite type and category sets \(T\) and \(J\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j>0\) with \(\sum_{j\in J}q_j=1\), and a bi-valued utility vector \(u_t\) for each type, compute an exact HZ allocation and supporting prices. The allocation must satisfy \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), \(\sum_{j\in J}x_{tj}=1\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}=q_j\); at prices \(p_j\ge 0\), each \(x_t\) must maximize utility over unit bundles \(z\ge 0\) with \(p\cdot z\le 1\), and be a cheapest unit bundle attaining at least \(u_t(x_t)\). Is such a solution computable in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|J|\), and the total input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of applicants represented by finitely many complete types, each with mass \(\mu_t\), assigned one unit each among interchangeable seat categories with per-capita supplies \(q_j\). Type-level bundles \(x_t\) clear category supplies in aggregate; the objective is to compute an HZ equilibrium allocation and supporting prices.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 3's HZ computation result and its EPS algorithmic basis. It leaves the paper's rule equivalences, strategic and fairness claims, and Nash bargaining results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the EPS bottleneck steps be computed in polynomial time for rational type masses and category supplies?
  • Can the exact allocation and supporting prices be output with polynomial bit length?
  • How does this type-compressed solution relate to finite markets where the category counts \(q_jN\) are integral?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Corollary 3. It says that, for \(n\) agents and \(n\) items with bi-valued utilities, an HZ assignment can be computed in \(O(n^3\log n)\) time. The paper proves this corollary from its HZ–EPS characterization, using the EPS algorithm’s running time. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result; I would not claim the paper shows that discrete hardness dissolves. [The paper’s PDF](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/HZ_binary.pdf)

A plausible setting is a large placement market: a very large cohort of applicants is assigned one seat each, with seats grouped into a few genuinely interchangeable categories. Applicants within a type have the same preferences and other problem-relevant parameters; there are \(N\) applicants but only \(\tau\) types, with \(\tau\ll N\). Category \(j\) has \(q_jN\) seats, where \(q_j\) is its per-capita supply and \(\sum_j q_j=1\). This fits settings such as a regional system assigning large cohorts to a small menu of standardized programs or positions. The premise is that seats within a category are interchangeable; if each seat’s identity matters, this aggregation is not the right mirror.

I would pose Type-Compressed Continuous HZ as follows. An instance gives finite sets of types \(T\) and seat categories \(J\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational supplies \(q_j>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has a bi-valued utility vector \(u_t\) over categories. The quantities \(\mu_t\) and \(q_j\) are exact binary rationals; let their total input bit length be \(L\). The question is to output an exact type-level allocation \(x_{tj}\) and supporting prices \(p_j\).

Here \(x_{tj}\) is the expected share of category \(j\) received by a member of type \(t\), so mass \(\mu_t x_{tj}\) goes from type \(t\) to that category. A valid solution must satisfy

\[ x_{tj}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{j\in J}x_{tj}=1,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}=q_j. \]

At prices \(p\), each type’s bundle \(x_t\) must maximize \(u_t(z)\) over unit bundles \(z\ge0\) with \(p\cdot z\le1\). It must also be a cheapest bundle achieving at least that utility:

\[ p\cdot x_t = \min\{p\cdot z:\ z\ge0,\ \sum_j z_j=1,\ u_t(z)\ge u_t(x_t)\}. \]

This is an equilibrium-computation problem, not a welfare-maximization problem: the output is any price-supported HZ assignment meeting those conditions. The proposed complexity question is whether one can compute such a solution in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|J|\), and \(L\), independent of the population size \(N\).

I expect this mirror to be tractable. For bi-valued utilities, the paper’s characterization reduces HZ to EPS on binary-reduced preferences. In the proposed model, the bottleneck calculation operates on type masses and category supplies: a mass-capacitated flow can replace counting individual agents and items. That suggests a route to an exact algorithm polynomial in the compressed input. The finite \(O(n^3\log n)\) bound alone does not establish this: a proof would need to show that EPS’s bottleneck steps and exact rational outputs remain efficient when the input is \((\mu,q)\), rather than \(n\) individual rows and columns.

This mirror covers Corollary 3’s HZ-computation result and its EPS algorithmic basis. It does not extend the paper’s claims about strategyproofness, envy-freeness, or Nash bargaining. A useful follow-up is whether the type-compressed algorithm has polynomial output bit length, and how its exact solution relates to large finite markets whose category counts \(q_jN\) are integral.

The soft spot is that the paper already studies fractional assignments and lottery outcomes, so a referee could mistake this for outcome-space continuity. The proposed mirror instead makes the population continuous: \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\), and supplies scale with that population. Still, its strongest case depends on interchangeable categories and on proving the compressed algorithm; the paper does not do either. The mirror looks like a sensible HZ problem, but its algorithmic gain remains a research question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only strong anchor is Corollary 3: it is a named computational result, so the paper cannot be dismissed as having no algorithmic claim to mirror.

The best objection is that the proposed compression needs symmetry on the item side as well as repeated agent types. In the paper, items are individually named: even with \(\tau=1\), agents could distinguish all \(n\) items, leaving an \(n\)-dimensional problem. Getting a bound in \(\tau\), \(|J|\), and \(L\) therefore requires grouping interchangeable items into categories. That makes the mirror a capacity-based extension of HZ, not an automatic consequence of duplicating agents.

But this does not defeat the stronger version. Large cohorts assigned to standardized positions are plausible, and per-capita supplies are a natural companion to population mass. Type-level allocations preserve each member’s unit-demand bundle and market clearing; for finite rational instances, they can also represent symmetric finite HZ marginals. The weighted EPS bottleneck problem is a clear computational question. Whether its solution turns out to be a routine weighted-flow extension is not, under the programme’s standards, a reason to reject the mirror.

The paper’s group-strategyproofness result is a weaker alternative anchor: unilateral deviations by measure-zero agents do not change an atomless society, and a positive-mass coalition version would need a new formulation. The Nash bargaining results are incentive and fairness claims, not computational complexity results. Neither gives the negative case a firmer footing than Corollary 3.

So the negative case is weak. The extra symmetry assumption limits the mirror’s scope, but a convincing scenario survives it. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists here; the strongest conclusion against it is that its research payoff is uncertain, not that the question fails.
