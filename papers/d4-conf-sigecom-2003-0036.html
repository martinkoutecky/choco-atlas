What the mirror covers

No result qualifies as a computational anchor; the feasibility and equilibrium theorems and the finite-instance experiments remain without a continuous computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large marketplace selling identical units, with many buyers drawn from a cohort whose members share the same valuation vector and information conditions. A type would be the complete vector \(v=(v^1,\ldots,v^K)\), together with any auction-relevant information; \(\mu_v\) would be that type’s population fraction. With supply scaled per buyer, a possible mass version would track each type’s demand at each price, aggregate demand, residual supply, and the purchase rights assigned per capita. The question would be whether truthful demand remains an equilibrium when a buyer can split bids across aliases.

That is a credible population-level direction, but it exposes the weakest point: in a genuinely nonatomic model, one buyer has no effect on aggregate demand, so the incentive result may become trivial. Making positive-mass deviations strategic would instead introduce coalition behavior. The mirror needs a principled way to retain the paper’s individual strategic question.

There is also a more basic limit on the positive case: the paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that whatever rights the agents exercise, the allocation cannot exceed supply. Theorem 2, proved here, says truthful bidding is weakly dominant when agents cannot observe one another’s bids. Theorem 3, also proved here, says that with private values, truthful bidding by everyone is an ex-post perfect equilibrium under any information-observation regime. These are feasibility and incentive results, not complexity classifications or algorithmic results about solving a computational problem.

The paper’s experiments compare social surplus and seller revenue, but they do not provide a numbered computational anchor either. So although a continuous-population auction is plausible to investigate, I cannot honestly state a qualifying continuous prover problem per anchor for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an allocation-feasibility claim; Theorems 2 and 3 are incentive results about truthful bidding. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm for one. Section 6’s comparison with VCG mentions computational cost, but does not formulate or analyze a computational problem. The experiments compare welfare and revenue for finite, randomly generated instances. So the proposed mass-auction setting is a possible new research question, but it has no qualifying result in this paper as its anchor.

The marketplace scenario is sensible at high multiplicity: many buyers can share a complete valuation type, and supply can scale with the population. But it does not preserve the paper’s central strategic question. A single buyer’s mass is \(1/N\), which vanishes as \(N\) grows. In the continuum, changing that buyer’s demand—or splitting it among aliases—does not change aggregate demand. The residual supply calculation that makes the paper’s incentive results meaningful depends on excluding a particular buyer. Excluding a measure-zero buyer leaves the aggregate unchanged.

Giving a valuation type positive mass does not repair this. If that mass can change its report together, the deviation is by a coordinated bloc, not by one buyer using false names. If the type instead represents many individual buyers, a unilateral deviation again has vanishing mass. A model with a continuum background and a few strategic atoms could preserve pivotal buyers, but then the strategic claims still concern those finite atoms; the continuous population is supplementary.

One could study welfare or revenue for a distribution of buyers, or how the protocol behaves in a large market. That might be worthwhile auction-design research. It would not be a computational mirror of a result in this paper: it would add a new asymptotic question where the paper offers finite experiments, not a computational theorem.

The negative case is strongest as a judgment about this paper’s fit for ChoCo. I cannot honestly prove that no continuous auction project could ever be worthwhile. But the proponent’s mass-market proposal does not supply the missing computational anchor, and its atomless limit removes the paper’s individual false-name incentive question.
