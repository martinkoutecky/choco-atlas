The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper. Theorem 3.1, proved here, says that every unanimous and strategy-proof social choice function on a top-circular domain with the maximal conflict property is dictatorial. Theorem 3.2, also proved here, says the same for a top-circular domain with the weak conflict property. Corollary 4.1 applies the result to the union of top-connected single-peaked and single-dipped domains; Corollary 4.2 recovers Sato’s result for circular domains. These are axiomatic results about which rules exist, not results about computational complexity or algorithms. So none qualifies as an anchor for the requested programme.

The strongest positive seed is the public-facility setting: many residents choose among finitely many locations, and a type is a resident’s ranking of those locations. A society could be represented by masses \(\mu_t\) over the admissible rankings, with many residents but relatively few distinct types. One could then ask about unanimous, strategy-proof rules \(F\) mapping \(\mu\) to a location. But that is an axiomatic continuization, outside the programme’s scope. There is also a substantive mismatch: in an atomless population, one resident’s report does not change \(\mu\), so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Treating a positive mass of residents as a strategic player would change the paper’s notion of agent and incentive compatibility.

Thus the facility scenario is plausible as a continuous-population setting, but I don’t see an honest computational mirror of the paper’s named results. Turning its theorems into one would require adding a computational problem the authors do not study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not supplied a computational result to mirror. The paper’s Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 and their corollaries characterize domains on which every unanimous, strategy-proof social choice function is dictatorial. They give no algorithm, complexity bound, or computational problem. Replacing profile counts with masses would not change that: it would produce another axiomatic question. One could invent a computational task, such as recognizing dictatorial domains from an input list of rankings, but that is a new problem, and the population distribution is not what the paper’s results study.

The public-facility example is a plausible setting for a population distribution over rankings, but it does not repair the mismatch. A distribution \(\mu\) forgets which person has which ranking, while the theorem’s conclusion singles out a particular individual as dictator. If each resident has zero mass, unilateral reports cannot change \(\mu\); strategy-proofness becomes vacuous, and individual dictatorship has no clear counterpart. Allowing a positive-mass bloc to deviate restores strategic influence only by changing the players and the incentive notion. Keeping a designated dictator as one voter instead gives that voter vanishing mass as the population grows; giving the dictator a fixed positive mass turns one person into a bloc.

So the strongest facility mirror remains an axiomatic extension outside ChoCo’s computational scope, with a further problem preserving the theorem’s individual-level content. This does not rule out useful continuous work near public-facility choice, or a separate complexity study of domain recognition. It does leave this paper without a worthwhile continuous-computational mirror of its results.
