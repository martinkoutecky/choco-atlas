The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: residual maximin share has a plausible high-multiplicity version, but this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor the requested mirror. Its theorems concern fairness guarantees and structural characterizations, not whether a problem is tractable or hard.

The closest result is Theorem 4.4, proved here: for every \(n\ge2\), the infimum of \(\RMMS/\MMS\) over finite additive instances with positive \(\MMS\) is exactly \(\lambda_n=2d_n/(3d_n-1)\). Theorem 7.1, also proved here, characterizes integer-threshold feasibility: \(T\le\RMMS\) iff every \(U\subseteq M\) satisfies \(\bp_T(U)+\cov_T(M\setminus U)\ge n\). And Corollary 6.3 says that no share-based lone-divider algorithm certified by residual self-feasibility can guarantee an \(\MMS\) factor above \(\lambda_n\). These are meaningful results, but none establishes a runtime bound or a computational hardness result. The paper itself leaves algorithms for computing \(\RMMS\) as future work.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large cohort of agents with the same additive values for a fixed set \(G\) of resource classes, such as course-seat categories. Give each class \(g\) a rational supply \(s_g\) per unit population mass. An individual still receives an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\), while an aggregate allocation can assign mass \(x_b\) of agents to each bundle, subject to \(\sum_b x_b=a\) and \(\sum_b b_gx_b\le s_g\). The regime is \(N\gg |G|\): the number of agents grows while the number of resource and valuation types stays small.

One precise, but unanchored, question for that setting is *Continuum-RMMS Threshold Feasibility*: given rational \(s_g\), additive values \(v_g\), and a threshold \(T\), is \(T\) residual self-feasible? That is, after every removal of mass \(\alpha<1\) of bundles \(b\) with \(\sum_g v_gb_g<T\), consuming at most the available supply, can the remaining goods be assigned to mass \(1-\alpha\) so every recipient receives an integral bundle worth at least \(T\)? A solution is a yes/no answer; one could study complexity in \(|G|\) and the input bit length. This is a recognizable continuous-population version of the paper’s underlying fairness question, with indivisible bundles preserved and aggregate assignments allowed to be fractional. Theorem 7.1 suggests a structural route, but the paper does not establish whether this problem is tractable or hard, so I cannot honestly assign it a class.

The strongest support for this direction is the paper’s exact frontier: its sparse witnesses use only a fixed number of good-value classes as \(n\) grows, and \(\lambda_n\) tends to \(2/3\). That makes a small-type, large-population regime credible. The weak point is decisive for the requested exercise: the paper proves a fairness frontier, not a computational result, and the common-valuation continuum model remains a proposed extension rather than a mirror of an algorithmic theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a mismatch with ChoCo’s computational brief. None of the three anchors is a named complexity result. Theorem 4.4 determines an extremal fairness ratio over finite instances; its natural population-limit counterpart would study the limiting guarantee \(2/3\), which the paper already identifies as the limit of \(\lambda_n\). That could be a meaningful limit theorem, but it would not chart an algorithmic complexity landscape. Corollary 6.3 is likewise a bound on fairness guarantees for a class of algorithms, not a runtime or hardness result. Its mass version would still be a guarantee statement.

Theorem 7.1 is the strongest anchor for a computational mirror: threshold feasibility is a decision problem, and the cut characterization could suggest algorithms. But the theorem characterizes finite subsets of a finite goods set. In a scaled cohort model, the natural variables instead describe masses assigned to integral bundle types and per-capita resource supplies. That is a coherent robust packing problem, but it is a new computational formulation, not the theorem’s cut problem carried over to a continuous society. Calling it a mirror would therefore depend on accepting a substantial model extension.

The best repaired version of that extension is plausible: identical agents with a fixed valuation over resource classes, per-capita supplies that scale with population, and aggregate assignments that give each agent an integral bundle. It avoids the objection that individual identity matters, and it makes high multiplicity sensible. Yet the paper does not analyze that model or give a complexity result that transfers to it. The proposal may be worth studying, but its value would come from the new algorithmic question, not from a continuous mirror of the paper’s established results.

So I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be posed. The negative case is strongest if “mirror” means a continuous-population counterpart to a computational result in this paper: on that reading, the paper supplies no such result. The universal claim is weak, though. Theorem 7.1 gives a natural bridge to a plausible high-multiplicity allocation problem, and I cannot honestly rule out that the resulting threshold-feasibility question is worth ChoCo’s time.
