The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for a follow-on model, rather than a continuous computational result already anchored in this paper. The paper has no named result about an algorithm, a complexity class, or a computational formulation. Its numbered results concern equilibrium existence and characterization.

The closest result is Proposition 4, proved here. For \(q<n\) and \(\delta\ge1\), it characterizes every stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium: the proposer keeps zero, selects \(q-1\) coalition members, offers each at least \(-\delta/n\), and distributes the remaining loss among the other members; a member accepts when offered at least \(-\delta/n\). The first-round proposal passes. That is a game-theoretic characterization, not a result about computing an equilibrium efficiently or about the complexity of a computational problem. So it cannot honestly serve as the required anchor.

A plausible setting for extending that question is a large climate-policy council of municipalities that must divide mandated emissions cuts. A type would record all bargaining-relevant features, such as a jurisdiction’s cost of cuts, delay sensitivity, and recognition probability; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of jurisdictions of type \(t\). A precise follow-on problem could take rational masses \(\mu_t\), type cost coefficients \(a_t>0\), a quota \(\alpha\), and \(\delta\ge1\). In a weighted-bloc version, a type \(s\) is selected to propose with probability \(\mu_s\), and proposes per-capita burdens \(\ell_t\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t\ell_t=1\). Each type votes as a bloc, and a proposal passes when the accepting types have total mass at least \(\alpha\). Type \(t\)’s round-\(r\) payoff is \(-\delta^{r-1}a_t\ell_t\). The computational question would be to find a stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium—proposal rules for each proposer type and voting responses for each type—and its induced allocation. This asks for an equilibrium of a continuous-population loss-bargaining model; it has no welfare-maximization objective because the paper’s game does not optimize one.

This is recognizable as a population version of the paper’s central bargaining question, but it is only a candidate extension. I would tentatively expect the variable-\(\tau\) case to be continuum-specifically hard because quota coalitions must be selected from weighted types; that is a conjecture, not a consequence of the paper. Further questions include whether Proposition 4’s proposer-zero and acceptance-threshold properties survive, and whether pure stationary equilibria always exist.

The soft spot is substantial: the candidate treats types as voting blocs, whereas the paper gives each individual one vote and selects an individual proposer. Also, the paper’s utmost-inequality equilibrium concentrates the entire loss on one member. In a type-mass model, that member has zero population mass; representing the outcome may require retaining exceptional individuals, which weakens the high-multiplicity story. There are zero qualifying computational anchors in this paper. The follow-on problem may be plausible to its authors, but the paper itself does not establish the computational mirror the programme is seeking.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest negative point is decisive for this paper: it states no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. Propositions 1–4 characterize equilibrium outcomes and strategies; the remaining named results concern equilibrium behavior, and the paper’s empirical findings concern observed bargaining. Proposition 4—the closest anchor—describes stationary equilibria, but asks nothing about computing one. So it cannot anchor a computational mirror of a result in the paper.

The proposed weighted-bloc climate model does not fix that gap. It turns each type into a voting bloc and lets a bloc’s mass count toward the quota. The paper instead gives each individual one vote and selects an individual proposer. A more faithful continuum version could keep a randomly selected, tagged proposer and let the rest of the population vote by type. But the paper’s experimentally supported utmost-inequality outcome puts the whole loss on one or a few individuals. Those exceptional recipients have zero mass in the continuum description. Encoding them requires retaining tagged individuals outside the type distribution; spreading their burden across a positive mass changes the outcome being represented. The issue is not that the continuous answer might differ. It is that the mass model cannot represent a central kind of allocation without extra structure.

One could add that structure, retain the tagged roles, and pose a new computational question about finding stationary equilibria in a heterogeneous bargaining game. That might be a worthwhile project. But it would be a follow-on model: the paper supplies neither an algorithmic task nor a complexity question for it. And broadening the setting to many similar municipalities does not remove the modelling choice between individual proposers and type-level blocs.

This case is strongest against calling the proposal a mirror of this paper. It is weaker as a universal claim that no continuous bargaining project could be worthwhile: the climate-policy setting is plausible, and a carefully specified extension may merit study on its own.
