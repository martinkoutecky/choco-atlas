What the mirror covers

The proposal attempts to cover the equilibrium behavior in Propositions 1–3; the experimental findings and treatment comparisons receive no continuous computational analogue, and the equilibrium distinctions collapse in the atomless version.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive opening is a population scale extension of the paper’s conformity game: a large referendum electorate with two complete voter types, A and B, sharing type masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\), where \(\mu_A+\mu_B=1\). Let \(x_t\) be the fraction of type \(t\) voting for B, and let B win when \(\mu_Ax_A+\mu_Bx_B\geq\theta\), for a quota fraction \(\theta\). Each voter gets \(v\) if the outcome matches their type, and a conformity bonus \(c\) if positive mass votes as they do, with \(v>c>0\). A possible computational question is: given rational \(\mu_A,\mu_B,\theta,v,c\), characterize the aggregate Nash equilibria and their possible truthful-voting fractions. The regime is plausible for a large referendum with many residents sharing one of two preference and payoff types; the number of voters grows while the number of types stays at two.

But this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are Propositions 1–3, proved here, and they characterize weakly undominated Nash equilibria in the fixed five-agent game. Proposition 1 gives truthful voting as the unique such equilibrium without conformity, for quotas \(q\in\{3,4,5\}\). Proposition 2 establishes nontruthful equilibria under conformity: type-A agents can vote either way for \(q\in\{3,4\}\), and type-B agents can vote either way for \(q\in\{3,4,5\}\). Proposition 3 gives the informational case with two forced type-B voters: type-A agents can vote either way for \(q\in\{3,4\}\), while type-B agents vote B for all three quotas; at \(q=5\), truthful voting is the unique equilibrium. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. These propositions are useful seeds for a new question, but they cannot serve as the required anchors.

The proposed continuum question also exposes its main weakness. In an atomless electorate, one voter cannot change the outcome or the mass on either ballot. That removes the individual pivotality on which Proposition 1’s unique-truthful-equilibrium result depends. Under the stated conformity rule, every aggregate vote split is an equilibrium: when both ballot masses are positive, an individual gets the conformity bonus either way; when only one is positive, a lone switch loses the bonus without changing the outcome. The equilibrium computation is therefore trivial and does not preserve the paper’s central strategic effect. Allowing blocs of positive mass to deviate could restore pivotality, but it changes who the strategic agents are.

So there is a plausible large electorate extension, likely easy to analyze, but no paper result to anchor it as a continuous computational mirror. Its weakest point is decisive: the source studies strategic groups of exactly five, and the atomless limit erases the pivotal-voter mechanism.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not attack the referendum setting on multiplicity: a large electorate with many voters of each complete type is plausible. The stronger objection is that Propositions 1–3 are equilibrium characterizations of a fixed five-person game, not computational results. They give no algorithmic problem or complexity classification for a continuous-population mirror to extend.

The natural atomless version also removes the mechanism those propositions study. Let \(x\) be the mass voting for B and let B win when \(x\geq\theta\). A single voter’s deviation changes neither \(x\) nor the outcome. Without conformity, every ballot choice is therefore a best response, so Proposition 1’s unique truthful equilibrium disappears. With conformity, if \(0<x<1\), either ballot has positive mass and a voter gets the bonus either way. At \(x=0\) or \(x=1\), an individual deviation creates no positive mass on the other ballot, leaves the outcome unchanged, and loses the bonus. Thus every aggregate split is an equilibrium. The forced-voter treatment fares no better: two named voters have zero mass in the limit; scaling them to a positive-mass forced cohort still does not make any individual pivotal. The three propositions’ equilibrium distinctions collapse.

A finite electorate with \(n\) voters can retain pivotality when it sits one vote from the quota. That incentive depends on a margin of \(1/n\), which disappears in the atomless model even as \(n\) grows. So this is not just a different equilibrium answer: the proposed continuum does not preserve the strategic feature being characterized. One could restore pivotality by making each type’s whole mass a coordinated bloc, but then the strategic players are blocs rather than the paper’s individual voters, and the five-person game has become a small finite game between two type groups.

A question about continuum equilibria could still interest game theorists. For ChoCo, though, it would be an analytic equilibrium extension, not a computational social-choice mirror. Any harder computational question—such as optimizing an intervention or selecting among equilibria—would need an objective or operation absent from the paper. The negative case is strongest on that scope and pivotality issue; it does not establish that every continuous mathematical extension of the paper is uninteresting.
