The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a theorem-backed positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered sections are exposition, and it gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or formal computational problem. So there are no eligible anchors to restate, and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The closest material is the prose in §§2.1–2.4: the English-auction discussion says the highest-valuation bidder wins and pays the second-highest valuation; the second-price discussion argues for truthful bidding; and the Dutch and first-price discussions describe bid shading. Those are not named computational results. A possible high-multiplicity setting would be wholesale auctions with many buyers sharing valuation, demand, and budget types. But turning the paper’s strategy discussion into a problem such as computing equilibrium bidding policies would add a formal equilibrium-computation question that the paper neither formulates nor solves.

That is the strongest honest positive lead, and also its weakness: a large population with few types is plausible for some auction settings, but the paper gives no result showing that this is the problem its authors are studying. The continuous analogue would be a new research question inspired by the topic, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its named sections describe four auction formats; none formulates an algorithmic problem or proves a complexity, optimization, or approximation result. The best candidate anchors are the claims about English-auction outcomes, second-price truth-telling, and bid shading in Dutch and first-price auctions.

For the English and second-price claims, the strongest continuous version would represent bidders by a valuation distribution and ask for the winner and payment. But those outcomes depend on individual order statistics. With finite support and positive mass at the highest valuation, the continuous society contains many top-valued bidders, so the second-highest value equals the highest. With an atomless distribution, there may be no highest-valued individual at all. In either case, the winner of a single object has zero mass and cannot be identified by the distribution. A tagged bidder could restore an individual incentive question, but then that bidder is an added finite agent, not an outcome computed from the continuous population. Truth-telling itself is also a unilateral-deviation claim: changing a type’s mass instead models a coordinated group deviation, which is a different question.

For Dutch and first-price auctions, a better proposed mirror would compute an equilibrium bidding function from a distribution of valuations. The paper does not specify the valuation model, beliefs, or equilibrium conditions needed to define that problem. More fundamentally, with a single object and a nonatomic population, an individual bidder’s chance of winning disappears. Keeping bidders pivotal requires retaining a finite bidder count; scaling the supply with population size gives a market-allocation problem with different strategic and computational questions.

A large market with typed buyers, per-capita supply, and repeated flower transactions could support worthwhile continuous auction research. But the paper states no computational result about such a market. It would require adding the supply model, objective, and algorithmic problem; it is a new question inspired by the subject, not a mirror of this paper. I would therefore reject this paper as a seed for the programme, while stopping short of claiming that no related continuous auction problem could ever be worthwhile.
