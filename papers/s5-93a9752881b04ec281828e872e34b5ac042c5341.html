The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible lead is Donor-Priority Allocation Optimization. There are no named P/NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity results in the paper. Its best algorithmic anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper’s appendix: under the paper’s regularity assumptions, for *any* blood-type classification, positive group masses, and compatible matching technology, reserving each group’s organs for that same group is an optimal allocation policy for maximizing aggregate donation. Under that policy, every group has the same transplant survival rate, \(p_i=\beta/\theta\). This is an exact solution to the paper’s optimization problem, though the theorem itself does not state a running-time bound.

For a computational version, fix a regular cost distribution \(F\) with \(F(0)>0\) and strictly convex \(1/F\), and fix rational shortage parameters \(0<\beta\leq\theta\). An instance consists of \(m\) blood groups, positive rational masses \(n_i\) summing to one, and a binary compatibility matrix \(\mu\) with \(\mu_{ii}=1\). The continuous population has mass \(n_i\,dF(c)\) of agents of group \(i\) and donation cost \(c\). A policy chooses \(\lambda_{ij}\), the share of group \(j\)’s organs offered to group \(i\), and survival rates \(p_i\). Feasible choices satisfy full organ use and market clearing,
\[ \theta F(\theta p_i)n_i p_i = \beta\sum_j F(\theta p_j)n_j\lambda_{ij}\mu_{ij}. \]
The objective is to maximize \(\sum_i n_iF(\theta p_i)\), the total donation rate. A solution is a feasible maximizing policy and its survival-rate vector.

Theorem 2 gives the solution directly: output \(\lambda_{ii}=1\), \(\lambda_{ij}=0\) for \(i\ne j\), and \(p_i=\beta/\theta\) for every group. For this fixed-\(F,\beta,\theta\) version, computing an optimum takes polynomial time in the encoded instance size; the policy itself has a compact description. This is a tractability result for the underlying allocation problem, not for an encoding of it. It preserves the paper’s objective, its market-clearing conditions, and the planner’s allocation decision. The most natural regime is a large deceased-donor population divided among a relatively small number of blood groups, with \(n_i\) representing population shares.

The main weakness is that the paper already makes the population continuous: it assumes a unit-mass population, group masses \(n_i\), and continuously distributed donation costs. So this is a useful computational reading of an existing continuum model, but a poor example of *introducing* population continuization. Also, a complete agent type includes both blood group and cost; the continuous cost distribution does not fit ChoCo’s finite-support, rational-mass representation. The strongest positive case is therefore narrow: this paper’s own continuous policy optimization has a clean, tractable computational formulation, but the population-continuization step is already present in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t see a worthwhile *continuization* mirror here: the paper has already made the population continuous. Section 2.1 explicitly has a continuum of agents in each blood group; Section 2.2 gives them unit total mass and a continuous donation-cost distribution. The proposed computational instance—mass \(n_i\,dF(c)\) for each group and cost—is essentially the paper’s own population model.

The proponent’s anchor is Theorem 2’s allocation problem, and that theorem already gives its exact solution under the stated regularity assumptions: \(\lambda_{ii}=1\), \(\lambda_{ij}=0\) for \(i\ne j\), and \(p_i=\beta/\theta\). Its objective is maximized at \(F(\beta)\). Turning this into an algorithm adds no substantive computational question: with \(F,\beta,\theta\) fixed, print the identity policy and common survival rate; the answer does not depend on the encoded group masses or compatibility matrix. The theorem is not stated as a complexity result, but its solution makes this particular computational formulation immediate.

A stronger nearby computational question might ask whether the optimal policy is unique. Theorem 3 already characterizes that: uniqueness holds exactly when the compatibility graph is acyclic. Testing that condition is cycle detection, and it depends on the compatibility graph, not on population multiplicity. Relaxing the cost-distribution assumptions could leave harder optimization questions, but those would extend an already continuous model and require specifying how \(F\) is encoded. They would not supply the missing population-continuization step.

This does not rule out useful computational work on organ-allocation models. It does make the negative case strong for this paper: its central population is already continuous, and its main policy-optimization result already supplies the answer the proposed mirror would compute.
