The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s lower bound for recruiting workers with identical unit value and private costs. Theorem 2—proved in this paper—says that for indivisible items no truthful budget-feasible mechanism can achieve an approximation ratio greater than \(1-1/e\). Its proof allows randomized mechanisms and budget feasibility in expectation. The hard family has \(N\) unit-utility sellers with i.i.d. costs from the stated distribution \(F\), and budget \(B=N\mathbb{E}[C]\).

A plausible regime is a large crowdworking platform offering the same one-off task to millions of workers. Workers with the same minimum acceptable payment are one type; their mass is the fraction of the workforce at that price. Costs can be binned into \(\tau\) rational values, with \(N\gg\tau\). A worker’s item remains indivisible—hire them or do not—but the allocation to a type can be fractional because it describes the share of that type hired. This is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s unit-value procurement problem.

I would pose the lead question as Continuum Truthful Procurement. An instance gives a finite list of cost types \(c_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and per-capita budget \(b=\sum_t\mu_t c_t\). Costs are private; the type distribution is known. Find a truthful, individually rational mechanism maximizing the hired mass subject to expected aggregate payments at most \(b\). A solution gives each reported type an allocation probability \(x_t\in[0,1]\) and expected payment \(p_t\), satisfying the truthfulness inequalities \(p_t-c_t x_t\ge p_s-c_t x_s\) for all types \(t,s\), individual rationality, and \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\le b\). The benchmark is the full-information fractional knapsack optimum
\[ \max \sum_t\mu_t y_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t\mu_t c_t y_t\le b,\qquad 0\le y_t\le1. \]
At this budget, buying the whole population is feasible, so the benchmark is \(1\). For finite support, the mechanism-design problem is a linear program with polynomially many variables and truthfulness constraints in \(\tau\).

The paper’s smooth hard prior gives the clean limiting case. There is mass \(1/e\) at cost zero and CDF \(F(x)=1/[e(1-x)]\) for \(0<x\le1-1/e\); its mean cost is \((e-2)/e\). A uniform posted price \(p^*=(e-2)/(e-1)\) spends exactly that budget in aggregate, since \(p^*F(p^*)=(e-2)/e\), and hires mass \(F(p^*)=1-1/e\). Theorem 2’s Lemmas 9 and 10 reduce truthful mechanisms under this prior to uniform posted pricing without improving their performance, giving the matching upper bound. Thus the continuous population has the same sharp \(1-1/e\) ceiling, now against a deterministic mass benchmark rather than a random finite electorate’s realized optimum. Rational finite-support approximations give explicit LP instances; convergence of their optimal ratios to the smooth-prior value is a natural follow-up, rather than a claim made by Theorem 2 itself.

I expect the finite-support mechanism-optimization problem to be tractable: its allocation, payment, incentive, and budget conditions are linear. The \(1-1/e\) limit survives for the paper’s hard prior, but it is an economic approximation impossibility, not NP-hardness or a transfer of computational hardness. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s unit-value, indivisible-item lower-bound setting and its expected-budget guarantee. It does not try to continuize the paper’s submodular results or its other mechanism constructions.

The weakest point is that the paper’s theorem is a Bayesian lower bound built from finite random markets, while this mirror treats the cost distribution as an exact population law and asks for a computationally explicit mechanism-design problem. The continuum comparison is well motivated by the theorem’s large-\(N\) argument and its posted-price reduction, but the finite-type approximation step needs its own proof.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it is a lower bound on achievable mechanism performance, not a computational complexity result. Its hard instance is already an i.i.d. cost model with \(N\) workers. As \(N\) grows, the empirical cost distribution concentrates around the prior, and the paper’s posted-price argument gives the limiting \(1-1/e\) ceiling. So a continuum version of that particular hard instance largely restates an asymptotic conclusion the paper already proves.

The proposed finite-support LP is a broader question, but its meaning shifts. If \(\mu\) is a prior over workers’ private costs, the LP describes an ex-ante Bayesian mechanism: each worker gets a menu based on their report, and only expected aggregate payments are constrained. The population mass then weights welfare and spending; it does not create a new aggregate computational problem. If \(\mu\) instead means the known, realized shares of cost types, the mechanism’s information and budget conditions need to be specified anew. In particular, the LP’s expected-budget constraint does not enforce the paper’s usual budget-feasibility requirement for every report profile. That is a substantive modeling choice, not a technicality about how to write the constraints.

The crowdworking scenario is nevertheless plausible. Many workers doing the same task can share a reservation-cost type, and maximizing hired mass does not depend on worker identities. A population model with private individual reports and an expected budget is coherent, and the resulting LP may be a worthwhile Class A result. But that supports a new Bayesian procurement problem; it does not by itself make Theorem 2 a new continuous-computation result. The source paper already supplies the central limiting performance bound for its hard prior, while the LP’s broader mechanism-design question is not what that theorem establishes.

So the best case against is that the proposed mirror either repeats the paper’s asymptotic lower-bound story or changes the anchor into a standard finite-type Bayesian design problem whose continuum interpretation does no distinct computational work. That case is only moderate. The high-multiplicity crowdsourcing regime is sensible, and I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every such formulation.
