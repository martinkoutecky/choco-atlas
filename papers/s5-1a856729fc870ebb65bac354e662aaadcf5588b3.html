The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no computational result as an anchor, and that is decisive for a mirror of this paper. Theorem 2, Theorem 14, and Theorem 17 establish semantic properties and correspondences; none classifies a computational problem. The conclusion even lists complexity analysis of ABA\(^+\) reasoning as future work. There is therefore no result here whose continuous analogue could test whether continuization changes a complexity landscape.

The proposed population scenario does not supply the missing anchor. If people share a complete ABA\(^+\) framework type, they share its answer to a fixed query. For a distribution over framework types, the fraction that skeptically accepts a claim is just the sum of the masses of types that accept it. Computing that fraction requires solving the semantics for each supported type; the population adds bookkeeping, not a computational question from this paper.

A stronger proposal would allow mass to move between framework types at some cost, then ask for the cheapest changes that make at least a target fraction accept a claim. That is a coherent continuous-population optimization problem, but its costs and interventions would be new modeling choices. Once acceptance is known for each type, the problem is an optimization over those labels; the paper supplies no result about it, and its framework syntax offers no natural analogue of the ranking-and-swap geometry that anchors the bribery examples in the programme.

This is a strong case against a *mirror of this paper*, but it does not establish that population-level argumentation is never worth studying. A later computational paper on ABA\(^+\) could provide a result worth continuizing. The present paper does not.
