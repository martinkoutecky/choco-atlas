The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 5 is an equilibrium-existence result about learning under costly observation; Theorem 3 characterizes when truth-telling observation occurs and gives a limit learning probability. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem. That is the strongest objection, because the programme’s gap is specifically computational.

The most charitable mirror of Theorem 5 would ask for the least community size—or, after a suitable payoff rescaling, the least population mass—at which an equilibrium can achieve a chosen learning level. For Theorem 3, it might ask whether a given observation policy yields truth-telling or what its limiting learning probability is. Those are conceivable new questions, but they are equilibrium and learning questions, not computational results supplied by the paper. The paper specifies no finite input representation or algorithmic target for them. Turning either theorem into an algorithmic problem would require adding a new problem, rather than continuizing the result.

Even the most natural population limit has little aggregate structure to exploit here. Agents in a community share the same signal and observations, and Proposition 1 establishes unanimous actions in equilibrium. A continuum version of a community therefore still has a common information state and a single binary community action; the action mass is concentrated at one endpoint. Scaling payoffs with community mass is a reasonable co-modification, but it does not produce the kind of rich distribution over types or mass-transfer problem at the centre of ChoCo.

Section 6.2 offers a stronger alternative: give agents independent private signals and represent their signal heterogeneity by a continuum of types. That could support a worthwhile continuous social-learning model. But its central object would still be equilibrium learning in a dynamic Bayesian game, rather than the computational social-choice questions this programme targets. I cannot rule out a useful computational extension of that game. The negative case is narrower: it would be a new research direction, not a continuous computational mirror anchored in this paper’s results.
