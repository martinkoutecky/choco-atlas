The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(X\), respondent classes \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), fixed rankings \(\succO_t,\succeqN_t,\succeqH_t\), and partial full-horizon data \(\mathcal D^F_t\), let \(P_t=\POH_t\cup R(\mathcal D^F_t)\). Given aggregate choice shares \(q_{S,x}\) for menus \(S\) in a finite set \(\mathcal Q\), decide whether there are nonnegative masses \(z_{t,R}\), indexed by \(R\in\operatorname{LE}(\operatorname{tc}(P_t))\), such that \(\sum_R z_{t,R}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), \(\succO_t\in\operatorname{LE}(\PNH_t)\) for each \(t\), and \(\sum_t\sum_{R:\topc_R(S)=x}z_{t,R}=q_{S,x}\) for every \(S\in\mathcal Q\) and \(x\in S\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over respondent classes with masses \(\mu_t\); each class has the paper’s fixed consequence rankings and partial observations. The decision variables \(z_{t,R}\) assign mass to admissible deterministic full-horizon rankings, and the question is feasibility: do those masses reproduce the observed choice shares while satisfying the paper’s order restrictions?

The objection that survived

Matching aggregate shares requires a latent distribution over full-horizon rankings, a heterogeneity and inference layer absent from the paper; this makes the mirror’s computational difficulty depend on the extension.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror uses Corollary 7.2’s incomplete-data rationalizability criterion and the protected-order structure to constrain type-level rankings and fit aggregate shares; it leaves the paper’s other individual-level identification, Kemeny geometry, and ordered-deliberation results outside the population problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the aggregate-share feasibility problem admit a polynomial-time separation procedure when parameterized by the number of alternatives, respondent classes, observed menus, and input bit length?
  • How does the continuous mass version relate computationally to finite populations with integer counts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result that explicitly classifies a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. Its best computational anchor is Corollary 7.2, proved in the paper. For fixed ordinary ranking \(\succO\) and Hereafter order \(\succeqH\), it says that incomplete full-horizon data \(\mathcal D^F\) are rationalizable by a path-independent choice rule satisfying Hereafter-Directed Revision exactly when \(\POH\cup R(\mathcal D^F)\) is acyclic. When they are, the compatible full-horizon rankings are precisely the linear extensions of its transitive closure, and that relation’s maximal elements give the sharp choices on unobserved menus. With the full model, it additionally requires \(\succO\in\LE(\PNH)\). The paper proves this criterion here, drawing on standard order-extension results; the polynomial-time cycle test is an immediate consequence, not a complexity classification stated by the authors.

The strongest positive mirror I can make from that anchor is a population rationalizability problem. Picture a large survey panel in which many respondents face the same finite menus, first under ordinary deliberation and then under a prompt emphasizing one of two fixed consequence dimensions. A type \(t\) records a respondent’s \(\succO_t\), \(\succeqN_t\), \(\succeqH_t\), and observed partial full-horizon dataset \(\mathcal D^F_t\). The population is given by rational masses \(\mu_t\) over \(\tau\) such types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). This is plausible when many respondents share the same elicited rankings and observation pattern, even if the total respondent count is much larger than \(\tau\).

Call the resulting question Mass Directional Rationalizability. In addition to those type masses and records, the instance gives a finite set of menus \(\mathcal Q\) and observed aggregate full-horizon choice shares \(q_{S,x}\), where \(q_{S,x}\) is the fraction of respondents choosing \(x\) from menu \(S\). For each type define \(P_t=\POH_t\cup R(\mathcal D^F_t)\). Ask whether there are nonnegative masses \(z_{t,R}\), one for each \(R\in\LE(\tc(P_t))\), such that
\[ \sum_{R\in\LE(\tc(P_t))}z_{t,R}=\mu_t \]
for every type, and
\[ \sum_{t}\ \sum_{\substack{R\in\LE(\tc(P_t))\\ \topc_R(S)=x}}z_{t,R}=q_{S,x} \]
for every \(S\in\mathcal Q\) and \(x\in S\). The ordinary ranking for each type must also extend \(\PNH_t\). The solution is a mass assignment to individual full-horizon rankings that fits the observed population shares. Each respondent still makes a single choice; the fractions describe how many respondents in each group have each admissible ranking.

The authors could recognize this as a population version of their incomplete-data rationalizability question: it retains their alternatives, fixed consequence rankings, directional restriction, and linear-extension characterization. It also stays focused on a continuous population, while fractional aggregate shares arise from that population. The closest connection to the paper is the feasibility of each type’s set of rankings, not a claim that the paper itself models respondent populations.

I would provisionally expect the unrestricted version to be continuum-specifically hard, with low confidence. Corollary 7.2 makes each type’s individual feasibility an acyclicity test, but matching aggregate shares couples choices across types. A linear program over the admissible rankings has potentially exponentially many variables, and its pricing problem optimizes menu-top rewards over linear extensions. The paper does not solve that pricing problem or establish hardness for it. Fixed \(m\) is tractable by enumerating rankings; the open question is the unrestricted case, parameterized by \(m,\tau\), the number of observed menus, and input bit length. Further questions include whether a polynomial separation oracle exists, and how much the answer changes when the population has finite integer counts instead of rational masses.

The weakest point is the change of object: this paper studies one decision maker’s choice functions, while the mirror studies population frequencies of such functions. The survey-panel scenario makes that lift intelligible, but it is an added population model, not a high-multiplicity regime established by the paper. If the criterion requires an anchor that explicitly states a complexity classification, then this paper offers no qualifying anchor; Corollary 7.2 is only the nearest exact computational test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s best anchor is Corollary 7.2, but it is a rationalizability characterization, not a complexity result. Given the ordinary ranking and Hereafter order, the data are compatible exactly when a directed graph is acyclic. That is an exact computational test, and its cycle check is straightforward; the paper neither identifies a difficult computational problem nor develops an algorithmic method whose tractability turns on population size. This weakens the case for a continuization programme built around the paper, though it does not by itself rule out a useful extension.

In the proposed Mass Directional Rationalizability model, a type records \((\succO,\succeqN,\succeqH,\mathcal D^F)\). For each type, Corollary 7.2 still gives the same independent acyclicity test. If the question is only whether the observed type records can be completed, assigning them masses adds no computational content: every positive-mass type must pass its graph test. The proposed aggregate shares \(q_{S,x}\) make the problem richer, but they also change its center. Feasibility then concerns fitting a population distribution over admissible rankings to choice-share data. Its potential algorithmic difficulty lies in that added mixture and aggregation problem, not in a continuous version of the paper’s result.

The strongest repair is a matched survey panel: many respondents face the same menus in both modes, and types retain each respondent’s paired observations and consequence rankings. That avoids losing the within-person comparison and makes repeated types plausible. But the paper’s model is still one person’s pair of deterministic choice functions. Moving to population shares requires a further model of heterogeneity, data aggregation, and which respondent-level features are observed. The paper supplies constraints on each respondent’s possible ranking; it does not establish that the resulting aggregate inference problem is one its authors would recognize as their question, or that the order structure gives a useful algorithm for it. Any exponential-variable LP and its pricing problem arise only after that extension.

So I would not count Corollary 7.2, on its own, as a strong continuous mirror: the faithful version reduces to weighted copies of a graph test, while the richer version is a new population-mixture problem. Still, the paired-panel scenario is coherent, and its aggregate feasibility question could be worth studying. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no such scenario is worthwhile; the negative case is strongest against treating this paper’s theorem as already furnishing one.
