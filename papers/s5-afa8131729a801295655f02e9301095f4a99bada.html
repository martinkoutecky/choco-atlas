The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(H,S,C\), rational masses \(\nu,\mu^S,\mu^C\), complete strict preferences for single and couple types, type-symmetric responsive hospital priorities with capacities, and fixed processing orders, run a mass-batched sequential-couples algorithm: deferred acceptance on single mass, followed by preferred-pair proposals by couple mass with capacity-respecting transfers of displaced mass and reprocessing of singles. Decide whether it succeeds without an aggregate collision; on success output the mass matching and certify that no positive-mass single/hospital or couple/pair block exists, while a collision is reported only as algorithmic failure.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity two-sided matching market with finite type sets and mass distributions \(\nu,\mu^S,\mu^C\), assignment variables \(x_{s,h}\ge0\) and \(y_{c,h,h'}\ge0\), aggregate capacity constraints, and a specified type-symmetric responsive choice rule. Each unit of couple mass receives an integral hospital pair, while mass represents repeated indistinguishable agents.

The objection that survived

The paper's collision predicate is copy-specific: a positive mass of identical hospitals requires a canonical aggregate allocation and collision rule, and different choices could induce different stability notions. The proponent sketches this issue but does not fully specify the aggregate rule.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Lemma 1's sequential-couples correctness and extends the paper's existence and Roth–Peranson questions to type masses. It leaves the approximate-truth-telling result, empirical claims, and Ronn's outside-literature NP-completeness result without a direct mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What aggregate choice and collision rule for hospital types makes the mass procedure the correct limit of many identical hospital copies?
  • For fixed type count \(\tau\), is batched sequential-couples success decidable in polynomial time?
  • Does positive couple mass \(\eta>0\) preserve stable mass matchings, or does it create continuum-specific hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a genuine, though qualified, positive case. This paper is not already a continuous-population paper: its agents remain individually represented, hospitals have individual preference orders, and the large-market limit is a probability statement over finite markets. It therefore supplies a strong motivation for a continuous mirror rather than a novelty collision.

One caveat is important. The paper contains no numbered theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, or a parameterized complexity classification. Its only explicit complexity statement is an unnumbered citation to Ronn (1990), which says that deciding whether a stable matching with couples exists is NP-complete. I therefore use the paper’s strongest named existence and algorithmic results as anchors, while not pretending that they are conventional complexity-class theorems.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Appendix A.3. It states that for a regular sequence of random markets, the probability that a stable matching exists converges to one as the number \(n\) of hospitals tends to infinity. “Regular” means, for constants \(\lambda>0\), \(a\in[0,\tfrac12)\), \(b>0\), \(r\ge1\), fixed preference-list length \(k\), and fixed capacity bound \(\bar\kappa\), that \(k_n=k\), \(|S_n|\le\lambda n\), \(|C_n|\le b n^a\), every hospital capacity is at most \(\bar\kappa\), and hospital-popularity ratios satisfy \(p_h/p_{h'}\in[1/r,r]\) and \(q_h/q_{h'}\in[1/r,r]\). Singles draw short preference lists from \(P_n\); couple members draw lists from \(Q_n\), and their joint preferences are generated by \(\rho_n\). The theorem says that, under those conditions, nonexistence becomes a vanishing-probability event.

The supporting anchor is Lemma 1, also proved here. It says that if the sequential couples algorithm succeeds, then the matching it produces is stable. Success means that the algorithm can place all couples and subsequently rejected single doctors without a couple member applying to a hospital already approached by another couple member, and without a displaced single doctor applying to a hospital previously approached by a couple member. The appendix proves the stronger statement that the resulting matching is strongly stable.

My lead mirror would be the following.

Call it \(\textsc{Continuum-Couples-Stability}\). An instance contains finite sets of hospital types \(H\), single-doctor types \(S\), and couple types \(C\), together with rational mass distributions \(\nu\) over \(H\), \(\mu^S\) over \(S\), and \(\mu^C\) over \(C\). A type includes the complete information relevant to matching: an individual preference list for a single doctor, a preference ordering over hospital pairs for a couple, or a responsive priority ordering and capacity for a hospital type. Hospital type \(h\) represents \(\nu_h\) units of capacity-bearing hospitals, with total capacity \(\kappa_h\nu_h\).

A solution consists of variables

\[ x_{s,h}\ge0 \]

for the mass of single type \(s\) assigned to hospital type \(h\), and

\[ y_{c,h,h'}\ge0 \]

for the mass of couple type \(c\) assigned to the ordered hospital pair \((h,h')\), including outside-option assignments. Every unit of couple mass receives one whole pair; the variables are fractional only because they describe a population of indistinguishable couples. Hospital loads must respect capacities.

Stability is the paper’s stability definition lifted to mass. A positive mass of single type \(s\) and hospital type \(h\) forms a block if those singles prefer \(h\) to their current assignments and a positive amount would be retained by the hospital’s responsive choice rule. A positive mass of couple type \(c\) and pair \((h,h')\) forms a block if the couple prefers that pair to its current pair and both members would be retained by the corresponding hospital choices; when \(h=h'\), the two members are treated jointly exactly as in the paper. The question is:

Does the instance admit a stable mass matching? If so, output one; otherwise output NO.

This is not an outcome-space relaxation and not a lottery over individual assignments. It continuizes the population while preserving the integral bundle received by every unit of couple mass.

The natural regime is a large residency or internship clearinghouse with many repeated cohorts. Doctor types could encode specialty, geography, short rank list, and whether the household is a couple; couple types encode the full joint ranking over compatible program pairs. Hospital types could encode location, specialty, capacity, and standardized priority rules over applicant categories. The number of agents is large while the number \(\tau=|H|+|S|+|C|\) of complete types is fixed or modest. A small but positive couple mass \(\eta=\sum_c\mu^C_c\) represents the empirically small fraction of couples without eliminating their complementarities.

My provisional forecast for \(\textsc{Continuum-Couples-Stability}\) is Class C in the unrestricted model. The cited NP-completeness result does not automatically transfer: its reduction may rely on many individually distinct preference profiles, so it is not evidence of high-multiplicity hardness. But fractionalizing population mass does not remove the combinatorics of complementary couple assignments. The continuous problem may therefore be hard for reasons intrinsic to couples, not because there are many named agents. A meaningful subproblem may instead be Class A when preference lists are bounded, hospital priorities are type-symmetric, and all pair types are explicitly enumerable; that is exactly the boundary worth investigating.

The second mirror is \(\textsc{Continuum-Sequential-Couples}\), anchored in Lemma 1. Its input is the same type-and-mass instance plus an ordering of couple types and single-doctor types. First run a mass version of doctor-proposing deferred acceptance on the single-doctor masses. Then process each couple type in preference order, assigning as much currently available mass as possible to its best feasible hospital pair and transferring displaced single mass onward. Finally process all displaced single mass. The output is either:

  • a mass matching together with a finite successful proposal/rejection transcript, or
  • the first collision showing that the aggregate sequential algorithm failed.

The expected result for this problem is Class A. With explicit finite type sets, proposals can be batched by type rather than repeated once per individual, and the computation involves only rational mass transfers over hospital types and hospital-type pairs. A successful transcript certifies stability by the continuous analogue of Lemma 1. Failure is not a certificate that no stable mass matching exists, just as in the paper.

This also explains why Corollary 1 is relevant without treating it as a separate anchor: it states, under the same regularity assumptions as Theorem 1, that the Roth–Peranson algorithm produces a stable matching with probability tending to one. The continuous question is whether the corresponding batch mechanism remains a useful constructive procedure on type masses, and when its success can be certified efficiently.

The authors should recognise both mirrors. They preserve the same objects—hospitals with capacities, singles, couples with preferences over pairs, responsive hospital choice, blocking, and the outside option. The main modification is exactly the one the continuization programme asks for: named agents with identical complete descriptions are replaced by masses of one type.

The weakest point is that the paper’s proof of Theorem 1 depends heavily on distinct hospital identities, random short lists, and a linear number of vacant hospitals. Moreover, its assumption \(|C_n|=O(n^a)\) with \(a<\tfrac12\) makes the couple fraction tend to zero, whereas a useful continuum model usually keeps \(\eta>0\). Thus the positive-mass problem is an extension, not a literal restatement of the theorem. That weakness is also the research opportunity: determine whether stability is tractable when couples have vanishing mass, whether a positive couple mass creates continuum-specific hardness, and whether Ronn’s discrete hardness can be compressed into a bounded-type high-multiplicity instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 is an asymptotic probability statement about existence; Lemma 1 is a correctness implication for a finite algorithm; and Corollary 1 says that an algorithm succeeds with probability tending to one. None gives a worst-case complexity, approximation, parameterized, or exact-algorithm result for a computational problem. The only explicit NP-completeness claim, Ronn’s result, is merely cited. Thus the proposed stable-mass feasibility problem is a new study inspired by the paper, not a continuization of a computational result proved in it.

Theorem 1 is especially resistant to the proposed mirror. Its large-market parameter is the number \(n\) of individually named hospitals. Doctors draw short lists from distributions over those hospital identities, while hospital preferences may be arbitrary rankings of individually named doctors. Preserving complete types therefore requires types that encode preferences over an expanding set of hospitals and, for couples, over hospital pairs. The type space grows with \(n\), destroying the high-multiplicity compression that would make a continuous formulation relevant.

Collapsing hospitals into finitely many hospital types does not repair this. It changes the object whose randomness drives Theorem 1: collisions are no longer rare encounters between independently chosen hospital identities, but systematic interactions among positive masses of identical hospital types. Priority orders, capacities, and couple preferences must all be redefined at the type level. That may produce a sensible new matching model, but it is not the theorem’s continuous counterpart.

There is also a sharper tension in the treatment of couples. The regularity assumption is \(|C_n|=O(n^a)\) for \(a<\tfrac12\), while the number of hospitals is \(n\). Hence the couple population has vanishing density. A continuum with positive couple mass, as proposed, leaves the regime of the theorem; a continuum faithful to the theorem assigns couples zero limiting mass, in which case the distinctive complementarity disappears from the society itself. A two-scale model retaining a vanishing but computationally visible couple population could be interesting, but that would be a new asymptotic programme rather than the paper’s high-multiplicity mirror.

Lemma 1 does not provide an independent rescue. It says that a particular finite proposal process is stable if it succeeds. Its key predicate is whether a named hospital has previously received an application from another named couple member. In a mass model, a hospital type may represent many copies. One must decide whether an application to the type collides with all copies, is routed to one copy, or is absorbed fractionally by aggregate capacity. These choices produce different algorithms and different notions of blocking. Refining the copies restores the original identity-level model; batching them requires a new aggregate choice rule and a new stability proof. A “successful transcript” is therefore only a proposed implementation of a new algorithm, not a computational question established by Lemma 1. Moreover, failure of that test is not failure of stability, so it does not even decide the natural existence problem.

Corollary 1 has the same defect in probabilistic form. Its conclusion concerns the probability, over independently generated finite preference lists, that the Roth–Peranson procedure returns a stable matching. A deterministic continuous society \(\mu\) has no such sampling noise. If one reintroduces random finite realizations, one has returned to the paper’s original model; if one introduces a random continuum of types, one has added a stochastic model whose computational treatment is not supplied by the paper. The proposed deterministic batch mechanism therefore drops the central probabilistic content of the corollary, while the stochastic repair ceases to be a pure continuization of the population.

The positive case is not absurd: repeated cohorts of doctors, couples, and hospitals are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a type-level matching problem could be worth studying for ChoCo in its own right. But that concession is precisely the weakness of the paper-specific mirror. The proposed questions require new definitions, new algorithms, and new results; they do not expose a computational result already present in this paper. Under the programme’s anchor rule, all three proposed anchors fail, and the cited NP-completeness result cannot be used to fill the gap.
