The case FOR (proponent)

On the brief’s literal anchor requirement, this paper has no qualifying computational result. Its named results are Propositions 1–4, but none asserts NP-hardness, membership in P, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic running-time bound. Proposition 1 proves an impossibility for stable mechanisms and WIM; Proposition 2 proves a one-half welfare bound; Proposition 3 proves an impossibility for Pareto optimality and IM; Proposition 4 gives asymptotic expected welfare gains. Thus I cannot honestly present any of them as a Class A, B, or C complexity anchor.

The strongest positive case, if structural matching results are admitted as provisional anchors, is nevertheless a genuine continuous-population mirror of Proposition 1.

Take finitely many communities \(C_1,\ldots,C_\kappa\). Within each community, there are finitely many male and female types. A type specifies the community, side of the market, eligibility, and a strict ranking over the finitely many opposite-side types. Let \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) be the masses of male and female types, with total male and female mass equal. Each type represents an atomless population of otherwise indistinguishable agents, so the number of agents is much larger than the number of types: \(N\gg \tau\).

This is plausible for a centralized school-admission or residency market. A type could mean, for example, students from one district with a given priority profile over school categories, or residents with a given ranking and eligibility profile over hospitals. The paper itself presents school districts, hospitals, and centralized clearinghouses as applications, so the continuous version preserves its institutional question rather than replacing it with an unrelated matching model.

For any set \(P\) of communities, a matching is a measurable measure-preserving assignment of individual mass between the male and female populations in \(P\). The aggregate variable \(x_{ab}\) records how much mass of male type \(a\) is matched to female type \(b\), but each infinitesimal agent still receives one integral partner. Stability means that no positive mass of male and female types can mutually prefer one another to their assigned partners. A matching scheme is a family \(\{\sigma_P\}_{P\subseteq\{C_1,\ldots,C_\kappa\}}\).

The lead problem is:

\[ \textsc{Continuous Stable-WIM Feasibility}. \]

Given the finite type masses, rankings, and community partition, does there exist a measurable matching scheme \(\{\sigma_P\}\) such that every \(\sigma_P\) is stable and, for almost every agent \(x\),

\[ \sigma_{\{C_1,\ldots,C_\kappa\}}(x) \succeq_x \sigma_{\{C(x)\}}(x)? \]

A solution is the complete matching scheme; a negative answer certifies that no stable WIM scheme exists.

This is the direct population-continuous analogue of Proposition 1, proved in the paper: for every society with at least two communities, no stable matching mechanism is weakly integration monotonic. The paper’s two-community counterexample survives verbatim at positive mass. Let each community contain unit mass of one male and one female type. Both male types rank the woman type in community \(A\) above that in \(B\); both female types rank the man type in \(B\) above that in \(A\). Segregated stability matches within communities. Integrated stability matches the \(B\)-men to the \(A\)-women and the \(A\)-men to the \(B\)-women, so positive mass of \(A\)-men and \(B\)-women is worse off. Replicating the four agents by arbitrary masses gives \(N\to\infty\) while \(\tau=4\).

I would expect this feasibility version to be structurally tractable in the finite-type setting: the obstruction is already visible in a constant-size type pattern. It is not hardness transferred from the discrete problem, nor continuum-specific hardness. More interesting computational variants would optimize the mass harmed by integration or the minimum compensation needed to restore WIM.

A second, weaker but useful mirror comes from Proposition 2, proved in the paper. In the paper’s notation, every stable matching scheme satisfies

\[ \left|\{x:\sigma^*(x,C_x)\succ_x\sigma^*(x,S)\}\right| \leq \frac{|M^S\cup W^S|}{2}, \]

and the bound is tight. The continuous question is:

\[ \textsc{Continuous Maximum Integration Harm}. \]

Given a finite type-mass instance and stable segregated and integrated matching schemes, compute or maximize

\[ H(\sigma) = \lambda\bigl(\{x:\sigma_{\{C(x)\}}(x)\succ_x\sigma_S(x)\}\bigr). \]

The continuous conjecture/theorem is

\[ H(\sigma)\leq \frac{1}{2}\lambda(\text{entire population}), \]

with equality attained by the replicated two-community construction above. The proof transfers by measure: every harmed agent’s segregated partner must be helped after integration, otherwise that pair blocks the integrated matching. The partner map is measure-preserving, so harmed mass cannot exceed helped mass. With finitely many types, I would expect this optimization to be approachable through aggregate matching and flow techniques, although the paper itself supplies no such algorithm.

The main weakness is decisive: these are faithful continuous matching questions, but not computational-complexity results from the paper. Proposition 4 is a poor anchor because its random preference model gives almost every agent a distinct full ranking, so it is not naturally high-multiplicity. The positive case therefore establishes that the paper’s integration problem has a sensible continuous-population formulation, especially for repeated preference classes in large school or hospital markets; it does not establish that the paper already contains a ChoCo-style complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is that this paper contains no ChoCo-style computational result to continuize. Propositions 1–4 are impossibility, welfare, and asymptotic statements. None defines a computational input/output problem, proves membership in a complexity class, or gives an algorithm. Thus any continuous optimisation problem extracted from them is a new research problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed mirror of Proposition 1 also changes its quantifiers. The paper proves that no stable matching mechanism \(\Gamma\), mapping every preference profile to a matching scheme, is WIM. The proposed problem instead fixes one finite type-mass instance and asks whether there exists a stable scheme \(\{\sigma_P\}_{P\subseteq S}\) satisfying WIM for that instance. That is a profile-specific feasibility problem, not the mechanism-level impossibility proved in the paper. If one restores the paper’s quantification over all preference profiles, the \(2\times 2\) positive-mass counterexample already settles the question; there is no nontrivial computational task left. If one retains the profile-specific question, one has invented a new problem.

The high-multiplicity construction is nevertheless mathematically legitimate. Replacing each of the four agents in the counterexample by a positive mass gives a sensible atomless matching market. But that establishes only that Proposition 1 has a measure-theoretic analogue. It does not give the programme’s missing computational content. Optimising harmed mass or compensation could produce a worthwhile new ChoCo problem, but those objectives are not in the paper and would no longer be mirrors of Proposition 1.

Proposition 2 has the same defect. Its continuous inequality

\[ H(\sigma)\leq \frac{1}{2}\lambda(\text{society}) \]

is a measure version of the paper’s counting argument. The proposed “compute or maximise \(H\)” adds an optimisation problem that the paper never states. Given explicit matching maps, computing \(H\) is just measuring a set; given only type-level transport matrices, \(H\) is not even determined. The same aggregate matching can assign better integrated partners to precisely those agents who were already better off, or to those who were worse off, producing different values of \(H\).

That ambiguity can be repaired by retaining a common atomless agent space and specifying a coupling between the segregated and integrated matchings. But this preserves the individual-level correspondence that the type-mass representation was meant to eliminate. Alternatively, one can use type-average or quantile welfare, but then one has replaced the paper’s per-agent statement by a new aggregate welfare criterion. Either repair may be worthwhile, but neither is supplied by the paper.

The proposed stability notion also requires a substantive choice. In a continuum, “no blocking pair” must be interpreted through blocking coalitions or positive-mass type flows if the proof is to work at the mass level. The one-half argument transfers only under that strengthened mass-stability definition. This is a reasonable modelling choice, not an impossibility, but it further confirms that the proposed result is a newly designed continuum theorem rather than a computational consequence of Proposition 2.

Proposition 3 would fare no better: a continuous Pareto/IM impossibility result would be a legitimate axiomatic analogue, but still not a computational mirror. Proposition 4 is even less suitable. Independent uniformly random rankings generate essentially unique preference types as the market grows, so its asymptotic regime is the opposite of finite-type high multiplicity. A finite-type random model could be constructed, but it would no longer be the paper’s random marriage model or its asymptotic calculation.

The honest negative conclusion is therefore narrower than the requested universal claim. The paper is a poor source for a ChoCo complexity anchor: its continuous versions are either direct measure-theoretic translations of welfare/impossibility statements or newly invented optimisation problems. I cannot honestly claim that no sensible continuous matching model exists—the repeated-type school or hospital interpretation is plausible. But the proponent has not shown a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s computational content, because the paper has no such content to mirror.
