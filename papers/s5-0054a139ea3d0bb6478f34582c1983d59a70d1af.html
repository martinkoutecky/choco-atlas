The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational population masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita chore supplies \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\), additive type costs \(c_t\), and comparison access to each \(c_t\), compute a finite-support allocation \(z_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\) satisfying \(\sum_b z_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}z_{t,b}b_e=q_e\) for every chore category \(e\), and, for every supported nonempty bundle, some \(e\) with \(b_e>0\) and \(c_t(b-\mathbf{1}_e)\le c_t(q)\). Determine the comparison-query complexity, especially for fixed \(|T|\) as \(m\) grows.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity configuration-allocation model with finite cost types \(T\), population mass \(\mu\), per-capita supply \(q\), integral configurations \(b\), and mass variables \(z_{t,b}\); feasibility is governed by supply constraints and support-wise PROP1 eligibility.

The objection that survived

The type-mass formulation has no established analogue of the top-trading invariant: one type may split across many integral bundles, and the stated comparison interface does not explain directly how to handle the fractional benchmark \(c_t(q)\); a prover must supply the missing mass-aggregation or separation theorem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the general PROP1 algorithm of Theorem 3.4; it leaves contiguous PROP1, approximate MMS, MMS distortion, and three-agent EF1 results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can support-wise PROP1 allocations be computed with complexity polynomial in \(|T|\) and logarithmic in \(m\) using only bundle comparisons?
  • Can the paper's top-trading proportionality invariant be replaced by a type-mass flow or configuration-LP argument?
  • Does the \(\Omega(\log m)\) comparison lower bound persist when mass can split across integral configurations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper does support a positive mirror, but its best case is algorithmic rather than hardness-based: it contains no proved NP-hardness or completeness theorem for the underlying fair-division problems. My lead anchor is Theorem 3.4.

Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper, states that for additive chore costs, Algorithm 1 computes a PROP1 allocation using \(O(n^3\log m)\) comparison queries. A query only asks one agent which of two bundles has lower cost; numerical item or bundle costs are never revealed. PROP1 means that every agent can remove one chore from her bundle so that the remaining cost is at most her proportional share \(c_i(M)/n\).

Here is a genuine continuous-population version.

There are \(m\) chore categories \(E=\{e_1,\ldots,e_m\}\), a finite set of complete cost types \(T\), and a population distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). Type \(t\) has an additive cost \(c_t(b)=\sum_e b_e c_t(e)\) for an integral multiset \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^m\). Agents of the same type have exactly the same costs and the same comparison answers. Let \(q_e\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) be the per-capita supply of chore category \(e\). Thus, the total supply scales with the population; chores remain indivisible for every individual.

An allocation is a family of masses \(z_{t,b}\ge0\), where \(z_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(b\). It must satisfy

\(\sum_b z_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,b}z_{t,b}b_e=q_e\) for every chore category \(e\).

The continuous proportional benchmark for type \(t\) is \(p_t=c_t(q)=\sum_e q_e c_t(e)\). A solution is PROP1 if every bundle \(b\) with \(z_{t,b}>0\) either is empty or has some \(e\) with \(b_e>0\) such that \(c_t(b-\mathbf 1_e)\le p_t\).

Call this problem Comparison-Based Continuous PROP1 Allocation, or \(\mathrm{PROP1}_\infty^{\mathrm{comp}}\): given \((T,\mu,q)\) and comparison access to the \(c_t\), output such a finite-support allocation \(z\), with complexity measured by the number of comparison queries.

This is the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem, not a divisible-chores reformulation. Each unit of population still receives an integral bundle. Only the aggregate assignment is fractional. For rational \(\mu\) and \(q\), clearing denominators gives a finite instance with many agents of each type and many repeated chore copies; conversely, aggregating any such finite instance gives \((\mu,q)\). The proportional benchmark agrees exactly, since a finite population of size \(N\) has total chore vector \(Nq\), so its proportional share is \(c_t(Nq)/N=c_t(q)\).

A plausible regime is a large hospital, municipal-service network, or industrial workforce with millions of workers but a small number \(\tau\) of contractual, skill, or physical-constraint types. The chore catalogue may contain many standardized task categories, while \(\tau\ll N\). Workers of one type genuinely share a cost model, and comparison access is plausible because they can compare workloads without assigning sensitive numerical disutilities.

I would expect \(\mathrm{PROP1}_\infty^{\mathrm{comp}}\) to be Class A, although this is a proposed research direction rather than a consequence already proved in the paper. The aggregate allocation has a configuration-LP form over integral bundles, while repeated types allow comparison information to be reused across the population. The balanced-block and top-trading invariant behind Theorem 3.4 suggests that the dependence on the number of named agents might be replaced by dependence on the number of types, perhaps yielding a bound of the form \(O_{\tau}(\log m)\) or a polynomial in \(\tau\) times \(\log m\).

The important caveat is that Theorem 3.4 itself does not establish this. Applied naively to a finite approximation with \(N\) agents, it gives \(O(N^3\log m)\), not a bound independent of population size. The missing theorem is an aggregation argument showing that the top-trading process can be performed on type masses or configuration classes rather than on individual agents. Exact per-agent PROP1 is also stronger than merely requiring the average cost of each type to be proportional.

That is the weakest point of the case. If the authors insist that all agents of a type must receive the same bundle, the model becomes too restrictive; if they permit arbitrary type-average fairness, it becomes too weak. The formulation above keeps the right middle ground: agents of one type may receive different integral bundles, but every bundle receiving positive mass must satisfy PROP1.

The paper’s Proposition B.1 suggests a natural follow-up: does the \(\Omega(\log m)\) comparison lower bound remain necessary in the atomless high-multiplicity model, or can population mass-splitting eliminate the adversary used for the finite problem? Further questions concern polynomial-size support decompositions, dependence on the number of types, a contiguous-chore variant based on Theorem 3.9, and whether the paper’s \((13/11+\varepsilon)\)-MMS result admits a similarly type-aggregated formulation.

My scope claim is therefore deliberately narrow: Theorem 3.4 gives a credible continuous-population PROP1 problem, with a natural high-multiplicity regime and a plausible Class A outcome. I am not claiming that the paper already proves the continuous algorithm, nor that its MMS and three-agent EF1 theorems automatically survive when the population becomes large.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that Theorem 3.4 is not really a population result. Its \(O(n^3\log m)\) bound comes from a top-trading graph on \(n\) named agents and binary search over the chores. Replacing agents by type masses does not preserve that proof: a single type may need positive mass on many different integral bundles, so there is no longer one tentative bundle or one outgoing edge per agent. The cycle invariant does not simply become a flow invariant.

The nondegenerate formulation also changes the problem in two substantial ways. If the chore set remains fixed while the population grows, almost everyone receives the empty bundle and PROP1 becomes essentially vacuous. To avoid that, the proposed mirror scales chores by introducing a per-capita supply vector \(q\), and replaces one finite allocation by a distribution \(z_{t,b}\) over integral configurations. That is a legitimate model, but it is a new configuration-allocation problem. Its natural constraints require every supported bundle to satisfy

\[ c_t(b-\mathbf 1_e)\leq c_t(q), \]

where \(c_t(q)\) is generally not the cost of an integral bundle and cannot itself be queried in the paper’s comparison model. Establishing a type-mass algorithm would therefore require a new separation or aggregation theorem; Theorem 3.4 provides no such bridge.

The weaker alternative—requiring only type-average proportionality or average PROP1—would be easier to formulate, but it is not faithful to the paper. Mass splitting could hide individuals receiving unacceptable bundles, and the “up to one item” guarantee would become an aggregate condition rather than the per-agent guarantee proved in Theorem 3.4. The stronger support-wise formulation preserves the guarantee, but then all of the interesting work has moved into a new configuration problem whose complexity is unknown.

Proposition B.1 does not strengthen the case. Its lower bound hides a few costly chores and uses pigeonhole forcing to put them together in one named agent’s bundle. Atomless mass can split across configurations, so that adversary may disappear; preserving it would reintroduce finite-agent granularity. Thus the proposition supplies no robust population-level phenomenon.

That said, this is not enough to defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s rules. The proposed hospital or standardized-task regime is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and for rational \(\mu_t=a_t/N\) and \(q_e=b_e/N\), clearing denominators gives exactly a finite instance with \(a_t\) repeated agent types and \(b_e\) repeated chores. The support-wise formulation is therefore a genuine high-multiplicity relaxation, not a divisible-chores mistake. The fact that its algorithm requires new work is precisely what could make it a worthwhile Class A question.

So the honest negative case is weak: Theorem 3.4 does not itself support the claimed continuous algorithm, and the proponent overstates the directness of the mirror. But I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The anchor survives the programme’s central test.
