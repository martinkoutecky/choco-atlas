The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\), rational per-capita capacities \(q\) with \(\sum_j q_j\ge 1\), and rational \(\rho\in[0,1]\), define continuum RSD and ABM by priority mass flows over rankings in \(\mathcal P=S_m\). Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is the paper’s maximal-mixing-factor problem, specialized to its Random Serial Dictatorship (RSD) and adaptive Boston mechanism (ABM) pair.

Take \(m\) objects and a set \(S\subseteq S_m\) of \(\tau\) complete preference rankings. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) on \(S\), where \(\mu_t\) is the share of applicants with ranking \(t\). Give object \(j\) per-capita capacity \(q_j\), with \(\sum_j q_j=1\); a dummy object can absorb excess supply. This describes, for example, a large school district with many applicants but relatively few ranking templates, and seat capacities that scale with the cohort. A type is just a ranking, as in the paper: agents of the same type are indistinguishable in every respect its mechanisms use. The random-assignment rows remain lotteries over indivisible objects; the continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\), not the outcome.

Define \(a^M_\mu(r)\) as the assignment lottery of a tagged, infinitesimal applicant who reports ranking \(r\), while the background population follows \(\mu\), for \(M\in\{\mathrm{RSD},\mathrm{ABM}\}\). These are the mechanisms’ natural mass-flow versions. For RSD, give each unit of population a uniform priority and process priorities in order, assigning each applicant their highest-ranked available object. For ABM, use the paper’s rounds: applicants apply to their highest-ranked object still available, and oversubscribed objects admit the highest-priority mass up to capacity. The tagged applicant’s priority is integrated out to obtain its lottery row. The background mass flow is unaffected by this one infinitesimal applicant.

Call the question \(\textsc{Continuum-BetaMax}_{\mathrm{RSD,ABM}}\). Its input is \(m,S,\mu,q\), and an acceptable incentive bound \(\rho\in\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\), with the masses, capacities, and \(\rho\) encoded as rationals. For each \(\beta\in[0,1]\), let
\[ a_{\mu,\beta}(r)=(1-\beta)a^{\mathrm{RSD}}_\mu(r)+\beta a^{\mathrm{ABM}}_\mu(r). \]
Find the largest \(\beta\) such that, for every true type \(t\in S\), every report \(r\in S_m\), and every utility vector \(u\) consistent with \(t\) and satisfying the paper’s \(\mathrm{URBI}(\rho)\) condition,
\[ u\cdot\bigl(a_{\mu,\beta}(t)-a_{\mu,\beta}(r)\bigr)\ge 0. \]
A solution is the exact value \(\beta^*\) and a certificate that it satisfies these constraints; if \(\beta^*<1\), a binding incentive constraint witnesses why no larger value works.

The anchor is Proposition 4 (proved here). It states that for a finite setting, a hybrid-admissible pair of mechanisms, and a bound \(\rho\), Algorithm 1, BetaMax, is complete and correct for finding the largest mixing factor for which the hybrid is \(\rho\)-partially strategyproof. The paper calls this a computability result, not a polynomial-time result; its stated algorithm can enumerate all finite preference profiles. The proposed mirror asks that same designer question with the society represented by rational type masses. It specializes to RSD and ABM because Theorem 4 (proved here) shows ABM is weakly less varying than RSD, and Corollary 2 (proved here) says their hybrids permit partially strategyproof trade-offs in rank dominance, with the paper’s stated exceptions. Those results make this pair a particularly recognizable choice for the authors.

I expect the continuous problem to be tractable parameterized by \(m\), with an exact algorithm running in \(f(m)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\), where \(L\) is the rational input length. The mass-flow versions have finitely many exhaustion events or rounds; with rational inputs their priority cutoffs and assignment rows can be computed exactly. Incentive checking ranges over \(\tau\) true types, \(m!\) possible reports, and a finite description of the \(\mathrm{URBI}(\rho)\) utility region. That gives a finite set of constraints linear in \(\beta\), with the factorial and utility-region dependence absorbed into \(f(m)\). This is a parameterized tractability expectation, not a claim proved by the paper.

The mirror covers Proposition 4’s maximal-\(\beta\) design problem for one concrete pair. It does not claim to continuize every result in the paper or to guarantee strict rank-dominance gains for every \(\mu\).

My weakest point is that an infinitesimal applicant cannot change aggregate capacities or admission cutoffs. The model preserves the paper’s unilateral incentive question as a tagged-applicant question, but it may wash out effects that matter when one person changes a finite market. A referee could reasonably ask whether that price-taking incentive problem is the authors’ intended large-market version of BetaMax. The case survives because the population itself is still the continuous object, the type and capacity scaling are explicit, and RSD/ABM are the paper’s own mechanisms.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed problem is not quite the problem in Proposition 4. In the paper, \(\beta_{\max}\) is chosen for a setting and a pair of mechanisms so that partial strategyproofness holds across all preference profiles in that setting. The proposed \(\beta^*\) is chosen for one fixed society \(\mu\), and checks incentives only for types in its support. That is a sensible price-taking question, but it makes the threshold depend on the particular market. Theorem 4 and Corollary 2 establish a mechanism-wide property; they support choosing RSD and ABM, but they do not establish the proposed fixed-\(\mu\) threshold problem.

The distinction matters especially because Proposition 4 proves computability, not an efficient algorithm. Its BetaMax algorithm enumerates profiles, and the paper explicitly leaves faster computation for restricted mechanisms to future work. So the proposed \(f(m)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) bound would be a new, stronger result, rather than a continuous restatement of the result the authors proved.

The natural repair is to ask for the largest \(\beta\) that works for every continuous society \(\mu\), every true ranking, every report, and every allowed utility. That better matches the paper’s universal incentive guarantee. But I can’t make that repair into a convincing objection: it is a well-posed computational question about the population distribution, and the priority-flow definitions give it a plausible continuous model. The concern that an infinitesimal applicant cannot affect cutoffs is not a decisive flaw; it is the standard price-taking interpretation of a large population.

Nor can I dismiss the anchor on the grounds that random assignments are already fractional: the proposed mirror continuizes the population, and the applicants, capacities, and type masses have a coherent scaling story. The continuous assignment mechanism itself looks natural. Whether the universal threshold has a positive bound or admits the predicted parameterized algorithm is not a reason to reject asking the question.

So the negative case is weak. I can show that the fixed-\(\mu\) proposal narrows and changes Proposition 4’s quantifiers, and that its tractability claim goes beyond the paper. But the stronger, uniform continuous version survives those objections. On the material here, I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
