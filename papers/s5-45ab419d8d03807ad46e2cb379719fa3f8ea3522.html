The continuous mirror question

Given a graph \(G\), form the three valuations and goods from Theorem 5.1, and take three types with masses \(\mu_i=1/3\) and per-capita supply \(q_g=1/3\) for every good \(g\). Decide whether there are nonnegative bundle masses \(x_{i,S}\) with \(\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{i,S}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_{i,S:g\in S}x_{i,S}=q_g\), such that every pair of positively represented type-bundle classes satisfies EF1 and no alternative allocation dominates under a type-preserving coupling that weakly benefits every person and strictly benefits a positive mass.

The model it lives in

A population of three recurring types, each with the corresponding valuation from Theorem 5.1 and mass \(1/3\); each person receives an integral bundle, while mass may split among bundles and each good category has supply \(1/3\) per person. EF1 is checked between every positively represented bundle class, and PO is defined by type-preserving couplings of assignments.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.1's restricted three-agent EF1-and-PO existence problem; it does not cover Theorems 3.1 or 4.4.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Theorem 5.1's Balanced Vertex Cover reduction remain sound when each valuation type may split across integral bundles?
  • What is the complexity of EF1-and-PO existence for this restricted type-mass family?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: Theorem 5.1, not the paper’s two-agent threshold results. Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper by a reduction from Balanced Vertex Cover, says that deciding whether an EF1 and Pareto-optimal allocation exists is NP-hard for three agents with normalized, integer-valued, monotone submodular valuations. It remains NP-hard when all zero marginals are confined to a fixed set of eight goods for agent 3: every other good has strictly positive marginal value for all three agents, and each of the eight core goods has strictly positive marginal value for agents 1 and 2. Thus only eight agent–good pairs have identically zero marginal value. The prior three-agent hardness result cited in the paper is Chandramouleeswaran and Nimbhorkar’s Theorem 3; the fixed-core strengthening is the result proved here.

Here is the mirror I find credible. There is a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types, each with a normalized, integer-valued, monotone submodular valuation \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\). The population has rational type masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\), and each good category \(g\in M\) has a rational supply \(q_g\) per person. Every person receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\); only the masses of people receiving each bundle may be fractional. This co-scaling of goods with population is part of the model: a category with supply \(q_g\) has \(Nq_g\) copies in a population of size \(N\).

For example, a large public programme might distribute copies of many resource categories to three recurring beneficiary cohorts. Members of each cohort share their entire valuation function over bundles, while cohort sizes and resource supplies grow with the population. The three types in the theorem become three large cohorts, so \(N\) can grow while \(\tau=|T|=3\) stays fixed. A type must capture everything relevant to valuation, including any differences in costs or constraints if those enter the allocation problem.

Type-Mass EF1–PO Existence has the following precise form. An instance consists of \(M,T,(v_t)_{t\in T},(\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), and \((q_g)_{g\in M}\), with rational masses and supplies. A proposed allocation is a finite list of nonzero masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{for each }t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}=q_g \quad\text{for each }g. \]
The second equality allocates all supplied goods, as the paper’s allocations are complete.

EF1 is required between every pair of bundle classes with positive mass: for all \(t,t',S,R\) such that \(x_{t,S},x_{t',R}>0\), either \(R=\varnothing\), or there is a \(g\in R\) for which
\[ v_t(S)\ge v_t(R\setminus\{g\}). \]
For PO, compare individuals, not average utilities. An alternative allocation dominates if, within each type, its bundle assignments can be paired with the old assignments so that every person weakly gains and a positive mass strictly gains. The problem asks whether such an EF1 and PO mass allocation exists; a solution is the finite-support bundle-mass list \(x\) satisfying both conditions. Complexity should be measured in \(|M|,\tau\), the valuation-description size, and the bit length \(L\) of the rational input.

This is a population continuization: people become mass, while each person’s bundle and each EF1 comparison remain integral and individual-level. With three type masses \(\mu_i=1/3\) and supplies \(q_g=1/3\), the theorem’s three-agent instance is represented by assigning one bundle to each whole type mass. Replicating those cohorts and good copies gives the corresponding high-multiplicity finite instances. But the continuous allocation also permits a type cohort to split across bundles, which is why the mirror asks a new question rather than simply restating Theorem 5.1.

My working bet is Class B: hardness transfers, provisionally. The theorem’s reduction encodes Balanced Vertex Cover in the graph-dependent goods and their valuations; it is not hardness caused by having a large number of agents. That makes transfer plausible, and the eight-pair restriction gives the mirror a concrete structural feature to preserve. But the paper does not prove the reduction survives mass splitting. Splitting could fractionalize the vertex-cover encoding and allow an EF1 and PO allocation where the three-agent instance has none. The key further question is whether the reduction can rule out such mixed bundle supports, or whether a different argument establishes hardness for type masses.

This is the soft spot in the positive case: the authors would recognize the allocation question and the high-multiplicity regime, but the paper’s hardness theorem alone does not settle the continuous problem. Theorems 3.1 and 4.4—the eight-good incompatibility result and the at-most-seven-good existence result—are relevant motivation, but they are not additional computational anchors here, and their fixed small populations make them a poor basis for this mirror. The proposed question covers Theorem 5.1’s EF1–PO existence decision only.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 5.1 does not establish hardness for the proposed mass problem. It concerns exactly three heterogeneous agents. The mirror must turn each role into a cohort, scale supplies with population, and allow a cohort to split across integral bundles. Those choices can fractionalize the reduction’s vertex cover construction. The theorem therefore supports posing the question, but not the other analyst’s Class B prediction. Showing that the gadget survives splitting—or finding a separate reduction—would be new work.

That gap is a reason to be cautious about the claimed transfer, but it does not defeat the mirror. A repeated public programme with three stable recipient cohorts and per-capita supplies of resource categories is a coherent high-multiplicity setting. Each person still receives an integral bundle; only the mass assigned to each bundle is continuous. EF1 can retain its individual guarantee across every bundle class with positive mass, and Pareto dominance can compare assignments within each cohort. Neither notion vanishes just because a person has measure zero.

There is a genuine modelling choice at the boundary: a class with arbitrarily small positive mass still triggers the full EF1 condition, while a class of zero mass does not. That makes EF1 sensitive to the support of the assignment. But it is also what preserving individual EF1 in a replicated population entails, so it is not a fundamental objection. Replacing it with an average or approximate fairness condition would change the question; keeping it gives a recognizable mirror.

The closest algorithmic work I found remains finite-agent and additive: Mahara treats a fixed number of agents, while Kawase and Mahara study balanced additive instances with at most two valuation types. Neither answers the proposed mass-allocation question for submodular valuations. ([Mahara](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01810), [Kawase and Mahara](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05956))

So the honest negative case is limited: the paper’s hardness theorem does not justify a hardness-transfer claim, and a mass version needs careful semantics. I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 5.1 is a valid computational anchor, and the cohort model is plausible enough that this anchor survives the strongest objection I can support.
