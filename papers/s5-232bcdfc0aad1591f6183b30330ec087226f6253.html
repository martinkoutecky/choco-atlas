The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s single-score admissions equilibrium.

Lead anchor: Theorem 8, proved in this paper. It states that in the single-score model with multinomial-logit student preferences, ordering schools by \(\gamma_c/q_c\) also orders their equilibrium cutoffs. After that ordering, the unique equilibrium is
\[ \hat p=\left[A^{-1}\left(q-\frac{\gamma}{\Gamma}\right)\right]^+, \qquad \Gamma=\sum_{c=1}^m\gamma_c, \]
where the positive part is componentwise and \(A\) is the matrix expressing demand as \(D=Ap+\gamma/\Gamma\). The theorem supplies the equilibrium formula and uniqueness; the paper does not state a bit-complexity bound.

MNL Common-Score Admissions Equilibrium. An instance has \(m\) schools, positive rational MNL weights \(\gamma_c\), and rational per-capita capacities \(q_c\). A unit-mass applicant population has a common score \(\theta\) uniformly distributed on \([0,1]\). Students’ school preferences follow the MNL model with weights \(\gamma\); a school admits students whose score meets its cutoff \(p_c\), and each student chooses among admitted schools according to that model. For sorted cutoffs, with \(p_{m+1}=1\), demand is
\[ D_c(p)=\sum_{d=c}^m \frac{\gamma_c}{\sum_{i=1}^d\gamma_i}(p_{d+1}-p_d). \]
The task is to find a cutoff vector \(p\in[0,1]^m\) such that \(D_c(p)\le q_c\) for every school and \(p_c>0\) implies \(D_c(p)=q_c\). A solution is the equilibrium cutoff vector and its induced demand allocation. There is no welfare objective in this problem: the solution is defined by equilibrium conditions.

This has a credible large-market setting: a national or regional admissions system with many applicants, relatively few schools, a shared standardized-score ranking, and capacities expressed relative to the applicant pool. Its continuous society can be represented by mass over preference and score types; the school weights and score distribution give a compact parametric description. With rational input, Theorem 8’s formula suggests a tractable exact algorithm: sort the ratios, form the rational system, and solve it. That polynomial bit-complexity conclusion is an inference from the formula, rather than a claim explicitly proved by the paper. I expect Class A: the stated problem is already solved by the theorem.

There are two qualifications to the regime. First, the model assumes a common score order across schools and MNL preferences, so its best setting is a system with a broadly shared admissions metric and a useful aggregate preference model. Second, a complete student type includes both preference ranking and score. The score dimension is continuous, so this is an analytically specified population distribution, rather than ChoCo’s usual finite, explicitly listed \(\tau\)-type input. That is a natural modeling choice for percentile scores, but it is a real change to the input representation.

The mirror covers Theorem 8 and the single-score MNL equilibrium computation in §3.2. It does not cover the paper’s broader admissions markets or its inverse estimation exercise. Further questions include whether the formula remains tractable under mixed MNL preferences or school-specific scores, how equilibrium cutoffs respond to noisy estimates of \(\gamma\) and \(q\), and what finite-sample error separates this population equilibrium from a sampled deferred-acceptance outcome.

The weakness is substantial: the article already makes the population continuous and proves the anchor directly. This is excellent evidence that admissions equilibrium is a sensible continuous computational object, but it creates a novelty collision for a proposed mirror of this paper: the proposed question substantially restates the article’s own continuum problem. The paper has no named hardness result to supply a different anchor; Theorem 8 is the best computational anchor available.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s Theorem 8 anchor is already a continuous-population computational problem. The paper models a unit mass of students, gives them a common continuous score distribution and MNL preferences, and asks for equilibrium cutoffs. It then supplies the exact formula
\[ \hat p=\left[A^{-1}\left(q-\frac{\gamma}{\Gamma}\right)\right]^+. \]
With rational inputs, sorting the competitiveness ratios and evaluating this formula takes polynomially many rational operations with polynomial bit growth. The paper does not state that complexity bound, but deriving it is routine. A mirror asking for the equilibrium in this same model would therefore restate a problem the paper already formulates and solves.

The strongest repair would widen the question to arbitrary admissions markets: give a finite list of complete student types—preferences and scores—with rational masses, then compute a stable fractional assignment or its cutoff representation. But §2 already defines the corresponding nonatomic market and proves its connection to stable assignments. For finite cohorts of identical types, aggregate deferred acceptance appears to give a direct algorithm: proposals and capacity changes can be processed by type rather than by named student. If the proposed mirror instead preserves continuously varying scores, it needs an explicit representation for the distribution \(\eta\); the paper supplies one only for special parametric cases such as MNL. Either way, the article itself has already made the population continuous, so the mirror would be an extension or complexity study of its own model.

That makes the case against the stated anchor strong, but the universal claim is weaker. A finite-support, high-multiplicity admissions problem is a sensible object, and the paper leaves general equilibrium computation unresolved. A type-level complexity analysis could be worthwhile. I can argue that this is not a new continuous mirror of this paper; I cannot honestly rule out every useful continuation of its continuous admissions model.
