The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) alternatives and a finite support \(S\subseteq S_n\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\pi\) summing to \(1\), replace ranking counts by masses in MC1, MC2, and MC3. What is each procedure’s worst-case approximation ratio, measured by \(C_\mu(\sigma)/\min_{\rho\in S_n}C_\mu(\rho)\), as a function of \(n\), \(\tau=|S|\), and mass bit length \(L\); in particular, is it bounded solely in \(n\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings \(\pi\in S_n\) with masses \(\mu_\pi\). A decision is a consensus ranking \(\sigma\in S_n\), evaluated by \(C_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{\pi\in S}\mu_\pi K(\sigma,\pi)\). The mass-weighted MC1–MC3 transition matrices use, respectively, \(\frac{\sum_\pi\mu_\pi\mathbf{1}[r_\pi(j)\le r_\pi(i)]}{\sum_\pi\mu_\pi q_\pi(i)}\), \(\sum_\pi\frac{\mu_\pi\mathbf{1}[r_\pi(j)\le r_\pi(i)]}{q_\pi(i)}\), and off-diagonal entries \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_\pi\mu_\pi\mathbf{1}[r_\pi(j)<r_\pi(i)]\), with diagonal entries completing each row. Each procedure orders alternatives by stationary probability.

What the mirror covers

The stated mirrors cover Theorems 7, 9, and 15. They leave the MC4 stationary-distribution characterizations, the strongly connected tournament bounds, and the restart-variant results without a separate continuous analysis.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is continuous Kemeny rank aggregation: choose a consensus ranking that minimizes the population’s average Kendall distance from it. Take \(n\) alternatives and a finite support \(S\subseteq S_n\) of \(\tau\) ranking types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_\pi\) summing to \(1\). A type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of society with that ranking. For a proposed ranking \(\sigma\), the objective is \(C_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{\pi\in S}\mu_\pi K(\sigma,\pi)\), and a solution is a permutation minimizing \(C_\mu\).

A plausible regime is a large online service asking users to rank the same fixed slate of products or candidates. Users with the same full ranking are indistinguishable to this objective, and their mass is their share of the user population. The intended high-multiplicity case has many users but relatively few distinct rankings, perhaps because the slate is bounded and rankings repeat. This is a population distribution, not a noise model; the decision remains a discrete ranking.

Lead: Theorem 7, proved in this paper. It states that MC1, MC2, and MC3 have no approximation guarantee bounded solely as a function of the number of ranked elements. The paper proves this with three elements: on an input with \(k-1\) copies of \(\pi_1=(0,1,2)\) and one copy of \(\pi_5=(2,0,1)\), each chain’s stationary distribution ranks \(2\) above \(1\). Any such output costs at least \(k-1\), while choosing \(\pi_1\) costs \(2\).

The continuous question is: given \((n,S,\mu)\), determine the worst-case approximation factor of the mass-based MC1, MC2, and MC3 procedures for minimizing \(C_\mu\). Define each procedure by replacing ranking counts in the paper’s transition matrices with masses: with \(r_\pi(i)\) the position of \(i\) in ranking \(\pi\), and \(q_\pi(i)=|\{a:r_\pi(a)\le r_\pi(i)\}|\), MC1 uses \(P_{ij}=\frac{\sum_\pi\mu_\pi\mathbf 1[r_\pi(j)\le r_\pi(i)]}{\sum_\pi\mu_\pi q_\pi(i)}\); MC2 uses \(P_{ij}=\sum_\pi\mu_\pi\mathbf 1[r_\pi(j)\le r_\pi(i)]/q_\pi(i)\); and for \(i\ne j\), MC3 uses \(P_{ij}=\frac1n\sum_\pi\mu_\pi\mathbf 1[r_\pi(j)<r_\pi(i)]\), with diagonal entries completing each row. Each procedure returns the ranking obtained by sorting stationary probabilities in nonincreasing order.

The paper’s example lifts directly. Put mass \(1-\epsilon\) on \(\pi_1\) and \(\epsilon\) on \(\pi_5\), for any positive rational \(\epsilon<1/2\). The lifted chains still rank \(2\) above \(1\), so their expected cost is at least \(1-\epsilon\); choosing \(\pi_1\) costs \(2\epsilon\). Their approximation ratios are therefore at least \((1-\epsilon)/(2\epsilon)\): unbounded even with \(n=3\) and \(\tau=2\). For these fixed procedures, the continuous question has a negative approximation answer, not a hardness result. The underlying exact optimization problem is expected to inherit discrete hardness by embedding any finite ranking profile as rational masses, but this paper’s theorem does not establish that hardness. Further questions include how a guarantee depends on the smallest positive mass and whether other ranking procedures avoid this failure.

A second mirror: Theorem 9, proved in this paper. It states that MC4 has no sublinear approximation guarantee. The paper’s family uses \(n\) copies each of \(\pi_1=(0,1,\ldots,n-1)\) and \(\pi_2=(1,\ldots,n-1,0)\), plus one copy of \(\pi_3=(n-c,\ldots,n-1,0,\ldots,n-c-1)\), with \(c\approx n/4\). The optimum costs \(O(n^2)\); MC4 costs \(\Omega(n^3)\), giving an \(\Omega(n)\) ratio.

The continuous question is to determine MC4’s worst-case approximation factor for the same expected Kendall objective, where MC4 moves from \(i\) to \(j\) with probability \(1/n\) when \(j\) beats \(i\) by population majority, stays otherwise, and outputs alternatives sorted by stationary probability. For the paper’s family, set \(\mu_{\pi_1}=\mu_{\pi_2}=n/(2n+1)\) and \(\mu_{\pi_3}=1/(2n+1)\). These masses induce the same majority tournament and the same output. Dividing both costs by \(2n+1\) leaves the ratio unchanged, so the continuous mirror also has an \(\Omega(n)\) ratio with support three. The expected direction for exact optimization remains hardness transfer; the anchored question about MC4’s quality is already answered by the linear lower bound. Further questions include whether that bound is tight across all societies, and how it relates to the paper’s strongly connected case.

A useful positive anchor: Theorem 15, proved in this paper. It states that sorting by Copeland score is an 11-approximation for rank aggregation. Its proof uses Lemmas 13 and 14, credited to Coppersmith et al., concerning the relationship between the majority-tournament and weighted feedback-arc objectives. The continuous problem is to output a ranking within a factor of \(11\) of \(\min_\sigma C_\mu(\sigma)\): compute each alternative’s number of opponents it beats by population majority, then sort by that score. The proof carries over because the weighted pairwise probabilities from \(\mu\) satisfy the same constraints used in the paper. This approximate optimization task is tractable: with support given explicitly, the pairwise majority masses can be computed from the \(\tau\) rankings. Further questions include the best possible constant for this method and whether the bound can be improved for particular population regimes.

These mirrors cover the paper’s rank-aggregation objective and the approximation behavior of MC1–MC4 and Copeland. They do not continuize the ranking outcome or ask an axiomatic question. The paper’s rankings are explicitly listed as a finite input, though, and its pairwise graph already uses fractions of those rankings. Any rational society here can be expanded into a finite multiset, so the approximation findings largely carry over unchanged. That is the weakest point of the case: these anchors establish a sensible high-multiplicity formulation, but they do not show that continuization changes the computational answer or dissolves hardness. The additional question the mirror makes explicit is how to analyze and solve such instances when the input is the support and binary masses, with running time measured in \(n,\tau\), and bit length \(L\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a strong case against these mirrors. The natural type space is the set of rankings, and the objective is the population’s expected Kendall distance,
\[ C_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{\pi}\mu_\pi K(\sigma,\pi). \]
Repeated ballot types in a large electorate—or repeated user rankings of a fixed slate—make a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The input rankings need not be individually identified for any of the paper’s objectives or algorithms.

Theorem 7 has a particularly direct mirror. Replace ranking counts in MC1, MC2, and MC3 with masses and ask for their worst-case approximation ratios on \((n,\tau,L)\)-encoded societies. With mass \(1-\epsilon\) on \(\pi_1\) and \(\epsilon\) on \(\pi_5\), the lifted chains still rank \(2\) above \(1\). Their expected cost is at least \(1-\epsilon\), while \(\pi_1\) costs \(2\epsilon\), giving an unbounded ratio as positive rational \(\epsilon\) decreases. One could object that the optimum tends to zero near a one-type society, but that does not make the question ill-posed: the poor performance occurs for every \(\epsilon>0\), and rare subpopulations are part of the stated model.

Theorem 9 also survives the lift: use masses \(n/(2n+1)\), \(n/(2n+1)\), and \(1/(2n+1)\) on its three rankings. The majority tournament and MC4 output are unchanged, and normalizing the costs preserves the \(\Omega(n)\) ratio. The small third mass may limit how representative this particular example is of a regime with no rare types, but it does not defeat the unrestricted mirror. A stronger question would ask for MC4’s worst-case guarantee over all population distributions, perhaps also studying restricted mass regimes.

Theorem 15 has an equally natural mirror: sort by population-majority Copeland score and compare its expected Kendall cost with the optimum. The proof’s weighted pairwise quantities are precisely the quantities obtained from \(\mu\), so its approximation claim carries over. That the calculation may be a direct extension is not a substantive objection to asking the question.

The best negative point I can find is about novelty: these are approximation analyses of fixed procedures, not a classification of the exact high-multiplicity complexity of Kemeny optimization, and much of the algebra transfers directly. But the paper does state named computational approximation results, and the programme explicitly treats approximation algorithms as in scope. A concern that the continuous answers may be immediate or match the discrete ones cannot, by itself, rule out a worthwhile mirror. The negative case is therefore weak: these anchors appear to establish sensible continuous-population questions rather than expose a fundamental obstacle.
