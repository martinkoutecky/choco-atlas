The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support rational distribution \(\mu_0\) over initial opinions in \(\mathbb{Q}^d\) and a common confidence radius \(\epsilon>0\), define each round by moving all mass at each support point \(x\) to \(g_{\mu_k}(x)=\frac{\sum_{y:\|x-y\|_2\le\epsilon}\mu_k(y)y}{\sum_{y:\|x-y\|_2\le\epsilon}\mu_k(y)}\), combining masses at coincident points. Compute the least \(t_*\) such that \(\mu_{t_*}=\mu_{t_*+1}\).

The model it lives in

A synchronous homogeneous Hegselmann–Krause process on a finite support rational mass distribution, where each opinion point carries mass and all mass at that point moves to the mass weighted average of opinions within \(\epsilon\); the task is to compute the first fixed round \(t_*\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 2’s synchronous homogeneous termination bound; it leaves the asynchronous expected time and switching topology bounds and the heterogeneous necessary condition aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(t_*\) be computed in time polynomial in the support size, dimension, and input bit length, without expanding the rational masses into an explicit population?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s synchronous homogeneous result, Theorem 2, proved here. It says that for homogeneous synchronous Hegselmann–Krause dynamics in any finite dimension, the maximum termination time \(T_n\) over all initial profiles of \(n\) agents satisfies \(T_n\le n^8+n\), independently of the dimension.

A natural regime is a large deliberation platform where many participants share one of a relatively small number of recorded opinion vectors, and everyone uses the same confidence bound and update rule. A type is an opinion vector \(x\in\mathbb{Q}^d\); its mass \(\mu(x)\) is the fraction of participants holding that opinion. The participant count \(N\) may be large while the number \(\tau\) of occupied opinion vectors is modest. Agents of the same type remain indistinguishable under the paper’s homogeneous synchronous update.

The corresponding computational question is Mass-HK Exact Stabilization Time. An instance is a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over opinion vectors in \(\mathbb{Q}^d\), with positive rational confidence bound \(\epsilon\); coordinates and masses are binary encoded. For each occupied opinion \(x\), define
\[ g_\mu(x)= \frac{\sum_{y:\,\|x-y\|\le\epsilon}\mu(y)y} {\sum_{y:\,\|x-y\|\le\epsilon}\mu(y)}. \]
One round sends all mass at \(x\) to \(g_\mu(x)\); masses that arrive at the same vector are combined. The task is to return the first round \(t_*\) for which the distribution is fixed, together with the stabilized distribution \(\mu_{t_*}\). There is no optimization objective here: the output is the exact stabilization time and state.

This is a genuine mass version of the paper’s process. If the rational masses are written \(\mu(x_i)=n_i/N\), replacing each atom by \(n_i\) identical agents gives the same trajectory after aggregating agents at equal opinions. Thus Theorem 2 supplies \(t_*\le N^8+N\). It gives a direct algorithm by simulation, polynomial in the expanded population size \(N\) and the number of rounds. For binary-encoded masses, however, \(N\) can be exponential in the input length. The theorem therefore does not establish polynomial time in \(\tau,d,L\), where \(L\) is the bit length of the mass input. I would expect tractability in the explicit-population regime; whether the exact task is tractable in the compact high-multiplicity encoding is an open question raised by this mirror.

This should be recognizable to the authors as the computational version of their termination question: the model uses their same confidence neighborhoods and averaging dynamics, while asking for the cost of finding the exact stopping time from a compact population description. The paper itself notes that continuum-opinion HK models exist; those are relevant analytic prior art, not a collision with this computational question. This mirror covers Theorem 2 only. I would leave the asynchronous results out: selecting one agent at a time has no immediate counterpart for nonatomic mass without adding a scheduling model.

The weak point is that Theorem 2 bounds the running time of the dynamics in \(n\); it does not classify the complexity of an input-output problem over rational mass distributions. In particular, it leaves unresolved the bit-complexity question that would make this a strong high-multiplicity result. Still, the type aggregation is exact, and the resulting stabilization-time question stays close to the paper’s named result rather than changing its underlying dynamics.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is a mismatch between what Theorem 2 proves and what the proposed task asks. The theorem bounds the number of rounds of the finite-agent dynamics by \(n^8+n\). It does not classify the complexity of computing a stopping time from an encoded input. For rational masses, expanding to identical agents gives a bound polynomial in a common denominator \(D\), but \(D\) can be exponential in the mass bit length \(L\). Returning the exact stabilized state could also require exponentially many output bits. So the proponent’s formulation does not inherit a polynomial-time result in \((\tau,d,L)\).

That objection does not defeat the stronger version of the mirror: ask only for the exact stabilization time, or for the time to reach a specified tolerance. These are well-defined computational questions, and the mass update exactly aggregates identical agents. Large survey or deliberation populations with a small number of repeated opinion profiles are plausible high-multiplicity instances. The paper’s cited continuum-opinion work establishes analytic precedent, but the supplied text does not show that it answers these computational questions. Treating it as a collision would be a mistake.

So I cannot make the universal negative case convincingly. The mass dynamics may be a less direct ChoCo fit than a discrete optimization problem, and the exact-state output is a poor complexity target without an output-size qualification. But the exact-time or approximate-stabilization mirror survives those repairs. The honest negative case is weak: the computational question is new relative to the paper’s theorem, but that is a limitation of the proposed anchor, not a fundamental reason the mirror is unworthy.
