The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), disjoint labels and interval or dominance constraints, a reference committee \(X\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a listed support of complete rankings, is there a feasible committee \(Y\) of size \(k\) whose SNTV or Bloc score under \(\mu\) is at least the score of \(X\)?

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete voter rankings. The committee remains a discrete \(k\)-element subset of candidates; each candidate's score is its total mass of first-place rankings under SNTV, or of top-\(k\) rankings under Bloc.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1's NP-completeness claims for SNTV and Bloc. It leaves Proposition 2.8 and the tree-like polynomial-time and few-label fixed-parameter results in Theorems 3.3 and 4.1 unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which restrictions on label overlap or dominance structure yield tractable continuous constrained-winner problems beyond the tree-like disjoint-label setting?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is the constrained winner existence problem with a continuous electorate and a discrete committee. Consider a large professional association choosing a fixed-size board. Each candidate has one primary constituency, so the candidate labels are disjoint; the constraints can require, for example, at least as many seats for one constituency as another. Many members may rank candidates according to the same small set of endorsed slates. A voter type is a complete ranking, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of members with ranking \(t\). Thus \(N\) can be very large while the support size \(\tau\) stays small. Rankings contain all the voter information this problem uses.

Here is the mirror, Continuous Constrained Winner Existence for SNTV/Bloc. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), pairwise disjoint labels and interval or dominance constraints, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a listed support \(T\) of complete rankings, a reference committee \(X\in\binom{C}{k}\), and a rule \(r\), either SNTV or Bloc. Let \(s_r(t,c)\) be \(1\) if \(c\) is ranked first under SNTV, or if \(c\) is in the top \(k\) under Bloc, and \(0\) otherwise. Define
\[ \mathrm{sc}_{\mu,r}(Y)=\sum_{c\in Y}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t s_r(t,c). \]
The question is whether there is a committee \(Y\in\binom{C}{k}\) satisfying every constraint and \(\mathrm{sc}_{\mu,r}(Y)\ge \mathrm{sc}_{\mu,r}(X)\). A YES solution is such a committee \(Y\); otherwise the answer is NO. Equivalently, one can maximize \(\mathrm{sc}_{\mu,r}(Y)\) over the feasible committees and compare the optimum with the reference score. The input uses the support size \(\tau\), not the number of possible rankings, and the binary encoding length of the masses.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper by reductions from clique. In full, it says that the constrained winner existence problem for weakly separable voting rules with disjoint labels is NP-complete even for SNTV, and that for Bloc it is NP-complete even with a constant number of voters. The mirror preserves both claims: it uses the same candidate-selection question, constraints, and score comparison, with the electorate represented by rational masses.

The transfer is exact. For a discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of ranking \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Then the discrete committee score is \(n\,\mathrm{sc}_{\mu,r}(Y)\), so all score comparisons and YES/NO answers are preserved. In the Bloc clause, the paper’s constant-voter instances give continuous instances with constant support \(\tau\); replicating the electorate leaves \(\mu\) unchanged and allows the represented population to be arbitrarily large. The continuous problem is in NP because a committee is a polynomial-size witness and its constraints and rational score can be checked in polynomial time. I therefore expect hardness transfers (Class B). In particular, making the population large or compressing it to a few ranking types does not by itself remove the hardness in the Bloc subcase.

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem because their election model uses voters through additive candidate scores, while the constraints and the selected committee are already population-independent. Replacing the voter list by its distribution is the natural high-multiplicity representation of that score calculation. The committee stays a \(k\)-element set: the mirror continuizes the population, not the outcome. It covers Theorem 3.1’s constrained winner existence hardness; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s polynomial-time or parameterized results.

The further question this raises is which restrictions on the candidate labels or their dominance structure permit tractability once population compression alone has failed. The theorem’s fixed-support Bloc hardness says that \(\tau\) alone cannot be the needed restriction unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

My weakest point is that this mirror can look like simply dividing vote counts by the electorate size: it introduces no new fractional choice or continuous optimization. That is a fair limitation. The case survives because the programme’s continuization is specifically about the population, and this exact representation remains meaningful at high multiplicity—even in the anchored Bloc hardness result with constant support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that this paper’s continuous population would be an input encoding, not a new layer in the committee problem. For SNTV, the ranking distribution \(\mu\) matters only through each candidate’s first-place mass. For Bloc, it matters only through each candidate’s top-\(k\) mass. Once those \(m\) scores are computed, the rest of the problem—the constraints and the choice of a \(k\)-candidate committee—does not use the society. Many distinct distributions therefore describe the same instance for the purposes of the result.

That remains true for the stronger mirror: arbitrary rational masses over complete rankings, any weakly separable scoring rule in Theorem 3.1, and the same constrained-winner existence question. The distribution maps to a rational score vector in polynomial time. Conversely, every rational distribution can be represented by a finite electorate after scaling by a common denominator. So the mirror adds no continuous decision variable, mass transfer, pricing problem, or optimization over a population. It asks for the paper’s same committee computation from normalized score data. Making the committee itself fractional would add an outcome-space question outside ChoCo’s scope.

This limits the mirror’s claim on the programme’s continuous-optimization motivation: there is no apparent LP or column-generation structure for that technology to exploit. It could still be useful as a clean high-multiplicity formulation, and the professional-association scenario is plausible. The paper’s fixed-voter Bloc result even makes the high-multiplicity regime easy to state. That is evidence for the model’s sensibility, not a collision with prior continuous work.

I cannot turn this limitation into a convincing objection to the mirror itself. The proposed question uses a distribution over voter types, has a precise computational task, and admits the programme’s discrete-to-continuous dictionary. Saying that its population dependence compresses to scores does not defeat it under the stated criteria. My case against is therefore weak: it argues for lower priority than mirrors where population mass is directly manipulated, but it does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
