The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), acceptable pairs, strict rankings over neighboring types, and integral per-agent lower and upper quotas, consider measurable matchings of the nonatomic populations in which each individual receives an integral bundle. Call an allocation critical if it minimizes the mass-weighted sum of individual quota shortfalls. Compare critical allocations by measure-preserving pairings within each type and the paper’s allowed slot correspondences; call an allocation popular if no rival has positive aggregate vote margin under any such comparison. Does a maximum-cardinality popular critical allocation always exist, and can one be computed from the finite type description?

The model it lives in

A categorical many-to-many market with rational mass at each type, integral individual bundles, mass-weighted quota deficits, and aggregate popularity under within-type pairings and slot correspondences. The objective is to maximize total matched mass while remaining critical and popular.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s existence and maximum-size computation question. It leaves Theorem 2’s invariance result and Theorem 3’s size guarantee unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a maximum-cardinality popular critical allocation always exist for these finite-support type populations, and can one be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the mass bit length \(L\), and the quota encoding?
  • How exactly do finite repeated-type instances map to this population model when individual bundles remain integral?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every bipartite matching instance in which each vertex has lower and upper quotas and a strict preference order over its neighbors, a polynomial-time algorithm computes a maximum-cardinality matching that is popular among all critical matchings. Here, *critical* means minimizing total lower-quota deficit, and *popular* means that no other critical matching can defeat the output under any choice of the vertices’ correspondence functions. The paper gives no hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolving mirror, so the positive case is for a population-scale version of this algorithmic problem.

A plausible regime is a large university system allocating students to course sections across recurring terms. Students could share types defined by program, prerequisites, quota needs, and course preferences; sections could share types defined by subject, capacity, minimum enrollment, and student-type rankings. Each student still receives an integral set of courses, and each section an integral set of students. What grows is the number of students and sections, while the number of distinct profiles \(\tau\) stays relatively small. The same model could describe worker–firm markets with repeated credential and employer profiles.

I would name the mirror Continuous Popular Critical Many-to-Many Matching. Its input is finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\) on the two sides, rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i+\sum_j\nu_j=1\), an acceptable-pair graph, strict rankings over neighboring types, and integral per-agent lower and upper quotas \(q_i^-,q_i^+\) and \(r_j^-,r_j^+\). A type is a complete profile for this problem: everyone in it shares the same quota and preferences. Rankings over types preserve strict preferences where distinctions matter; individuals within a type are interchangeable.

A solution assigns each unit of mass an integral bundle of distinct counterpart agents. It can be represented by a distribution \(\pi_i(z)\) over integer bundle-count vectors \(z\) for each type \(i\), and \(\rho_j(w)\) for each type \(j\). For example, \(z_j\) is the number of type-\(j\) partners assigned to an individual of type \(i\), with \(\sum_j z_j\le q_i^+\). The mass \(x_{ij}\) of pairs between types \(i\) and \(j\) must be consistent from both sides: \(x_{ij}=\mu_i\sum_z\pi_i(z)z_j=\nu_j\sum_w\rho_j(w)w_i\), and \(x_{ij}=0\) for unacceptable pairs. Thus individual bundles stay integral even though the type-level masses \(x_{ij}\) can be fractional.

For an allocation \(P\), define its total deficit as the mass-weighted sum of individual shortfalls: \(D(P)=\sum_i\mu_i\sum_z\pi_i(z)(q_i^--\sum_jz_j)_+ + \sum_j\nu_j\sum_w\rho_j(w)(r_j^--\sum_iw_i)_+\). Critical allocations are those minimizing \(D\). Using expected degree alone to calculate shortfall would not preserve the paper’s definition, so the bundle distributions are part of the model.

To extend popularity, compare two critical allocations by pairing old and new bundles across the mass of each type, then allowing the same arbitrary correspondences between changed partner slots that the paper allows for individual agents. Pad bundles with \(\bot\) up to the upper quota; rank partner types above \(\bot\). Integrate the resulting votes over the mass. An allocation is popular if no other critical allocation can receive positive total votes under any such type-preserving pairing and slot correspondences. The problem is: prove that a popular critical allocation always exists and compute one maximizing \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\). That states Theorem 1’s underlying matching question at population scale, rather than asking for a fractional outcome alone.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, initially with bounded per-agent quotas and a target running time polynomial in \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) and the input bit length \(L\). The paper’s proposal levels, cloned graph, and dual certificate make a mass-capacitated flow or LP formulation plausible. That is a research direction, not a consequence of Theorem 1: its algorithm and proof use named agents and clone them, and whether those structures compress to a small type support is open. Further questions include whether existence holds for every nonatomic instance, whether the algorithm can be polynomial in \(\tau,L\) when quotas vary, and how finite repeated-type instances map exactly to the continuous problem. Theorem 2’s invariance of matched sets and Theorem 3’s \(2/3\) size guarantee invite analogous questions, but this mirror covers Theorem 1 alone.

The weakest point is that the paper ranks named neighbors strictly, while this type model ranks counterpart types; people within a type are interchangeable. That changes how identity-sensitive votes are represented. It is credible in markets where preferences and priorities are genuinely category-based, and types can be split whenever an identity distinction matters. If splitting makes \(\tau\) grow with the population, the high-multiplicity advantage vanishes for that regime. The other concern is computational: the paper’s level process may resist aggregation. Those are real limits on the mirror, while its criticality, majority comparison, and maximum-size objective remain the paper’s problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest problem with the proposed mirror is that its type-level rankings discard information that Theorem 1 uses to define popularity. In the paper’s six-to-one example, the six vertices on one side have the same preferences and quotas, so they look like one type. But the other side ranks those six named vertices individually. For the same pair of matchings, changing the allowed correspondence between partners makes either matching win. A model that records only partner types cannot express that comparison: it makes all six partners equivalent. Bundle distributions preserve integral bundles, but they do not restore the missing rankings between individuals in a type.

The better repair is to include each person’s rank position, or full profile of positions across counterpart types, in their type. That preserves strict preferences and the paper’s vote rule. But in large markets those rank profiles can be population-sized or continuously varying. Then \(\tau\) need not stay small, and the input is no longer the finite-support distribution over types that makes the programme’s high-multiplicity question compact. One could define a new model using continuous rank coordinates and specify how to encode their distributions and optimize over correspondences. That may be worthwhile research, but it is not yet the mirror the proponent has stated; its central computational question depends on choices the paper does not settle.

This is a substantial objection to the proposed formulation, not a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. A genuinely categorical market, where agents rank counterpart categories and category members are interchangeable for every comparison, could support a coherent continuous version. The negative case is therefore weak on the universal claim: the paper itself gives a sharp warning about identity loss, but it does not rule out a category-based scenario that the authors would recognize.
