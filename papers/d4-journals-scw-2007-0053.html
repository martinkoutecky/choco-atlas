What the mirror covers

No result supports a continuous computational mirror; Theorems 1 and 2 concern analytic genericity, and Lemma 1 derives a probability-of-victory derivative.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper’s electorate has a natural high-multiplicity regime. Consider a large election in which many voters share the same complete probabilistic response rule: a type \(t\) is a function \(p_t:Y\to\Delta(J)\), and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s population share. Then expected vote share is \(V_j(\mu,x)=\sum_t\mu_t p_t^j(x)\). A plausible regime is a large electorate with millions of voters but relatively few response types, such as broad demographic or policy-response groups. The candidates’ platform choices and the distinction between maximizing vote share and probability of victory remain recognizable parts of the paper’s problem.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for any interior policy profile \(x^*\), the response-function profiles satisfying both candidates’ expected-vote-share and probability-of-victory first- and second-order conditions form a finitely shy subset of those satisfying the expected-vote-share conditions. Theorem 2, stated without proof as a corresponding result, gives the same conclusion for an individual candidate. These are analytic genericity results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or parameterized-tractable. The lemmas are also analytic. So there is no qualifying anchor, and consequently no continuous problem per anchor to specify under the requested standard.

The central obstacle to turning the natural population model into a mirror of the paper’s main result is probability of victory. With a finite electorate of size \(N\), fixed type shares \(\mu_t\) correspond to counts \(N\mu_t\), which must be integers to preserve the paper’s model of independent individual votes. As the population grows, the realized vote shares concentrate around their expectations; in the limit, the winner is determined by those expected shares, apart from ties. The finite electorate’s pivotal probabilities—the mechanism distinguishing the two objectives in Lemma 1—do not carry over cleanly. Defining a continuous problem that retains them would require an extra finite-sample or sampling convention, and that risks changing the question the authors studied.

So the positive case is that *the domain* has a credible continuous-population regime, and the paper’s expected-vote-share objective has an exact type-mass expression. The case does not extend to an anchored computational result: the paper neither studies computational complexity nor establishes that its generic non-equivalence theorem survives a population-continuum limit. That is the weakest point, and it is central.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are numbered, but they establish a genericity claim about first- and second-order conditions at a fixed policy profile, using finite shyness in a space of \(C^2\) response functions. They say nothing about the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The expected-vote-share formula and Lemma 1’s pivot-probability expression are likewise not computational results. That leaves no result of this paper for a ChoCo computational mirror to continue.

The proposed high-multiplicity electorate is plausible, and \(\sum_t \mu_t p_t^j(x)\) is the natural expected vote share. But that identity does not carry over the paper’s central comparison. Its other objective is the probability of victory in a finite election of independent votes. In a continuum limit, a strict expected-share lead makes victory probability collapse to an indicator; at a tie, a nontrivial limit requires specifying the finite-population fluctuations and their scaling. One can build a careful asymptotic model around those fluctuations, but its limit is a new analytic question, not a continuization of the paper’s genericity theorem.

The strongest alternative would retain a finite electorate, encode its type counts as rational masses, and ask for algorithms to compute or optimize probability of victory. That preserves the stochastic objective, but keeps electorate size \(N\) essential and asks a computational question the paper never posed. To make such an algorithmic problem finite-input, one would also need an encoding for the response functions; the paper allows arbitrary \(C^2\) functions. Restricting them to an encoded parametric family may be sensible, but it adds another modeling choice and still does not mirror either theorem.

So the negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper*: its results are analytic, and preserving probability of victory in the proposed continuum changes the object that makes the comparison interesting. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous computational problem could be built from probabilistic voting. One could build one, but it would be a new project using this paper’s setting rather than a computational mirror of its results.
