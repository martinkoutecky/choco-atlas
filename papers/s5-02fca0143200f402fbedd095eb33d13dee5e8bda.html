The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. It states no numbered result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or has another complexity classification. The closest named results are structural correspondences, so I would not present them as complexity anchors.

Theorem 5.4 says that \(m \mapsto W(m)\), where \(W(m)(s)\) is the set of buyers matched to seller \(s\), is a bijection between complete stable matchings and stable equilibria. The paper attributes this theorem to [12]. Theorem 7.1, proved here, says that \(m \mapsto g(m)\), where \(g(m)(s)\) consists of the buyers at least as high as \(s\)’s worst matched buyer in its priority order, is a bijection between stable matchings and boundary equilibria. Neither theorem analyzes the complexity of finding a matching or equilibrium.

There is still a plausible continuous problem suggested by the paper, though it is not an eligible anchor under your criteria. In a large school admissions market, let buyer types encode complete rankings of the finite set of schools and their priority classes at each school. Let \(\alpha_i\) be the population mass of type \(i\), and let \(\lambda_s\) be school \(s\)’s capacity per unit population. A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{is}\geq 0\) and unmatched mass \(u_i\geq 0\), with \(\sum_s x_{is}+u_i=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{is}=\lambda_s\). Call it stable if there is no pair \((i,s)\) for which positive mass of type \(i\) is assigned below \(s\) (or unmatched) and \(s\) is serving positive mass of types it ranks below \(i\). Each person still receives at most one school; \(x_{is}\) records the fraction of a type assigned there.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: many applicants share a small number of preference and priority types, while the number of schools and types stays comparatively small. The authors would likely recognize it as a continuous version of their school-matching model. I would expect a tractability result, perhaps via type-level deferred acceptance, with complexity measured in the number of schools, the number of types, and the input bit length. Theorem 7.1 also suggests asking whether stable mass assignments correspond to boundary equilibria.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper assumes strict rankings of individual buyers, whereas repeated types make their members indistinguishable and hence tied from a school’s perspective. The paper itself notes that ties require an extension. So this is a sensible research direction, but it does not turn either theorem into a computational result or supply the required anchor-backed positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 5.4, which the paper attributes to earlier work, and Theorem 7.1, proved here, are bijections between stable matchings and two forms of equilibrium. Neither states the complexity of a problem, gives an algorithm, or classifies an input regime. Turning either into “compute a stable matching” or “compute a boundary equilibrium” adds a task the paper does not study. Under ChoCo’s stated criterion, that is a decisive failure of the proposed anchors.

The proponent’s mass-assignment model is a plausible adjacent question, but it needs a model change. In the paper, every seller strictly ranks every named buyer; that makes each buyer’s priority position part of their complete type, so the literal model has no repeated buyer types. Grouping applicants requires priority classes and allowing a class’s mass to be split across seats. The paper itself recognizes ties as an extension requiring soft limits, so this is not a fatal objection to the model—but it means the proposed continuous problem is a new variant, not a computational consequence of either theorem.

Continuum school-choice models already characterize stable outcomes using cutoffs and supply-and-demand equations, with finitely many colleges and a continuum of students. That work is analytic, so it does not count as a computational collision under the programme’s rules; it does show that the proposed mass model is a natural extension of established continuum matching theory. [Azevedo and Leshno, “A Supply and Demand Framework for Two-Sided Matching Markets”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/687476?download=true)

So the strongest defensible negative verdict is narrow: this paper is a poor target for a continuous mirror anchored in its results, because it contains no computational result. I cannot honestly defend the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists in any scenario. The applicant-mass model may be worthwhile on its own; it simply does not rescue the paper as a ChoCo mirror.
