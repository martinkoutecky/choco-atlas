The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_o\), nonnegative additive values \(v_t(o)\), and fixed \(\epsilon>0\), find a finite-support assignment \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^q\), with \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_o=\rho_o\). For each \((t,B)\) with \(x_{t,B}>0\), require either \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(C)-v_t(o)\) for every occupied bundle \(C\) and every \(o\) with \(C_o>0\), or \(v_t(B)\ge (1-\epsilon)M_t(\rho)\), where \(M_t(\rho)=\max_y\min_{B:y_B>0}v_t(B)\) over finite-support distributions satisfying \(\sum_B y_B=1\) and \(\sum_B y_BB_o=\rho_o\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of households in two complete additive valuation types. Each household receives an integral bundle; \(x_{t,B}\) assigns population mass to bundles, and \(\rho_o\) gives per-capita supply. The task is to find a feasible assignment meeting the EFX-or-approximate-MMS condition, with no welfare objective.

The objection that survived

The cut-and-choose proof relies on an exceptional agent’s choice, and the paper does not show that allocations from finite populations converge to a mass assignment satisfying EFX against every bundle in the limiting support.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the additive-goods approximation branch of Corollary 2 and motivates a corresponding chores question. It does not cover the fixed-population exact mixed-items branch or convert the finite impossibility and fractional-Pareto results into population-continuum results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can such a finite-support \(x\) always be constructed in time polynomial in the binary input length of the masses, supplies, and values?
  • Can finite allocations be chosen so their EFX certificates remain valid against every positively occupied bundle in the limiting support and their MMS thresholds match \(M_t(\rho)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s goods approximation result. It keeps the agents’ bundles indivisible while making the population and its item supply continuous.

For a concrete regime, consider a very large housing or relief programme distributing standardized goods to households. Each household receives a bundle of goods, and many households share one of a small number of complete valuation profiles. Let there be two additive goods types, \(t\in\{1,2\}\), with population masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\). There are \(q\) item categories, with \(\rho_o\) copies of category \(o\) per capita; in a population of \(N\) households, that means \(N\rho_o\) indivisible copies. The input gives the rational masses, supplies, and per-item values. Here \(N\) can be enormous while the number of valuation types stays at two.

My lead anchor is Corollary 2, stated in the paper and assembled there from its allocation results and approximation procedures. In full, it says: for integral additive \((n_1,n_2,1)\)-type instances, an exact \(\notion\) allocation is computable in pseudopolynomial time for fixed \(n=n_1+n_2+1\), including mixed items; and, for fixed \(\epsilon>0\), an \(\EFX{}\vee(1-\epsilon)\)-\(\MMS{}\) allocation for additive goods and an \(\EFX{}\vee(1+\epsilon)\)-\(\MMS{}\) allocation for additive chores are computable in polynomial time. The paper’s goods procedure is its own; the chores procedure uses a routine cited from earlier work. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result. The paper’s nonexistence theorems are combinatorial impossibilities, not computational hardness results.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Two-Type Continuous Approximate \(\notion\) Allocation. For goods, an instance consists of the two rational masses \(\mu_t\), the rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_o\), and nonnegative additive values \(v_t(o)\). A bundle is an integral multiset \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q\), with value \(v_t(B)=\sum_o v_t(o)B_o\). The decision variable is a finite-support mass assignment \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,B}\) is the population fraction of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_o=\rho_o\ \text{for every }o. \]
Define the continuum MMS benchmark for type \(t\) as
\[ M_t(\rho)= \max_y\ \min_{B:y_B>0}v_t(B), \qquad \sum_B y_B=1,\quad \sum_B y_BB_o=\rho_o\ \text{for every }o, \]
where \(y\) ranges over finite-support mass assignments to integral bundles. For every bundle \(B\) assigned positive mass to type \(t\), require either that it is EFX against every occupied bundle \(C\),
\[ v_t(B)\ge v_t(C)-v_t(o) \quad\text{for every occupied }C\text{ and each }o\text{ with }C_o>0, \]
or that \(v_t(B)\ge(1-\epsilon)M_t(\rho)\). The chore variant uses type-specific additive costs, the paper’s chore EFX comparison, and the bound \(c_t(B)\le(1+\epsilon)M_t^{\mathrm{cost}}(\rho)\). The task is to construct such an \(x\); there is no welfare objective.

This is a population mirror, not outcome-space continuity: every household still receives an integral bundle, while the variables record how much population receives each bundle. It also has a direct high-multiplicity reading: rational masses and supplies describe normalized sequences of finite instances. In the paper’s \((n_1,n_2,1)\) result, the exceptional agent may have the same valuation as one of the repeated types, so two positive-mass types are not an alien regime. The paper’s main structural result and Corollary 2 make the resulting question recognizable as their allocation problem.

I would provisionally expect the goods version to be Class A: with few valuation types, a type-mass formulation may admit a compact optimization method, perhaps with a pricing step over bundles. That is a research expectation, not a consequence of Corollary 2. In particular, its polynomial-time guarantee on an expanded finite instance does not automatically give an algorithm polynomial in the bit length of compressed rational supplies. Whether the support-dependent EFX conditions permit such a method is a central further question.

The mirror covers the arbitrary-population goods approximation branch, and has a corresponding chores question. It does not claim to mirror the fixed-\(n\) exact mixed-items branch: a fixed number of agents does not itself provide a positive-mass population limit.

The weakest point is the supply model and its encoding. The paper treats a finite set of goods; this mirror scales the counts of standardized item categories with the population and asks for an algorithm on their rational per-capita supplies. That co-scaling is part of making a large household population meaningful, and the bundles remain indivisible, but the paper does not prove a bit-polynomial algorithm for this compressed input. That gap is the question the mirror poses, rather than a result it can claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 2 is the only strong computational anchor, and it does not transfer directly to the proposed continuous problem. Its exact algorithm is pseudopolynomial for fixed \(n\); its approximation guarantee is stated for explicitly represented finite instances. If rational masses and per-capita supplies encode a population of size \(N\), the denominator of the input can be \(O(\log N)\) bits while the finite algorithm’s running time depends on \(N\). The corollary therefore gives no polynomial-time bound in the continuous input length \(L\).

There is a second gap. The paper’s existence proof works by selecting one exceptional agent and using that agent’s choice to organize the allocation. In the proposed limit, that agent has mass zero. One can approximate two positive-mass types by finite instances and designate a member of one type as the exception, but the paper gives no argument that the resulting allocations converge to a feasible mass assignment. A vanishingly small group can still affect which bundles are occupied, and EFX compares against every occupied bundle. The proposed continuous benchmark and support conditions thus need their own analysis.

That is the best objection to the anchor, but it only shows that the paper’s algorithm does not already solve the compressed problem. It does not show that the problem is a poor mirror. A finite, capped-bundle version would avoid some limit issues, yet leave a recognizable high-multiplicity allocation question. The relief-program scenario is stylized—complete valuation types may be rare once household needs are included—but recurring cohorts with standardized entitlements are plausible enough that I cannot rule them out. Theorem 4 and the approximation branch make the connection stronger; the nonexistence results themselves are not computational anchors, and Theorem 3’s fractional allocations concern outcome-space continuity rather than continuous populations.

So the strongest case against is that the proponent has identified a new compressed-input problem, not transferred a known algorithmic result, and has not yet established that its limit semantics match the finite theorem. That makes the claimed tractability speculative. It does not make the question unworthy under ChoCo’s stated scope. The negative case is weak against the best version of this mirror, and I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
