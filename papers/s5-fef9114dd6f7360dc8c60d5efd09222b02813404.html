What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a continuous computational mirror. Propositions 1–7 and Corollary 1 characterize equilibria and their relation to deterministic mechanisms; Proposition 8 addresses best responses in a sequential game.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that college admissions has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: large student-placement or public-housing markets where many workers share a small number of complete preference rankings, and many institutions share preference rankings and quotas. A type would be a worker’s full ranking, or a firm’s full ranking together with its quota. The input could be rational masses \(\mu\) over these types, with support \(\tau\) much smaller than the population.

A matching could then be represented by type-to-type flows \(x_{tf}\), with worker mass and per-capita firm capacity constraints. Fractional values describe aggregate assignments; each actual worker still receives at most one firm. Reports remain preference lists, and the mechanism returns a lottery over allocations stable for the reported preferences. The natural question is whether equilibrium outcome characterizations survive for these mass distributions.

The best substantive results to mirror would be Propositions 2 and 3, both proved in this paper. Proposition 2 says that, for any random stable matching rule, every individually rational matching under the true preferences can be supported as an ordinal Nash equilibrium. Proposition 3 says that every such equilibrium has a unique outcome, and that outcome is individually rational under the true preferences. Together they characterize equilibrium outcomes exactly as the individually rational matchings. Proposition 1, also proved here, supplies the key intermediate claim: every ordinal Nash equilibrium produces a degenerate lottery. These are meaningful anchors for a continuous *equilibrium-theory* question.

But none is an anchor of the kind this task requires. The paper has no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification. Its numbered propositions and corollary characterize strategic behavior; they do not classify the complexity of a problem. So I cannot honestly present them as computational anchors or assign their mirrors to Classes A, B, or C.

There is also a real modeling obstacle. If strategies are recorded only as mass distributions over reported types, a single agent’s deviation changes zero mass. Defining deviations by an entire cohort’s mass preserves a meaningful aggregate game, but changes individual ordinal Nash equilibrium into a cohort-deviation concept. Preserving the paper’s individual incentives instead requires an agent-level mechanism that specifies how a person’s report affects that person’s lottery, even when it has no effect on aggregate type masses. That weakens the high-multiplicity computational advantage and may make the proposed question less recognizable to the authors.

Thus the paper offers a plausible population-continuous model and strong equilibrium findings one could investigate in it. The proposed scope would be Propositions 1–7, especially the exact characterization in Propositions 2–3; Proposition 8’s sequential-game extension adds a separate complication because it introduces history-dependent strategies. My weakest point is that the plausible mirror is not yet a faithful, computationally anchored mirror of the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is also the narrowest: the proponent’s anchors are not computational results. Propositions 2 and 3 characterize ordinal Nash equilibrium outcomes as exactly the individually rational matchings; Proposition 1 says an equilibrium lottery is degenerate. They establish strategic properties, but none states the complexity of a computational problem. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, that is a decisive mismatch.

A stronger population model is conceivable. For example, represent workers by preference types and assignments by mass flows, then ask whether the equilibrium characterization survives. But preserving the paper’s individual deviations requires a representative worker’s report to affect that worker’s assignment even though it has zero effect on aggregate masses. A mean-field model could supply that interpretation, but it would be a new equilibrium model, not a computational mirror of these propositions. If instead whole cohorts deviate, the player and equilibrium concepts change. Proposition 1 has a related ambiguity: a deterministic mass flow can still give each individual a lottery over destinations, so the paper’s claim about a degenerate lottery over complete matchings does not transfer without specifying what counts as the continuous outcome.

There is another obstacle to making these into algorithmic questions: the paper permits an arbitrary random stable matching rule, defined as a map from preference profiles to lotteries. It gives no finite representation of that map. A computational study would need to choose and encode a particular mechanism, then formulate a new problem such as computing equilibria for it. That could be worthwhile research, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

I would not argue that no plausible high-multiplicity regime exists: large school-placement or housing markets could support one. Nor can I rule out an interesting continuous equilibrium-theory extension. The negative case is strong for the requested ChoCo fit, but weak as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
