The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive reading is that this paper suggests a continuous mechanism-design question, but it supplies no named computational result to anchor the mirror required here.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: for \(|N|\geq 4\) and \(|O|\geq 3\), every mechanism satisfying \(\SETE\), strategy-proofness and ex-post weak non-wastefulness is \(q\)-agent-object-wasteful for some \(q\geq \frac{1}{6}\); the same lower bound holds for every mechanism satisfying \(\ETE\), strategy-proofness and ex-post efficiency. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives the corresponding lower bound \(q\geq \frac{1}{4}\) for object-wastefulness under the same two classes. These are axiomatic impossibility and welfare-bound results, not classifications of a computational problem. The lemmas showing that RSD attains bounds in specified cases are likewise mechanism-performance results, not complexity results.

A plausible extension would take types to be complete preference rankings, society to be a distribution over those types, and object supplies to scale with population. A rule would assign each type a lottery over objects and the outside option, subject to per-capita feasibility and type-level incentive constraints. One could then ask for the minimum worst-case aggregate agent-object waste across such rules. That is a concrete direction related to the paper’s subject, and scaling supply is a defensible co-modification for a large-population regime.

But it is a new mechanism-design problem, not a continuous version of a named computational result in this paper. The paper studies finite-agent strategic and fairness properties; it gives no algorithmic question whose hardness might dissolve, transfer, or arise specifically in the continuum. Simply treating its random assignment probabilities as population mass would confuse continuity of outcomes with continuity of society. So the positive case is, at best, a plausible future research question—not an anchored continuous mirror of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that both apparent anchors are the wrong kind of result for ChoCo. Theorem 1 gives a lower bound of \(1/6\) on agent-object waste across classes of mechanisms; Theorem 2 gives a lower bound of \(1/4\) on object waste. Neither asks for an algorithm or establishes a complexity bound. They are axiomatic results about which mechanisms can satisfy specified properties.

The best continuous version is coherent: take a distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference types, scale school capacities per capita, and give each reported type a lottery over objects and the outside option. Strategy-proofness can still be meaningful: an individual’s report changes their own type-contingent lottery even though it leaves the population distribution unchanged. Waste can likewise be defined by integrating over type masses. But translating either theorem into that model asks for new axiomatic bounds on feasible mechanisms. It does not mirror a computational result in the paper.

That remains true if the question is sharpened to “find the minimum worst-case waste among continuous mechanisms.” Theorems 1 and 2 already concern that kind of minimum over mechanism classes; their proofs reduce the lower-bound argument to four agents and three objects. The population distribution would change the domain of the axiomatic analysis, but it would not supply an algorithmic input or computational task. Likewise, asking for the exact waste of RSD or RDA on a high-multiplicity instance could be a separate algorithmic project, but this paper does not study that computation. A continuous priority process would also need its own definition.

So the proponent’s proposed mirror survives as a plausible continuous mechanism-design question, perhaps worthwhile in its own right. It does not survive as an anchored continuous computational mirror of either theorem. The negative case is strong within ChoCo’s remit; it cannot establish that no continuous research question about this paper could ever be worthwhile.
