The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case rests on Theorem 6, the paper’s hardness result for CLB-2. Its proof already uses a high-multiplicity regime: for odd \(n\), roughly half the agents share valuation \(V_1\), roughly half share \(V_2\), and one agent has their midpoint valuation; for even \(n\), only the two repeated types are needed. The theorem, proved in this paper, says that for every \(n\ge 3\), computing a CLB-2 allocation requires infinitely many Robertson–Webb queries. The proof shows why: any such allocation would yield an exact division into pieces with specified values under both \(V_1\) and \(V_2\), and exact division is known to have unbounded query complexity [from Robertson and Webb (1998), cited in the paper]. The paper also states that the same lower bound extends to every CLB-\(k\) with \(k\ge2\).

Here is a continuous-population version I would be willing to put to a prover. Let \(\Omega=[0,1]\) be a population of unit mass, divided into finitely many valuation types \(T_t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The number of types is \(\tau\), potentially small while the represented population is large. Each type has a normalized nonatomic valuation \(V_t\) over the cake, available through Robertson–Webb queries. A feasible allocation assigns each cake point to one population point; formally, it is a measurable map \(f:[0,1]_{\mathrm{cake}}\to\Omega\). For a group \(S\subseteq\Omega\), write
\[ G_t(S)=\int_{f^{-1}(S)}v_t(x)\,dx, \]
the value, to type \(t\), of the cake held by that group. Require these value measures to have densities with respect to population mass, so \(g_t=dG_t/d\mu\) is the per-capita value density. For fixed rational \(\alpha>0\), Continuous CLB-\(\alpha\) asks for a complete allocation such that, for every measurable \(S\) with \(0<\mu(S)\le\alpha\), and every type \(t\) represented by positive mass in \(S\),
\[ G_t(\Omega\setminus S)\le 1-\mu(S), \]
or equivalently \(\int_S g_t\,d\mu\ge\mu(S)\). The computational question is whether a finite-query protocol, with complexity stated in \(\tau\) and the bit length of the masses, can output such an allocation. This is the mass-scaled version of the paper’s group guarantee: a coalition’s size is its population mass, and its collective value is measured by the shared preference of a type represented in it.

The case for authors recognizing this question is that their central contribution is about how group fairness changes with coalition size, and Theorem 6’s obstruction already survives when the electorate has only two valuation types. For fixed \(\alpha>0\), the corresponding finite CLB hierarchy asks about groups of size proportional to \(n\); the continuous problem asks what happens to that guarantee as the electorate is represented by type masses. I would expect exact Continuous CLB-\(\alpha\) to be query-hard for reasons tied to simultaneous exact balancing across valuation types. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 6: its proof concerns finite-agent CLB-2 and does not establish a lower bound for this measurable-allocation problem. The paper’s \(\Omega(n^2)\) CHB-2 result is a poorer anchor: its proof makes an agent-by-agent query argument, so it does not provide the same evidence for hardness with small \(\tau\).

The weak point is substantial. In the continuum model, individual fibers \(f^{-1}(y)\) have zero cake value; the meaningful objects are group holdings and their value densities. That changes the paper’s individual-piece allocation problem into a problem about measurable population-level assignments. It also raises a hard representation question: can a finite-query protocol describe the required map, or does exact fairness demand an infinite refinement? The finite exact-division reduction suggests a genuine obstacle, but it does not settle either question. The mirror is strongest as a proposed limit problem for the paper’s group-fairness hierarchy; its acceptance turns on whether the authors regard value density over measurable coalitions as a faithful continuous form of their guarantees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is the strongest anchor, and its repeated valuation types are real evidence that high multiplicity is sensible here. The difficulty is that repetition is not where its hardness comes from. The proof turns a CLB-2 allocation for \(n\) named recipients into exact division among \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1\) pieces. Its hard object grows with the number of individual allocations, even though there are only two valuation functions. A model that compresses recipients to a fixed number \(\tau\) of types cannot retain those pieces as its output; a model that keeps them must explain how a finite protocol describes allocations to a continuum of individuals.

The proposed Continuous CLB-\(\alpha\) also changes the hierarchy’s scale. CLB-2 concerns coalitions of mass \(2/n\), which tends to zero. A fixed \(\alpha>0\) instead concerns coalitions whose size grows proportionally with \(n\). In fact, with the proposed absolute continuity condition, that version becomes much stronger. Fix a type \(t\) and a set \(B\) of population mass less than \(\alpha\). If \(B\) contains no members of type \(t\), add a set \(E\) of type-\(t\) members with positive mass, then let \(\mu(E)\) tend to zero. The constraint and \(G_t\ll\mu\) imply \(G_t(B)\geq\mu(B)\). Applying this to small sets gives \(g_t\geq1\) almost everywhere; since \(\int g_t\,d\mu=1\), it follows that \(G_t=\mu\). Thus every valuation type must value the cake assigned to every population group exactly in proportion to that group’s mass. At type level, this requires \(V_t(A_u)=\mu_u\) for every pair of types \(t,u\): a type-wise exact division problem, rather than the paper’s CLB-2 problem.

That is a coherent question, but it does not preserve Theorem 6’s growing collection of individually allocated pieces. If the output is only the \(\tau\) aggregate bundles, the population dimension has collapsed. If each unit of mass must still receive an integral bundle, the type-level density records only averages, and the mirror needs an additional finite representation for the individual assignment. Allowing fractional average bundles supplies a representation, but changes the output object; it does not establish that the original theorem’s exact-division reduction survives. Scaling the coalition size as \(2/n\) instead leaves only zero-mass coalitions, whose pairwise constraints are absent from ordinary almost-everywhere mass conditions. Preserving them requires individual or pair-indexed allocation data that type masses alone do not encode.

The CHB-2 lower bound is weaker support. Its \(\Omega(n^2)\) proof obtains information separately about each agent’s valuation. In a high-multiplicity instance, agents of the same complete type share one valuation oracle, so those queries cannot simply be counted independently; the proof’s adversary also varies agents’ valuations independently. Scaling CHB to coalitions of mass \(\alpha\) fares no better: its bound \((n-|S|)/(n-|S|+1)\) tends to \(1\) when \(|S|\) grows proportionally with \(n\), making the group constraint vacuous in the limit. A local pair version again needs the individual-level structure the type distribution was meant to compress.

I would not call this a conclusive universal veto. The authors could choose to study a type-level exact-division problem with a specified way to lift aggregate allocations to individuals. But that would need its own model and output representation; Theorem 6 does not establish that it is the continuous mirror of their CLB-2 result. The negative case is strongest on that mismatch, and weaker against the possibility that the authors would find the new type-level problem worthwhile.
