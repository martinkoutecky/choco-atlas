Given a finite-support initial measure \(\mu_0=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\delta_{a_i}\), with binary-rational masses \(w_i>0\), rational priors \(a_i\in[0,1]\), a fixed detection model, and threshold \(\theta\), iterate \(F_{\mu}(a)=\frac{\int_{\{b:d(a,b)\le\theta\}} b\,d\mu(b)}{\mu(\{b:d(a,b)\le\theta\})}\) and \(\mu_{t+1}=(F_{\mu_t})_\#\mu_t\); compute the exact terminal atoms and their masses, and determine the worst-case running time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: this paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind the anchor requirement calls for. Proposition 1, proved here, says that if one decision weight starts no greater than another, that ordering is preserved at every update. Proposition 2, also proved here, says the decision weights converge in finite time and that any two distinct limits are separated by at least \(\theta\) in one of the two Bayes-risk-divergence directions. Neither proposition gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic running-time bound. The simulations are not numbered computational results either. So there is no eligible anchor, and I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem per anchor.

The nearest plausible population-continuum direction would be a large public deliberation with many people sharing a small set of initial priors and the same detection model and error costs. A type could record an initial prior and any other agent attributes the model uses; its mass would be that type’s share of the population. At each round, mass at prior \(a\) would move to the weighted mean of priors \(b\) satisfying \(d(a,b)\le\theta\). One could ask whether the measure-valued dynamics converge, how many clusters form, and what aggregate detection risk results. This is a recognizable extension of the paper’s model, but it is an analytic dynamics question without an objective or computational-complexity anchor. As a case for ChoCo’s computational programme, it remains weak.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no eligible anchor, and that is the strongest ground against a mirror here. Propositions 1 and 2 establish order preservation and finite-time convergence; neither states the complexity of a computational problem. The cluster and Bayes-risk plots are empirical results. There is no theorem here about an algorithm, an optimization problem, or its running time for ChoCo to continuize.

The best candidate is to encode initial priors as a rational, finite-support distribution \(\mu\), then ask for the limiting clusters. At each round, an opinion \(a\) would move to
\[ \frac{\int_{\{b:\,d(a,b)\le\theta\}} b\,d\mu(b)} {\mu(\{b:\,d(a,b)\le\theta\})}, \]
with the distribution pushed forward by that update. This is a sensible population model, and the paper’s signal-detection setting gives it a plausible interpretation.

But turning it into a complexity question requires choosing a new problem: how the distribution is encoded, whether the output is exact cluster locations or an approximation, and whether the query concerns convergence, cluster count, or aggregate risk. With finite support, the dynamics remain on at most \(\tau\) opinion values per round, so the natural task is to compute the endpoint of a compressed dynamical system. With a diffuse distribution, one must supply a representation and an error criterion the paper does not give. Adding a more recognizable control or campaigning problem would require intervention rules and costs absent from the model.

That is a case against the paper as an anchor for ChoCo, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Computing the endpoint from rational masses is well-posed, and its complexity might be nontrivial; the paper provides no basis for ruling that out. So the negative case is strongest on the absence of a named computational result and the distance from a computational social-choice problem. The universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror remains weak.
