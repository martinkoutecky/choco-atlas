The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a continuous version of the Byzantine-agent attack problem. There is a significant qualification: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity result. Its closest candidate is the experimental finding in Section 5.2, Figure 7. The authors simulate teams of \(1{,}000\) agents with \(50\) sensors, sensor reliability \(0.55\), and average degree \(4\), varying the Byzantine share and how attackers are selected. They report that small Byzantine populations can sharply reduce the number reaching the correct conclusion: for random networks, the reported drop has a threshold at \(6\%\) Byzantine agents under random or maximum-degree selection and \(4\%\) under maximum-influence selection; in scale-free networks, selecting maximum-degree nodes leaves fewer than \(10\%\) reaching the correct conclusion at just \(1\%\) Byzantine agents. This is an empirical result, not an eligible named complexity anchor, so I would not present it as one.

A concrete mirror is Minimum Byzantine Mass for Majority Failure. Its input is a finite set \(T\) of standardized agent classes; rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); a type-to-type communication rule; and the paper’s sensor reliabilities, trust values, belief updates, decision thresholds, and initial-belief distributions. A class records the fixed features relevant to the process, including sensor access and communication role. In the large-population dynamics, the state tracks the distribution of beliefs within each class. The attacker chooses Byzantine masses \(b_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), whose members ignore observations and send false conclusions as in the paper, and an admissible attack policy. The question is to minimize \(\sum_t b_t\) subject to at most half of the population reaching the correct conclusion by a specified horizon. A solution is a minimizing mass allocation and attack policy, with the resulting final correct-conclusion mass.

The regime I find plausible is a large fleet of standardized robots, or a similarly repeated team, with many agents but relatively few sensor, trust, and communication roles. A fraction of the fleet is a more natural unit for describing compromise than an exact count. Modeling communication between those roles also leaves room for the paper’s central concern: the placement and timing of attacks can matter as much as their number. This is a plausible continuous analogue of the problem the authors study, though it does not mirror a formal complexity result from the paper.

For this general dynamic optimization problem, my tentative expectation is continuum-specific hardness: the attacker chooses both where and when to intervene in belief dynamics with cascades. That is a conjecture about the proposed mirror, not something established by the paper. Further questions include whether a type-level attack can match the paper’s local percolation strategy, which features of the interaction structure must be retained, and whether the minimum attack mass is efficiently approximable when the number of classes is small.

The weakest point is that the paper’s effects depend on particular sparse network structures and influential individual nodes. A type-level communication model could smooth away the correlations and local topology that create those effects. Retaining enough neighborhood structure may make the number of types too large to give the intended high-multiplicity benefit. This is the main modeling question a referee would need to accept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The clearest negative is that the paper establishes no named computational result. Its analysis concerns percolation probabilities, and its findings about Byzantine agents come from finite simulations. Section 6 gives a local attack heuristic and tests it experimentally; it gives no complexity or approximation result for an algorithmic problem. So the proposed “Minimum Byzantine Mass for Majority Failure” is a new optimization problem motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.

That distinction does not settle whether the new question is worthwhile. A stronger version than the one proposed would take a sequence of sparse network models for standardized robot teams and ask for the minimum compromised fraction that causes a specified loss of correct conclusions by a given horizon. A fraction is a natural quantity in a large fleet, and the paper’s objective of counting correct agents adapts naturally to mass. The paper itself offers a credible high-multiplicity setting.

The difficulty is preserving the network features that make the paper’s results about attacks meaningful. Two networks can have the same masses of sensor and trust roles, and the same rates of communication between roles, yet differ in hubs, neighborhoods, and local correlations that affect cascades. A type defined only by role therefore does not capture all the information the process uses. A type that includes an agent’s relevant network position may need to distinguish almost every agent, making the support grow with the team. Replacing the graph with a sparse random-network limit or degree classes is a better prospect, but it adds a network model and assumptions about which local information attackers can use. Those choices define a more specific adversarial network-control problem.

That is the best case against: the most promising mirror is a substantial new model-building project, with no complexity result in this paper to anchor it and no obvious finite-support population model that retains its network-specific attack mechanisms. Still, I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario. Standardized fleets on a suitable repeated network could support a meaningful continuous study; whether its computational questions are substantial would depend on the network limit and attack policies the authors accepted.
