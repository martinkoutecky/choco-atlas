Every anchor argued

The continuous mirror question

Given distinct rational bichromatic signal distributions \(P_i=(p_i,1-p_i)\) for \(i\in[n]\), choose a common row-stochastic policy \(x_{c,j}\), where \(x_{c,j}\) is the fraction of signal-type \(c\) mass assigned a ballot for urn \(j\). For each possible correct urn \(i\), let \(v^{(i)}_j=\sum_c p_{i,c}x_{c,j}\). Maximize \(\delta=\min_{i,\ j\ne i}(v^{(i)}_i-v^{(i)}_j)\).

The model it lives in

For each possible state \(i\), the society is the distribution \(\mu^{(i)}=P_i\) over signal types. The policy \(x\) assigns ballot mass by type, each unit of mass casting one integral ballot; the objective is to maximize the guaranteed plurality margin across states. This is a linear program with rational input.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2.1 bounds voters needed for a finite-sample success probability; maximizing the limiting margin does not preserve its quantitative dependence on \(n\), \(\epsilon\), and \(\eta\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers bichromatic plurality strategy design in Theorem 2.1. It leaves the finite-sample lower bound of Theorem 4.5, the multicolor sample bound, and the cumulative and Condorcet voting results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize the optimal guaranteed margin \(\delta^*(P_1,\ldots,P_n)\) and its dependence on \(n\) and the minimum gap \(\epsilon\).
  • Determine whether the policy used for Theorem 2.1 is optimal for this margin objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is for the paper’s plurality strategy design. Let the voter types be signal colors \(c\in[C]\). If urn \(i\) is correct, the continuous society is \(\mu^{(i)}_c=p_{i,c}\): the fraction of the population that receives signal \(c\). A common voting policy is a row-stochastic matrix \(x_{c,j}\), where \(x_{c,j}\) is the fraction of type-\(c\) voters who vote for urn \(j\). The vote mass for \(j\) under state \(i\) is then \(v^{(i)}_j=\sum_c p_{i,c}x_{c,j}\).

Call the problem Continuous Signal-Plurality Synthesis: given the distinct rational signal distributions \(P_1,\ldots,P_n\), choose \(x\) to maximize the worst-case plurality margin
\[ \delta=\min_{i,\ j\ne i}\left(v^{(i)}_i-v^{(i)}_j\right). \]
The solution is a policy \(x\) and its margin; \(\delta>0\) means every urn wins when it is the correct one. This is a linear program with \(Cn\) policy variables and \(n(n-1)\) margin constraints, so this mirror is in P. Here the number of agents can be very large compared with the \(C\) signal types. The candidate set remains finite, while the society’s type masses vary with the unknown correct urn.

The closest positive anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved in the paper: for bichromatic urns with \(0\le p_1<\cdots<p_n\le1\) and minimum consecutive gap \(\epsilon\), there is a plurality voting scheme under which \(O(n^3\epsilon^{-2}\ln\eta^{-1})\) voters suffice for the correct urn to receive the most votes with probability at least \(1-\eta\). In the continuous model, the same scheme gives the correct urn strictly greater expected vote mass than each rival, so the finite society’s vote proportions converge to a profile with a positive margin.

Theorem 4.5, also proved here, supports the finite-size side of that bridge: for the evenly spaced family \(\mathcal I(n,\epsilon)\), where \(n\ge120\) and \(\epsilon\le1/[11(n-1)]\), there is a constant \(H>0\) such that for \(\eta<H\), any voting scheme using at most \(O(n^3\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\eta))\) voters fails with probability \(\Omega(\eta)\). It identifies a regime where finite populations need that order of magnitude, even though the limiting mass profile can have a strict winner. This theorem is about sample size, not the complexity of solving the continuous LP.

The mirror is plausible because the paper’s object is already a rule mapping private signals to votes, and its correctness condition is plurality. Expected vote mass is exactly the population-limit counterpart of the random vote counts they analyze. Concentration connects the two: positive mass margins yield high-probability finite-population winners as the electorate grows. I would expect the authors to recognize this as their model’s continuum limit, though not as a replacement for their sample-complexity question.

The weakness is substantial: the paper contains no named theorem asserting a time-complexity classification such as P or NP-hardness. Its named results concern how many random voters are needed, not the runtime of computing a voting scheme. Continuous Signal-Plurality Synthesis is a sensible new computational question, but its LP and tractability are consequences of the proposed mirror, not results established by this paper. So this is a defensible, narrow population mirror, but it does not provide a strict computational-complexity anchor of the kind the programme asks for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the named results is a computational-complexity result. Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 4.5 bound how many independent voters are needed to achieve a stated probability of success; Theorem 6.2 does the same for cumulative voting. They do not classify the time needed to compute a solution. The paper gives voting strategies, but its main contribution is their sample efficiency. That leaves ChoCo without the kind of computational result its mirrors are meant to chart.

The proposed LP is coherent, but it changes the question. It chooses a signal-to-vote policy to maximize the worst-case *expected* plurality margin. Theorem 2.1 instead asks how the number of voters must grow with \(n\), \(\epsilon\), and \(\eta\) to make the *random* election succeed. A positive limiting margin does not capture that tradeoff: finite-sample success also depends on vote variance. A closer formulation would retain a population-size parameter and optimize a probability of correctness. But then the key quantity is still the accumulation of independent voter-level randomness, rather than a computation over an atomless society.

Theorem 4.5 makes that mismatch especially clear. Its lower bound comes from anti-concentration for finitely many individual votes. In an atomless population, those fluctuations disappear and vote masses are deterministic. Keeping them requires retaining finite random draws, perhaps through a Poissonized model; that could be a useful extension, but it keeps the paper’s sampling problem rather than producing a continuous-population complexity question.

There is also a modeling gap in calling this a society mirror. The urns are alternative hidden states, each inducing a signal distribution \(P_i\). The paper does not supply one known society distribution as input: each voter sees only a private draw, and the group must communicate enough through ballots to identify the state. If the exact limiting signal mass \(P_i\) is instead given to a decision-maker, the distinctness assumption makes the state identifiable directly. The LP avoids that shortcut by designing a common policy for all candidate states, but that is best described as a new robust strategy-synthesis problem, not the computation studied in the paper.

So I would reject these anchors as mirrors of computational results: the LP is a plausible adjacent problem, while Theorems 2.1 and 4.5 concern finite-sample information and reliability. The negative case is not airtight. A large cohort sharing a small signal alphabet is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and policy synthesis over its type masses may be worthwhile on its own. What the paper does not establish is that this new question is a continuous computational mirror of one of its results.
