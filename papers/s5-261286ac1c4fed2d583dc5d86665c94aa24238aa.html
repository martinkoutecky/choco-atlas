What the mirror covers

The characterization theorem and the cited characterization facts are axiomatic; the equilibrium-existence facts state no construction or complexity guarantee. No computational result in the paper is covered by a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper’s *market* has a credible high-multiplicity regime. Think of a large recurring cohort of buyers, each wanting at most one item from a fixed set of heterogeneous item classes. Buyers with the same complete preferences over items and payments form a type; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share, with \(N\gg\tau\). To keep scarcity meaningful as the cohort grows, item supplies must scale with it. That is a co-modification of the model, but it preserves the population as the continuous object.

The formal model is straightforward: a finite support \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), classical preferences \(R_t\) over \(L\times\mathbb R\), and per-capita supplies \(q_a\) for item classes \(a\in M\). A mass flow \(y_{t,a}\) assigns each type’s mass among items and the null option, subject to \(\sum_{a\in L}y_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_{t,a}\le q_a\). At prices \(p\), positive assigned mass must receive a demanded item, and any item with \(p_a>0\) must be fully allocated.

But there is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its main Theorem—stated without a number in §3 and called “Theorem 1” in §4.5—says that, on the full classical domain and for arbitrary \(n,m\), an allocation rule satisfies efficiency, individual rationality, no subsidy, and strategy-proofness if and only if it is a minimum-price Walrasian rule. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The cited and derived Facts 5–7 are characterizations too. So there are zero anchors, and no per-anchor complexity mirror to offer.

The nearest adjacent computational question would be: Continuous Minimum-Price Walrasian Computation. Given \((\mathcal T,\mu,M,q,(R_t)_{t\in\mathcal T})\), with each preference supplied in a finite encoding such as rational piecewise-linear utilities, output the least Walrasian price vector \(p\) and a feasible type-to-item mass flow \(y\) supporting it. The output prices determine per-unit payments. This asks for the computation of the paper’s equilibrium object in a continuous population, but it is a new algorithmic question, not a result of the paper; its complexity is not established here.

The weakest point is that this extension does not preserve the paper’s main question. A type-level flow needs a careful individual incentive notion: a single agent’s report has zero mass and cannot affect aggregate prices or allocations. The paper also allows arbitrary classical preferences, which need a finite representation before a complexity question is defined. Scope: the population mirror is plausible; the paper itself supplies no computational result for ChoCo to classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Its main theorem characterizes which allocation rules satisfy individual rationality, no subsidy, efficiency, and strategy-proofness; Facts 1–2 give equilibrium existence and a minimum-price property, while Facts 5–7 are also characterizations. None states an algorithm, complexity bound, or hardness result. The paper’s contribution is extending an axiomatic characterization to arbitrary numbers of agents and objects.

The recurring-buyer scenario is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and scaling item supplies with the cohort is a coherent population model. But that reconstruction does not create a computational result from the paper. Computing a minimum-price Walrasian equilibrium for finitely encoded preferences, type masses, and per-capita capacities would be a new problem: the paper establishes existence, not the computational complexity of finding such an equilibrium.

The closest attempt to preserve the theorem would be a continuous-population characterization of mechanisms under the same four axioms. That remains axiomatic work, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. It also needs a new incentive formulation: if outcomes depend only on the aggregate distribution, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot change them. Making the mechanism respond to an individual report while prices remain fixed gives a different, pointwise demand problem; allowing positive-mass coalitions changes the strategic property.

So no qualifying mirror survives, including the proponent’s proposed equilibrium-computation question: it may be a worthwhile adjacent project, but it is not anchored in a computational result of this paper. The negative case is weaker against the literal claim that *no* continuous auction-computation problem could be worthwhile—the cohort market is sensible, and such a project might be valuable on its own. The strong conclusion is narrower: this paper supplies no computational mirror for ChoCo.
