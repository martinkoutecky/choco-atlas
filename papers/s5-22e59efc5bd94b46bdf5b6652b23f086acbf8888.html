The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_p\) summing to one over pair types \(p=(X_p,Y_p)\), with \(X_p,Y_p\in\{0,1\}^3\), define \(D=\sum_{p:X_p\le Y_p}\mu_p\). Let \(\{p,q\}\in\mathcal E\) exactly when \(p\ne q\), both pair types are incomparable in their own patient-donor relation, and \(X_p\le Y_q\) and \(X_q\le Y_p\). Choose exchange masses \(x_{pq}\ge0\) subject to \(\sum_{q:\{p,q\}\in\mathcal E}x_{pq}\le\mu_p\) for every \(p\). What is the maximum of \(D+2\sum_{\{p,q\}\in\mathcal E}x_{pq}\), when cohorts may be split among exchanges but each patient-donor pair receives one whole outcome?

The model it lives in

The types are complete patient-donor pairs \(p=(X_p,Y_p)\), with rational masses \(\mu_p\). Directly compatible mass \(D\) receives direct left-lobe transplants; each allowed reciprocal exchange uses equal mass from two types. The resulting fractional matching LP has \(O(\tau^2)\) exchange variables and is solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\).

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named NP-hardness, P, FPT, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its strongest computational anchors are algorithmic results: one optimizes the number of transplants, and the other constructs a mechanism with incentive and efficiency properties. The two-size, left-lobe-only result is my lead because its continuous question is especially direct.

Theorem 1 (proved here) says: “The two-size left-lobe-only sequential matching algorithm maximizes the number of exchanges and the number of transplants in any individually rational matching.” The mirror is a maximum-mass liver-exchange problem. A unit of population is a patient-donor pair. A type is \(p=(X_p,Y_p)\), where \(X_p,Y_p\in\{0,1\}^3\) are the patient’s and donor’s blood-and-size types in the paper’s two-size model. The input is a finite-support distribution \(\mu_p\) of binary-rational masses summing to one. Mass means the share of pairs of that type in a large exchange pool.

For every individually rational, reciprocal left-lobe exchange between distinct types \(p,q\), let \(x_{pq}\geq0\) be the mass of exchanges using one pair of each type. Such an edge is allowed when each type is incomparable in its own patient-donor relationship, and the donors can give left lobes to the other type’s patients: \(X_p\not\leq Y_p\), \(Y_p\not\leq X_p\), \(X_q\not\leq Y_q\), \(Y_q\not\leq X_q\), \(X_p\leq Y_q\), and \(X_q\leq Y_p\). Each exchange uses one unit of mass from each endpoint, so \(\sum_{q:\{p,q\}\in\mathcal E}x_{pq}\leq\mu_p\). Directly compatible pairs are assigned their direct left-lobe transplant, as individual rationality requires. The question is to choose feasible \(x\) maximizing \(D+2\sum_{\{p,q\}\in\mathcal E}x_{pq}\), where \(D=\sum_{p:X_p\leq Y_p}\mu_p\). A solution is a mass allocation to whole direct transplants and whole two-way exchanges; only the cohort is divisible.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a multi-center liver-exchange registry using standardized size categories: thousands of patient-donor pairs could occupy at most \(64\) physical pair types in the two-size model, so \(N\gg\tau\). That is a proposed scale-up scenario, not a claim that today’s liver-exchange pools have that size. The paper itself studies much smaller simulated pools. The setting is still recognizably theirs: pairs, ABO and size compatibility, and bilateral donor exchange remain the problem. A rational-mass fractional matching LP has \(O(\tau^2)\) exchange variables and is solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. I expect this mirror to be Class A. It makes the society continuous while keeping each patient’s outcome indivisible.

A second, more ambitious anchor is Theorem 3 (proved here): “The general left&right-lobe priority mechanism is individually rational, Pareto efficient, and incentive compatible.” Its mirror keeps the paper’s multiple sizes, right-lobe function \(\rho\), and private willingness to donate a right lobe. The input is a finite-support rational mass distribution over physical pair types \((X,Y)\), together with a continuum of pairs whose willingness is either \(w\) or \(u\). A direct mechanism takes their willingness reports and assigns each pair to a feasible direct transplant, a feasible two-way exchange with specified lobes, or no transplant. It may divide a type cohort among outcomes, but it must assign each individual one whole outcome. The required properties are individual rationality, Pareto efficiency against all feasible mass allocations, and dominant-strategy truthfulness for each individual report. A solution is a measurable allocation rule for every such population, with a way to compute its mass flows in terms of \(S,\tau\), and the rational input bit length \(L\).

I tentatively expect Class A here too: the paper’s precedence relation is acyclic, and the allocation subproblems are matching problems over finitely many types. But the theorem is not a printed polynomial-time complexity claim, and the continuum rule needs to preserve individual incentives, not just return feasible exchange totals. A natural further question is whether the paper’s Proposition 1 boundary survives: it says there is no incentive-compatible mechanism maximizing either total transplants or left-lobe transplants. Does that impossibility remain for divisible cohort masses, or can a continuous mechanism maximize transplanted mass while remaining truthful?

My weakest point is the gap between the proposed large, size-binned registry and the modest pools the paper actually discusses. Theorem 1 is also the paper’s simpler left-lobe-only result, rather than its central dual-technology mechanism. Theorem 3 is closer to the main contribution, but its continuous incentive-compatibility condition is delicate: with atomless agents, one agent’s report does not change aggregate mass, so the mirror must still specify how that agent’s assigned outcome depends on their report. I would cover Theorems 1 and 3, not claim that the simulation results or Proposition 1 already have settled continuous counterparts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about fit to actual liver-exchange pools. A pair is the unit of participation, and a faithful type must capture the patient’s demand and the donor’s supply. In a real registry, exact sizes and clinical compatibility details can make almost every pair distinct, so the support size \(\tau\) may grow with the pool rather than stay small. The paper describes liver exchange as operating on modest pools and simulates pools of 50, 100, and 250 pairs. A much larger registry with only a few repeated types is a possible scale-up scenario, but the paper does not establish that it is a realistic one. Adding clinical features to make the types complete would make that compression harder.

That objection is limited, though. The paper’s own two-size model deliberately reduces compatibility to a finite set of characteristics. Within that model, a large population distributed over repeated pair types is coherent: the pairs remain whole, while the mass of each cohort can be divided among whole direct transplants and exchanges. Theorem 1 therefore has a clean mirror. The type-level fractional matching LP is a different object from the finite matching problem, and calling it a generic LP or expecting it to be tractable does not make the question out of scope. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor.

Theorem 3 is more vulnerable to a formulation gap. Aggregate exchange totals alone do not define a truthful mechanism: the model must say what outcome an individual gets when they report willing rather than unwilling. In an atomless population, one person’s report does not change aggregate feasibility, while it may change that person’s assigned outcome. The paper’s finite matching algorithm does not by itself specify the corresponding individual assignment rule or its computational representation. But the better mirror can supply one—by defining report-contingent assignments for each type cohort and checking individual incentive constraints—so this is a specification burden, not a fundamental obstruction. It may be a new mechanism-design problem, but it is still plausibly one the authors would recognize.

Proposition 1’s impossibility raises the same issue rather than resolving it: a continuous version could ask whether a mechanism can maximize transplanted mass while satisfying individual truthfulness. Its answer is not a reason to reject the question, and the need to define continuum truthfulness does not rule out a type-based formulation.

So the negative case is strongest against the empirical relevance of a low-\(\tau\) registry, and against treating Theorem 3’s aggregate flows as a complete mechanism. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The two-size mirror of Theorem 1 survives the objections I can make; on the programme’s stated criteria, that is enough to reject the case against.
