The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of seat classes G, rational type proportions μ over a finite set T of strict ranking templates, and rational per-capita supplies q_g with ∑_g q_g > 1, consider every admissible population size N for which Nμ_t and Nq_g are integers. Each type template ranks the Nq_g indivisible seat copies by a fixed class priority and within-class copy order; one tagged student has a specified ranking and a fixed finite target set of seat copies. Under the paper’s all-reporting protocol, is there an N_0 such that for every admissible N ≥ N_0 the tagged student has a well-defined reporting strategy that secures every target seat for every lottery outcome, when the other students report truthfully?

The model it lives in

The background society is the distribution μ over ranking types, with per-capita seat supplies q_g scaling the indivisible goods as the population grows. A single tagged student remains an atomic query agent whose reporting strategy is evaluated against finite lottery outcomes in each admissible realization; success means securing the specified target seats in every outcome.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The one result I can anchor this case on is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It characterizes the manipulation problem for a single agent under the all-reporting policy: the other agents report truthfully, the manipulator knows their rankings, and success means getting every object in a target set \(S\), regardless of lottery outcomes. Starting with all objects available, form \(S_k^*\) from the target objects that some truthful agent prefers to every available non-target object; form \(O_k^*\) from the non-target objects that are someone’s best available non-target object; then remove \(S_k^*\cup O_k^*\) to obtain the next set of available objects. The theorem says a successful strategy exists exactly when, for every \(k\ge1\),
\[ k>\left|\bigcup_{t=1}^{k}S_t^*\right|. \]
When this condition holds, any well-defined strategy that starts by reporting the objects in \(S\), reports each \(o\in S\) before its first removal stage \(\operatorname{app}_S(o)\), and is completed to be well-defined succeeds. The theorem gives the characterization; Algorithm 1 and the accompanying text establish that a successful strategy can be found in polynomial time. This is a small but real computational anchor. The paper has no named hardness result: its conjecture about optimal Borda manipulation is not proved, so I would not use it as an anchor.

My proposed mirror is Tagged-Student High-Multiplicity Robust Manipulation under All-Reporting. Consider a large course allocation with a finite set \(G\) of course or seat classes. Each class has indivisible seat copies. A finite library \(T\) of ranking templates specifies strict rankings over the copies at every population size. The instance gives rational type proportions \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita seat supplies \(q_g\) for \(g\in G\), with \(\sum_g q_g>1\), a tagged student’s ranking, and a finite target set of designated seat copies \(S_N\). For each sufficiently large admissible \(N\), there are \(N\mu_t\) truthful students of type \(t\), \(Nq_g\) seats of class \(g\), and the one tagged student. Everyone reports their favourite remaining seat each round under the paper’s all-reporting protocol. The continuous question is: is there some \(N_0\) such that, for every admissible \(N\ge N_0\), the tagged student has a well-defined sequence of reports that secures every seat in \(S_N\) for every lottery outcome? A YES solution is a strategy for each such \(N\), together with a proof of the guarantee; NO means that successful strategies fail to exist for infinitely many admissible population sizes.

The regime is a large university allocation with many students but relatively few recurring preference templates, and seat supplies that grow with enrollment. The tagged student is an infinitesimal query agent, analogous to the single manipulator in the theorem; the remaining society is represented by \(\mu\). This keeps the strategic question recognizable: a student who knows the other rankings tries to secure a specified bundle against lottery uncertainty. Seats remain indivisible. Their supply is stated per capita because the number of seats must grow with the population for the allocation model to remain meaningful.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable, because Theorem 1’s test is structural and uses rankings and availability rather than enumerating preference profiles. A continuous analogue might test the corresponding type-level demand and supply conditions. That is a prediction, not a result: the theorem itself does not establish such a test for the scaled-seat model. Useful follow-up questions are whether eventual success can be decided in time polynomial in \(|G|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length; how equality at supply-demand boundaries affects the tagged student’s guarantee; and how the answer changes when success means a probability threshold rather than success under every lottery outcome. This mirror covers Theorem 1’s target-set manipulation result only, not the paper’s welfare comparisons or its unproved conjectures about optimal manipulation.

The weakest point is that the tagged student has zero mass in the limit, while the success guarantee can depend on a one-seat difference or on which individual loses a lottery. The proportions \(\mu\) alone cannot record those details. My formulation keeps them through the family of finite realizations and the “for every sufficiently large \(N\)” condition, which makes the question precise but leaves it open to the objection that the difficult part is still asymptotic discrete bookkeeping. The case survives that objection only as a narrow mirror: it preserves the paper’s individual strategy, indivisible seats, and worst-case lottery guarantee while asking whether the large, type-homogeneous population yields a cleaner structural test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the paper’s one solid computational anchor: a single named agent asks whether she can secure a specified set of indivisible objects against every lottery outcome, given the other agents’ exact rankings. The difficulty for a continuous mirror is that its guarantee is about that agent’s individual fate, while the proposed society records population proportions.

The theorem’s characterization makes this mismatch concrete. The sets \(S_k^*\) and \(O_k^*\) are formed by unions and existence tests over truthful agents. Duplicating an agent with a given ranking changes neither set, so the success condition is unchanged by replacing one such agent with many. For a fixed finite type set and fixed \(\mu\), every type with \(\mu_t>0\) is eventually present, and the theorem’s robust guarantee distinguishes its presence from its absence—not a small mass from a large one. If the tagged student is retained, she remains an individual outside the distribution; if she is absorbed into it, her zero limiting mass cannot record her target bundle or strategy.

The lottery makes that gap persist even under the most charitable version of the course-allocation story. For the tagged student, one truthful claimant to a target seat is enough for an adverse lottery outcome to deny it. A continuum lottery over atomless agents does not represent the finite lottery in which one named student wins an indivisible seat. Retaining finite lotteries preserves the tagged student’s question, but then the eventual-\(N\) formulation is a family of discrete instances, and the background masses do not govern the robust guarantee. Treating lotteries as mass shares or asking about expected group allocation would make proportions meaningful, but would change the result’s objective.

There is a plausible alternative: let a positive-mass cohort coordinate its reports and ask whether it can secure a positive share of some seats. That could be a sensible continuous strategic-allocation problem. But it changes the paper’s single manipulator and her guarantee over all lottery outcomes into a coalition and an aggregate objective. I would not count it as a mirror of Theorem 1.

So the negative case is that this theorem’s computation concerns a tagged individual facing exact indivisible lotteries, not the quantitative composition of a continuous society. A tagged-agent query over a continuum background is still conceivable, and course allocation is a credible setting for it. That is the weak point: I cannot rule out authors recognizing that hybrid as worthwhile. But preserving the theorem’s central guarantee seems to leave population mass without a meaningful role; making mass central changes the question.
