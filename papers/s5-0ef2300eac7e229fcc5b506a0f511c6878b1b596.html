What the mirror covers

The nearest analogue would extend the decision rule associated with Theorem 2 to population types; Theorems 1 and 2 themselves are axiomatic characterizations, and the paper gives no computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is a continuous-population version of the paper’s already assessed social welfare rule. For a public decision, let each type \(t\) specify agents’ von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities and beliefs, let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s population share, and let \(k_t\) be its assessed social weight. Given the types’ expected utilities \(v_t(a)\) for each alternative \(a\), ask for an alternative maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t k_t v_t(a). \]
A plausible regime is a large population with relatively few shared utility-and-belief types. This is recognizable as choosing for a high-multiplicity group; with an explicit finite support, the calculation takes \(O(\tau |A|)\) arithmetic operations. Theorem 2 makes the weighted-sum form a natural extension to a continuum.

But that is not a qualifying anchor under your criteria. The paper has no named computational result: Theorem 1 characterizes the certain-alternative group utility functions consistent with B1–B5 as those with nonnegative partial derivatives and at least two partial derivatives not identically zero. Theorem 2 characterizes the uncertain-alternative functions consistent with B1–B5 as \(u_G=\sum_i k_i u_i\), where \(k_i\ge 0\) and at least two weights are positive; its proof invokes Harsanyi’s additivity result. Neither theorem classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard.

So the proposed maximization problem is a plausible extension, but its easy computation is immediate from the stated input and does not mirror a computational result of this paper. The paper’s contribution is axiomatic characterization and interpersonal weight assessment. That is the weak point—and, under the required anchor standard, it prevents an honest paper-anchored positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes certain-alternative group utility functions satisfying B1–B5; Theorem 2 characterizes the uncertain-alternative functions as \(u_G=\sum_i k_i u_i\). Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a hardness result. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, that leaves no paper-anchored computational claim to mirror.

The best repair is the proponent’s population model: let types encode expected utilities and social weights, assign each type mass \(\mu_t\), and choose an alternative maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t k_t v_t(a)\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, and type-level weights are legitimate. But it asks how to compute a choice under the rule, not for a continuous version of either theorem. Asking which welfare functionals satisfy population analogues of B1–B5 would stay closer to the theorems, but it would be an axiomatic continuization, outside the programme’s scope. Making the alternatives implicitly combinatorial could create a computational problem, but the difficulty would come from an added representation of alternatives that this paper does not study.

This is a strong objection to a *paper-anchored* mirror, not proof that no useful related problem could be posed. The introduction does present selecting the highest-utility alternative as the decision purpose, so the authors might recognize that operational question as theirs. The negative case is weakest there: a continuous population model could make the rule useful in an application, even though it would not continue a computational result of this paper.
