What the mirror covers

No eligible named computational result was anchored; Section 6.3 reports empirical axiom-guided synthesis, while the other experiments are empirical evaluations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive intuition is a high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s axiom-guided rule synthesis: a large online consultation with a fixed slate of alternatives and many repeated rankings. A type would be a ranking \(t\in S_m\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of participants who submit it. The rule would take the mass vector \(\mu\) and return a nonempty set of winners. The authors might recognize axiom-guided synthesis over these population distributions as a natural extension of Section 6.3.

But the paper has no qualifying named computational result. Its numbered experiments and tables report empirical neural-network performance; they do not establish an algorithm, runtime, or complexity classification. The universal-approximation claim in Section 7 cites earlier results and is not a computational result of this paper. So there are no eligible anchors, and I can’t honestly give a continuous problem per anchor or predict whether one would be tractable, inherit hardness, or be continuum-specifically hard.

The mass-vector version is a plausible follow-on, but it would need a fresh computational formulation. The weakness is decisive for this assignment: the paper studies axiomatic and empirical behavior, not the complexity landscape of a voting problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper gives no named computational result to continuize. Its three research questions and Section 6.3’s synthesis findings are empirical: they report how trained networks perform on sampled finite profiles. The paper establishes no algorithm, runtime bound, or complexity classification. The universal approximation results in Section 7 are cited background, not computational theorems proved here. So there is no result-level anchor for a ChoCo mirror.

The proposed population model is nevertheless sensible. For fixed \(m\), a large consultation can be represented by \(\mu\in\Delta(S_m)\), with a rule mapping \(\mu\) to a nonempty set of winners. The axioms have plausible mass versions: anonymity follows from using the distribution, while neutrality, Pareto, Condorcet, and independence can be stated for mass profiles. That makes axiom-guided synthesis over such profiles a reasonable follow-up to Section 6.3.

It still would be a new learning problem, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper. The paper trains networks on sampled profiles to produce a rule; it does not compute an answer for a supplied society. Extending the input domain to mass profiles leaves the computational task to be specified: for example, whether to optimize over a chosen network class, which distribution over mass profiles to use, and how to measure exact axiom satisfaction. Any complexity result would depend on those new choices.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit to this programme’s result-anchored computational agenda. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous follow-up exists: the mass-profile extension is natural, and could support a separate project on learning rules over a simplex.
