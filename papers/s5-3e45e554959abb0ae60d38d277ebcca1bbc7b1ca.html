The continuous mirror question

For each \(q\), take \(q\) agent types of mass \(1/q\), each with an independently uniform strict ranking of the \(q\) partner types. Consider nonnegative couplings \(\pi_{ab}\) with every row and column sum \(1/q\), stable in the sense that for positive-mass arcs \((a,b)\) and \((d,c)\), \(c \succ_a b\) implies \(d \succ_c a\). Let \(R_q\) be the minimum of \(\sum_{a,b}\pi_{ab}r_a(b)\) over stable couplings, and set \(R_q=+\infty\) if none exists. Determine the high-probability asymptotic behavior of \(R_q\), including whether \(R_q/\sqrt q\) converges in probability to \(1/2\), or find the correct scale and constant.

The model it lives in

A continuous roommate society with \(q\) peer types, mass \(1/q\) per type, and independent uniform rankings over partner types. A stable mass coupling assigns each type’s outgoing and incoming mass; the objective is its total mass-weighted successor rank.

The objection that survived

The paper bounds ranks for proposal-selected \(\Pi_0\)-like permutations, whereas the proposed question minimizes rank over all stable couplings; those optima need not correspond.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers random successor-rank behavior for stable type-level assignments; it leaves aside the exact individual optimality guarantee of Theorem 1.1 and the paper’s other random counts, cycle, and fixed-pair results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do stable couplings exist with probability \(1-o(1)\) in the independent uniform type-ranking model?
  • What is the asymptotic scale and leading constant of the minimum mass-weighted successor rank?

The case FOR (proponent)

My positive case is narrow: the paper’s continuizable object is its one-sided stable-partner problem, especially the quality of the partnership selected by the proposal algorithm. The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result. Its strongest computational anchor is instead an average-case bound on that algorithm’s work.

Theorem 1.1, proved here, says the terminal directed partnerships form a permutation satisfying the paper’s stability condition: if \(i\) prefers \(\Pi(j)\) to \(\Pi(i)\), then \(\Pi(j)\) prefers \(j\) to \(i\). It also proves that stability of \(\Pi\) implies stability of \(\Pi^{-1}\); each agent’s terminal successor is their best successor in any stable permutation, and their terminal predecessor is their worst stable predecessor. Every execution yields the same \(\Pi_0\), with each agent preferring \(\Pi_0(i)\) to \(\Pi_0^{-1}(i)\). Every pair matched in \(\Pi_0\) remains matched in all stable permutations, and either all stable permutations are fixed-point-free or they share a single fixed point.

That suggests the Continuous Stable-Permutation Coupling problem. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of \(\tau\) agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of partner types for each \(t\), and a rational threshold \(B\). Type includes all attributes the matching problem uses; people of one type have identical rankings. A solution is a nonnegative coupling \(\pi_{tu}\), where \(\pi_{tu}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents whose successor is type \(u\), with

\[ \sum_u \pi_{tu}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t \pi_{tu}=\mu_u. \]

Require stability as follows: for every two positive-mass arcs \((a,b)\) and \((d,c)\), if \(c\succ_a b\), then \(d\succ_c a\). This is the type-level version of the paper’s condition, applied to an agent of type \(a\) whose successor has type \(b\), and another agent of type \(d\) whose successor has type \(c\). Let \(r_a(b)\) be the rank of \(b\) for type \(a\). The question is whether a stable coupling exists with \(\sum_{a,b}\pi_{ab}r_a(b)\le B\); the output is such a coupling or a proof that none exists. The rank objective is a natural mass-level counterpart of the paper’s best-successor result. It also permits fractional type-level assignments, as a continuous mirror should.

Theorem 4.8, also proved here, is the strongest anchor for computational behavior. Under independent uniform rankings of the \(n\) agents, with probability \(1-o(1)\), every \(\Pi_0\)-like stable permutation satisfies

\[ \frac{n^{3/2}}{2}\left(1-2.2n^{-1/2}\log n\right) \le R_s(\Pi)\le R_p(\Pi)\le \frac{n^{3/2}}{2}\left(1+2.2n^{-1/2}\log n\right). \]

Here \(R_s\) and \(R_p\) are the total ranks of successors and predecessors, and \(\Pi_0\)-like means stable, fixed-point-free, and preferred by each agent to its inverse. In particular, the theorem proves that the proposal algorithm uses asymptotically \(0.5n^{3/2}\) steps with high probability.

Its mirror is the Random Cohort Stable-Rank Law. Take \(q\) types with equal mass \(1/q\), and draw each type’s strict ranking over the \(q\) possible partner types independently and uniformly. Among stable couplings, let \(\pi_q^*\) minimize \(\sum_{a,b}\pi_{ab}r_a(b)\). Determine the high-probability asymptotic of \(R_q=\sum_{a,b}\pi^*_{ab}r_a(b)\) as \(q\) grows. The source theorem motivates testing whether \(R_q/\sqrt q\) converges in probability to \(1/2\); the problem should establish that law or determine the correct scale and constant.

The natural regime is a large population of peers drawn from a small set of recurring profiles—for example, residents seeking housemates, where people with the same needs and schedule share the same ranking of partner profiles. Population size \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\), so each type has many agents. The mass coupling records fractional assignments between types, while every individual still receives one partner. For the first problem I expect at least fixed-parameter tractability in \(\tau\): enumerate possible supports, check their stability conditions, and solve a linear program for each valid support. Whether the problem admits an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) remains open. For the random-rank problem, a mean-field or probabilistic analysis seems plausible, but the proposed \(1/2\) law is a conjectural target, not a consequence of Theorem 4.8.

The weakest point is the random model. Pittel’s theorem samples independent rankings over named individuals; the cohort mirror gives many agents identical rankings over types. That changes the dependence structure and the rank scale, so the \(\tfrac12\) constant cannot simply be carried over. The case survives as a mirror of the paper’s stable-partnership and successor-rank questions, not as a claim that its random-instance theorem already applies to high-multiplicity cohorts. This scope covers Theorem 1.1’s selected partnership and Theorem 4.8’s rank behavior; it does not stretch to the paper’s other random counts or asymptotic claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed type coupling is the strongest version of a possible mirror: cohorts of roommates with shared rankings over partner profiles are a sensible high-multiplicity scenario. That makes it a plausible *new* matching model. The problem is that it does not preserve the result it is meant to mirror.

Theorem 1.1 identifies the proposal algorithm’s output: each individual gets their best successor among all stable permutations. In the paper, “individual” means someone with a strict ranking of named partners. Keep those rankings and their distinctions, and repeated agents generally are not the same type. Collapse partners into profiles to get positive-mass types, and agents become indifferent among members of a profile, or need extra tie-breaking information that distinguishes them again. A mass coupling can then assign different fractions of one type to different successor types. Minimizing aggregate rank, as the proposed threshold problem does, is a coherent objective, but it replaces individual proposer-optimality with a new social optimization criterion. Requiring one successor per type would preserve a sharper analogue only when the type masses happen to balance along the resulting cycles. Neither choice is a continuous restatement of Theorem 1.1’s guarantee.

Theorem 4.8 is a named computational result, so the “no computational result” objection does not apply literally. But its \(0.5n^{3/2}\) bound concerns the number of sequential proposals among \(n\) individually ranked agents under independent uniform rankings of those agents. The natural cohort model changes both ingredients. Rankings shared across a cohort are dependent at the individual level, and ranks over partner profiles are not ranks over named partners. One can define a better quality analogue using each type’s mass of more-preferred partner profiles and integrate that quantity over a stable coupling. One can also choose random rankings over types and ask for concentration. Those are well-posed questions, but the paper supplies no canonical random type model or continuum proposal process that makes either question its analogue. In an atomless model a single proposal has zero mass; batching proposals by type, or discretizing back to a population of size \(N\), introduces an algorithm and a work measure that the paper’s theorem does not determine.

So I would not claim that roommate matching can never have a worthwhile continuous model. High-multiplicity roommate cohorts are plausible, and studying their stable mass assignments could be valuable. The negative case is narrower: neither cited theorem gives this paper a strong ChoCo mirror. The first yields a new type-level allocation problem only after its individual optimality guarantee changes; the second yields a new probabilistic model without a matching continuous notion of proposal work. The universal claim that no such scenario could ever be worthwhile is the weak point—I cannot honestly establish it from this paper.
