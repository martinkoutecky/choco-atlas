The continuous mirror question

Given finite firms \(F\), finite worker types \(T=S\cup U\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), type rankings over \(F\cup\{\varnothing\}\), and explicitly represented rational piecewise-affine mass-choice maps \(C_f:\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) satisfying \(C_f(x)\le x\) and the mass analogue of USC, does a stable allocation always exist, and can one be computed exactly in time polynomial in the input length? An allocation \(y\) splits each type’s mass among firms and the outside option, preserves \(\sum_f y_{t,f}=\mu_t\), and is stable when assignments are individually rational and \(C_f(a_f(y))=y_{\cdot,f}\) for every firm, where \(a_f(y)\) is the mass of types that weakly prefer \(f\) to their assignment.

The model it lives in

A continuum of workers represented by rational masses over finitely many complete types, with finite firms and explicit rational piecewise-affine mass-choice maps. The decision variables \(y_{t,f}\) split each type’s mass across firms and the outside option; the task is to find an individually rational allocation that each firm chooses from the mass of workers willing to accept it.

The objection that survived

The proponent acknowledges that arbitrary rankings over worker sets do not induce mass-choice maps, but leaves the maps’ encoding and exact evaluation model unspecified.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is USC-Stable Mass Matching, anchored on Theorem 1. The paper proves Theorem 1 itself: when each firm’s preference satisfies the unidirectional substitutes and complements (USC) condition, the two-stage worker-proposing Deferred Acceptance mechanism produces a stable matching. Here USC means workers are divided into \(S\) and \(U\); there are no complements within either group, and a worker in \(U\) is neither a substitute nor a complement to a worker in \(S\). This is a constructive algorithmic result, though the paper gives no complexity-class statement or running-time bound. Theorem 2, proved in the paper, shows that its controlled school-choice preferences satisfy USC; that supports a setting for the mirror, but it is not a separate complexity anchor. Theorem 3 concerns continuous monetary transfers while workers remain discrete, so it is not the population-continuum result I am proposing.

In the mirror, an instance has a finite set \(F\) of firms and a finite support \(T=S\cup U\) of worker types. A type records a worker’s complete ranking of firms and every attribute that affects any firm’s choice. Two workers of the same type are indistinguishable for the purposes of the model. The input gives each type’s rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Each firm has a finitely encoded, rational mass-choice rule \(C_f\): given available mass \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T}\), it chooses a vector \(C_f(x)\le x\). Require the mass analogue of USC: adding mass of another type in the same group cannot increase the chosen amount of a given type, and the firm’s chosen amounts of \(S\)-types do not depend on available \(U\)-mass. The choice rules are part of the input because the paper’s rankings over discrete bundles do not, by themselves, specify choices from fractional bundles.

The decision variable is \(y_{t,f}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to firm \(f\), including the outside option \(\varnothing\), with \(\sum_{f\in F\cup\{\varnothing\}} y_{t,f}=\mu_t\). The task is to find a stable allocation, not to maximize welfare. For each firm \(f\), let \(A_f(y)_t\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(f\) or to an option that type \(t\) likes less than \(f\). Stability requires individual rationality and \(C_f(A_f(y))=y_{\cdot,f}\) for every firm: from all workers who would accept \(f\) over their current assignment, the firm chooses exactly the mass it currently employs. This is the choice-based version of the paper’s no-blocking-coalition condition. The output may split a type across firms; it describes shares of a large population, while each person still receives one firm.

The regime is a large hiring market with many workers but relatively few skill, preference, and other relevant types—for example, a large cohort of skilled and unskilled workers applying to a modest set of employers whose decisions depend on those categories. The paper’s own school-choice application also suggests a setting where many students fall into a small number of district, preference, and priority classes. In either case, the population size \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau=|T|\). Firm capacities and related limits should scale per capita, as the mass model requires. This preserves the paper’s question: which stable matching results follow when firms have the one-directional choice effects captured by USC?

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The natural conjecture is that two-stage DA carries over to mass proposals, with exact operations on the finitely represented choice rules. The continuous question is: given \((F,T,\mu)\), type rankings, and rational mass-choice rules satisfying USC, can we return an exact stable \(y\) efficiently in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length? I would ask a prover to establish existence and give the algorithm and its bit-complexity analysis. Questions that follow include whether the result holds for the paper’s school-choice constraints under a clear per-capita scaling, and whether a continuous allocation can be rounded to a discrete large-market assignment while preserving stability or a stated approximation to it.

The weak point is real: Theorem 1 defines USC through adding or removing individual workers, while the paper allows arbitrary strict rankings over discrete worker sets. There is no canonical way to extend every such ranking to mass bundles. My mirror therefore applies only where an anonymous, finitely encoded mass-choice rule is a sensible description of firms’ decisions; it does not claim to continuize every instance covered by Theorem 1. Nor does the finite theorem prove that mass DA terminates efficiently or that its exact arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity. Those are the questions the mirror asks. I still think the authors would recognize it as their stable-matching problem: it keeps firms, worker preferences, stability, and the USC structure, and changes the population to a mass distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious computational anchor is Theorem 1. It is constructive, so I would not dismiss it as a purely axiomatic result. But it gives a mechanism over individually named workers, not a complexity claim with a specified input encoding or running-time bound. Each firm has an arbitrary strict ranking over \(2^W\), and the mechanism repeatedly queries choices from individual sets. With those preferences given explicitly, the description can itself be exponential in \(n\); with choices treated as an oracle, the cost of a query is unspecified. The theorem therefore supplies no compact computational problem for ChoCo to continuize.

The proponent’s mass model supplies that missing problem by adding new primitives: fractional choice rules \(C_f\), a mass version of USC, and a fixed-point definition of stability. That is a plausible model, but it is not determined by Theorem 1. In the paper, stability depends on firms’ strict rankings of worker sets, which can distinguish workers with the same school preferences or skill label. A type-complete grouping must preserve those distinctions, so exact types can grow with the population. To keep \(\tau\) small, one must make workers within a type interchangeable and specify how firms rank fractional bundles. The paper’s ordinal rankings do not specify that extension, and USC alone does not determine the rules’ representation or evaluation cost.

The best proposed case is controlled school choice: scale capacities and proportional ceilings, and group students into a small number of preference and priority classes. That may be a worthwhile model, but it changes the paper’s strict individual priorities into class-level ties or requires a separate distributional representation for priority scores. Theorem 2 establishes USC for the paper’s finite choice rules; it does not show that their mass extensions inherit USC or admit an efficient algorithm. Existing continuum admissions work has DA-style stable-assignment methods for other preference and priority structures, but does not settle this USC case ([Kapur](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.01340), [Arnosti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)). Theorem 3 is about continuous monetary transfers with discrete workers, so it adds no population-continuum anchor.

That is the strongest honest case against: Theorem 1’s computational content is tied to individual workers, while the proposed mass problem needs a new preference domain and an explicit representation before its complexity can be assessed. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. If the authors accept anonymous worker classes and a concise, rationalizable mass-choice rule—especially for the school-choice application—the mirror could survive. The negative case is therefore weak precisely at the universal claim: it exposes missing foundations in this proposal, but does not defeat every plausible USC mass-matching model.
