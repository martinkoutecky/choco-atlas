The continuous mirror question

Given \(g\) divisible goods, a finite type set \(T\) of size \(\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-agent budgets \(w_t>0\), rational per-capita supplies \(c_j>0\), and \(2\)-linear, \(81\)-bounded, at-most-\(43\)-sparse additively separable PLC utilities \(u_t(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{g}r_{t,j}(a_j)\) satisfying the Maxfield condition for every positive-mass type, find a nonzero price vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}_+^g\) and representative bundles \(a_t\) such that \(a_t\in\arg\max\{u_t(a):a\ge 0,\ p\cdot a\le w_t\}\) and \(\left|\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_{t,j}-c_j\right|\le g^{-21}c_j\) for every good \(j\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity or atomless Fisher market in which types \(t\) carry masses \(\mu_t\), each mass unit has budget \(w_t\) and utility \(u_t\), and per-capita supplies are \(c_j\); prices \(p\) and type-level demands \(a_t\) are the decision variables, with the search objective of finding an approximate equilibrium rather than maximizing welfare.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity Fisher equilibrium problem. I would use only the paper’s main result as an anchor.

The paper’s Theorem 1 (Main), proved in this paper, states that Fisher is PPAD-complete. In the paper’s notation, an instance is a Fisher market with \(n\) divisible goods and buyers having positive budgets and additively separable, piecewise-linear concave utilities. The market is restricted to be 2-linear, 81-bounded, 43-sparse, and to satisfy the Maxfield existence condition: every buyer has at least one good whose utility component is strictly monotone. The task is to find an \(n^{-21}\)-approximate market equilibrium: a nonzero price vector \(p\), together with an allocation in which every buyer receives a utility-maximising bundle subject to her budget, and, for every good \(j\),

\[ \left|\sum_i a_{i,j}-c_j\right|\le n^{-21}c_j. \]

The theorem says that this search problem is PPAD-complete. The paper proves the hardness by reducing Sparse Bimatrix, itself known to be PPAD-complete, through price-regulating markets. The resulting prices encode mixed strategies and force the Nash constraints through buyers’ optimal purchasing behaviour.

My continuous problem would be called \(\mathrm{Fisher}_\infty\)-Approximate Equilibrium.

An instance consists of \(g\) divisible goods and a finite set \(T\) of buyer types, with \(|T|=\tau\). Type \(t\) has rational population mass \(\mu_t\ge0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a rational per-agent budget \(w_t>0\), and an additively separable PLC utility

\[ u_t(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{g}r_{t,j}(a_j). \]

The utility functions satisfy exactly the restrictions in Theorem 1: two segments, 81-boundedness, at most 43 goods of interest per type, and the Maxfield condition for every positive-mass type. Good \(j\) has per-capita supply \(c_j>0\). Thus a finite population of size \(N\) would have total supply \(Nc_j\); the continuum formulation records only supply per unit population.

The question is to find a nonzero price vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}_+^g\) and one representative bundle \(a_t\) for every positive-mass type such that

\[ a_t\in \arg\max\{u_t(a):a\ge0,\ p\cdot a\le w_t\} \]

and

\[ \left|\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_{t,j}-c_j\right| \le g^{-21}c_j \]

for every good \(j\). Allowing agents of the same type to receive different optimal bundles would not change the formulation: because the utilities are concave, the mass-average of optimal bundles is itself optimal.

This is a genuine population continuization. The agents are an atomless population, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction with complete profile \((w_t,u_t)\). The action variables are type-level demand bundles and market prices; the objective is equilibrium, not welfare maximisation. Goods remain divisible exactly as in the paper, while the buyer population—not the outcome space—is made continuous.

A plausible regime is a large platform or regional resource market with millions of recurring customers or firms, but only a moderate number of purchasing profiles. A type might specify a budget and piecewise-linear marginal values for electricity, compute capacity, bandwidth, or other divisible services. Many customers can share exactly the same budget and utility profile, so \(N\gg\tau\) is natural. The paper’s gadget buyers can also be interpreted as recurring cohorts: replicate every buyer profile \(K\) times, giving population \(N=Kb\) while leaving the number of distinct profiles \(\tau\) unchanged.

The hardness direction should transfer. Given a finite Fisher instance with \(b\) buyers, make each buyer profile a type of mass \(1/b\), and set the continuous per-capita supply to \(c_j/b\). Then

\[ \sum_t\mu_ta_{t,j} =\frac{1}{b}\sum_i a_{i,j}, \]

so the continuous market-clearing inequalities are exactly the finite inequalities after rescaling. Duplicate buyers with identical complete profiles can be merged into one type. Conversely, a rational type distribution can be denominator-cleared into a finite replicated market. Thus the mirror is naturally a Class B problem: I expect PPAD-hardness to transfer, and likely PPAD-completeness under an appropriate weighted-market membership proof.

The mirror covers precisely the computational content of Theorem 1, not the paper’s internal price-regulation lemmas as separate anchors. It also generates useful questions: does the problem become tractable for fixed \(g\) or fixed \(\tau\)? Does PPAD-hardness persist when every type has very large multiplicity? Can one obtain approximation schemes when the type support is small? And what changes if the type distribution is given succinctly or by an oracle rather than explicitly?

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s reduction uses many auxiliary buyer profiles, so its hardness does not show difficulty with a fixed small number of types. Moreover, since Fisher markets already have divisible goods, a sceptic may view this as a weighted or compressed Fisher market rather than a dramatic new continuum phenomenon. I would concede that. The positive claim is narrower: the paper’s finite-buyer equilibrium problem has a precise, economically plausible continuous-population analogue, and its PPAD hardness survives that change. It is a credible Class B mirror, though not evidence that continuization makes this particular problem easier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent has not actually exposed a population-level computational phenomenon. Theorem 1 survives their formal translation, but that translation is almost exactly a weighted restatement of Fisher’s model.

In the paper’s reduction, buyers are gadget components whose complete utility profiles encode the bimatrix game. The auxiliary buyers \(T_u\) and \(T_v\) depend on particular pairs of strategies and particular payoff differences. In hard instances, these profiles can be essentially all distinct, with \(\tau=\Theta(n^2)\). Thus the reduction does not use population multiplicity at all. Replicating every buyer \(K\) times merely produces \(K\) copies of the same reduction and leaves the normalized society unchanged. It demonstrates scale invariance, not a computational role for a continuum of agents.

The proposed continuous market is also exactly reducible to an ordinary finite weighted Fisher market. For a type \(t\) of mass \(\mu_t>0\), define an aggregate buyer with budget

\[ \widetilde w_t=\mu_t w_t \]

and utility

\[ \widetilde u_t(z)=\mu_t u_t(z/\mu_t). \]

An aggregate bundle \(z\) is optimal for this buyer precisely when \(z/\mu_t\) is optimal for the original type. Market clearing is unchanged because \(z_t=\mu_t a_t\). For PLC utilities, this transformation only rescales the breakpoints. Hence the entire “continuum” can be collapsed exactly to \(\tau\) weighted buyers, with no limiting argument and no new equilibrium object.

That is not an objection that the answer is unchanged; it is an objection that the proposed mirror has no distinct population semantics beyond input compression. A genuinely compressed regime with \(\tau\ll N\) is not what drives the PPAD reduction. If one insists on a fixed small catalogue of buyer types, the paper’s theorem supplies no hardness anchor for that regime. If one instead allows \(\tau\) to grow with the game, the continuous instance retains essentially all of the paper’s buyer-specific information and the population has become a bookkeeping device.

A richer proposal—a distribution over a genuinely continuous space of budgets, slopes, and breakpoints—does not repair this. With finite support it collapses to the weighted formulation above. With infinite support, the paper provides no representation of the distribution or method for evaluating aggregate demand; one must introduce an oracle, a density class, or an integration model. That creates a new market-computation problem, but it is no longer a continuization of Theorem 1 in any precise sense.

The case against is therefore not that recurring cohorts are impossible. Standardized customers or firms can make high multiplicity economically plausible, and buyer identity is not intrinsically needed in Fisher equilibrium. The objection is narrower: this paper gives no reason to study such a continuum. Its main theorem concerns the combinatorics of a collection of distinct utility profiles, while the proposed population model either exactly aggregates those profiles or moves to a different, newly specified distributional problem.

This negative case is honestly weak under ChoCo’s stated rules. The high-multiplicity Fisher problem is well posed, and the exact reduction above may itself be a legitimate Class B mirror. Since existing high-multiplicity structure is supporting evidence rather than a collision, I cannot truthfully claim that Theorem 1 has no worthwhile continuous analogue. The best defensible conclusion is only that the paper does not provide strong programme-specific motivation for one; the proponent’s anchor probably survives.
