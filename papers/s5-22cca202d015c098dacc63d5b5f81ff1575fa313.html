The continuous mirror question

Given finite supports of advisor, student, and co-advisor types, rational masses \(\alpha_a,\sigma_s,\chi_c\), and complete type-level preferences and acceptability matching the paper’s assumptions, determine whether a stable mass allocation exists and, if so, compute one. An allocation is \(x_{asc}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{s,c}x_{asc}\leq\alpha_a\), \(\sum_{a,c}x_{asc}\leq\sigma_s\), and \(\sum_{a,s}x_{asc}\leq\chi_c\); unmatched mass is allowed. Stability rules out any positive mass reassignment to a mutually acceptable triple for which the selected advisor and co-advisor mass and student mass satisfy the paper’s blocking condition: for an unmatched student slice both two-sided pairs block, while for a matched slice either pair blocks.

The model it lives in

A finite-type, nonatomic three-sided market with rational mass for each role and variables \(x_{asc}\) assigning mass to individually rational triples. Types have the same complete preferences and acceptability information; type-level assignments may split mass across triples, while individual agents receive one complete triple or remain unmatched. There is no optimization objective: the task is to find a feasible allocation with no positive-mass blocking triple, or report that none exists.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s core three-sided market with consistent student preferences and no incompatible advisor pairs. It leaves the quota and \(n\)-sided extensions, the incompatible-pairs discussion, and the empirical comparison aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a stable mass allocation always exist under Theorem 5’s preference assumptions?
  • Can one be computed in time polynomial in the support size and binary input length, with polynomial-size rational output?
  • How do stable mass allocations relate to stable matchings in finite replications of the same type profile?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that the PhD algorithm returns a stable matching for the paper’s three-sided market: students rank advisors ahead of co-advisors, advisors and co-advisors rank students, and preferences may be incomplete. Every student must receive both an advisor and a co-advisor or remain unmatched. The algorithm repeatedly runs Gale–Shapley on the advisor–student market and then on the matched students–co-advisor market, removing students who found an advisor but no co-advisor. The paper’s running-time analysis gives a polynomial implementation, with total time \(O((|A|+|C|)|S|)\) up to logarithmic factors. The paper also cites hardness results for other matching models and conjectures hardness when advisor pairs are incompatible, but neither is a proved hardness anchor for this theorem’s problem.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large, centralized research-placement clearinghouse: many applicants and available mentor slots share standardized research-track profiles and use the same ranking rubrics. For example, a consortium might process a very large annual cohort using a few hundred recurring applicant and mentor preference profiles. The regime depends on those profiles actually being shared; arbitrary personal rankings would make the number of types large. The paper’s discussion of ties helps: people within a type can be indifferent among members of the same counterpart type, while ranking counterpart types strictly. Its strictification result, Proposition 12, supports this kind of type-level indifference.

My proposed mirror is Three-Sided PhD Stable Mass Matching. An instance gives finite supports of advisor types \(A\), student types \(S\), and co-advisor types \(C\), each with a nonnegative rational mass. The masses may be normalized across the whole population; the choice of scale does not matter. Each type has its complete preferences and acceptability information over the other types, with student preferences consistent in the paper’s sense. Let \(x_{asc}\geq 0\) be the mass assigned to triple \((a,s,c)\). Feasibility requires

\[ \sum_{s,c}x_{asc}\leq \alpha_a,\qquad \sum_{a,c}x_{asc}\leq \sigma_s,\qquad \sum_{a,s}x_{asc}\leq \chi_c, \]

where \(\alpha_a,\sigma_s,\chi_c\) are the input masses. Triples used in positive mass must be individually rational; the leftover mass on each side stays unmatched. The task is to output a feasible allocation \(x\) that is stable.

For stability, lift the paper’s blocking-triple definition to positive-mass slices of types’ current assignments. A triple blocks if some \(\varepsilon>0\) mass of each of its three types can be reassigned to that triple, with all participants mutually acceptable, and the selected student mass satisfies the paper’s condition: if unmatched, both the advisor–student and student–co-advisor pairs block their current two-sided assignments; if matched, either pair blocks. The two-sided blocks require both members of the relevant pair to prefer each other to their current partner. The matching is stable exactly when no such positive-mass reassignment exists. Although every individual still receives one complete triple or stays single, type-level assignments may split mass across several triples.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The direct route is a mass-based version of the paper’s two Gale–Shapley stages, followed by its iterative removal of the student mass that receives an advisor but no co-advisor. The meaningful complexity question is whether those stages and removals can be implemented in time polynomial in the number of supported types and the binary input length \(L\), rather than in the represented population size. That would make the paper’s stable-matching construction a high-multiplicity algorithm, not merely a continuous restatement.

The mirror covers the core, no-incompatible-pairs result of Theorem 5. It does not claim to cover the paper’s quota or \(n\)-sided extensions. The central further questions are whether an exact algorithm is polynomial in support size and \(L\), whether its rational output has polynomial encoding length, and how its stable mass allocations relate to stable matchings in finite replications of the same type profile.

The weakest point is that the continuum changes the algorithm’s bookkeeping: a student type can be partly matched and partly unmatched, so the discrete operation “remove the students who failed to get a co-advisor” becomes a mass update. Also, the mirror is persuasive only where complete preferences really repeat. If each person has distinctive rankings over named individuals, grouping them into a few types changes the problem rather than continuizing it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a real computational anchor: it proves that the PhD algorithm returns a stable matching, and the paper gives a polynomial running time. So “there is no named computational result” does not defeat this mirror.

The strongest case against it is about how much the high multiplicity model preserves. A type has to include a student’s full rankings and acceptability over advisor and co-advisor types, and an advisor’s ranking over student types. In the paper’s PhD setting, those rankings can depend on particular research fit and personal judgments. The paper’s own synthetic preferences include individual random jitter, which tends to make profiles distinct. Grouping people by broad field or credential would then omit information that stability uses. The obvious large supply-chain version has a related problem: mass often represents throughput or capacity, rather than a population of interchangeable agents.

The best version of the mirror handles those issues by using separate masses for the three roles, type-level rankings, and a fractional allocation over triples. Stability would rule out any positive mass that can form a blocking pair or triple under the paper’s definition. Students could split across assignments at the type level while every individual still receives a complete triple or stays unmatched. That is a coherent high multiplicity question, and a centralized placement market with recurring preference profiles is plausible. I cannot dismiss that scenario as a distortion: if the profiles really capture everything the matching uses, the types satisfy the programme’s definition.

My remaining concern is the likely research payoff. With a fixed support of preference types, the PhD algorithm’s two Gale–Shapley stages and removal step appear to have a direct mass analogue: accept or reject quantities of each type, then update the remaining student mass. Continuum deferred-acceptance frameworks already exist for two-sided matching, including models with finite capacities, though they do not settle this paper’s three-sided problem ([Leshno’s supply-and-demand framework](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/), [A Continuum Model of Stable Matching With Finite Capacities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)). This makes the mirror look more like a targeted extension of familiar machinery than a problem that opens a new optimization toolkit.

That is a case for lower priority, not a fundamental objection. The other analyst’s anchor survives: a credible repeated-profile placement market exists, and its stable mass matching is a sensible computational question. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
