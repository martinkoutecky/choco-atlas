What the mirror covers

The proposed restatement concerns Theorems 5.5 and 5.6; the paper already studies quota-share geometry and probabilities in Theorems 5.8, 5.10, 6.6, and 6.7.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive reading is a high-multiplicity apportionment problem. Let the three types be residents of the three states, with masses \(\mu_i=p_i/(p_1+p_2+p_3)\); a national population can have millions of residents but only three types. Given \(M\) seats and one of the named divisor methods, ask whether the resulting allocation violates quota, and return the violating state and allocation if it does. The quotas are \(q_i=M\mu_i\), and the output still assigns indivisible seats.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 5.6, proved in this paper. For the Adams, Dean, and Huntington–Hill methods, order the populations \(p_1<p_2<p_3\). A lower quota violation occurs if and only if all three conditions hold:
\[ q_3d(\lfloor q_1\rfloor)<q_1d(\lfloor q_3\rfloor-1),\qquad q_3d(\lfloor q_2\rfloor)<q_2d(\lfloor q_3\rfloor-1), \]
and
\[ \lceil q_1\rceil+\lceil q_2\rceil+\lfloor q_3\rfloor=M+1. \]
The accompanying Theorem 5.5, also proved here, says that any such violation has allocation \((\lceil q_1\rceil,\lceil q_2\rceil,\lfloor q_3\rfloor-1)\). This gives a precise mass-based detection question with a natural expected direction: tractable for these three methods and three types, since the theorem reduces detection to explicit arithmetic conditions. It also suggests follow-up questions about variable numbers of types and exact complexity as a function of \(M\) and input bit length.

The caveat is decisive for the requested anchors: the paper contains no named theorem establishing a complexity classification such as membership in P or hardness. Theorem 5.6 supplies an exact criterion, but does not state a running-time bound. Also, the paper already works with standard quotas across a continuous simplex of population proportions, so this mirror changes the interpretation to resident masses more than it changes the mathematics. Its probability theorems integrate over distributions of population compositions; that is valuable analysis, but by itself is uncertainty over inputs rather than a computational result about a continuous society.

So there is a plausible high-multiplicity interpretation and a useful theorem to point to, but under the stated anchor rule I cannot make this a qualifying positive case: the proposed mirror is essentially already represented in the paper, and its named results do not classify computational complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has a credible high-multiplicity story: a country can have millions of residents, grouped by state. I would not reject it on the ground that apportionment lacks multiplicity. The difficulty is that this model is already present in the paper. Set \(\mu_i=p_i/\sum_j p_j\); then \(q_i=M\mu_i\), and the three masses range over the same simplex of population shares the paper studies. Its homogeneity lemma makes resident counts irrelevant beyond those shares. Expanding each state’s population therefore adds no new population model.

Theorem 5.5 classifies the violating allocation, and Theorem 5.6 gives an iff test for detecting it. Those are named structural criteria, not complexity classifications, as the proponent acknowledges. But posing the same test as “compute whether a society of state-types violates quota” only rewrites \(q_i\) as \(M\mu_i\). Theorem 5.8 gives the corresponding regions in the simplex, and Theorem 5.10 integrates over them to calculate probabilities. The Jefferson results do the same. A question about running time as \(n\), \(M\), or input bit length varies could be a worthwhile study of apportionment algorithms; it would study the complexity of a model already expressed in population shares, rather than supply a new continuous mirror.

The stronger alternative would ask for the least population mass that must move between states to create or remove a quota violation. The paper’s regions already describe the relevant points in the quota simplex. Adding migration costs or an intervention mechanism could define a new control problem, but the paper supplies no such action model: changing a state’s population changes census inputs, rather than changing an agent’s preferences or behavior. That extension may be interesting on its own; it does not rescue a mirror of the named results.

So the negative case is strongest as a collision claim: the paper already studies apportionment over continuous population shares, and its named results characterize violations and their probabilities there. I cannot establish that no future computational project on apportionment could be worthwhile. I can argue that these theorems do not call for a separate continuization.
