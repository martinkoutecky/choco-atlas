The continuous mirror question

Given a finite supported type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a source \(s\), and symmetric nonnegative rational type-pair costs \(c_{tu}\) and binary qualities \(q_{tu}\), partition an atomless population \(X\) into type classes \(X_t\) of measure \(\mu_t\). Find a measurable parent map \(p\) to other individuals or \(s\), with every parent path reaching \(s\) in finitely many steps, that lexicographically maximizes \(\left(\int_X \prod_{e\text{ on }x\text{’s path}}q_e\,dx,\;-\int_X c_{\theta(x),\theta(p(x))}\,dx\right)\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population with type masses \(\mu_t\), where type determines all connection costs and qualities. Each person chooses a parent; a finite set of measure-zero representatives provides a source-reaching skeleton. Optimize the fraction receiving high quality, then minimize per-capita connection cost.

The objection that survived

The zero-mass representatives make their backbone links free per capita, so most of the finite tree’s global cost coupling disappears; the proponent acknowledges lost attachment costs but does not establish that the authors would accept this simplified object as their network-design problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is narrow: the paper’s network-design result has a credible continuous-population mirror; its cost-sharing results do not automatically acquire one.

My lead anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says that the authors’ modified Prim procedure produces a max-quality optimal tree: first find the agents that can reach the source using only high-quality edges; connect those agents with a minimum-cost tree that forbids low-quality edges among them; then attach the remaining agents by continuing Prim’s algorithm with the original costs. The resulting tree maximizes the number of agents whose whole path to the source is high quality, and, among trees achieving that maximum, minimizes total edge cost. Proposition 2 asserts correctness, rather than stating a complexity-class bound; its transitive-closure and Prim steps give a polynomial-time implementation. The paper has no comparable numbered hardness result. Its introductory remark about the general Pareto-set problem being NP-hard is not a named result about this paper’s particular lexicographic problem, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A plausible regime is a large water-supply population divided among a small number \(\tau\) of connection types. A type completely describes the connection costs and qualities its members have to every other type, including their own, and residents of a type share the same quality along their route. Millions of residents might therefore be represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over a much smaller support of service-use profiles. This is most plausible when the network serves cohorts with shared connection characteristics, rather than a small committee of individually distinctive users.

I would hand a prover the Per-capita Maximum-Quality Tree\(_\infty\) problem. Its input is a finite set of supported types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a source \(s\), symmetric nonnegative rational connection costs \(c_{tu}\) (including \(c_{ts}\)), and binary qualities \(q_{tu}\) (including \(q_{ts}\)). Realize the population as a nonatomic space \(X\), partitioned into sets \(X_t\) of measure \(\mu_t\). A solution is a measurable parent map \(p\) assigning each person to another person or to \(s\), such that every person reaches \(s\) in finitely many parent steps. It must lexicographically maximize
\[ \left( \int_X \prod_{e\text{ on }x\text{’s path to }s} q_e\,dx,\; -\int_X c_{\theta(x),\theta(p(x))}\,dx \right), \]
where \(\theta(x)\) is the type of \(x\). Thus the first coordinate is the fraction receiving high quality, and the second is the negative per-capita connection cost. The requested output is an optimal parent map, representable by finite type-level choices and a finite connecting skeleton.

This keeps the paper’s central objective intact: maximize high-quality service first, then minimize connection cost. It is a genuine high-multiplicity reading of its individual-as-node model. For a finite society with \(n\) agents, give each person mass \(1/n\): a discrete tree embeds as a parent map constant on one cell per agent, with quality count divided by \(n\) and total cost divided by \(n\). The continuous problem also permits mass to choose connections by type rather than by named person.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. Let \(R\) be the types reachable from \(s\) through high-quality edges. The maximum possible high-quality mass is \(\sum_{t\in R}\mu_t\), as all reachable types can be served at high quality together. For \(t\in R\), ordinary mass can use its cheapest high-quality first edge to \(s\) or to another type in \(R\); for \(t\notin R\), it can use its cheapest edge to any supported type or to \(s\). A finite set of measure-zero representatives can be assigned as a connecting skeleton, so the rest of the population attains those per-type minima while still reaching the source. Reachability and the minima take polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). This is the continuum counterpart of Proposition 2’s reachability-plus-tree construction, with an additional typewise cost decomposition.

The scope is Proposition 2’s network-selection result. I would not claim to mirror Propositions 3 or 5 on core allocations, or Propositions 6 and 7 on q-Folk properties. In particular, their “Continuity” property concerns how shares vary with connection costs; it is not evidence that the population has been continuized.

The soft spot is the zero-mass skeleton. It can connect the continuum at no per-capita cost, so the limit loses some finite-population attachment costs and may look too permissive to authors who regard those costs as essential. I think the mirror survives because it follows from normalizing the paper’s additive per-agent edge costs, while retaining its quality rule, source-rooted connectivity, and lexicographic objective. If connection capacities or fixed infrastructure costs remain substantial as the population grows, this mirror would need a different model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the one credible computational anchor: it proves that the modified Prim procedure finds a max-quality optimal tree. So the objection cannot be that the paper has no algorithmic result to mirror. Nor can it simply be that agents have no plausible types. A large service population with repeated connection profiles is possible if costs and qualities are constant within type pairs.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror uses exactly that regime. Give each type a mass, make the first objective the high-quality mass, and minimize connection cost per capita. But the tree requirement behaves badly under this limit. A finite set of representative agents can connect the types to the source while having zero mass. Their connections still ensure every person has a finite path, but their own costs contribute nothing to the per-capita objective. Positive mass can then attach to whichever type representative offers its cheapest admissible edge. The global tree structure that Proposition 2 optimizes has largely disappeared: what remains is type reachability plus per-type edge minima. This is a well-defined limit, but it risks being a degenerate mirror of the paper’s network-design question.

The obvious repair is to keep backbone connections costly, or add capacities or infrastructure nodes. That could preserve meaningful network structure, but those features are absent from this model, where nodes are agents and the project cost is the sum of selected edge costs. Adding them would need authors to recognize a new problem as theirs. Cost-sharing results do not supply an escape hatch: their core constraints concern named coalitions, and in a nonatomic population individual deviations have zero mass.

That is the best case against funding this mirror: its most natural high-multiplicity version may shed the very tree-design structure it is meant to continuize, while restoring that structure appears to require extra modeling assumptions. The case is still weak. The large-population regime is plausible, and the resulting reachability-and-cost problem is a legitimate Class A question even if it simplifies sharply. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario; the argument is that this particular anchor offers a poor complexity landscape, not that the question is incoherent.
