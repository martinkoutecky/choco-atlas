The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of applicant types \(T\), each with a strict ranking of at most two acceptable courses, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita course supplies \(\kappa_c\), and a rational bound \(b\), does there exist a feasible allocation with matched mass at most \(b\) that is Pareto optimal against all feasible measurable reassignments of the same population? Each individual receives at most one acceptable course or remains unassigned, ranked below acceptable courses; a reassignment is a Pareto improvement if it weakly improves every individual and strictly improves a positive mass.

The model it lives in

A finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with masses \(\mu_t\); each type has the same strict ranking of at most two courses and the same singleton-or-empty feasible bundles. Use masses \(x_{t,c}\) and \(x_{t,\bot}\), with \(x_{t,\bot}+\sum_{c\in P(t)}x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,c}\le\kappa_c\). Minimize \(\sum_t\sum_{c\in P(t)}x_{t,c}\) subject to Pareto optimality under feasible measurable reassignment.

The objection that survived

In the atomless model, a Pareto improvement must benefit positive mass, so Theorem 4’s discrete optimum need not be the limit of the continuous optimum; the cases give no convergence result.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s one-to-one, two-choice minimum-cardinality hardness result. It leaves aside Theorem 5’s maximum-cardinality result and the paper’s general many-to-many characterization, checking algorithm, and GSD results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize Pareto optimality for these atomless short-list allocations.
  • Determine the complexity of minimizing matched mass and whether the problem reduces to flow or linear programming.
  • Establish when discrete minimum-cardinality POM instances approximate the mass optimum, including the rounding loss as support size and population size vary.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that deciding whether an instance has a Pareto optimal matching of cardinality at most \(k\) is NP-complete even in the one-to-one case when every applicant’s preference list has at most two courses. The paper gives a reduction from Vertex Cover; the short-list strengthening is proved here, building on earlier minimum-POM hardness results it cites.

A natural mirror is Min-Mass Pareto-Optimal Course Allocation. Let \(C\) be a finite catalogue of course categories and \(T\) a finite set of applicant types. A type gives an applicant’s complete relevant description; in this restricted mirror it is a strict ranking of at most two acceptable courses, with feasible bundles limited to the empty set or one course. The input gives each type a rational population share \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and each course a rational supply \(\kappa_c\) per unit of population. Thus a population of size \(N\) has \(N\mu_t\) applicants of type \(t\) and \(N\kappa_c\) seats for course \(c\), when those quantities are integral.

An allocation assigns each member of each type either an acceptable course or no course, subject to the course supplies. Write \(x_{t,c}\) for the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(c\), and \(x_{t,\bot}\) for its unassigned mass. Feasibility requires
\[ x_{t,\bot}+\sum_{c\in P(t)}x_{t,c}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t x_{t,c}\le \kappa_c. \]
Its matched mass is \(\sum_t\sum_{c\in P(t)}x_{t,c}\). Call an allocation Pareto optimal if no feasible reassignment of the same atomless population weakly improves every individual’s assignment and strictly improves a positive mass of individuals, using each type’s course ranking and placing “unassigned” below acceptable courses.

Given rational \(b\), the problem asks whether there is a Pareto optimal allocation with matched mass at most \(b\). A solution is such an allocation, or equivalently a measurable assignment of individuals that induces the stated masses. This asks the same welfare question as Theorem 4—how few applicant-course pairs can a Pareto efficient outcome contain—while letting cohorts split fractionally. In the one-to-one restriction, each assigned individual contributes one pair, so the objective is exactly the continuous counterpart of matching cardinality.

The regime is a large centralized placement system for a recurring student or trainee cohort, with a finite course catalogue and many applicants sharing a small set of short preference lists. The shared type includes the whole preference description, so applicants within a type are genuinely interchangeable for this problem. Millions of applicants can therefore be represented by a moderate support \(T\), while course supplies grow with cohort size. This makes the population itself the continuous object; it does not rely on fractional outcomes or a noise model.

I expect this restricted mirror to be Class A, though that is a conjecture to investigate, not a result of the paper. The paper’s hardness reduction encodes Vertex Cover. When a type’s mass can be split, the corresponding cover choices appear to relax toward fractional vertex cover, which is expressible by a linear program. A useful next step is to characterize continuous Pareto optimality for these short-list instances and test whether minimizing matched mass reduces to flow or LP optimization. That would also raise the high-multiplicity question: can a continuous solution yield an additive approximation for the discrete problem, and what rounding loss is unavoidable?

The weak point is that Theorem 4’s reduction uses many distinct applicants with graph-specific preferences. Its support size grows with the graph, and its discrete hardness depends on one-applicant granularity. The theorem therefore does not establish hardness in a fixed- or small-support regime, nor does it guarantee that fractional cohorts preserve the reduction. Still, the mirror is a recognizable version of the authors’ underlying problem: it asks for the smallest population share that can be assigned while retaining Pareto efficiency, under the same preference and capacity logic. It covers Theorem 4’s one-to-one, two-choice hardness result; it makes no claim about Theorem 5 or the paper’s full many-to-many model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the bridge from Theorem 4’s reduction to the proposed population model. The reduction’s applicants encode graph incidences: each endpoint applicant has a short list involving its edge course and vertex course. Thus the construction has no repeated applicant types; its support grows with the graph. To create a large cohort, one would have to replicate those applicants and scale the course supplies. That produces a genuine atomless allocation problem, but the theorem’s individual choices have become divisible mass. A discrete Pareto improvement can be witnessed by one applicant; the proposed continuum definition requires strict improvement for positive mass. The paper gives no result showing that minimum-cardinality POMs, normalized by population size, behave consistently under this change.

That objection weakens the claimed connection, but it does not defeat the best mirror. A recurring placement system with repeated preference types and course sections scaled to cohort size is plausible, and the programme explicitly allows mass supplies and changed answers. It would be wrong to treat the loss of one-person granularity as a disqualifier. The fact that the reduction uses graph-specific types also does not rule out asking the continuous problem on other, higher-multiplicity instances.

So the negative case is weak. The proponent has identified a named NP-completeness result and a recognizable mass version with a sensible population scenario. Their fractional-cover intuition is unproved, and the computational value still needs establishing; neither point shows that the question is not worth asking. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists for this anchor.
