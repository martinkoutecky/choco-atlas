The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\) fixed binary questions, \(\tau\) worker types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), \(p_{\mathrm{obs}}\in(0,1]\), and exact rational report masses \(\nu_{j,+},\nu_{j,-},\nu_{j,0}\), promised to arise from unknown labels \(x\in\{-1,+1\}^d\), abilities \(a_t\in[\tfrac12,1]\), and difficulties \(h_j\in[0,1]\) with \(Q_{tj}=a_t(1-h_j)+\tfrac12h_j\), compute \(\hat x\) with \(L_Q(\hat x,x)=0\), where \(L_Q(\hat x,x)=\frac1d\sum_j\mathbf 1\{\hat x_j\ne x_j\}\sum_t\mu_t(2Q_{tj}-1)^2\). The report masses satisfy \(\nu_{j,+}-\nu_{j,-}=p_{\mathrm{obs}}x_j\sum_t\mu_t(2Q_{tj}-1)\); under R1, choose the sign of each nonzero imbalance and either label on a tie.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic worker population with rational type masses \(\mu_t\); each type’s mass is distributed among unobserved, correct-report, and incorrect-report outcomes according to \(p_{\mathrm{obs}}\) and \(Q_{tj}\). The questions and labels remain discrete, the exact aggregate report masses are input, and the task is to recover labels with zero population \(Q\)-loss.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s \(C_{\mathrm{Int}}\) label-recovery task in the exact population-report limit; it leaves the finite-sample minimax bounds and the broader \(C_{\mathrm{Perm}}\) guarantees aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a limit version of the paper’s label-estimation problem: many workers, a fixed finite set of questions, and only a small number of worker types. I would anchor it on Theorem 3, proved in this paper (Section 5.7). There is no named computational-hardness result here: the lower bounds in Theorems 1(b) and 4(b) are statistical, not computational. Theorem 3 is the best algorithmic anchor.

Theorem 3 says that under the paper’s regimes (R1) and (R2), if \(Q^*\in C_{\mathrm{Int}}\) has parameters \((\widetilde q,h^*)\) satisfying
\[ \|\widetilde q-\tfrac12\|_2^2\|1-h^*\|_2^2 \ge \widetilde c\,\frac{d\log^{2.5}(dn)}{p_{\mathrm{obs}}}, \]
then OBI-WAN recovers each question \(j\) with
\[ (1-h_j^*)^2\|\widetilde q-\tfrac12\|_2^2 \ge 5\,\frac{\log^{2.5}(dn)}{p_{\mathrm{obs}}} \]
with probability at least \(1-\exp(-c_H\log^{1.5}(dn))\). Here \(C_{\mathrm{Int}}\) means
\[ Q^*=\widetilde q(1-h^*)^T+\tfrac12 1(h^*)^T. \]
The theorem gives a statistical guarantee for the OBI-WAN algorithm specified in the paper; it does not itself classify a problem as being in P.

A plausible regime is a large annotation operation with many workers in a modest number \(\tau\) of stable skill bands, all labeling the same fixed corpus of \(d\) questions. A worker’s type is their ability \(a_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of workers in that band. Question difficulty \(h_j^*\) remains question-specific, and the questions and labels stay discrete. The worker population is what becomes continuous. Each type’s mass splits fractionally among “not asked,” “answered correctly,” and “answered incorrectly,” according to the paper’s observation probabilities. That fractional split is the natural aggregate of integral reports from many workers.

Continuous CInt Crowd Label Recovery is the mirror I would put to a prover. The underlying society has support \(t=1,\ldots,\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and type abilities \(a_t\ge\tfrac12\); the fixed questions have difficulties \(h_j^*\in[0,1]\), with correctness probabilities \(Q_{tj}=a_t(1-h_j^*)+\tfrac12h_j^*\). The latent answer vector is \(x^*\in\{-1,+1\}^d\). The observable input is the exact aggregate mass \(\nu_{j,+},\nu_{j,-},\nu_{j,0}\) of each report for each question, with total unobserved mass \(1-p_{\mathrm{obs}}\); the type parameters remain nuisance parameters, as \(Q^*\) does in the paper. The task is to output \(\hat x\) minimizing the continuous version of the paper’s \(Q^*\)-loss,
\[ L_Q(\hat x,x^*)=\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^d \mathbf 1\{\hat x_j\ne x_j^*\} \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t(2Q_{tj}-1)^2. \]
A valid solution must choose \(\hat x_j=\operatorname{sign}(\nu_{j,+}-\nu_{j,-})\) whenever that difference is nonzero; ties can be answered either way.

The reason this works is that
\[ \nu_{j,+}-\nu_{j,-} =p_{\mathrm{obs}}x_j^*\sum_t\mu_t(2Q_{tj}-1). \]
Under R1 the terms in the sum are nonnegative. A nonzero imbalance therefore gives the true label’s sign; if the imbalance is zero, every type has \(Q_{tj}=\tfrac12\), so that question contributes zero to the loss. This mirror is tractable by a linear-time sign calculation in the aggregate input size. I expect Class A, through exact population aggregation, rather than hardness transferring from the finite-sample problem.

The mirror covers the label-recovery aim and the intermediate-model guarantee of Theorem 3; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s finite-sample minimax bounds or its broader \(C_{\mathrm{Perm}}\) guarantees. Its weakest point is also clear: exact aggregate masses remove the sampling noise that makes OBI-WAN and its signal thresholds matter. The authors could recognize this as a continuous-population limit of their inference problem, but it is a modest computational mirror: it captures what the labels mean at population scale more strongly than it captures the paper’s algorithmic challenge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the only anchor, and its result is statistical: OBI-WAN recovers labels with high probability under signal conditions. The paper gives an efficient estimator, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem in the sense ChoCo is charting. Its objects also resist a direct social-choice reading: workers provide noisy evidence about exogenous correct labels; they do not express preferences over alternatives, and the algorithm estimates truth rather than selecting a collective outcome.

The proposed high-multiplicity setting is plausible. Repeated workers in stable skill bands are a sensible regime, and grouping them into types is not a distortion. The trouble is what happens to Theorem 3’s challenge when that population becomes an exact continuum. For the proposed \(C_{\mathrm{Int}}\) model, the report masses satisfy
\[ \nu_{j,+}-\nu_{j,-} =p_{\mathrm{obs}}x_j^*\sum_t\mu_t(2Q_{tj}-1). \]
Under R1, every term in the sum is nonnegative. A nonzero difference reveals \(x_j^*\); if the difference is zero, every positive-mass type has \(Q_{tj}=\tfrac12\), so the question’s contribution to the continuous \(Q\)-loss is zero. Thus the proposed mirror has zero \(Q\)-loss by a sign check, without recovering worker order or estimating question difficulty. This is a real continuum degeneration: exact population proportions remove the sampling noise that makes the theorem’s signal thresholds matter.

The strongest alternative would retain a finite noisy sample while describing workers’ skills through a distribution with small support. That could lead to useful statistical questions about estimation and sample complexity. But it keeps the finite observations as the source of difficulty; the continuous population mainly compresses the worker model. It is therefore a neighboring high-multiplicity statistics project, not a new computational social-choice problem whose complexity turns on a continuous society.

So the case against a worthwhile mirror is strongest for ChoCo’s stated remit, rather than as a proof that no useful continuous statistical extension could exist. If the programme includes statistical inference from a finite sample drawn from a continuous population, the negative case is weak: the skill-band regime makes that extension quite reasonable.
