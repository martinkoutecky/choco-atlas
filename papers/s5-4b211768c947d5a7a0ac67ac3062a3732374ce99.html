What the mirror covers

No continuous computational mirror is supported by the named results. They cover restricted-claims equilibrium characterization, payoff convergence as each finite player's claim grows, and proportionality under arbitrary claim heights.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational result, so I can’t responsibly state a continuous computational problem per anchor.

Theorem 4.5, proved in the paper, characterizes Nash equilibria for restricted claims problems and describes their payoff vectors. Theorem 5.11, also proved here, shows that equilibrium payoffs converge to the proportional division as each player’s claim grows. Proposition 6.2 shows that allowing arbitrary claim heights gives a unique equilibrium with proportional payoffs. These are results about equilibrium structure and limits; none establishes a complexity classification or an algorithmic result of the kind required for an anchor.

There is a possible population regime to investigate: a large body of creditors grouped by complete claim and contract type, with many creditors but relatively few types. But the paper’s continuum is the estate interval \([0,E]\); its strategic player set remains finite. A population version would need a new model of mass-weighted strategies and payoffs, and the paper’s results do not anchor its computational questions. That is a research direction, not a positive mirror case grounded in this paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject a green verdict for this paper under ChoCo’s stated test. The strongest objection is that none of the three named results is a computational result. Theorem 4.5 characterizes equilibria and their payoff polytope; Theorem 5.11 proves a limit; Proposition 6.2 gives a unique equilibrium under continuous claim heights. None studies the complexity of a computational problem.

Theorem 4.5 is the closest candidate. One could ask whether a proposed payoff belongs to the equilibrium payoff set, or compute an extreme payoff. But the theorem already gives that set through explicit bounds and linear equations. Those are possible algorithmic questions inferred from the characterization, not complexity questions the paper raises. A stronger population version would ask for the equilibrium correspondence over a distribution of creditor types. Yet that changes the strategic model: an individual in an atomless population has no effect on aggregate claims, while treating each type’s whole mass as one player makes that mass a strategic bloc. Neither preserves the paper’s unilateral Nash deviations.

Theorem 5.11 does not supply a population limit to mirror. It fixes \(n\) and sends each player’s claim to infinity. Replacing that with many creditors requires a different scaling of claims and the estate. If creditors become individually negligible, the strategic effect of a unilateral deviation changes; if type masses act as players, the model again changes. The proportional limit is an equilibrium result, not a computational classification.

Proposition 6.2 is continuous in the claims players can place across the estate, while the player set remains finite. Its proportional outcome does not make it a continuous-population result. A population extension could be interesting, but its natural question is about the equilibrium behavior of a new nonatomic game, not the computational complexity of a result in this paper.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many creditors with the same claim and contract terms. That makes a population model conceivable, and I would not claim that no such model could ever be worthwhile. The weak point in the negative case is precisely that one might formulate a new, computationally interesting game around those creditors. But it would need a new strategic foundation before it could count as a mirror. On the material here, the proposed anchors do not establish one.
