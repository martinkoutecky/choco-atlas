The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. It reports experiments comparing four choice functions, but does not prove that any underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise complexity-classified. So there is no qualifying anchor, and I cannot give the required one-continuous-problem-per-anchor case without inventing a result.

There is a plausible population-level direction: in a large recommendation service, each period’s arrivals could be represented by masses of users sharing the same recommendation-relevant profile and tolerance vector. The decision variable would be the fraction of each type assigned to each re-ranker, with historical exposure fairness and recommendation accuracy evaluated across those assignments. The paper’s batch-based experiments make that scenario recognizable, but they do not establish that the number of exact user types is small or analyze the resulting problem’s complexity. That is a possible research extension, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper proves no computational result to mirror. It compares four choice functions experimentally; it does not classify the complexity of computing them, optimizing their tradeoffs, or finding fair re-rankings. So there is no named result here for ChoCo to continuize.

The obvious salvage is a society whose types are users’ preference rankings over re-rankers, with mass assigned to each type, then a continuous version of the Allocation Lottery’s probabilistic-serial step. That is a coherent population model. But it is a new computational question about the mechanism, not a continuous counterpart of a complexity result in this paper. If types must also capture the personalized recommendations and accuracy effects, they may be nearly as numerous as users; if they capture only tolerance rankings, the model preserves the allocation step but abstracts away those personalized consequences.

That makes the case against a *paper-anchored* mirror strong. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: the mass-based probabilistic-serial model is a plausible counterexample, even though the paper provides no complexity result for it.
