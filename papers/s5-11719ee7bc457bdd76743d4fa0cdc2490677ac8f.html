What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Chapter 2’s population-changing public-goods problem. But the paper has no eligible computational anchor under your rule: its named results do not assert that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The theorems characterize social choice rules from axioms. So I can give a plausible continuous-population research question, but I cannot honestly present it as a mirror anchored in a computational result or assign it a Class A/B/C outcome.

The closest result is Theorem 2.2, proved in this thesis. It says that, for single-peaked preferences, a choice correspondence satisfies efficiency and population-monotonicity if and only if it is a target-set correspondence. A target interval \([a,b]\) is chosen when it intersects the convex hull of the agents’ peaks; if it lies wholly to one side, the nearest extreme peak is chosen instead. The theorem covers a variable finite population, and the target interval is constant across populations and preference profiles.

A credible setting is a large municipality planning public parking along a street. A type is a resident’s complete symmetric single-peaked preference over locations, identified by their ideal location; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents of type \(t\). The regime could have millions of residents but relatively few distinct ideal locations. The decision variable is the interval of locations the planner selects. The paper supplies no objective function: its question is which decision rules satisfy efficiency and solidarity.

A precise continuous analogue would ask for a characterization of rules \(F\) mapping each finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over such types to a nonempty closed interval \(F(\mu)\). Require \(F(\mu)\) to be efficient, and require solidarity when the population expands: for every newcomer distribution \(\eta\) and \(0<\lambda<1\), set \(\mu'=\lambda\mu+(1-\lambda)\eta\). Either all incumbent types weakly prefer \(F(\mu)\) to \(F(\mu')\), or all weakly prefer \(F(\mu')\) to \(F(\mu)\), using the thesis’s best-worst ranking of intervals. The question is whether every such rule must be a target-interval rule for one fixed \([a,b]\). If so, its output is the intersection of \([a,b]\) with the incumbents’ peak hull when they overlap, and otherwise the nearest endpoint of that hull. A proof of that characterization—or a counterexample—is a solution.

This is a natural continuation of Theorem 2.2: mass records population shares, and adding residents becomes mixing the incumbent and newcomer distributions. The thesis’s authors would likely recognize the connection, since population change and solidarity are already central to their problem. The expected result is a target-set characterization by analogy with Theorem 2.2, but tractability versus hardness is not applicable to this axiomatic question. A computational version would need an additional problem—such as computing a rule’s output from an encoded distribution—and fixing the rule in advance would make that task essentially just endpoint clamping.

My weakest point is also decisive for the ChoCo brief: this candidate is axiomatic, exactly the kind of continuization the programme puts out of scope. The underlying population regime is convincing, but that does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is categorical: the thesis states no computational problem and proves no complexity result. Chapter 1 studies choice axioms; Theorem 2.2 in Chapter 2 characterizes rules satisfying efficiency and population-monotonicity; Chapter 3 characterizes strategy-proof rules. None gives an algorithmic problem with an encoded input, running time, or complexity classification. That leaves no computational result for a continuous-population analysis to mirror.

The proponent’s Chapter 2 candidate is nevertheless the strongest possible one. A large municipality with many residents sharing a relatively small number of preference types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. A distribution over complete preference types, with population expansion represented by mixing in newcomer mass, gives a coherent axiomatic question. Theorem 2.2’s continuous analogue could ask whether efficiency and solidarity characterize rules with a fixed target interval. I would not reject that question because mass is inappropriate, or because the discrete and continuous answers might coincide. It is a plausible axiomatic extension, and the authors could recognize it as their problem. But axiomatic characterization is explicitly outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

Turning that extension into an algorithmic task requires adding a new problem. If the target interval is fixed, one could ask how to evaluate the rule from a distribution. If the rule itself is input, one must first specify how arbitrary correspondences are represented and what computational question is being asked. Neither task comes from Theorem 2.2’s characterization. The same gap applies to the other chapters’ results: a computational project could be built around them, but it would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this thesis.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s stated gate, but it does not establish the broader claim that no worthwhile computational follow-on could ever be invented. It establishes that this paper offers no computational anchor for one; its best continuous candidate is a potentially worthwhile axiomatic study, which the programme has ruled out of scope.
