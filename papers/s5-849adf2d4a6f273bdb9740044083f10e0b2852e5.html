The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of expert roles \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), represent each role by an interval \(I_t\) of length \(\mu_t\). Each role has an independent, homogeneous signal measure \(W_t\) with \(\operatorname{Var}(W_t(A))=(v_t/\mu_t)\lambda(A)\) for measurable \(A\subseteq I_t\), where \(v_t>0\). The target is \(Y=\sum_t W_t(I_t)\). Before observing signal realizations, choose measurable subsets \(A_t\subseteq I_t\) with \(\sum_t\lambda(A_t)\le\delta\), observe their reports \(W_t(A_t)\), and estimate \(Y\). What is the minimum, over randomized selection policies and estimators based on the reports, of the worst-case normalized squared error \(\sup_{v\ne0}\mathbb{E}[(\hat Y-Y)^2]/\sum_t v_t\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of experts grouped into finitely many roles of masses \(\mu_t\), with independent private signal increments and variance density \(v_t/\mu_t\) within each role. The principal selects queried mass totaling at most \(\delta\), observes the selected signal aggregates, and estimates the total signal \(Y\), minimizing worst-case normalized mean squared error. Querying a \(\delta\)-fraction of each role and summing the reports gives value \(1-\delta\).

The objection that survived

The continuum reports \(W_t(A_t)\) are aggregates over blocks of signal mass rather than individual experts’ forecasts, so the mirror depends on accepting this signal scaling as a meaningful limit of the paper’s query model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror captures Theorem 3’s \(d/n\) query-budget tradeoff in the independent-private-signal regime; it leaves the broader overlapping-signal setting, Theorem 4’s order and agent complexity tradeoff, and Theorems 1–2’s exact reconstruction results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a comparable value or tractable characterization hold when signals overlap across roles, as in the paper’s full partial-information model?
  • How does the optimum change when query costs vary by role?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named theorem about running time or classes such as P and NP-hardness. Its “complexity” measures the burden of asking and answering queries. The strongest anchor for a continuous mirror is therefore Theorem 3, proved here (with its lower and upper bounds established in Appendix Lemmas 5 and 6): in the paper’s partial-information model, for \(d<n\), the minimum worst-case normalized squared error over approximators implemented by randomized DAG-elicitable rules using at most \(d\) queries is exactly \(1-d/n\). This is a query-budget result, not a Turing-complexity result.

A direct population analogue is Fractional-Budget Robust Forecast Aggregation. Its instance has a finite set of expert roles \(T\), with masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a query budget \(\delta\in[0,1]\). All experts in a role share the same information and elicitation role, signal law, and query cost; their signal realizations are independent. This is a high-multiplicity regime: the number of experts grows while the number of roles stays fixed.

For a precise continuum model, represent role \(t\) by an interval \(I_t\) of length \(\mu_t\). Its private signals form a centered independent-increment random measure \(W_t\), with total variance \(v_t\); increments are independent across roles and disjoint subsets. The target is \(Y=\sum_t W_t(I_t)\). The principal chooses, before seeing signal realizations, measurable subsets \(A_t\subseteq I_t\) with total mass \(\sum_t\lambda(A_t)\le\delta\). The selected experts return their truthful private forecasts, whose aggregate is \(\hat Y=\sum_t W_t(A_t)\). A solution is a randomized selection policy and an estimator based on its reports, minimizing worst-case normalized error over all nonzero variance vectors \(v\):
\[ \inf_{\Pi,f}\ \sup_{v\ne 0} \frac{\mathbb E_{\Pi,W}\!\left[(f(\text{reports})-Y)^2\right]}{\sum_t v_t}. \]
This is the continuum version of querying a fraction of a large panel; at finite population size \(N\), a mass budget \(\delta\) corresponds to about \(\delta N\) individual forecast queries.

The value is \(1-\delta\). For an upper bound, select a \(\delta\)-fraction of each type and sum their reports; the unqueried independent signal mass has variance \((1-\delta)\sum_t v_t\). For the lower bound, take equal variance per unit of population mass, so the variance density is uniform. Any policy querying at most mass \(\delta\) observes at most a \(\delta\)-fraction of that variance. The rest is independent of every report, so no estimator can reduce the residual error below \(1-\delta\). The mirror is therefore tractable in the programme’s sense: it has an exact value and an explicit optimal policy.

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ question because it keeps the central tradeoff—robust aggregation quality versus query burden—and Theorem 3’s lower bound is witnessed by independent private signals, precisely the regime continued here. The paper proves Theorem 3 for the broader partial-information model, including overlapping signals; this mirror covers its private-signal regime and its \(d/n\) tradeoff, not every feature of the full theorem.

The soft spot is the resource measure: a mass budget \(\delta\) means soliciting roughly \(\delta N\) reports in a finite population, whereas the paper counts queries. That is a per-capita version of the same burden, and the theorem’s \(d/n\) dependence makes it a natural continuation, but an author could reasonably ask whether it answers a distinct query-cost question. The signal model is also narrower than the paper’s overlapping-information setting. Natural follow-ups are whether overlapping signals admit a comparable mass-budget bound, and how the value changes when query costs differ by type.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but it is a worst-case error theorem, not a computational-complexity result. Its “query complexity” counts how many reports the principal solicits; the theorem gives the best error under that elicitation budget. It says nothing about the running time of finding an aggregation rule from an encoded instance, or about exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithms. The other main results have the same character: they bound aggregation error and query burdens. So Theorem 3 does not supply the kind of computational question ChoCo is set up to mirror.

The proposed mass-budget model is a coherent statistical continuation, and its value \(1-\delta\) follows naturally from the finite result. But its continuum is more than a distribution over expert roles: the random measures \(W_t\) carry a field of private signal noise over each role’s interval. Since an individual point has zero mass, its report vanishes; the model’s nontrivial reports are aggregates over blocks. That may be a sensible scaling limit of summed individual reports, but it changes the query object. More importantly for ChoCo, it still asks for a performance guarantee, not the complexity of computing a solution.

One could add type-dependent query costs or richer overlapping signals and ask for an optimal mass allocation. That might become an interesting computational problem, but the costs and allocation input would be new structure; the resulting problem would be a research extension, not a computational mirror of Theorem 3. The objection is not that the answer \(1-\delta\) is easy or unchanged. It is that the proposed question belongs to elicitation and statistical aggregation rather than the programme’s computational-complexity agenda.

So the negative case against this anchor is strong as a ChoCo paper-to-mirror assessment. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous research problem can arise from this paper is weaker: the mass-allocation extension could be worthwhile on its own, but this paper does not provide the computational result needed to make it a ChoCo mirror.
