The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), an integer \(k\), and an ordered list of rankings \((\pi_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\) occupying consecutive intervals of rational masses \((\lambda_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\) on \([0,1]\), with each \(\lambda_i>0\) and \(\sum_i \lambda_i=1\), is there a subset \(X\subseteq C\) with \(|C\setminus X|\le k\) such that, for every pair in \(X\), its relative order along the population axis switches at most once, up to measure-zero points?

The model it lives in

An ordered nonatomic population on \([0,1]\), represented by finitely many ranking types \(\pi_i\) on consecutive intervals of mass \(\lambda_i\). Choose a candidate subset \(X\subseteq C\) minimizing \(|C\setminus X|\), subject to every candidate pair changing relative order at most once along the axis, almost everywhere.

What the mirror covers

The stated mirror covers Theorem 13’s Candidate Deletion hardness. The case mentions the companion \(k\)-Candidate Partition result but does not state a separate continuous question for it; graph implementation and characterization results are left aside.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is the Candidate Deletion result in Theorem 13, proved in this paper. Given an election \(E=(C,V)\) and an integer \(k\), Candidate Deletion asks whether some \(X\subseteq C\), with \(|X|\ge |C|-k\), makes the restricted election single-crossing in the given voter order. Theorem 13 proves this problem NP-complete. Its companion result proves \(k\)-Candidate Partition NP-complete for every fixed \(k\ge3\); I focus on Candidate Deletion as the anchor here.

My lead mirror is Ordered-Measure Candidate Deletion. An instance has candidates \(C\), an integer \(k\), and an ordered list of cohorts \((\pi_i,\lambda_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\), where each \(\pi_i\) is a ranking of \(C\), each \(\lambda_i>0\) is rational, and \(\sum_i\lambda_i=1\). Cohort \(i\) occupies an interval of length \(\lambda_i\) on a normalized population axis, in the listed order. The cohort’s complete type is its ranking together with its ordered covariate band. The question is whether there is an \(X\subseteq C\) with \(|C\setminus X|\le k\) such that, for every pair of candidates, their relative order switches at most once along the axis, almost everywhere. A solution is the surviving candidate set \(X\); equivalently, the objective is to minimize \(|C\setminus X|\).

This is a continuous electorate: each interval contains a continuum of voters, and its length is their population share. It also has a direct high-multiplicity regime. Give each of the \(\tau\) cohorts \(q\) voters with the same ranking and band. Then \(n=q\tau\) while the number of types stays \(\tau\); \(q\) can grow arbitrarily. The reduction behind Theorem 13 uses an election implementing an arbitrary graph with at most \(2|C|+1\) voter positions. Turn each position into a positive-mass interval, or replicate its voters within that interval. This preserves the multi-crossing graph and the candidate-deletion answer. The continuous problem therefore remains NP-complete by the same independent-set reduction. I expect hardness transfers (Class B): the reduction’s combinatorics live in the candidate set, while the ordered population is retained exactly.

The mirror preserves the paper’s fixed public order, which is essential to its definition of single-crossing. The authors could recognize it as their problem under high multiplicity: the same candidate set is being repaired against the same ordered preference pattern. The ordering must be represented in the input; an unordered distribution over rankings would lose information needed to tell whether a pair switches once or several times.

My weakest point is that the masses affect the question only through which cohorts have positive mass and their order. Their magnitudes do not change the deletion objective. This is a faithful continuous-population mirror, but it does not create a new continuous-optimization algorithm or dissolve the paper’s hardness. A useful next question is where tractability ends as \(\tau\) grows: the paper proves Candidate Deletion is polynomial-time solvable with at most three voters in Theorem 14, so the continuous version invites a support-size threshold or parameterized-complexity analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 13’s NP-completeness of Candidate Deletion. It is a real computational result, and the proposed ordered-interval model is a coherent way to preserve its fixed voter order. But it also exposes the main objection: the decision problem uses the *order of preference blocks*, not their population masses.

In the proposed instance \((\pi_i,\lambda_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\), deleting candidates works exactly as it does on the finite sequence \((\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_\tau)\). The values \(\lambda_i\) never affect whether a pair of candidates crosses more than once; they matter only insofar as they are positive. Consecutive blocks with the same ranking can be merged. So the model retains the whole ordered profile while adding mass labels that do no work in the question. The two obvious alternatives fare poorly: a distribution over rankings alone loses the order, while a general measurable ranking path needs additional assumptions or an encoding. Restricting that path to finitely many constant intervals restores an input, but it restores the finite ordered sequence too.

The best version of the proponent’s scenario—income cohorts with shared rankings—is plausible, and it can have arbitrarily many people per cohort. That makes the high-multiplicity regime sensible. But replication within a cohort changes no Candidate Deletion answer. Moreover, any positive-mass interval that introduces a second reversal can cause a crossing, however small its mass; a zero-mass interval disappears. The exact property is therefore discontinuous in the masses. A more robust question that weights violations by mass could give population shares real force, but it would change the paper’s candidate-deletion problem. The same objection applies to \(k\)-Candidate Partition, whose answer also depends on the crossing graph rather than cohort weights.

The proposed support-size question following Theorem 14 has a related limitation: its support counts ordered preference blocks, not population multiplicity. It could be a useful study of ordered profiles, but varying the mass inside a block does not explore the tractability boundary.

This is the best case against the mirror: continuization preserves the paper’s order data, but the population measure is computationally idle. Still, it does not defeat the mirror outright. The income-cohort construction is credible, and the authors could recognize the ordered-measure formulation as their problem. The negative case is therefore weak on the user’s universal standard: it argues that the mirror may be a relabeling rather than a fruitful use of continuous-population methods, but it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario.
