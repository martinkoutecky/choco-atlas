The continuous mirror question

Given finite buyer and good type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i=\sum_{j\in J}\beta_j=1\), and explicitly encoded, strictly decreasing rational piecewise-linear utility curves \(u_{ij}(p)\), compute a least-price competitive equilibrium. Return prices \(p_j\), utilities \(U_i=\max(0,\max_{j\in J}u_{ij}(p_j))\), and assignment masses \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\). Require \(x_{ij}>0\) only when \(u_{ij}(p_j)=U_i\), unmatched buyer mass only when \(U_i=0\), and unmatched good mass only when \(p_j=0\); the returned price vector must be coordinatewise least among all equilibria.

The model it lives in

A two-sided market with finitely many complete buyer and good types carrying rational masses. Each buyer type has a rational piecewise-linear utility curve \(u_{ij}\) for each good type; decision variables \(x_{ij}\) allocate mass between types, and the output is an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and induced utilities \(U\) whose price vector is least coordinatewise.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers computing the least-price equilibrium associated with Theorem 2; it does not transfer the single-agent deletion identities or the theorem’s highest-equilibrium claims. It leaves the group-strategyproofness and ad-auction results aside; Theorem 3’s continuum of equilibria concerns the equilibrium set, not a continuous population.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity theorem asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 2 (Inductive Equilibrium Characterization), proved in this paper. For any market, it says that buyer \(i\)’s utility at the lowest competitive equilibrium equals the utility induced by the highest equilibrium prices after removing \(i\); good \(j\)’s price at the highest equilibrium equals the price induced by the lowest equilibrium utilities after removing \(j\). It also gives the bounds \(p^j(E_L)\le p^j(E_{H,-i})\), with equality if \(i\) is matched to \(j\) at \(E_L\), and \(U_i(E_H)\le U_i(E_{L}^{-j})\), with equality if they are matched at \(E_H\). The paper says these identities can be applied recursively to compute the extreme equilibria and give a constructive existence proof. It gives no running-time bound, so this is an algorithmic anchor, not an “in P” result.

The continuous problem I would defend is Lowest-Price Mass CE Computation. Its instance has finite sets of buyer types \(I\) and good types \(J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) with \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\), and a strictly decreasing, rational piecewise-linear utility curve \(u_i^j(p)\) for each type pair. The curves are explicit, with their breakpoints and slopes included in the input. A type is complete for this market: its buyers share the same utility curves across all good types; goods of a type are interchangeable. The question is to compute a coordinatewise least-price CE. A solution consists of prices \(p_j\), utilities \(U_i=\max(0,\max_j u_i^j(p_j))\), and assignment masses \(x_{ij}\ge0\). They must satisfy \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\alpha_i\), \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\); positive \(x_{ij}\) is allowed only when \(u_i^j(p_j)=U_i\); any unmatched buyer mass must have \(U_i=0\); and any unmatched good mass must have \(p_j=0\). The reported price vector must be no greater coordinatewise than that of any other CE.

A plausible regime is a large housing or goods market with many households and many units, but relatively few complete household and unit types: for example, millions of households and units represented by dozens or hundreds of utility-and-supply types. The mass \(x_{ij}\) is the fraction of the population of buyer type \(i\) assigned to good type \(j\); each actual household still receives at most one unit. Repeated units of a type have per-capita supply. This keeps the paper’s unit-demand, price-mediated equilibrium question while allowing type-level allocations to be fractional. The paper’s Theorem 1, attributed to Demange and Gale (1985), supplies the finite-market lattice result behind the least-price notion: the minimum and maximum of two equilibria are equilibria, and the equilibrium set has unique least and greatest elements. For rational masses, cloning types up to a common denominator gives a finite market; the least equilibrium is symmetric across identical copies and aggregates to type-level masses.

My tentative forecast for this bounded-input problem is Class A: at fixed or moderate type support, the mass representation may permit an exact algorithm polynomial in the number of types, curve breakpoints, and input bit length, independent of the number of cloned agents. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 2. The key technical question is whether the equilibrium and matching conditions for piecewise-linear, non-quasilinear utilities admit a compact tractable formulation.

The weakest point is that the theorem’s distinctive recurrence removes one named buyer or good. In the continuous population, removing one agent has zero mass, so that recurrence does not yield a nontrivial limit algorithm. The mirror is therefore to the paper’s underlying task—computing a lowest-price CE—not to a direct continuous version of its deletion identities. I would cover Theorem 2 and its equilibrium-computation problem alone; I would not claim that the paper’s group-strategyproofness results also carry over. The next questions are whether the least-equilibrium lattice and a polynomial algorithm hold for these type markets, and whether group-strategyproofness survives when a coalition can control positive mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that it loses the paper’s distinctive result at the point where the population becomes continuous. Theorem 2 gives a real recursive characterization, so I would not dismiss it as noncomputational. But it states no running time or complexity bound, and the paper allows arbitrary continuous utility functions without specifying a finite input encoding. Theorem 1, which supports the least-price notion, is attributed to Demange and Gale; it establishes a lattice, not an algorithm. Under ChoCo’s focus on complexity over encoded instances, there is no named complexity result here to continuize.

Theorem 2’s key move is removing one named buyer or good. In an atomless market, removing one buyer has zero mass, so the market’s prices are unchanged; the buyer’s utility equation reduces to ordinary best response at those prices. Removing an entire buyer type would have a nonzero effect, but that asks a different question: the effect of deleting a positive-mass group. The theorem’s identity does not establish that counterfactual. Neither that type-removal question nor the proposed mass CE computation is therefore a continuous version of the result that makes this paper distinctive.

The proposed mass model is coherent. A large housing market with repeated household and unit types is a credible regime, and fractional type-level assignments can represent integral assignments to individual households. I would count the cloning argument as evidence that the regime makes sense, not as an objection. But it supports the new problem—computing a least-price CE from rational type masses and encoded utility curves—rather than supplying its computational result. A piecewise-linear restriction also overlaps with earlier work giving finite-step procedures for buyer-optimal equilibrium prices, though that work does not settle the proposed support-size or bit-complexity question. [Alkan’s result](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0176268089900505) narrows the novelty claim; it is not a continuous computational collision.

A more faithful attempt might study the effect of deleting a whole type or ask whether a mechanism remains group-strategyproof against positive-mass coalitions. Those could be worthwhile questions, but they add new objects and guarantees rather than continuizing Theorem 2. Likewise, existing continuum matching models establish useful analytic context without answering this computational question. [Leshno’s continuum model](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/) is such context, not a collision.

So the negative case is strong against *this paper as a ChoCo complexity anchor*, but weak against the universal claim. I cannot honestly say that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: mass-market CE computation remains a sensible independent problem. The sharper criticism is that the proponent’s plausible problem is not a continuization of the paper’s central result.
