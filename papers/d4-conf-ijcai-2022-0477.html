What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that a population-continuous version of this model is plausible, but the paper supplies no named computational-complexity result to anchor it. Its opinions are already continuous; its population is finite.

The nearest results are Theorem 2 (proved here), which says that in well-ordered games the equilibria are exactly the global minima of the potential, form a convex set, and are unique when the non-null public influence functions are strictly convex; and Theorem 6 (proved here), which bounds the price of anarchy for well-ordered games by \(\max\{2\lambda,1-\lambda\}/\min\{2\lambda,1-\lambda\}\). Theorem 3 (also proved here) shows that the price of anarchy can be infinite for an unconstrained, convex isolation game with two agents. These are game-theoretic results about equilibria and efficiency, not results that place a computational problem in P, prove it hard, or give another complexity classification. So none qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be a large population in which many people share the same complete opinion-formation data: private belief, group-imposed opinion interval, private cost function, and influence functions toward each type. The support could be small relative to the population: many people in a few political or cultural groups with repeated beliefs and influence patterns. That preserves the paper’s ingredients and makes mass a meaningful quantity.

One concrete follow-on problem would be mean-field GOF equilibrium computation. Give \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_a\), rational group intervals \(I_a\), and rational piecewise-linear private costs \(g_a\) and symmetric public influence functions \(f_{ab}\). Let \(\nu_a\) be a probability distribution over opinions in \(I_a\) for type \(a\). A type-\(a\) individual’s cost of choosing \(x\) against those distributions is

\[ c_a(x;\nu)=g_a(|x-s_a|)+\sum_b \mu_b\int f_{ab}(|x-y|)\,d\nu_b(y). \]

Given rational \(\varepsilon>0\), the task is to output finite-support rational distributions \(\nu_a\) such that every opinion in their support is an \(\varepsilon\)-best response:

\[ c_a(x;\nu)\leq \min_{z\in I_a}c_a(z;\nu)+\varepsilon. \]

One could also report the corresponding \(\lambda\)-social cost,

\[ \lambda\sum_{a,b}\mu_a\mu_b \iint f_{ab}(|x-y|)\,d\nu_a(x)d\nu_b(y) +(1-\lambda)\sum_a\mu_a\int g_a(|x-s_a|)\,d\nu_a(x). \]

I think the authors would recognize this as a mean-field extension of their equilibrium problem. The main caveat is that a type cannot simply be replaced by one representative opinion: in an atomless population, individuals can deviate separately, so a type may need a distribution of opinions. The paper’s convex-potential theorem for finite profiles does not by itself establish that solving this distribution-valued problem is tractable. I therefore would not assign it to Class A, B, or C on the evidence here. This is a credible research question suggested by the paper, but it is not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch of subject: none of the results the proponent names is a computational result. Theorem 2 characterizes equilibria and gives conditions for uniqueness; Theorems 3 and 6 concern efficiency ratios. They do not classify the complexity of finding an equilibrium, computing a price of anarchy, or solving another computational problem. So there is no named computational result here for a continuous population to mirror.

Theorem 2 offers the best candidate for a follow-on. One could group agents into types with masses \(\mu_a\), impose a mean-field scaling on their interactions, and ask for an equilibrium over distributions of opinions. A version using one opinion per type might minimize a potential such as
\[ \Phi_\mu(x)=\sum_a \mu_a g_a(|x_a-s_a|) +\frac12\sum_{a,b}\mu_a\mu_b f_{ab}(|x_a-x_b|). \]
That is a plausible research problem. But it adds a scaling convention and a type-level model that the paper does not study. The paper assumes only that cost functions are continuous; it gives no finite representation for them, so it does not even specify the input model needed for a complexity question. Adding rational piecewise-linear costs would make computation well-posed, but that would be an additional modeling choice, not a computational mirror of Theorem 2. Allowing distributions \(\nu_a\), as the proponent suggests, makes the extension more faithful to nonatomic strategic behavior while moving farther from the paper’s finite-profile theorem.

Theorems 3 and 6 do not supply separate computational anchors. Theorem 3 exhibits unbounded price of anarchy in a two-agent instance; Theorem 6 proves a bound for a restricted class. A continuum analogue could ask about the same efficiency concepts, but neither theorem poses an algorithmic task. Their limits and bounds are analytic claims, and making agents into mass does not turn them into complexity results.

The proponent’s \(\varepsilon\)-best-response problem is therefore the strongest proposal, but it is best described as a new mean-field equilibrium-computation project suggested by this paper. It is not a continuous version of a computational problem the paper states or classifies. That distinction is decisive for a programme whose gap is computational complexity, rather than continuum modeling alone.

I would not claim the universal negative is airtight: a type-homogeneous opinion population is plausible, and the proposed equilibrium problem could be worthwhile in its own right. The case against is strongest on the narrower point that this paper provides no computational result to anchor a continuous mirror.
