The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(H\), a finite support \(T\) of student ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_h\) summing to \(1\), find a mass assignment \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}=\kappa_h\) that lexicographically maximizes \(q_k=\sum_{t,h:r_t(h)=k}x_{t,h}\). Require weak stability under the induced priorities: no positive mass can be exchanged from \(t\) at \(h'\) and \(u\) at \(h\) to \(t\) at \(h\) and \(u\) at \(h'\) when \(r_t(h)<r_t(h')\) and \(r_t(h)<r_u(h)\).

The model it lives in

Represent each complete student ranking as a type carrying mass \(\mu_t\), and each assignment as a flow \(x_{t,h}\) to schools with capacities \(\kappa_h\). Sequentially maximize the rank-mass coordinates \(q_1,\ldots,q_z\), fixing earlier optima; the paper's induced school priority ranks \(t\) above \(u\) at \(h\) when \(r_t(h)<r_u(h)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers student-oriented weak stability and preference-utility maximization in Theorems 1 and 2. It does not cover the experiments comparing tie-breaking or the cited egalitarian and minimum-regret objectives.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does tractability persist when school priorities are specified independently of student rankings?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem asserting that a problem is in P or NP-hard. The \(O(n^3)\) claim appears in §3.5, outside a numbered result. The closest named computational anchor—and my lead—is Theorem 2, proved here: in the paper’s symmetric-preference formulation, if \(w\) satisfies condition (1) and \(M^*\) is a matching maximizing \(\sum_{(s,h)\in M}w(s,h)\), then \(M^*\) maximizes the paper’s preference utility \(Q\). Theorem 1, also proved here, supplies the paired guarantee that this maximum-weight matching is weakly stable. I would not describe either theorem as a numbered in-P result; together with §3.5, they support the paper’s polynomial-time claim.

A concrete mirror is Student-Optimal School Assignment\(_\infty\). An instance has a finite school set \(H\), a support \(T\) of complete weak rankings of those schools, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_h\) summing to \(1\). Each type is a complete description for this problem: its students share the same ranking and are otherwise indistinguishable. The decision variable \(x_{t,h}\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\) assigned to school \(h\), with
\[ \sum_{h\in H}x_{t,h}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,h}=\kappa_h,\qquad x_{t,h}\ge 0. \]
Each person still gets one school; fractional \(x_{t,h}\) records how a type’s mass is divided.

Let \(r_t(h)\) be type \(t\)’s rank for school \(h\), and let \(q_k(x)=\sum_{t,h:r_t(h)=k}x_{t,h}\). Return a feasible \(x\) whose vector \((q_1(x),\ldots,q_z(x))\) is lexicographically maximal. This is a direct continuous version of the paper’s preference utility: for a finite population, its base-\((|S|+1)\) number encodes the lexicographic order of the integer rank counts; in the mirror, those counts become real-valued masses.

To carry over the paper’s one-sided setting, give each school the induced priority used in its symmetric-preference construction: school \(h\) strictly prefers type \(t\) to type \(u\) exactly when \(r_t(h)<r_u(h)\). Define weak stability at the mass level: a block exists if some positive mass of type \(t\) is assigned to a less-preferred school \(h'\), while some positive mass of type \(u\) occupies \(h\), with \(t\) strictly preferring \(h\) to \(h'\) and \(h\) having strict priority for \(t\) over \(u\).

The expected classification is Class A. The lexicographic objective can be solved by at most \(z\) linear programs: maximize \(q_k\) in turn, fixing the earlier optima each time. The optimum is weakly stable under the induced priorities. If a blocking pair existed, shifting a small equal mass from \(t\)’s worse assignment and \(u\)’s assignment at \(h\) to \(t\) at \(h\) and \(u\) at \(h'\) would improve the rank-mass vector lexicographically: the added mass appears at a rank better than either removed rank, unless \(u\)’s new rank is better still, in which case that added mass improves an earlier coordinate. Thus the LP’s optimum also answers the stable-matching question. Its size is polynomial in \(|H|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the rational input bit length \(L\).

The regime is a large annual school intake in a district where many applicants share a relatively small number of ranked school lists—for example, tens of thousands of students, dozens of schools, and hundreds or thousands of distinct preference types. Capacities scale with the cohort. This is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own school-assignment problem; the mass output describes assignment proportions, not fractional people. When masses and capacities are counts divided by a common population size, the scaled feasible set is a transportation polytope with integer margins, so an integral optimal vertex can represent an actual finite assignment.

My weakest point is that the paper defines \(Q\) for integer counts, not fractional masses, and does not state its polynomial-time claim as a numbered complexity theorem. Lexicographic optimization of rank masses is a principled continuation of its integer utility, but it is a new continuous formulation whose tractability argument must be proved. That is also why this is a focused mirror: it covers the paper’s student-oriented, maximum-preference-utility assignment result and its stability guarantee, not arbitrary two-sided priorities or the cited egalitarian and minimum-regret problems. Further questions include whether the LP approach survives when school priorities are independent of student rankings, and whether type-specific costs for changing preferences can be added without losing tractability.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror needs a modeling choice the paper does not make explicit. Its utility \(Q\) uses integer rank counts and a base of \(|S|+1\). For a continuous society there is no smallest positive mass, so no fixed scalar base preserves the same ordering. After normalization, the finite scalar’s limit keeps only the first-rank share. The proposed lexicographic objective on rank masses is a sensible repair, but it is a deliberate extension of the paper’s objective, not a uniquely forced continuation.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror. Lexicographic optimization is well-defined for the mass vectors, and it matches the finite utility’s ordering for each fixed population size. The stability result also has a natural mass version: a blocking exchange of any positive amount would improve the lexicographic rank-mass vector, so a lexicographic optimum cannot admit one. Neither theorem is therefore intrinsically tied to named individuals.

The case against a useful population model is weaker still. A large school intake with a moderate number of shared preference lists is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and capacities can scale with the cohort. The mirror can treat each list as a type and each assignment as a mass flow. That fits the paper’s one-sided-preference setting; it does not need to represent every detail of school choice to mirror that problem.

There is a real qualification about the anchors: Theorems 1 and 2 establish stability and utility properties, while the \(O(n^3)\) claim appears in §3.5 rather than a numbered complexity theorem. That makes the paper less direct as a complexity-theory anchor, but it still presents an algorithm, a polynomial-time claim, and an optimization reduction. I can make a case that the continuous objective needs a careful definition; I cannot honestly make the stronger case that no reasonable scenario yields a worthwhile mirror. The proposed mirror survives the best objections I can support.
