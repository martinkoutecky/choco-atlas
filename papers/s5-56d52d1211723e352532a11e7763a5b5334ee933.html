The continuous mirror question

Given a finite \(V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(V\) with \(\LOT(\mu)>0\), consider populations of \(N\) agents with \(N\mu_v\) agents of value \(v\), arriving in uniformly random order. What is the largest universal \(c\) for which one family of deterministic, truthful, individually rational online posted-price policies, not given \(\mu\), guarantees \(\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[U_N]/\LOT(\mu)\ge c\) for every fixed \(\mu\), where \(N\) ranges over sizes making all \(N\mu_v\) integral and \(\LOT(\mu)=\max_{p\ge0}\frac{\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v(v-p)^+}{\sum_{v\in V:v>p}\mu_v}\)?

The model it lives in

Each type is a value \(v\in V\) with population mass \(\mu_v\). Approximate this society by \(N\) agents carrying one indivisible item, with agents arriving in random order. A policy observes bids online, uses a growing but sublinear prefix to choose a posted-price cutoff, then offers the item to later eligible arrivals; its objective is expected winner surplus relative to \(\LOT(\mu)\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: in the random-order, single-item setting, the deterministic mechanism \(\mdet\) achieves a \(1/(625e)\)-approximation to \(\LOT\), the expected utility of the optimal \(p\)-lottery. This is an algorithmic approximation result, not a complexity-class result: the paper gives no named NP-hardness, membership-in-\(\mathrm P\), or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its fit to ChoCo is therefore narrower than a hardness anchor, but it is a concrete mechanism guarantee to mirror.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large service or consumer pool with one scarce unit to allocate. A type is a consumer’s value \(v\) for that unit; \(\mu_v\) is the fraction of the pool with value \(v\). The support \(V\) is finite, the masses are binary rationals, and \(\tau=|V|\). In finite approximations, the pool has \(N\mu_v\) consumers of each type, arriving in uniformly random order, with \(N\) growing while \(\mu\) stays fixed. Each consumer is individually small relative to the pool, but the mechanism still allocates the single unit to one person. A posted price \(p\) selects among consumers with \(v>p\); in the mass limit, the selected type has probability proportional to its eligible mass.

Here is the mirror question I would hand to a prover, named Mass-LOT Random-Order Utility. Given a rational type distribution \(\mu\), consider deterministic, truthful, individually rational online posted-price mechanisms that observe reports in random order but are not given \(\mu\); they may use the observed prefix to set their prices. Define the benchmark
\[ \LOT(\mu)=\max_{p\ge 0} \frac{\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v(v-p)^+} {\sum_{v\in V:\,v>p}\mu_v}. \]
For each fixed \(\mu\), take the limit along population sizes \(N\) for which every \(N\mu_v\) is integral. Determine the best universal limiting approximation ratio: does one mechanism family guarantee \(\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[U_N]/\LOT(\mu)\ge c\) for every such \(\mu\), and what is the largest possible \(c\)? A solution is a mechanism policy and a guarantee, or an upper bound ruling out a larger ratio. The continuous benchmark is well-defined because a \(p\)-lottery’s expected surplus is exactly the eligible-mass-weighted average in the formula.

I expect this question to be tractable in the fixed-\(\mu\), high-multiplicity limit. A positive fraction of a uniformly mixed population reveals the type proportions increasingly accurately; with finite support, the mechanism can use a sample to estimate \(\mu\), scan the support cutoffs for a price maximizing the displayed expression, then post that price to the remaining population. Evaluating the benchmark itself takes polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). This mirrors the paper’s lottery-price approach and its \(\LOT\) objective, while focusing on the mass regime where sampling noise disappears. It covers Theorem 2’s lottery/robustness guarantee, not its secretary-style capture of the top individual’s surplus.

The weak point is real: the continuum limit removes finite-sample uncertainty, and allocating a single indivisible unit from a non-atomic population needs an allocation lottery, defined here as the limit of selecting the first eligible arrival in finite populations. Also, Theorem 2 is stated for distinct values, whereas high multiplicity creates repeated values; extending its guarantee to such profiles would need to be established. Those caveats make this a modest mirror of the theorem’s lottery component, not a claim that the paper already solves the continuous problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real result to anchor on: it gives a named approximation guarantee for a deterministic mechanism. The strongest mirror is the proposed one: a finite-support value distribution \(\mu\), random-order arrivals, and the best limiting approximation to \(\LOT(\mu)\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity model, and the random-draw allocation can preserve the lottery benchmark’s expected utility. So the objection is not that the mirror is ill-posed.

The difficulty is that the continuous limit removes the paper’s online information problem. With fixed positive mass on each of finitely many values, a positive-mass sample reveals \(\mu\) exactly in the limit: by time \(t\), the observed mass of each type is \(t\mu_v\). Computing \(\LOT(\mu)\) then means scanning the support’s price cutoffs. The remaining mechanism can post an optimal price and draw an eligible recipient. This is a coherent limiting mechanism, but it leaves little of the paper’s random-order algorithmic challenge to study; it does not open a comparable complexity landscape over population instances.

The secretary-style contribution fares worse under the same model. If the highest value has positive mass, there are many agents tied at that value, so the gap \(v_1-v_2\) that the max phase captures disappears. If the highest value belongs to one named agent, that agent has zero mass and cannot be represented by \(\mu\). The lottery component survives as a draw from the eligible population, but retaining the unique top agent or the finite-population uncertainty requires adding a tagged individual, arrival-time marks, or finite-size corrections. Those may motivate useful research, but they move the question back toward finite-agent online mechanism design rather than a continuous-population computation.

I would not claim this proves that no application of the mirror is worthwhile: a service pool might value the limiting lottery analysis. The negative case is narrower. The best version preserves the benchmark, but its continuum either smooths away the paper’s central random-order difficulty or needs finite-agent structure to restore it. That makes it a weak fit for ChoCo’s computational programme.
