The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper has no numbered result about computational complexity or an algorithm’s running time.

The closest numbered results are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in the paper. Proposition 1 identifies the probabilities of the nonempty sets of schools that give a student positive value, under the complete-variation assumption. Proposition 2 identifies the conditional joint distribution of differences in log values within each such set. These are results about econometric identification, not about solving a computational problem. Appendix A’s equilibrium-existence and finite-convergence discussion is the nearest algorithmic material, but it is unnumbered and gives no complexity bound.

There is a plausible *new* mirror here, though it cannot serve as an anchor for this paper’s results. Take a large annual applicant cohort in which many students share a complete type: the same school values, grade-shock distributions, and other decision-relevant attributes. Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population share, scale school capacities per applicant, and let the action be a school choice or ranked list. Given a selection mechanism, ask for a type-to-action mass allocation and exam thresholds that form an equilibrium: each type chooses an action maximizing expected utility given the resulting success probabilities, and the induced masses fill the scaled capacities. The output could include the equilibrium allocation and its expected welfare.

This is recognizable as the population version of the paper’s strategic college-choice and selection model. Large, repeated admission cohorts with a relatively small number of shared applicant profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But the paper does not analyze that regime: its equilibrium conditions are finite-sample conditions, and its named propositions establish identification rather than equilibrium computation. So I would not claim that this mirror is one of the paper’s computational results, or assign it a tractability class on the paper’s evidence. The positive case is a research direction suggested by the model, not a case anchored in a qualifying result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent did not actually raise an anchor-compliant result. Proposition 1 identifies probabilities of sets of positively valued schools; Proposition 2 identifies distributions of log-value differences. Both are statistical identification results: they say which objects can be recovered from observed choices under assumptions, not how hard a computational problem is or how fast an algorithm runs. Appendix A’s finite best-response convergence argument is the nearest computational material, but it is unnumbered, limited to a simplified game, and gives no complexity bound. So there is no named computational result in this paper for a continuous mirror to inherit.

The proposed mass-equilibrium question has a plausible large-cohort story. Annual applicant cohorts can be large, and a continuum model of choices, capacities, and exam thresholds could be worth studying. But it changes a consequential part of the paper’s model. Under the paper’s complete-information assumption, each student’s realized preference shock is known to the players and directly affects that student’s choice. Since those shocks are continuously distributed, exact decision-relevant types generally have zero mass. Grouping students by shock *distribution* and assigning a common mass response instead changes the information structure and equilibrium problem. Keeping realized shocks requires a continuum of types and a way to encode their distribution, beyond the programme’s stated finite-type input. Either route may define a new model; neither mirrors Propositions 1 or 2.

That distinction is the strongest negative case: the paper’s results concern identification and empirical counterfactuals, while the proposed continuum problem concerns computation of a different equilibrium model. I would not claim the paper rules out a worthwhile population-equilibrium project. The negative case is decisive against treating this paper as an anchored continuization result, but weaker against a separate theory paper inspired by its model.
