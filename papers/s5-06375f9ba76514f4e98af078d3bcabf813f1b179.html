What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and Proposition 3 concern policy performance; Propositions 1 and 2 concern random-graph structure. No surviving continuous computational question covers these results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that the paper studies a plausible high-multiplicity regime. In its kidney exchange model, an \(E\) type can match an \(H\) type with probability \(p\), \(E\)-\(E\) matches occur with probability \(q\), and \(H\)-\(H\) matches are impossible. The types share those compatibility laws and a departure rate \(d\). As the market grows, arrivals scale as \(m\) for \(E\) and \((1+\lambda)m\) for \(H\), while the type set stays fixed. That is a credible setting for representing the population by type masses and matching decisions by flows of mass.

The paper’s strongest supporting result is Theorem 1, proved here: greedy matching is asymptotically optimal, while batching and patient matching are not. Its definition requires that, for every \(\epsilon>0\), once \(m\) is large enough, switching to any other policy improves any type’s match rate and expected waiting time by at most \(\epsilon\). Proposition 3, also proved here, gives the limiting rates and waits: greedy and patient matching both approach match rates \((1/(1+\lambda),1)\) for \((H,E)\); greedy waits approach \((\lambda d/(1+\lambda),0)\), while patient waits approach \((d,0)\). These results make the population-scale policy question concrete. Propositions 1 and 2, proved here, further show why the two-type structure is useful: the model has limiting maximum-matching size \(2/(2+\lambda)\) and vanishing fraction without a partner, while a homogeneous single-type model cannot reproduce both properties.

But none of these is a qualifying anchor under your rules. Theorem 1 is an asymptotic policy-performance result; Proposition 3 gives limiting performance; Propositions 1 and 2 concern random-graph structure. The paper contains no named result classifying a computational problem as hard, tractable, FPT, or similar. So there are zero valid anchors, and I cannot supply a per-anchor continuous problem without misrepresenting the paper. A continuous mass-flow optimization over dynamic matching policies is a plausible follow-on, but it would be a new question, and in its natural form it is an analytic fluid or stochastic-control problem rather than the computational mirror ChoCo is seeking.

The soft spot in the positive case is therefore decisive: the high-multiplicity regime is convincing, but the paper offers no computational result for a continuous mirror to explain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against a mirror is that the paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 compares the asymptotic performance of specified matching policies; Proposition 3 gives limiting match rates and waiting times; Propositions 1 and 2 describe random graph limits. None states the complexity of an input problem or gives an algorithmic classification. They are results about stochastic performance, not computational complexity, so none supplies a qualifying anchor.

The strongest continuous question one could derive from Theorem 1 is whether greedy matching is optimal when the population is represented by type masses and matching is modeled as flows over time. For Proposition 3, it is how to compute the policies’ limiting performance from those masses. For Propositions 1 and 2, it is how the mass distribution determines the limiting matching and isolation fractions. These questions are natural, but the first is a mean-field or stochastic-control question, the second restates performance analysis, and the third is a static random-graph question. Recasting them continuously does not turn any into a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

A stronger follow-on would ask for an algorithm that chooses a welfare-maximizing dynamic policy from a supplied compatibility structure, arrival and departure parameters, and objective weights. But that needs choices the paper does not make: how policies are represented, which information they may use, what welfare trade-offs count, and what constitutes an input instance. Adding detailed compatibility profiles or individual clocks could make the problem computationally richer, but it would also make this a new policy-optimization project rather than a mirror of the paper’s named results.

The negative case is weaker as a universal claim than as a verdict on this paper. Its high-multiplicity regime is credible, and the paper already studies large-market limits. Those facts support the modeling regime; they do not create a missing computational result. So I can argue that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor, but I cannot honestly claim that no nearby continuous computational project would be worthwhile.
