The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its computational findings are simulation results in Tables 1–3. For example, Table 2 reports that Copeland was conditionally Arrow-fair in 66.6% of generated empirical profiles and strategy-proof in 97.5%. Those are empirical rates, not named complexity results. Arrow’s theorem and the manipulation results cited in the introduction are attributed to earlier work, not proved here as numbered results.

The strongest plausible positive mirror is a large design-user population choosing among a fixed set of candidate designs. A type is a complete ranking of those designs; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users with ranking \(t\). Given rational masses on the supported rankings, compute the mass-weighted Copeland group ranking and check its unanimity and IIA properties. That is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s aggregation setting, and the weighted calculation is tractable in \(m\) and support size \(\tau\). But extending the paper’s single-person manipulation test would require a positive-mass coalition, which changes the question.

So this gives a credible continuous-population analogue of part of the simulations, but it cannot satisfy the assignment’s required result-anchored case. There is no anchor for which to state one of the required continuous problems, and the paper’s simulated rates do not establish that any such problem is tractable, hard, or continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no named result to anchor a mirror: this paper proves no theorem, lemma, or corollary about computational complexity. Its main numbers are simulation rates for finite profiles. That is the strongest objection, and it rules out the required result-anchored case. The manipulation-complexity result cited in the introduction belongs to earlier work, not to this paper.

The proposed Copeland mirror is still a sensible high-multiplicity *model*. For a large population of design users, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction with ranking \(t\). Weighted pairwise totals determine Copeland’s ranking, and unanimity can be checked directly from the supported types. With \(\tau\) supported rankings, these calculations take polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(m\). That establishes a natural continuous version of profile aggregation, but it does not mirror the paper’s central simulation results: each continuous society produces a particular ranking and fairness verdict, not the reported probabilities across sampled finite profiles. Recovering those probabilities requires retaining a distribution over random profiles; that is a different computational question, not a continuization of the population alone.

The paper’s manipulation result fares worse under the population limit. A single person has zero mass, so changing their report cannot change a rule that takes only \(\mu\) as input. The paper’s individual-manipulation test therefore becomes vacuous. One could instead ask how much mass must coordinate to change the outcome. That is a coherent, potentially interesting mass-manipulation problem, but it replaces the paper’s individual question with a new one. The authors’ use of continuous design attributes and a Gaussian distribution over utility parameters does not fill this gap: those continuities concern designs and preference generation, while the simulated societies remain finite.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo anchor: it offers no named computational result, and its most natural continuous analogue either reduces to routine weighted Copeland aggregation or makes individual manipulation vanish. I would not claim, however, that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from this setting. A coalition-mass version could be worth studying; the honest objection is that it would be a new project, not a mirror of a computational result this paper established.
