The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper: none of its theorems or corollaries says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. That means I cannot honestly supply the kind of anchor your instructions require. The paper’s main results are analytic characterizations.

The closest support for a positive case is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that for an interior alternative \(x\), \(x\) is in the critical optima set \(IO(W,D)\) if and only if it satisfies the pivotal gradient restrictions (PGR): for every voter subset \(L\) and every coalition \(M\) pivotal in \(L\)—meaning that under every partition of \(L\setminus M\), at least one side joined to \(M\) is decisive—the zero vector belongs to the semi-positive cone generated by the gradients of the voters in \(L\) whose gradients lie in the span of \(M\)’s gradients. Corollary 1 says PGR is necessary for an interior core point, and sufficient when preferences are semi-convex. These are substantial results, but they characterize geometry; they do not give algorithms or complexity bounds.

The strongest continuous-population question I can motivate from them is Continuous \(\alpha\)-Core-at-\(x\). Consider a large policy electorate whose alternatives lie in \(W\subseteq\mathbb R^w\), with \(x\) an interior candidate. Its finitely many utility types \(t\) have rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\), and rational gradients \(p_t=\nabla u_t(x)\). Assume the utility types are semi-convex, as in the paper’s setting where critical optimality characterizes core membership. A coalition is decisive when it contains at least an \(\alpha\) fraction of the population. Since the population is atomless, a coalition can take any required fraction of a type’s mass.

The problem is to compute
\[ H=\max_{d\in\mathbb R^w\setminus\{0\}} \sum_{t:\,p_t\cdot d>0}\mu_t. \]
The requested solution is the exact value of \(H\), with a direction attaining it. The candidate is a core point exactly when \(H<\alpha\): if \(H\ge\alpha\), at least an \(\alpha\)-mass coalition can improve along a common direction; under the semi-convexity assumption, the gradient test also rules out other blocking improvements. For this fixed-\(x\) question, the gradients capture the information the result uses; the underlying types are the agents’ utility functions.

The regime would be a large electorate making a multidimensional policy choice, with many voters but relatively few recurring utility types—for example, a population represented by a small set of preference archetypes. That keeps the paper’s policy space and core question, while making the population itself the continuous object. The paper’s existing continuous object is \(W\), the alternative space; that alone is outside ChoCo’s target. The authors could plausibly recognize this as a population version of their core-point question, especially because the paper explicitly treats quota voting rules. But this proposed problem is an extension, not a result proved in the article.

My expectation is tractability for fixed \(w\), by enumerating the sign regions of the hyperplanes \(p_t\cdot d=0\). With variable \(w\), I would bet on hardness transferring from the corresponding finite weighted electorate: the search complexity appears to lie in the dimension, not in the population multiplicities. That is only a conjectural direction; this paper establishes neither the discrete hardness nor the continuous complexity. Natural follow-ups include parameterized complexity in \(w\), approximation of \(H\), and whether the pivotal-gradient characterization extends to arbitrary mass-based voting rules or to cycle-set questions.

The weak point is decisive: the article contains no computational anchor, and the proposed mirror changes its finite electorate into an atomless one. It is a plausible, sharply stated ChoCo question prompted by Theorem 1, not a continuous counterpart of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor has a decisive problem: Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 characterize core points through gradient geometry; neither states a computational problem or gives a complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1’s “if and only if” is about membership in \(IO(W,D)\), not an algorithmic classification.

The proposed \(\alpha\)-Core-at-\(x\) question is also exactly the weighted open hemisphere problem. Drop types with zero gradient and treat each remaining \(p_t\) as a point with weight \(\mu_t\). Then \(H\) is the maximum weight in an open hemisphere, since \(p_t\cdot d>0\) means that point lies in the hemisphere selected by \(d\). Normalizing the weights to sum to one changes nothing. Johnson and Preparata studied this problem, originally motivated by voters’ preferences over political issues; they established NP-completeness when dimension varies and polynomial-time solvability in fixed dimension. Their report also treats weighted points, matching the proposed type masses. [The 1978 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(78)90006-3), [the 1977 report](https://hdl.handle.net/2142/74081). So the proposed complexity landscape is already known for this exact mathematical problem.

A broader mirror of PGR for arbitrary voting rules would need a finite encoding of decisiveness over a continuum of voters. The paper supplies no such encoding. If the rule depends only on coalition mass, the natural version is a quota rule and returns to the hemisphere problem. A richer rule representation might define a worthwhile new computational problem, but it would be an added modelling choice, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

I would reject the proposed anchor as a ChoCo target. The limit of this negative case is that it cannot rule out every useful follow-up inspired by the paper: for example, core existence with a carefully chosen succinct rule and utility encoding could be new. That would be a new research question, though, and the article itself offers no named computational result for it to mirror.
