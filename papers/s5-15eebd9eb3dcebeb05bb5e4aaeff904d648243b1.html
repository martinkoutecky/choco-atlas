The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(R\), rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), initial product sets \(P_r\), product-independent thresholds \(\theta_r\), and rational influence shares \(\alpha_{s,r}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s\alpha_{s,r}\leq 1\), consider states \(x_{r,q}\) recording the mass of type \(r\) that has adopted product \(q\). An unadopted portion of type \(r\) may adopt \(q\in P_r\) when \(\sum_s\alpha_{s,r}x_{s,q}/\mu_s\geq\theta_r\); types with no incoming influence may adopt as in the paper. Each adoption is irreversible, and any permitted portion of a type may adopt. Among states reachable by such adoption schedules in which no further adoption is possible, what is the minimum of \(\sum_r x_{r,q^\star}\) for a designated target product \(q^\star\)?

The model it lives in

A population distributed over types with common initial product options, thresholds, and cohort-level influence shares. The decision variables are adopted masses \(x_{r,q}\); the objective is to minimize target-product mass across reachable terminal states.

The objection that survived

The cohort-level influence model changes the graph’s causal structure, so the paper’s PARTITION reduction and its approximation gap do not carry over to this continuous problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the minimum-spread question of Theorem 6.3(iii) and the all-adopted question of Theorem 6.1. It leaves the arbitrary-network characterizations, individual-node adoption questions, and the paper’s other results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For unrestricted \(\tau=|R|\), is the minimum-spread problem polynomial-time solvable, fixed-parameter tractable, or hard?
  • For fixed \(\tau\) and a fixed number of products, can adoption orders be enumerated and each resulting case solved by linear programming?
  • Can a rounding or approximation result connect this model to finite block networks, and what parameter could replace \(n\) in an approximation guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror I believe in is Block-Continuum MIN-ADOPTION. Take a finite set of types \(R\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_r\), where \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). A type records its initially available products, its product-independent threshold \(\theta_r\), and its influence profile: \(\alpha_{s,r}\) is the share of an \(r\)-agent’s incoming influence supplied by type \(s\), with \(\sum_s\alpha_{s,r}\leq1\). Let \(x_{s,q}\) be the mass of type \(s\) that has adopted product \(q\). Type \(r\)’s exposure to \(q\) is \(\sum_s\alpha_{s,r}x_{s,q}/\mu_s\). Whenever that exposure reaches \(\theta_r\), any remaining mass of type \(r\) that can choose \(q\) may adopt it. Adoption is irreversible; a cohort may split across products when the process permits it. A final state is one in which no remaining mass can adopt any available product.

Given such an instance and target product \(q^\star\), the problem asks for the least possible \(\sum_r x_{r,q^\star}\) over reachable final states. A solution is a legal adoption schedule reaching a final state with minimum target-product mass. Equivalently, one can ask the decision question for a rational bound \(B\): is there such a schedule with \(\sum_r x_{r,q^\star}\leq B\)?

The main anchor is Theorem 6.3(iii), proved in this paper: for at least three products, even with product-independent thresholds, it is NP-hard to approximate MIN-ADOPTION within a ratio better than \(\Omega(n)\), where \(n\) is the number of nodes. In the paper, MIN-ADOPTION asks for the minimum number of nodes adopting a specified product in any final network. The reduction from PARTITION creates a gap: a yes-instance has a final network with 3 adopters of the target product, while a no-instance forces \(M+5\) adopters in every final network, for a polynomially chosen \(M=\Omega(|V|)\).

A useful second anchor is Theorem 6.1, also proved here: FINAL is NP-complete even with two products and product-independent thresholds. FINAL asks whether, from a given initial network, there exists a final network in which every node has adopted some product. Its continuous counterpart, Block-Continuum FINAL, asks whether a legal schedule can assign all population mass to products. These two questions use the same type and adoption model; only their objective differs.

The regime is a large school-choice or communications market organized into a modest number of cohorts. Members of a cohort face the same options and resistance threshold, and receive the same proportions of influence from each cohort. The population might number in the millions while the number of distinct types \(\tau\) is dozens or hundreds. Rational masses are the active variables: the question optimizes how much population adopts each product and in what order the adoption conditions become available. This is a block-network extension of the paper’s weighted threshold model, not a claim that ordinary sparse social graphs can be compressed to a few marginal type counts. The paper itself leaves hardness on equitable networks open, which makes restricted network structure a recognizable question in its own terms.

I expect the PARTITION hardness to dissolve in the low-type regime, rather than transfer directly. In the paper’s reductions, a whole item node must choose one product; continuum mass can split, so the exact-partition constraint may disappear. The \(\Omega(n)\) approximation bound also cannot simply be carried over: a mass problem has no population count \(n\) unless one specifies a clone-size or type-count parameter. My tentative classification is Class A for fixed \(\tau\) and \(|P|\), since one might enumerate activation orders and solve a linear program for each; for unrestricted \(\tau\), I lean toward Class C, where complexity could come from the combinatorics of activation orders among types. Neither is established by the cited theorems. The questions this raises are whether the problem is polynomial or fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), whether a rounding theorem relates it to finite block networks, and what parameter—if any—replaces \(n\) in an approximation guarantee.

The weakest point is the model change required to get few types. The paper’s input is a particular graph; in a sparse graph, agents with different neighborhoods may require different types, potentially making \(\tau\) nearly \(n\). The block influence model replaces those individual links with cohort-level exposure, and fractional adoption changes the all-or-nothing choices used by the reductions. So this is an extension, not a result-preserving translation of Theorem 6.3(iii). What survives is a precise computational question about worst-case product spread under the paper’s threshold and irreversible-adoption semantics, with the population—not the product set, time, or outcome space—as the continuous object. This mirror covers Theorems 6.3(iii) and 6.1; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s unique-outcome characterizations or individual-node adoption questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s computational questions use the network position of each node as part of the input. A type that preserves the adoption behavior must capture the node’s options, threshold, and relevant incoming influence. On the instances behind the hardness results, those positions are distinct: the PARTITION items have different edge weights, and the later chain nodes occupy different places in the propagation path. Preserving that information leaves essentially one type per node. Grouping nodes by options and threshold instead erases the connections that determine which adoptions are possible.

That is the problem with Theorem 6.3(iii). Its gap comes from specific item nodes feeding specific gates, followed by a path that amplifies a forced adoption. In the proposed block model, item mass can split between products. That removes the all-or-nothing assignment used by the reduction, while the path and gate become cohort-level influence rules. This is a coherent mean-field diffusion problem, but the theorem’s graph has been replaced by a different interaction model. The same issue affects the proposed mirror of Theorem 6.1: FINAL asks whether a particular sequence of whole-node adoptions can reach a state where every node has adopted. The continuous schedule can divide a type across products and optimize over fractional adoption amounts. That may be a useful question, but it does not preserve the reduction’s node-by-node choices.

A stronger alternative would replicate each graph position into a large cohort. If replicas remain in separate copies of the original network, adoption states within a copy are correlated, so the masses of vertex types alone do not describe the process. If replicas instead interact uniformly across types, those correlations disappear because the network has become a block-mixing model. Either construction could motivate a new project, but neither yields a direct population continuization of the paper’s arbitrary-network results. In the exact version, the type count grows with the graph; in the compressed version, the graph’s causal structure changes.

This is a serious objection to treating these theorems as strong mirrors of the paper. It does not, however, establish the universal negative the prompt asks for. A large market or school system with genuinely homogeneous cohorts and cohort-level influence is plausible, and the paper’s authors could recognize its block-continuum adoption questions as theirs. The negative case is strongest against the claim that Theorems 6.1 and 6.3(iii) survive as mirrors of the paper’s graph problem; it is weak against the existence of any worthwhile continuous-population question in this area.
