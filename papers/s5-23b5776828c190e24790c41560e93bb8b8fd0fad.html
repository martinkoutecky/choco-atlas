The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Section 6.3 is a reported result, even though it is not a complexity theorem: both models produced efficient allocations in all 30 trials, while their truthfulness rates differed sharply. That combination motivates a concrete computational question. Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over bidder types—where a type includes valuation, model, prompt, and report behavior—how much mass of reports must change before the allocation loses a specified fraction of its welfare? For example, define
\[ \rho_\alpha(\mu)=\min \sum_{t,b,b'}x_{t,b,b'} \]
subject to the changed reports reducing welfare to at most \(\alpha\) times its baseline, with \(x_{t,b,b'}\) bounded by the mass of type \(t\) reporting \(b\). Exact, approximation, and parameterized algorithms for this threshold would make the model-dependent truthfulness finding a population-level allocation-robustness problem.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large procurement platform with many bidders drawn from recurring valuation, model, and protocol classes. Those are complete types when members share the preferences, behavior, and intervention costs used by the problem. Scale supply with the market and measure allocations by type-level averages; individual agents can still receive integral outcomes across repeated transactions. The society is the bidder population itself, and \(\tau\) is the number of supported bidder types. This is not a continuum of experimental trials.

The proponent is right that the paper has no numbered theorem giving a complexity bound, and this mirror extends its three-bidder experiment to a larger market. That makes the connection less direct than a mirror of a proved algorithmic result. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists: the paper’s named empirical finding concerns a computational allocation mechanism, and the continuous question studies how robust that mechanism’s outcome is to the mass of model-conditioned misreports. If the programme requires an existing complexity theorem as the only admissible anchor, this case is weak; under its broader remit to formulate computational questions over continuous societies, the proposed mirror survives.
