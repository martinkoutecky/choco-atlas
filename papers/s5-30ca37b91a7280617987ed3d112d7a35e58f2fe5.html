What the mirror covers

Neither the lower-bound theorem nor Theorem 3 yields a computational mirror. The proposed type-mass model with per-capita supplies is a possible extension, but it is not anchored in a computational result of the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous mirror at the model level, but this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its results concern existence and worst-case fairness guarantees, not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest result to a hardness anchor is the introduction’s restatable lower-bound theorem: for \(n\) agents in \(k\ge2\) groups, with \(n\ge k+4\), it constructs binary additive valuations for which every EF\(c\) allocation has \(c\ge \frac{1}{96}\sqrt{\frac{n-k}{2}-1}\). That is an extremal existence bound, not a computational hardness result. Theorem 3 bounds private-envy discrepancy by \(O(\min\{t,\sqrt{t\log k}\})\) when \(k\) is a prime power, with an additional factor \(O(\frac{\log m}{\log k})\) otherwise; it proves an existence bound, not a polynomial-time algorithm. The paper explicitly says the upper-bound proof is nonconstructive and that polynomial-time construction is unknown. Corollary 1 likewise gives an EF\(c\) existence guarantee. Theorem 1 is attributed to Manurangsi and Měka, and Theorem 2 is a cited necklace-splitting result. None is an eligible computational anchor under your criteria.

A reasonable unanchored follow-up question would take a population of recurring couples, with \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of distinct complete couple profiles. Let each profile \(\theta\) specify both partners’ binary additive valuations over a fixed set \(Q\) of item categories, and let rational \(\mu_\theta\) give its fraction of the population. For per-capita supplies \(b_q\), a solution assigns each profile’s mass across integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{Q}\): \(x_{\theta,B}\ge0\), \(\sum_Bx_{\theta,B}=1\), and \(\sum_{\theta,B}\mu_\theta x_{\theta,B}B_q\le b_q\). Ask for the smallest \(c\) such that every positive-mass assigned bundle is EF\(c\) against every bundle used by positive mass: for each partner’s valuation, at most \(c\) item units can be removed from the other bundle to eliminate envy. The population is continuous, while each couple still receives an integral bundle. This is a plausible analogue of the paper’s group-allocation problem, and the authors could recognize the fairness question. Its weak point is the necessary per-capita supply model: the paper studies a finite item set, so scaling supply with the population is a real co-modification.

That candidate cannot be tied to a named computational result in this paper, and I would not assign it a tractability class on this evidence. The honest anchor list is empty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the missing anchor. The lower-bound theorem gives instances where every allocation has large EF\(c\); it says nothing about the complexity of finding or recognizing such an allocation. Theorem 3, the upper-bound theorem, and Corollary 1 are existence guarantees. The paper explicitly leaves polynomial-time construction open. The cited necklace-splitting theorem concerns a continuous item space, and the discrepancy lower bound is prior work. None is a named computational result whose complexity a population mirror could classify.

The proposed recurring-couples model is coherent, but with a fixed set \(Q\) of item categories and per-capita supplies \(b_q\), it has a built-in common bundle: give every group \(\lfloor b_q\rfloor\) units of each category \(q\), then distribute the residual \(b_q-\lfloor b_q\rfloor\) units as at most one extra unit of category \(q\) to some fraction of the population. Every bundle is the common bundle plus a subset of at most \(|Q|\) extras. Removing those extras from any envied bundle leaves the common bundle, which every agent weakly prefers to their own bundle’s common part. Thus EF\(|Q|\) is immediate, independent of the number of couples or their profile masses. For fixed \(Q\), the paper’s growing-population guarantee has collapsed to a constant-size construction.

Allowing \(Q\) to grow could preserve a substantive allocation question, and the paper’s open interest in efficient construction gives that direction some motivation. But it would be a new computational project: none of the paper’s named results classifies that problem, and the text does not establish that its population-continuous version is worthwhile. That is where the negative case is weakest. I can make a strong case that this paper has no anchored ChoCo mirror, and that the proposed fixed-category mirror degenerates; I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile new model with a growing item universe.
