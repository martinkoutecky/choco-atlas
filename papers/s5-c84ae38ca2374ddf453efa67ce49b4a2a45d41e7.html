The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large technical registry with many independent, one-item contributors but relatively few recurring curator profiles. A type could record a curator’s field, signal distribution, DG13 report-history class, and citation-network role. The society is the mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(N\gg\tau\). To preserve CitedTCR’s central mechanism, the citation network would be represented by a type-level transition kernel \(K\). For a proposal whose references induce personalization \(b\), its PPR distribution satisfies
\[ \pi=(1-\alpha)K^\top\pi+\alpha b. \]
Eligible curator mass is then selected in proportion to its PPR weight, and expected DG13 rewards are integrated over pairs of curator types. This retains citation-based assignment and peer-prediction rewards; it does not continuize token outcomes or time.

An unanchored computational question suggested by that model is Mass-CitedTCR Reward Evaluation. Given rational type masses, a type-level citation kernel, proposal personalization, eligible fractions, signal-pair distributions, past-report distributions, and reporting strategies, compute the exact PPR-weighted assignment distribution and each type’s expected DG13 reward. For example, if \(\widehat\nu_u\) is the eligible PPR mass of type \(u\), \(q_{tu}\) is the current joint signal law, and \(h_t\) is type \(t\)’s past-report distribution, the expected reward for type \(t\) is
\[ R_t=\sum_u\widehat\nu_u\left[ \sum_{a,b\in\{0,1\}}q_{tu}(a,b)\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_t(a)=\sigma_u(b)\} -\sum_{r,r'}h_t(r)h_u(r')\mathbf{1}\{r=r'\} \right]. \]
The solution is the assignment distribution and reward vector. For finite support and explicit rational data, I would expect this evaluation problem to be tractable by solving the PPR linear system and summing over type pairs. That is a plausible computational mirror of CitedTCR’s components, but it is a proposed question, not a result established by this paper.

The anchor audit is the limiting fact: Theorem 1 is the paper’s only numbered theorem, and it is proved in Appendix B. It states that DG13 satisfies strong truthfulness: under the paper’s assumptions of positively correlated binary signals and its specified feasible reporting strategies, a curator’s expected reward under truthful reporting is at least that under arbitrary strategies, with the stated opposite-strategy equality case. This is an incentive property, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Algorithms 1 and 2 describe the protocol, and Section 4 reports simulations, but neither supplies a named computational-complexity result. So there are zero qualifying anchors, and I can’t honestly provide a compliant per-anchor mirror.

The weakest point in the positive case is that PPR depends on the citation graph at node level. Aggregating it to \(K\) is exact only for suitably interchangeable, or lumpable, citation roles; otherwise the type system may need to distinguish so many nodes that \(\tau\) approaches \(N\). The paper’s one-user-per-node assumption also helps define the regime but makes it less general. Thus this is a plausible high-multiplicity extension the authors might recognize, but the paper itself offers no named computational result to anchor the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor audit is decisive: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is a game-theoretic incentive result about DG13’s expected rewards under correlated binary signals. Algorithms 1 and 2 describe the protocol, and Section 4 reports simulations; none establishes the complexity of a computational problem. So there are no qualifying anchors for a continuous mirror.

The strongest fallback is the proposed Mass-CitedTCR Reward Evaluation. But its type-level kernel \(K\), signal-pair laws, and report-history distributions already summarize the graph and behavior that determine the answer. The task then evaluates PPR weights and expected rewards for a different, aggregated model. That may be useful as a model-scaling exercise, but it is not a complexity question the paper poses, and it does not mirror Theorem 1’s incentive claim. A stronger proposal—such as finding the smallest mass of altered reports that flips acceptance—would add a strategic optimization problem absent from the paper.

The faithful way to preserve the paper’s mechanism exposes the obstacle: curator assignment depends on particular citation nodes, proposal-specific reference sets, eligibility, and individual report histories. Since the model identifies each user with one content node, a type that preserves all those distinctions will generally be a singleton. Grouping nodes into a few types requires extra assumptions, such as interchangeable citation roles or a type-level stochastic graph. Those assumptions may describe a plausible registry, but they change the graph model that drives CitedTCR’s assignment mechanism; the resulting evaluation problem is a new model, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The negative case is strongest against this paper as a source of programme anchors, not against every possible continuous CitedTCR study. A large registry with repeated curator profiles and a well-motivated type-level citation process is conceivable. If the programme wanted to study that new model, the mass-based question might be worth pursuing. The paper itself, though, supplies no computational result for it, and the proposed reward calculation does not fill that gap.
