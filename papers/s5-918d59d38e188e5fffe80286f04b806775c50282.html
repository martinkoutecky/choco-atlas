The continuous mirror question

Given finite valuation types \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\), a compact polygonal estate \(C\), a scaled per-capita capacity field \(B\), and absolutely continuous valuations \(V_t\), define \(\mathcal{Q}_t(r)=\{q\subseteq C:q\text{ is a parallel square and }V_t(q)\ge rV_t^{\mathrm{Squares}}(C)\}\). Decide whether there exist measures \(\lambda_t\) over whole-square placements with \(\lambda_t(\mathcal{Q}_t(r))=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\int \mathbf{1}_q(x)\,d\lambda_t(q)\le B(x)\) almost everywhere; equivalently, maximize \(r\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic linear measure-packing model over valuation types: \(\mu_t\) is type mass, \(\lambda_t\) distributes that mass among integral parallel-square bundles, \(B\) represents scaled land supply, and the objective maximizes the common relative value \(r\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is that this paper does not supply a fully compliant computational anchor. Its numbered results are geometric existence, impossibility, and fairness-guarantee statements; none asserts NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), fixed-parameter tractability, or a comparable complexity classification. The remark that minimum square-covering is solvable in linear time is attributed to Bar-Yehuda et al., not proved here. Thus, under the strict anchor rule, the paper has no qualifying computational result.

The best positive rescue is nevertheless quite natural. I would use Claim 5.3 as the lead constructive anchor, with the caveat that it is an algorithmic fairness result rather than a complexity theorem. Claim 5.3, proved in this paper, says that for every compact cake \(C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}\) and every \(n\ge1\),
\[ \operatorname{proprel}(C,n,\text{Parallel squares})\ge \frac{1}{8n-6}. \]
Equivalently, after normalizing every agent’s best available square to value \(8n-6\), the paper gives a procedure that allocates pairwise-disjoint parallel squares, each worth at least \(1\) to its recipient. The construction gives each agent \(4n-3\) candidate squares, repeatedly selects a smallest candidate, and removes at most four overlapping candidates from every other agent. The geometric bound guarantees that enough candidates remain.

The corresponding continuous problem would be:

\[ \textsc{Continuous Relative Fair-Square Packing}. \]

An instance consists of a finite set of valuation types \(T\), masses \(\nu_t\ge0\) with total population \(M=\sum_t\nu_t\), a compact polygonal estate \(C\), a per-capita land-capacity measure \(B\) that scales with \(M\), and for each type \(t\) an absolutely continuous valuation \(V_t\). The usable pieces are parallel squares contained in \(C\). For a proposed fairness level \(r\), type \(t\) accepts exactly the squares
\[ \mathcal Q_t(r)= \{q\subseteq C:q\text{ is a parallel square and } V_t(q)\ge rV_t^{\mathrm{Squares}}(C)\}. \]

A solution is a family of measures \(\lambda_t\) over square placements such that:

\[ \lambda_t(\mathcal Q_t(r))=\nu_t \]

for every type \(t\), and the aggregate occupancy of the estate respects its scaled capacity:
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\int \mathbf 1_q(x)\,d\lambda_t(q)\le B(x) \quad\text{for almost every }x\in C. \]

Thus every infinitesimal household receives one whole square, not a fractional square; only the mass of indistinguishable households is divisible. The optimization version maximizes \(r\).

This is a credible mirror because the type is a complete household profile: the same valuation density, normalization, and geometric requirement. The intended regime is a large housing or land-development programme with millions of households but relatively few recurring valuation types—for example, standardized household classes differing by location preferences, sunlight valuation, or access to infrastructure. The estate and available plots scale with population, so the continuum is not being imposed on a seven-person committee or a fixed finite cake. Rational masses \(\nu_t/M\) expand directly into finite high-multiplicity populations, while atomic solutions contract back to allocations of repeated households.

I would expect the fixed-\(r\) feasibility version to be a Class A candidate when the estate and square placements admit a polynomial separation procedure, since it is a linear measure-packing problem. Exact optimization over arbitrary square placements may instead reveal a Class C phenomenon: the difficulty would come from geometric packing and separation, not from population multiplicity. The paper’s Claim 5.3 supplies useful structural evidence for the tractable approximation side, but not yet a complexity proof for this continuum problem.

The weakest point is decisive: Claim 5.3 is not itself a computational-complexity result, and the paper already permits continuously varying valuation measures over a geometric cake. The proposed mirror therefore extends the paper’s setting into a high-multiplicity population model, but it does not continuize a discrete hardness theorem. I would present it as a strong natural research question, not as a formally qualifying ChoCo case unless constructive algorithmic anchors are admitted.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the level of the anchor itself. This paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary giving a computational-complexity result. Claim 5.3 is a constructive fairness guarantee, not a statement about an encoded input, running time, approximation scheme, or complexity class. Its “algorithm” assumes arbitrary valuation measures and abstractly asks agents to draw suitable squares. Thus there is no named computational result here whose population-continuation would fill ChoCo’s stated gap.

The proposed mirror also changes the problem in a more fundamental way. In the paper, the feasible allocation is a finite family of pairwise-disjoint, positive-area squares, one for each agent. A genuine continuum population cannot retain that requirement. Any pairwise-disjoint family of nonempty squares in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\) is countable, since each square contains a distinct rational point. Hence a countably additive atomless population cannot assign almost every household a distinct whole square: assigning at most one household to each square covers only a countable, measure-zero set of households. Allowing a positive mass of households to receive the same square violates the paper’s disjointness requirement.

The measure \(\lambda_t\) does not repair this. The constraint
\[ \sum_t\int \mathbf 1_q(x)\,d\lambda_t(q)\le B(x) \]
controls aggregate coverage, not pairwise disjointness. Two positive masses can be assigned overlapping squares while satisfying this inequality; that is fractional use of the physical square, not merely fractional mass of otherwise integral bundles. Enforcing genuine disjointness requires a nonlinear support-packing condition, after which the atomless continuum allocation degenerates as above.

Scaling \(B\), duplicating the estate, or permitting overlapping plots can produce a meaningful new model, but each abandons the paper’s problem: it either gives households replicated resources, removes geometric competition, or continuizes the outcome/resource space. Shrinking squares to accommodate a continuum instead drives their value to zero under the paper’s absolutely continuous valuations.

A finite high-multiplicity version with repeated valuation types is perfectly sensible, and may be worth studying. But that is a discrete high-multiplicity cake-packing problem, not a nontrivial continuous-population mirror of Claim 5.3. I therefore find the honest negative case strong for this paper: no qualifying computational anchor exists, and the proposed continuous formulation survives only by replacing the paper’s indivisible geometric allocation with a different fractional packing problem.
