The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchor-based positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result. Its numbered results characterize asymptotic wisdom properties; none establishes that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

The closest candidate is Theorem 5.3, proved in this paper. It says that for a sequence of equal-neighbor matrices, the absence of a prominent individual is equivalent to one-time wisdom and to pre-uniform wisdom. The paper also derives that one-time wisdom then implies finite-time wisdom, and, for primitive equal-neighbor sequences, wisdom. That is a substantial characterization, but it is not a complexity result: its input is an entire sequence of growing matrices, and its conclusion is an asymptotic equivalence. Theorem 2.2 similarly characterizes one-time and finite-time wisdom through limiting maximum column averages, and infinite-time wisdom through the limiting largest stationary-distribution entry. Neither gives an algorithm for an encoded instance.

An exploratory mirror could take types to be organizational roles with identical influence behavior, mass \(\mu_t\) to be each role’s population share, and a row-stochastic kernel \(K_{tt'}\) to specify how much weight a member of role \(t\) gives to role \(t'\). Each agent could distribute that weight equally across the members of the target role. The question would be whether the resulting growing societies preserve wisdom after each fixed number of rounds, or uniformly over time. This is a plausible finite-type DeGroot model, but with fixed positive type masses its wisdom follows readily as the number of agents grows. It also removes much of the graph structure—local neighborhoods and uneven individual influence—that drives Theorem 5.3’s examples. So I don’t think it is a strong mirror of that theorem’s contribution.

The paper does study growing populations, but its results are analytic statements about sequences of individual-level networks, not computational questions over a continuous distribution of voter-like types. The strongest honest positive conclusion is therefore limited: a related continuous population model is easy to formulate, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with the strongest issue: there is no computational result in this paper to mirror. Theorem 5.3 is a characterization of wisdom properties for sequences of growing equal-neighbor matrices. Theorem 2.2 gives limiting criteria for wisdom, and Theorems 2.5 and 4.2 give sufficient conditions. None studies an encoded computational problem or establishes an algorithmic complexity result. The inputs are whole sequences \(\{P^{[n]}\}\), and the conclusions concern their limits as \(n\) grows. Without a finite encoding of such a sequence, asking for its complexity is not yet a well-posed computational question.

The proposed role-based model makes the gap clearer. If each member of role \(t\) distributes weight \(K_{tt'}\) equally among members of role \(t'\), fixed positive role masses give an exchangeable block model. It preserves the role-level influence pattern, but not the local network structure that produces the paper’s prominent individuals and families or its counterexamples. Refining roles to preserve that structure can require a growing number of types, potentially one per individual; then the type distribution no longer compresses the network in the way the proposed mirror needs.

A graphon or measure-space model with a possibly singular influence kernel is a stronger population-continuum candidate: it could retain more of the network structure. But it would still need a finite input representation and a precisely posed decision or optimization problem. The paper supplies neither. Developing that representation and complexity question could be worthwhile in its own right, but it would start a new computational agenda rather than mirror Theorem 5.3 or Theorem 2.2.

So the case against is strong on programme fit, but not a proof that no worthwhile mirror can exist. A rich continuum-network model might support one; the paper itself gives no computational anchor for it.
