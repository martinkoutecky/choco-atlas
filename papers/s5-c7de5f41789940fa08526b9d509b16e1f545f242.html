The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(d\), given distinct rational ideal points \(x_1,\ldots,x_\tau\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), and \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]\), find a proposal \(p\) such that \(\beta_\mu(p)\ge(1-\varepsilon)\beta(\mu)\), where \(\beta_\mu(p)\) is the supremum of values \(\beta\in(0,1]\) for which, for every \(q\in\mathbb{R}^d\), the mass of types satisfying \(\beta\|x_i-p\|<\|x_i-q\|\) is at least the mass satisfying \(\beta\|x_i-p\|>\|x_i-q\|\), and \(\beta(\mu)=\sup_p\beta_\mu(p)\). Can this be done in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\)?

The model it lives in

Types are rational ideal points \(x_i\in\mathbb{Q}^d\) with masses \(\mu_i\); proposals \(p,q\in\mathbb{R}^d\) are compared by mass won versus mass lost under the same distance-scaling rule. The objective is to output a proposal whose stability parameter is within a factor \(1-\varepsilon\) of the maximum.

The objection that survived

The opponent suggests the geometric machinery may admit a routine weighted adaptation, limiting the technical novelty; the proponent acknowledges the approach appears amenable but does not establish whether a polynomial bound in \(\tau\) and \(L\) follows.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.4’s approximation of the society-specific optimum and Theorem 2.6(i)’s sharp planar guarantee. It leaves the exact algorithm, the higher-dimensional universal bounds, and lower-bound questions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(d\), can the approximation algorithm run in time polynomial in support size \(\tau\), mass and coordinate bit length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\), without expanding rational masses into repeated voters?
  • Can the weighted balanced-line construction give a support-sensitive algorithm for the sharp planar guarantee in Theorem 2.6(i)?
  • What are the exact-computation and lower-bound complexities for weighted-support \(\beta\)-plurality?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a weighted spatial electorate. A type is an ideal point \(x_i\in\mathbb{Q}^d\): in the paper’s Euclidean model, that point completely determines how the voter compares every pair of proposals. The society is \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\delta_{x_i}\), where the positive rational masses sum to \(1\). For proposals \(p,q\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and advantage \(\beta\), the mass won by \(p\) is \(\mu(\{x:\beta\|x-p\|<\|x-q\|\})\), and the mass lost is defined by reversing the strict inequality. Define \(\beta_\mu(p)\) by requiring won mass to be at least lost mass for every \(q\), and let \(\beta(\mu)=\sup_p\beta_\mu(p)\).

This makes the population the continuous object: the masses are proportions, not indivisible headcounts. The proposal space remains \(\mathbb{R}^d\), as it already is in the paper; I am not counting that existing feature as continuization. A plausible regime is a large electorate voting on a standardized policy space, where many residents share one of a relatively small set of calibrated ideal points. Then \(n\) can be very large while \(\tau\) is modest. This grouping is especially credible here because the paper already models voters as a multiset, allowing many voters at the same location.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper. It says that, for a voter multiset \(V\subset\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]\), one can compute a point \(p\) with \(\beta(p,V)\ge(1-\varepsilon)\beta(V)\) in time
\[ O\!\left(\frac{n^2}{\varepsilon^{3d-2}}\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{d-1}}\log^2\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right). \]
The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Weighted-Support Max-\(\beta\) Approximation: given fixed \(d\), a rational finite-support society \(\mu\) as above, and rational \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]\), output a proposal \(p\) satisfying \(\beta_\mu(p)\ge(1-\varepsilon)\beta(\mu)\). A solution is the proposal, with its guarantee. I expect this to be tractable for fixed \(d\): the paper’s geometric candidate generation and approximate depth tests appear amenable to mass-weighted versions. A concrete benchmark is the theorem’s bound with \(n\) replaced by \(\tau\), plus polynomial bit complexity in the input length \(L\).

That benchmark is a research question, not a result of Theorem 3.4. Expanding rational masses into repeated voters can require a common denominator exponentially large in \(L\), so the theorem’s \(n\)-based algorithm alone does not provide a support-sensitive algorithm. That gap is precisely what makes this a computational mirror rather than just a change of notation. Follow-up questions include exact computation of \(\beta(\mu)\), whether approximation can be subquadratic in \(\tau\), and what changes when \(d\) is part of the input.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 2.6(i), also proved here. It states that \(\beta^*_2=\sqrt{3}/2\), and that for every multiset of \(n\) planar voters one can compute a point \(p\) with \(\beta(p,V)\ge\sqrt{3}/2\) in \(O(n\log n)\) time. Its mirror is Sharp Planar Mass Guarantee: given a rational finite-support society on \(\mathbb{Q}^2\), output \(p\) with \(\beta_\mu(p)\ge\sqrt{3}/2\); the accompanying universal claim is that \(\sqrt{3}/2\) is the largest constant guaranteed for every such society. The constant transfers directly: clearing denominators turns rational masses into repeated voters, and three equally weighted locations at the vertices of an equilateral triangle give the matching upper-bound example. The algorithmic mirror is still interesting in compressed form: can the proposal be found in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), rather than in the potentially enormous expanded electorate size? I expect yes, through a mass-weighted version of the concurrent balanced-line construction underlying the theorem.

Theorem 3.4 is my lead because it optimizes stability for the particular society; Theorem 2.6(i) supplies a sharp universal guarantee and a simpler weighted problem. Both point toward tractability through computational geometry. The paper gives no named hardness result, so this case does not claim a hardness-dissolution result; it covers its algorithmic results, not an unreported negative side.

The soft spot is the type regime. Exact shared ideal points are plausible for standardized voter clusters, and the paper’s allowance for multisets supports that reading, but a diffuse electorate with nearly all distinct locations may have no useful small \(\tau\). The other essential caveat is computational: the paper proves algorithms in terms of voter count, while the proposed mirror earns its value only if weighted mass can be handled efficiently in terms of support size and bit length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that these are poor candidates for a *substantial* continuization result, not that their mirrors are incoherent. I cannot make the universal negative case hold.

For Theorem 2.6(i), the sharp constant needs no new continuous proof: clearing denominators turns any rational finite-support society into a multiset, and the theorem already establishes the bound for every finite multiset. The equilateral-triangle example transfers too. The stronger mirror is the support-sensitive one: find the guaranteed point in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\). Here the negative case is that the planar proof’s balanced-line construction seems to invite a weighted version using weighted medians and weighted order statistics. That makes the proposed advance look like a compressed-input adaptation, though I cannot establish that the required running time follows. Earlier work already formulated spatial plurality points with voter weights, albeit for a different problem and without the \(\beta\)-plurality objective; it supports the weight model’s naturalness but does not settle this mirror. [Lin et al., “Forming Plurality at Minimum Cost”](https://yupinglu.me/files/WALCOM2015.pdf)

Theorem 3.4 is the harder anchor to defeat. Its strongest mirror is precisely the one the proponent gives: maximize \(\beta\) for a rational weighted support and approximate the optimum in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\). The paper’s algorithm uses geometric candidate generation and depth tests. It appears that duplicate locations can be merged, and counts replaced by weighted sums, throughout much of that machinery. That suggests the result may be a fairly direct weighted extension. But the paper’s running time is in the expanded electorate size, so rational weights can make denominator expansion exponentially large in \(L\). A plausible route to an algorithm is not a support-sensitive bound, and I cannot treat the latter as already proved.

The regime itself is hard to dismiss. A voter’s ideal point completely determines the comparisons used in this paper, so residents sharing a point are a valid type; the objective tracks only mass won and lost, not individual identities. Exact shared points are restrictive for a diffuse electorate, but standardized policy scales or calibrated finite categories can produce large groups at the same point. In a regime with almost all distinct ideal points, the support advantage vanishes. That limits where the mirror pays off; it does not rule out the clustered regime. Nor does the objective disappear when agents become mass: each atom retains positive weight, and the winning condition remains meaningful. Recent work also relates \(\beta\)-plurality to proportional clustering, but that connection does not answer the weighted-support complexity question. [Kellerhals and Peters, “Proportional Clustering, the \(\beta\)-Plurality Problem, and Metric Distortion”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10068)

So the negative case is incrementalism: the sharp guarantee transfers exactly, and the approximation algorithm may admit a routine weighted adaptation. That is a fair reason to doubt the research payoff. It does not defeat either mirror: the support-sensitive running time remains unestablished, and the proposed population regime is sensible. I would call the case against weak, especially for Theorem 3.4.
