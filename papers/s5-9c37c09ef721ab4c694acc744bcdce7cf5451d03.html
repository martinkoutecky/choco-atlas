The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-type menus \(H_t\) of item roles with values \(v_{t,h}\), and an explicitly specified population-scaled matroid rank function \(r\), compute a welfare-maximizing measurable assignment of integral bundles to agents. The aggregate selected masses \(x_h\) must satisfy \(x(S)\le r(S)\) for every \(S\subseteq H\), \(x(H)=r(H)\), and \(0\le x_h\le\mu_t\) for \(h\in H_t\). For each positive-mass type-and-bundle cohort \((t,S)\), also compute its marginal externality price density \(\pi_{t,S}=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}[F_{-\epsilon(t,S)}-F+\epsilon\sum_{h\in S}v_{t,h}]/\epsilon\), where \(F_{-\epsilon(t,S)}\) is optimal welfare after removing \(\epsilon\) mass of that cohort and its associated item copies.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic market of type-mass buyers with integral bundles and additive values, where aggregate item-role allocations lie in an explicitly scaled matroid or polymatroid base. The objective is welfare maximization, with marginal Vickrey externality densities reported for allocated cohorts.

The objection that survived

The case does not establish when the marginal cohort price density is the limit of the paper’s discrete buyer-level Vickrey price; that correspondence needs assumptions on the scaled matroid family and how the limit is taken.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s welfare-maximizing allocation and Vickrey-price guarantee in markets with an explicit scaling of matroid capacity. It leaves Theorem 5’s ex-post equilibrium, continuous clock-auction implementation, and the paper’s simplified proof itself aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which succinctly represented scaled matroid families can the allocation and externality densities be computed efficiently?
  • Under what scaling and tie conditions do the continuous price densities equal limits of discrete buyer-level Vickrey prices?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named hardness result. Its useful computational anchor is Theorem 4, proved here as a simpler proof of Theorem 13 from Bikhchandani et al. [BdVSV11]. Under truthful signals, the Ascending Matroid Auction returns a welfare-maximizing base \(\widehat B\) and charges each buyer \(i\) the Vickrey price
\[ \max_{B\in\mathcal B(M\setminus E_i)} b(B)-b(\widehat B\setminus E_i). \]
The paper also says the auction runs in strongly polynomial time, using a long-step variant. The theorem’s conclusion was already known; the paper simplifies its analysis.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large market for local capacity or project slots subject to a matroid constraint: many local operators have the same menu of slots and the same value for each slot, while the planner must choose a feasible base. One can imagine bandwidth or infrastructure markets of the kind the paper cites. There are many operators but relatively few complete operator types. A type includes the full menu, valuation vector, and all relevant parameters. Each operator still receives an integral bundle; only the mass of operators receiving each bundle becomes fractional.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Matroid-Base Welfare and Externality Pricing. The input is a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and, for each type \(t\), a menu \(H_t\) of per-capita item roles and values \(v_{t,h}\). The menus are disjoint, as the paper assumes each item belongs to only one buyer. A population-scaled matroid specifies which aggregate selections are feasible; represent its normalized base constraints by a rational rank function \(r\). Thus the selected mass vector \(x\) must satisfy
\[ x(S)\le r(S)\quad(S\subseteq H),\qquad x(H)=r(H),\qquad 0\le x_h\le \mu_t\quad(h\in H_t). \]
The upper bound says that a type cannot supply more copies of a role than it has agents. A solution must give a measurable assignment of integral bundles \(S(a)\subseteq H_t\) to agents \(a\) of type \(t\), inducing a feasible \(x\), and maximizing total welfare
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\int_{A_t}\sum_{h\in S(a)}v_{t,h}\,da. \]
Because the population is nonatomic, the aggregate masses can be realized by splitting a type across bundles; no individual receives a fractional item.

To mirror the theorem’s price guarantee, include a Vickrey externality density for each positive-mass type-and-bundle cohort. Let \(F\) be optimal total welfare, and let \(F_{-\epsilon(t,S)}\) be optimal welfare after removing \(\epsilon\) mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\), along with that cohort’s item copies. The cohort’s price per unit mass is
\[ \pi_{t,S} = \lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0} \frac{F_{-\epsilon(t,S)}-F+\epsilon\sum_{h\in S}v_{t,h}}{\epsilon}. \]
This is the marginal version of the paper’s Vickrey formula: welfare achievable without the cohort, minus the welfare the other agents receive in the chosen allocation. The problem asks for a welfare-maximizing assignment and these externality price densities.

I expect the aggregate allocation part to be Class A. It is linear optimization over a matroid or polymatroid base with a finite type support, so an LP with separation, or greedy optimization when the scaled structure permits it, is the natural route. The price densities look like parametric LP sensitivity questions. A direct continuous clock implementation—where type mass drops roles at its value thresholds and forced mass is clinched—would be a further mechanism-design question, not an assumption needed to define this optimization problem.

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s welfare and Vickrey-pricing guarantee, not the paper’s separate ex-post equilibrium theorem or its proof simplification as such. It raises useful follow-ups: when do the discrete Vickrey prices converge to these densities; can a continuous clock auction implement them truthfully; and how closely does the mass solution approximate finite instances with moderate support?

The weak point is the population-scaled matroid. The paper’s objects are finite, distinguishable items, and its theorem does not establish that arbitrary matroid markets have a natural role-symmetric limit. The mirror needs repeated item roles and constraints that scale with the population. Vickrey prices may also be sensitive to ties or to how the limit is taken. That makes this a restricted regime, but a credible one: where repeated capacity slots and matroid constraints are already the right model, the continuous question preserves the paper’s welfare objective and externality pricing rather than replacing them with a different outcome problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the only anchor here with computational substance, but the proposed mirror does not follow from its population data. In the paper, the matroid is defined on named items, each owned by a unique buyer. A buyer’s externality depends on deleting that buyer’s entire item set \(E_i\); the proof’s price argument then tracks how cocircuits change under those deletions. So a buyer’s complete type must capture their items’ position in the matroid, not just their menu and values. In a general matroid, buyers with identical valuations and menus can still have different effects on feasible bases. Grouping them by those visible traits loses information the theorem uses.

Cloning buyers does not resolve this automatically. For example, start with a rank-one matroid on two buyers’ items. One clone expansion can make all copies parallel, keeping total rank one; another can replicate the original matroid in independent components, making rank grow with the population. Both match the original market at one copy, but their limiting welfare and externalities differ. The proposed population-scaled rank function chooses an expansion; it is additional market structure, not something determined by the paper’s instance or by a distribution over buyer types.

A stronger mirror could explicitly give a symmetric family of clone-expanded matroids and a normalized rank limit. Then an aggregate allocation LP and marginal externality densities are coherent questions. But this repairs the model by adding the very scaling law that controls the answer. If the copies form independent repeated components, the problem decomposes into repetitions of the finite auction. If they interact across agents, the new matroid family—not the continuous population alone—determines the computational question. And the derivative defining a price density is a marginal effect of removing mass; it matches the paper’s buyer-level Vickrey price only with further assumptions connecting that limit to removal of one whole buyer and their items.

That is the strongest case against this anchor: the paper supplies no canonical continuous population model, and the proposed pricing guarantee silently depends on one. Still, the negative case is not airtight. The paper itself points toward polymatroidal auction settings, and authors could plausibly recognize a carefully specified, symmetric large-market version as their problem. I would reject the proponent’s current formulation as under-specified, but I cannot honestly claim that every such mirror is unworthy.
