What the mirror covers

The proposed extension concerns multi-school approximate stability. It leaves single-school acceptability and equilibrium-existence results aside; its fixed tolerance does not directly follow from scaling Theorem 4.1’s additive adjustments by population size.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a stable mass assignment with the specified parameter tolerances exist for every input, and can one be computed efficiently in \(|H|\), \(|T|\), committee sizes, input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • How should boundary support be defined when a committee cutoff passes through a type’s mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or otherwise. The closest positive anchor is its constructive existence result, Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: for each school \(h\), it gives adjusted capacities and committee rank thresholds with
\[ |c'_h-c_h|\le 2|K_h|+1,\qquad |\alpha'_k-\alpha_k|\le 2|K_h|+2,\qquad \beta_h\le \left\lceil\frac{\sum_{k\in K_h}\alpha_k}{c_h}\right\rceil, \]
and guarantees a matching stable under those adjusted parameters. The proof uses Lemma 4.10’s iterative-rounding algorithm, but that lemma rounds an already-given stable fractional matching; it does not compute one. So this is a weak algorithmic lead, not a result showing the matching problem is in P. If the anchor rule requires an explicit complexity classification, there are no qualifying anchors.

The strongest mirror I can make from that lead is a high-multiplicity committee matching problem. Take a large admissions or residency market where many applicants share a small number of complete profiles. A type \(t\) specifies the student’s ranking of schools and their evaluation profile under every committee member. The committee ranks types, with applicants of the same type indistinguishable. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). School capacities \(\kappa_h\) and committee rank cutoffs \(a_{hk}\) are measured as fractions of the population. This is a plausible regime when a very large applicant pool has relatively few preference-and-evaluation profiles.

Call the problem Typed-Mass Approximate Stable Matching. Given \(H,T,\mu\), the type preferences, committee rankings, capacities \(\kappa_h\), cutoffs \(a_{hk}\), and tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), find an assignment \(x_{th}\) of type mass to schools, adjusted parameters \(\kappa'_h,a'_{hk}\), and thresholds \(\beta_h\), or report that none exist. Require
\[ \sum_h x_{th}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{th}=\kappa'_h,\qquad |\kappa'_h-\kappa_h|\le\varepsilon,\qquad |a'_{hk}-a_{hk}|\le\varepsilon. \]
For each committee member, the benchmark is the type at which cumulative assigned mass in their ranking reaches \(a'_{hk}\); at a boundary type, use the paper’s weak and strong approval convention. A positive assignment to \(h\) must give that type weak support at least \(\beta_h\). A type with mass assigned below a school it prefers must receive strong support there at most \(\beta_h\). Each mass unit gets one school; \(x_{th}\) records how a type is split across schools, not a lottery over an individual’s bundle.

This is a population mirror rather than an outcome-space continuation: the input society is a distribution over applicant types. The bridge to Theorem 4.1 is that, in an \(n\)-applicant realization with \(n_t=n\mu_t\), its additive capacity and rank adjustments become per-capita changes of at most \((2|K_h|+1)/n\) and \((2|K_h|+2)/n\). The proposed question is to compute the mass assignment and its stability conditions, with complexity measured in \(|H|,\tau\), committee size, input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\). It covers the paper’s multi-school approximate stability result, not its single-school results or its equilibrium existence theorem.

My tentative expectation is Class A for this finite-support mirror: once the committee boundary types are fixed, many assignment constraints have a linear form, and the paper already supplies a fractional-equilibrium-plus-rounding route. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 4.1; the number of boundary configurations and the cost of computing the equilibrium could defeat it. Further questions are whether a rational mass solution can be computed efficiently, whether the discrete approximations converge under within-type ties, and whether variable support size yields hardness.

The weakest point is substantial: strict rankings over named students are part of the paper’s model, while applicants sharing a type require committees to rank type classes and treat members of a class alike. That extension is plausible for rubric-based admissions, but it changes the preference domain. More fundamentally, the paper leaves computation of the equilibrium and stable matching open. Theorem 4.1 supports the *existence* and the shape of this mirror; it does not establish its computational tractability.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.1 is not a computational result. It proves existence of a stable matching after bounded adjustments, but the rounding algorithm in Lemma 4.10 needs a stable fractional matching as input, and the paper does not give an algorithm for finding one. The MEO existence proof is a fixed-point argument. So the proposed mirror would launch a new computational question rather than continue a complexity result established in this paper. The authors themselves identify computation as future work, but that makes the problem an open direction in either the finite or continuous model.

There is also a scaling gap. If capacities and rank cutoffs grow with a population of size \(n\), the theorem’s adjustments of at most \(2|K_h|+1\) seats and \(2|K_h|+2\) ranks become \(O(|K_h|/n)\) per capita. A faithful limit therefore removes those adjustments; the proposed fixed tolerance \(\varepsilon\) is a new relaxation, not the theorem’s bound. And Theorem 4.8 already proves existence of a stable *fractional assignment* at the original parameters for a finite set of named students. That is not a continuous-population result, but it means the mirror needs a computational contribution beyond simply obtaining fractional stability.

The typed-mass proposal has a real modeling obstacle. In the paper, committee members strictly rank named students. If a student’s complete type includes their position in every committee member’s ranking, then no two students have the same type: at least one committee ranking distinguishes every pair. Thus \(\tau=n\), and the proposed compression gives no high-multiplicity regime. A better version would group applicants with the same school preferences and committee evaluation profile, treating committee members as indifferent within each class and defining benchmarks that can cut through a type’s mass. That is plausible, but it changes strict rankings to tied profiles and requires new boundary semantics. The authors might well recognize it as their problem; it is not a decisive objection.

So the negative case is limited. The paper supplies no computational theorem to mirror, and the cleanest type compression requires a meaningful extension of its preference domain. But the paper’s large-applicant, small-committee setting makes that extension plausible, and computing stable mass assignments could be worthwhile. I cannot honestly rule out every useful continuous mirror; the strongest case against is that this is a less direct fit for ChoCo than the proponent’s framing suggests.
