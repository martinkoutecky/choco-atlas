What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation duplicates the weighted-group learning problems in Theorems 7.1 and 7.2; it leaves the paper’s axiomatic, statistical, and other learnability results without a distinct mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is continuous fair risk minimization. Let \(T\) be a finite set of group types, with support size \(\tau\). Type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a data distribution \(D_t\) over examples \((x,y)\). A type is a stratum that the problem treats as having a shared data-generating law; its sentiment under model \(h\) is the type’s expected loss,
\[ R_t(h)=\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim D_t}[\ell(h(x),y)]. \]
The decision is one model \(h\) for the population. For \(p\geq1\), its social harm is
\[ M_p(h;\mu)=\left(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t R_t(h)^p\right)^{1/p}, \]
with \(M_\infty(h;\mu)=\max_t R_t(h)\).

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: a large credit, health, or public-benefit system serves millions of people, while the decision concerns a modest number of protected or operational strata. Many people contribute to each type’s mass, so \(\tau\) is small relative to the population. The type-level risk is an average over that stratum’s data distribution, which is the model’s natural co-modification. The authors would recognize this as their problem: the paper already defines per-group distributions, group weights that can represent population frequencies, and malfare minimization over group risks.

Lead — Continuous Fair Risk Minimization for Convex Models. An instance consists of \((T,\mu,(D_t)_{t\in T},H_d,\ell,\epsilon,\delta)\), with sample access to each \(D_t\), and a specified malfare \(M_p\). The question is to return \(h\in H_d\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\),
\[ M_p(h;\mu)\leq \inf_{h'\in H_d} M_p(h';\mu)+\epsilon. \]
The sample-access qualification matters: the theorem’s input distributions are accessed by sampling, so its runtime is not a bit-complexity bound for explicitly encoded distributions.

The anchor is Theorem 7.1, proved in this paper. It says that this learning problem has a polynomial-time solution when \(H_d=\{h(\cdot;\theta):\theta\in\Theta_d\}\), the parameter dimension and \(\operatorname{Diam}(\Theta_d)\) are polynomial in \(d\), evaluating \(h\) and projecting onto \(\Theta_d\) take polynomial time, and each example’s loss is convex in \(\theta\). It also requires polynomially bounded Lipschitz constants for loss and hypotheses, plus a polynomial uniform-convergence sample bound \(m_{\mathrm{UC}}(\epsilon,\delta,d)\). Under those conditions, the paper’s algorithm handles arbitrary group distributions, weights, and fair malfare functions; it uses \(\tau\,m_{\mathrm{UC}}(\epsilon/3,\delta/\tau,d)\) samples and runs in \(\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon,1/\delta,d,\tau)\) time. This is my strongest anchor: it computes over the population’s weighted harm while retaining the paper’s single-model fairness tradeoff.

Second anchor — Continuous Fair Risk Minimization by Covers. The instance and requested output are the same, but the computational regime differs: the loss is bounded, and for every sample and resolution \(\gamma\), the loss family has a cover of size at most \(N(\ell\circ H_d,\gamma)\in\mathrm{Poly}(1/\gamma,d)\). An algorithm must also be able to enumerate a cover of polynomially related size in time polynomial in the sample size, \(1/\gamma\), and \(d\). The solution is a model whose population malfare is within \(\epsilon\) of the optimum with probability at least \(1-\delta\).

The anchor is Theorem 7.2, also proved here. It states that under those covering and enumeration conditions, empirical malfare minimization over the cover gives polynomial-time FPAC learning. This extends the positive case beyond convex parameterized models: the decisive structure can instead be a manageable, efficiently enumerable approximation of the loss family. The expected classification is Class A under either theorem’s assumptions. Extending the guarantee beyond those assumptions, and accounting explicitly for the bit lengths of rational masses and malfare parameters, are further computational questions.

The paper supplies no named NP-hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer or continuum-specific-hardness question. These anchors therefore establish tractability for specified regimes; they do not show that continuization dissolves a discrete hardness result.

My weakest point is also substantial: the paper already puts probability mass over groups and optimizes expected group risks. That makes this a very natural ChoCo mirror, but it also creates a serious collision concern under ChoCo’s novelty test. If a model that already represents the population by group masses counts as the continuous-population model, then these questions are not a new mirror of the paper—they are almost exactly the paper’s stated problems. The positive case is strongest as evidence that this is a sensible continuous computational object with explicit algorithms, and weakest as a claim that the continuous population formulation is missing from the original work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a collision, not a claim that continuous fair learning would be uninteresting. The proposed instance maps directly onto the paper’s model: set each type’s mass to its group weight, \(\mu_t=w_t\), and its type-level risk to \(R_t(h)=\mathbb E_{D_t}[\ell(h(x),y)]\). Then the proposed objective is exactly the paper’s weighted malfare objective \(M_p(h;\mu)\). The paper explicitly defines welfare and malfare over a population measure, allows weights to represent relative group sizes, and defines learning over per-group distributions.

That defeats the Theorem 7.1 anchor directly. The theorem already asks for a model minimizing malfare over group risks and gives the convex-optimization conditions, sample bound, and runtime the proponent cites. Calling the groups “types,” or adding that the masses are rational and have bit length \(L\), does not create a new continuous-population question: finite rational masses are instances of the paper’s weighted-group formulation. The theorem uses sample access to the data distributions, so a fuller bit-complexity analysis could sharpen its computational accounting. It would not supply the missing population continuization; that part is already present.

The same collision defeats Theorem 7.2. Its cover-based algorithm minimizes the same empirical malfare objective over the same weighted group risks. Replacing convexity with efficient cover enumeration changes the tractability conditions, not the population model. Nor does refining the strata evade the issue: the theorems quantify over arbitrary finite group counts and distributions, so any finite refinement remains within their formulation.

A richer model with a genuinely uncountable space of types might escape those theorems, but it would need new assumptions about the type distribution, conditional risks, and how they can be accessed or approximated. That could be worthwhile work; it is not a better version of either proposed anchor, and it goes beyond ChoCo’s finite-type, finite-support society model. Likewise, modeling interventions on people or allocations rather than selecting one shared model would change the computational problem and needs its own anchor.

So the negative case is strong against both stated mirrors: their continuous population objective and computational results are already in the paper. It is weaker as a universal claim. The paper does not rule out a distinct continuous control or allocation problem inspired by its fairness setting; it simply offers no basis for treating that as a mirror of Theorems 7.1 or 7.2.
