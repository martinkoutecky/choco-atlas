What the mirror covers

No result is covered as a computational mirror; Theorem 1 and the paper's cutpoint and ergodic results analyze stable-matching counts in finite Mallows preference structures.

The case FOR (proponent)

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For every fixed \(q\in(0,1)\), it says there is a constant \(g_r(q)\in(0,\infty)\) such that
\[ \frac{1}{n}\log \#\mathrm{StabMatch}(\mathrm{MalPref}_{q,n}) \ \xrightarrow{d}\ g_r(q), \]
and \(g_r\) is continuous and tends to \(0\) as \(q\to0\). In other words, the number of stable matchings is typically exponential in \(n\), with a positive per-person growth rate.

That is a strong result about the number of solutions, but it is not a computational-complexity result: it does not classify a problem as hard or tractable, or analyze an algorithm. So it is not a qualifying anchor under the requested rule, and I can’t honestly provide a continuous problem per anchor. The paper’s discussion of complexity in its references does not supply a named result proved here.

The most tempting positive direction would be a continuum stable-matching entropy question: replace the \(n\) rank positions on each side by a unit-mass population indexed by \(x\in[0,1]\), retain the Mallows preference noise around rank order, and ask for the limiting log-count of stable matchings per unit mass. Theorem 1 makes that limit a natural quantity to investigate. But it does not yet define a continuous matching problem the authors would recognize as theirs. Their preferences rank distinct named individuals, and independent Mallows draws generally give agents distinct complete types; grouping them into finitely many types would discard the rank-level variation that drives their argument. A continuum of rank positions also does not by itself define a count of stable matchings.

So the best positive case is only a research lead: Theorem 1 identifies a per-capita entropy quantity that invites a continuum interpretation. Its weakness is decisive for this task: the paper supplies neither the required computational anchor nor a well-defined high-multiplicity mirror of its model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 1. The other analyst is right that it is not a complexity classification: it studies a random Mallows ensemble as \(n\to\infty\), rather than an algorithmic task on an input society. One could try to repair the anchor by calling \(\#\mathrm{StabMatch}\) a counting problem and asking for its continuous analogue. But that runs into a problem with what the theorem counts.

The natural population limit replaces a matching by a mass assignment, a coupling between the two sides. A coupling is not one of finitely many matchings, so the theorem’s count has no direct continuous counterpart. Counting individual matchings in a market with repeated types would include permutations among indistinguishable agents; counting aggregate type-to-type assignments would quotient those permutations away. Neither count is selected by Theorem 1. One could instead ask for the dimension, volume, or metric entropy of the set of stable couplings, but each choice adds a new statistic and, for a continuum of types, a new way to measure it. Those may be worthwhile questions in their own right; they do not mirror the paper’s result.

The strongest version of the proposed entropy mirror keeps rank position \(x\in[0,1]\) and asks for stable matching entropy under a continuum preference kernel. But with fixed \(q<1\), Mallows noise acts at the scale of a bounded number of rank positions. After rescaling ranks by \(n\), those displacements vanish: the macroscopic preference limit retains the common rank order and loses the local fluctuations that create the stable-match count. A coarse population model therefore erases the theorem’s signal. Refining the bins to retain those fluctuations brings back a growing number of distinct preference profiles and the original microscopic model. The paper’s stationary construction on \(\mathbb Z\) already captures that local structure; it is an ergodic analysis of discrete agents, not a continuous population.

A different stable-matching market with many agents per complete preference type could be a sensible high-multiplicity problem. But it would replace the independent, rank-specific Mallows preferences with replicated types, and an aggregate stable-flow question would replace the theorem’s count of individual matchings. That could motivate a separate project; it supplies no continuous mirror of this paper’s named result.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: it has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor, and the best attempt to turn its asymptotic count into one either loses the local randomness or changes what is being counted. The universal claim is weaker: a continuous stable-allocation problem may be worthwhile on its own. What the paper does not provide is a computational result for that problem to mirror.
