The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of interchangeable radio types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), the paper’s channel and utility parameters, a coalition-size bound \(B\), and an initial distribution over coalition patterns and history states, does every asynchronous sequence of legal switches terminate? A legal switch transfers some mass \(\varepsilon>0\) of type \(t\) from a pattern \(Q\) to a pattern \(R\), updating the pattern masses and histories; the mover must strictly improve its per-radio utility, and destination members must not lose utility under the resulting population externalities. Termination means that no legal switch remains.

The model it lives in

A continuum of radios distributed over complete physical and channel types, represented by masses \(\mu_t\). The state records mass across bounded-size coalition patterns and history states; an \(\varepsilon\)-mass switch changes those distributions, and each type-pattern utility uses the paper’s sensing, access, and population-dependent interference model.

The objection that survived

Arbitrarily small mass moves can split histories repeatedly, creating infinite sequences with no counterpart in the finite-agent process; the proponent specifies this dynamic but does not show it is canonical for the paper’s model.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s convergence guarantee for coalition formation. It leaves the paper’s sensing and access analyses and simulation results in the finite-agent setting.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is narrow: a continuous analogue of the coalition formation algorithm’s convergence guarantee. The paper does not give a named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification. Its strongest named computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Section IV-E: starting from any initial network partition, the proposed coalition formation phase always reaches a final partition into disjoint coalitions. That is an algorithmic result, rather than a complexity-class result, but it is a genuine claim about what the paper’s algorithm computes. I would anchor on it alone.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large industrial telemetry network with many secondary radios deployed in repeating building or factory zones. Radios in the same zone might share hardware, known channels, calibrated per-channel link profiles, power limits, and interference relationships. Those complete profiles define the SU types; common quantities such as primary-user channel availability remain part of the environment. The regime is many radios and relatively few distinct profiles, \(N \gg \tau\). This depends on a real instance regime: if each radio’s relevant fading and interference values are idiosyncratic, the number of types could approach \(N\), and the case weakens.

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study: High-Multiplicity Switch Convergence for Joint Sensing and Access. An instance gives a finite set \(T\) of complete radio types, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, the channel and network parameters used to compute the paper’s utilities, an initial coalition configuration, and a maximum coalition size \(B\) for the deployment. The cap represents a finite coordination group size in the operating regime. A coalition pattern \(Q\) is a multiset of types, with \(1 \leq |Q| \leq B\). Let \(\lambda_Q\) be the normalized number of coalitions of pattern \(Q\). Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_Q Q_t\lambda_Q=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
Thus \(\lambda\) is the mass-level counterpart of a partition of individual radios.

A type-\(t\) radio in coalition \(Q\) receives the paper’s per-radio utility, \(\bar C_t(1-\tau_t^Q)\): channel knowledge is shared, channels are sorted as in Algorithm 1, and access capacity is computed using the paper’s channel-selection and power-allocation model. The external-interference term is determined by the population configuration \(\lambda\), using the types’ interference profiles. This is a population co-modification of the paper’s partition-dependent externalities; it leaves sensing, access, and the individual utility intact.

A legal mass switch moves some positive mass \(\varepsilon\) of type-\(t\) radios from coalitions of pattern \(Q\) into coalitions of pattern \(R\), changing those patterns to \(Q-\{t\}\) and \(R+\{t\}\). The mover must strictly improve its utility, members of the destination coalition must not lose utility, and the mover’s history must permit the move, following the paper’s switch rule. Histories are tracked for each mass cohort, as the paper tracks each SU’s history. The question is whether every asynchronous sequence of such accepted switches, from a given initial configuration, terminates at a configuration with no legal switch. A solution is a finite sequence ending at such a configuration; a negative result can give an instance and an infinite legal sequence.

The expected direction is continuum-specific hardness, or a continuum-specific failure of finite termination. Theorem 1’s proof relies on the finite set of network partitions; the mass configuration \(\lambda\) instead ranges over a continuum, even with finitely many types and bounded coalition size. The finite-state argument therefore does not transfer. This is not a hardness transfer from the paper: it contains no named discrete hardness result for coalition formation to embed. Nor do I assume that a counterexample to finite termination is automatic; establishing one, or finding a potential argument that restores convergence, is the point of the mirror.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem because it keeps their coalition membership decisions, externality-sensitive utilities, consent rule, history rule, and physical-layer payoff calculation, while replacing the finite population by a distribution over complete radio types. It mirrors only Theorem 1’s convergence claim; it does not try to continuize the paper’s simulations or every sensing-and-access result.

The weak point is the transition from individual radios to mass switches. The paper’s SUs have individual channel profiles and make atomic moves; this mirror assumes repeated profiles and allows fractional mass to move. The bounded coalition-size regime and the population-level external-interference calculation also need to be accepted as part of the model. If the authors regard those changes as replacing their coalition formation problem, the mirror fails. But if they accept high multiplicity as a sensible regime for standardized deployments, the question preserves the paper’s central computational object: how utility-improving, consent-based coalition formation behaves when the society itself is represented continuously.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real algorithmic result, so I would not dismiss the paper as having nothing computational to mirror. Its claim is that this particular switch procedure terminates. The difficulty is that its proof rests on the finite state space of partitions of named radios and on each radio’s own history \(h(i)\). The result says nothing about the complexity of finding a good partition, and the paper gives no objective whose optimum a continuous optimization method could seek.

The strongest high-multiplicity setting would use repeated, physically interchangeable radios. That is plausible in standardized deployments, and identical channel profiles could define sensible types. The problem is what happens to the theorem’s state and moves. A switch is made by one radio, requires consent from a destination coalition, and is constrained by that radio’s history. In a mass model, moving an amount \(\varepsilon\) splits a type cohort: the movers and those left behind can now have different histories. One can track distributions over coalition patterns and histories, but then the state is continuous and the finite-partition termination argument disappears.

The proposed question—whether every asynchronous sequence of accepted mass switches terminates—needs additional rules before it has a stable meaning. With divisible mass, switches can be arbitrarily small, so an infinite sequence of improving moves may approach a limit without reaching a terminal state. A minimum move size or a cohort-level atomic move could prevent that behavior, but either choice adds a step rule absent from the paper and can restore the very discreteness the mirror was meant to remove. Asking instead for a stable mass configuration would be cleaner, but that is a new equilibrium-computation problem. The paper supplies no potential function or welfare objective for it: utilities depend on partition externalities, coordinated channel rankings, feasible channel selections, and power allocation.

The physical model also makes aggregation costly. Repeated radios with identical channel and interference profiles are possible, but a general deployment depends on radio locations and interference relationships. A finite type distribution can retain those features only under strong symmetry; a spatial continuum with pairwise interaction data makes the interaction structure itself central. Either way, there is no evident LP or pricing problem of the kind that motivates the programme’s continuous mirrors.

So I would reject this anchor as a good fit for the continuization programme: its convergence theorem is driven by finite, identity-level coalition moves, and the closest mass analogue needs a new dynamic and a new computational question. The negative case is not airtight. A well-defined stable-configuration problem for a carefully specified homogeneous network could be worthwhile to those authors. The paper itself does not provide enough structure to make that a compelling continuous mirror of Theorem 1.
