The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible anchor for this case. Its numbered “Relationship 1–16” statements concern implications between axioms; it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. The AVR tables, the exhaustive search used to label training examples in §4.1, and the simulated-annealing experiment are empirical or methodological results, not named complexity results. So I can’t honestly give an anchor-specific continuous problem or claim that a result’s hardness dissolves in the continuum.

The best positive lead is an unanchored follow-on to the paper’s minimum-violation committee search. Let \(M\) be the alternatives, \(k\) the committee size, and \(T_+\) the support of a society: complete rankings \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Derive each type’s approval set from its top \(k\) rankings, as the paper does. Define the paper’s axioms on this society by replacing voter counts with mass, and let coalitions be measurable subsets of the population. For example, majority thresholds become \(1/2\), Dummett’s quota becomes \(\ell/k\), and the local-stability blocking quota becomes \(1/k\). The instance also specifies the axiom family \(\mathcal A\) and these mass-based conventions. The problem, Continuous Minimum-Axiom-Violation Committee, is to return a committee \(C\in\binom{M}{k}\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\mathbf{1}\{A\text{ is violated by }(\mu,C)\}, \]
with the paper’s lexicographic tie-breaking. The solution is the minimizing committee and its violation count.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization electing a committee: millions of members, but repeated ballots yield a much smaller set of distinct rankings. Their shared preference is the type; its mass is the population fraction holding that ranking. This connects directly to the label oracle in §4.1, which searches for the committee with the fewest axiom violations, and to the paper’s study of how preference composition affects rule performance. The authors could plausibly recognize it as a population-scale version of that objective. It covers that search objective, not the paper’s neural-rule learning or its comparisons across sampled profiles.

For this proposed problem, I would not assign a defensible A/B/C classification from the paper. Choosing among committees still depends on \(m\), and the paper establishes no hardness result for its finite minimum-violation search. A discrete-to-continuous embedding may be possible under carefully aligned quotas, but it would not supply the missing hardness premise. The lead’s weakest point is therefore also decisive: it is a plausible new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the anchor problem: the paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its numbered Relationships 1–16 are implications between axioms. The AVR and committee-overlap tables are empirical measurements, and §4.1’s exhaustive search is a label-generation procedure, not a complexity result. So there is no named computational result here to continuize. The proposed *Continuous Minimum-Axiom-Violation Committee* is an unanchored follow-on, not a mirror of a result the paper establishes.

That distinction also limits what the proposed problem captures. For a society \(\mu\), minimizing the number of violated axioms selects the best committee for one input; in the paper, that search supplies labels to a learner. The main evaluation instead measures how rules perform across sampled profiles from preference distributions, while the learned rule maps aggregated profile features to committees. A stronger proposal could study a rule’s expected violation rate over a distribution of continuous societies, but that adds a distributional learning or risk-optimization problem. It does not recover an absent complexity result, and its computational formulation would need to specify the rule class, input distribution, and learning objective.

I cannot make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A large membership organization with repeated rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and mass-based versions of the paper’s axiom tests and minimum-violation labels are coherent. That makes the proposed follow-on plausible. The honest negative verdict is narrower: the paper supplies no eligible computational anchor, so the claimed mirror is not established by this paper; the broader claim that no useful follow-on exists is weak.
