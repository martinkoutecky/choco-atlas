The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is that this paper has no eligible computational anchor. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), parameterized tractability, approximation guarantees, or a comparable computational result. The claims about deferred acceptance and top trading cycles are cited from Roth and others, not stated as numbered results proved here. Tables 13 and 14 are empirical results, not computational theorems.

The closest candidates are:

  • Table 13 reports that, among unassigned students eligible for the authors’ individual-deviation exercise, \(64\%\) at elementary level, \(61\%\) at middle-school level, and \(84\%\) at high-school level could have received one of their listed schools after removing one or more overdemanded schools from the top of their lists.
  • Table 14 compares the three mechanisms empirically. For elementary applicants, the Boston mechanism assigned \(77.9\%\) to their first choice and left \(8.7\%\) unassigned, versus \(73.7\%\) and \(6.2\%\) under student-proposing deferred acceptance and \(74.1\%\) and \(6.1\%\) under TTC.

Neither is an eligible anchor under the stated rules. Thus there is technically no anchor-specific continuous problem to report.

The best positive case, if the source gate is relaxed, is nevertheless quite natural. Consider a large urban school district in which many students share the same complete profile: school ranking, zone eligibility, sibling or guaranteed priority, walk-zone status, and priority class. Let \(T\) be the finite set of such profiles, with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Let each school \(s\) have per-capita capacity \(q_s\), and let \(\pi_s\) order the types by priority.

A direct continuous counterpart of the paper’s assignment computation is the following:

Fluid Boston Assignment Evaluation. Given \(S,T,\mu,(q_s)_{s\in S}\), type-specific submitted rankings, and school priorities, run the Boston mechanism on masses. At round \(k\), the unassigned mass whose \(k\)-th choice is \(s\) applies to \(s\); \(s\) admits the highest-priority applicant mass up to capacity \(q_s\), splitting a boundary priority class if necessary. Return the mass assigned to each rank, together with the unassigned mass. The same input can be evaluated under student-proposing deferred acceptance.

This is a credible high-multiplicity mirror of the mechanism comparison behind Table 14. It preserves the schools, capacities, rankings, priorities, and assignment rule; only student counts become rational masses and capacities scale with the population. In a district with hundreds of thousands of applicants but perhaps only a few hundred recurring preference/priority profiles, the regime is plausible. The evaluation problem should be tractable by direct finite-round simulation, hence Class A, although that would be a new observation rather than a result of this paper.

A more active mirror of Table 13 would be:

Continuous Rank-Edit Rescue. Add true rankings \(P_t\), current submitted rankings \(R_t\), and an allowed edit set \(D_t\), such as the lists obtained by deleting an initial block of overdemanded schools. Choose \(x_{t,r}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(t\) submitting edited report \(r\in D_t\), subject to
\[ \sum_{r\in D_t}x_{t,r}=\mu_t. \]
Run the mass Boston mechanism on the resulting report distribution. Minimize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{r\in D_t} c_t(r)x_{t,r} \]
subject to at least an \(\eta\)-fraction of the population receiving one of its true top-\(k\) schools, or one of its originally listed schools.

This is recognizably the paper’s “remove overdemanded choices and rescue unassigned students” exercise, but it is an extension rather than a direct mirror: the paper studies isolated individual deviations, whereas a positive-mass population makes the intervention collective. With fixed explicit type and edit sets, the mechanism evaluation is tractable; optimizing over rankings when the number of schools is variable may retain combinatorics in the school/ranking axis, so hardness could plausibly transfer. The paper provides no classification.

The central weakness is therefore decisive: atomless agents cannot perform the paper’s unilateral manipulation in the ordinary sense. Replacing one student’s deviation by a positive-mass cohort deviation changes the strategic problem. Random individual tie-breakers and TTC’s priority-token trading also require additional type-level conventions. I would defend the school-choice continuum as a sensible, author-recognizable high-multiplicity extension, but not as a genuine mirror of a named computational result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with the source gate: this paper has no eligible computational anchor. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity or algorithms of a computational problem. The results on deferred acceptance and TTC are cited from earlier work; Tables 13 and 14 are empirical exercises. A continuous school-choice problem can certainly be invented from the paper, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

Table 13 does not rescue the case. Its \(64\%\), \(61\%\), and \(84\%\) figures are dataset-specific estimates of isolated individual deviations. Each student’s deviation is evaluated while everyone else’s submitted list and priority number remain fixed. In an atomless society, one student has mass zero, so changing that student’s report leaves the mass profile and the Boston allocation unchanged. The central counterfactual therefore disappears.

The proposed repair—letting a positive mass of a type delete an initial block of schools—changes the question. It is no longer an individual missed opportunity but a coordinated reporting intervention. Its outcome includes strategic externalities: rescuing one cohort can alter capacities and assignments for every other cohort. To make it meaningful one must add a planner, an intervention cost, a collective objective, and true preferences. That may define an interesting new campaigning problem, but it is not the exercise reported in Table 13. Preserving individual pivotality would instead require non-negligible atoms or explicit identities, which abandons the atomless high-multiplicity interpretation.

Table 14 is no stronger. It is a descriptive comparison of three mechanisms on one fixed collection of submitted lists. A fluid version can certainly replace counts by masses, scale capacities per capita, and split boundary priority classes. Those are sensible modelling choices, and I would not object to them. But the resulting computation merely evaluates supplied reports. It does not capture the paper’s substantive claim, which concerns why those reports are unreliable: strategic manipulation, unequal sophistication, private information, and the transition to truthful reporting under a new mechanism.

A mass distribution over submitted rankings cannot recover those distinctions. If one holds submitted reports fixed, the fluid model reproduces the paper’s naive table while deliberately omitting the behaviour the authors say makes that table uninformative. If one adds true preferences, information, sophistication, risk attitudes, withdrawal decisions, and learning, one has built a new strategic model whose computational problem and complexity are absent from the paper. The same issue affects random priority and TTC: they can be given fluid conventions, but those conventions are additional modelling machinery, not results mirrored from this paper.

Thus the proponent’s “Fluid Boston Assignment Evaluation” is a plausible high-multiplicity implementation of a school-assignment mechanism. It is not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s computational contribution, because the paper has no such contribution to mirror. The “Continuous Rank-Edit Rescue” is potentially a new continuous intervention problem, but its positive-mass coordination is precisely what replaces rather than continuizes the paper’s individual-deviation exercise.

The honest limitation is that I cannot prove that no future continuous school-choice project inspired by Boston would be worthwhile. A fluid matching model itself is not nonsensical. The stronger and defensible conclusion is narrower: neither proposed anchor survives the computational-result gate, and every substantive repair either collapses individual incentives in the continuum or introduces a new optimization or strategic problem not present in the paper.
