The continuous mirror question

Given atomless populations on sides \(A\) and \(B\), each represented by finitely many types with rational masses and strict rankings over counterpart types, a set of critical \(A\)-types whose entire mass must be matched, and an allowed type pair \((t,s)\), does there exist a feasible measurable one-to-one allocation that is popular against every other feasible allocation and assigns positive mass to \((t,s)\)? Popularity compares the mass of agents on both sides who prefer each allocation’s counterpart type.

The model it lives in

Use finite type sets \(T_A,T_B\) with rational masses, strict preferences over opposite-side types, and a critical subset \(C\subseteq T_A\). An allocation is a measurable one-to-one matching of the underlying atomless populations, inducing type-pair masses \(x_{ts}\); it must match all mass of each type in \(C\). It is popular when no other feasible allocation is preferred by more total population mass than prefers it. The target condition is \(x_{ts}>0\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers popular-feasible edge membership, represented by positive mass on a type pair, for one-to-one markets with critical mass on the \(A\) side. It leaves the paper’s many-to-one counterexample outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can popular type-pair membership be decided in time polynomial in the number of supported types and the bit length of their masses?
  • Does the minimum-size popular-feasible or dominant-feasible characterization have a valid analogue for these mass allocations?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is the Popular Feasible Type-Pair Edge problem. The regime I have in mind is a large one-to-one placement market—say, an annual sailor-to-billet assignment—with many agents but relatively few standardized preference and qualification profiles. A type records an agent’s side, preference order over counterpart types, and, for sailors, whether they are critical. Give each type a rational mass. Members of a type are interchangeable for the model; preferences are strict between counterpart types. Critical sailors’ entire mass must be matched.

An allocation is a measurable one-to-one matching between subsets of the two atomless populations: each matched agent gets one partner, while a type’s mass may be split across several counterpart types. For any two feasible allocations \(M,N\), count the mass of agents who prefer their partner under \(N\) to their partner under \(M\), on both sides. Call \(M\) popular if that mass is no greater than the mass preferring \(M\), for every feasible \(N\). The input also gives an allowed type pair \((t,s)\). The question is whether some popular feasible allocation assigns positive mass between \(t\) and \(s\). A yes-solution is such an allocation, including its type-pair masses. Positive mass is the meaningful analogue of an edge in an atomless population: an isolated individual edge has zero mass.

My anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that for a marriage instance \(G=(A\cup B,E)\), critical men \(C\subseteq A\), and edge \(e\in E\), \(e\) belongs to a popular feasible matching if and only if it belongs to a minimum-size popular feasible matching or to a dominant feasible matching. The paper combines this characterization with Theorem 6, also proved here: it gives polynomial-time reductions from an instance with critical men to marriage instances \(G'\) and \(G''\), with surjective maps from stable matchings in \(G'\) to the minimum-size popular feasible matchings in \(G\), and from stable matchings in \(G''\) to the dominant feasible matchings in \(G\). Together with the stable-edge algorithm cited in the paper, these results yield its polynomial-time popular-feasible-edge algorithm. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result.

I expect the type-pair problem to be Class A, likely polynomial in the number of supported types and the bit length of their masses. The reason is specific to the anchor: its edge test reduces to membership in two structured classes, and the paper reaches those classes through stable-matching reductions. In a finite replica of the type population, rational masses become multiplicities; in the continuum, mass flows between types could make it possible to test whether a type pair can carry positive flow in either class. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence already proved by Theorems 5–6.

The main further questions are whether the theorem’s “minimum-size popular feasible or dominant” characterization survives for mass allocations, whether continuous analogues of the two stable-matching reductions capture all such allocations, and whether positive type-pair mass can be decided efficiently from those representations. I would keep the scope to one-to-one matching with critical mass on the \(A\) side. The paper’s many-to-one counterexample is a reason not to claim the result extends to hospital capacities.

The soft spot is real: the paper assumes strict preferences over individual neighbors, while a finite-support continuum model makes same-type agents interchangeable and ranks counterpart types. That changes the preference model, and the paper’s theorem need not survive it. The other unavoidable coarsening is replacing “this named edge occurs” with “this type pair carries positive mass.” I think the authors would recognize the underlying popular-feasible matching question, but whether they would accept this type-based version as *their* continuous analogue is the case’s boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 5’s object is an individual edge in a named graph, and its proof relies on that identity-level structure. Theorem 6 creates \(\ell+1\) or more copies for each named critical man, where \(\ell=|C|\); the proof of Theorem 5 then follows alternating paths and moves named edges between parts. With only \(\tau\) types and binary-encoded masses, expanding a finite replica could require a number of copies exponential in the mass bit length. That construction therefore gives no compressed algorithm for the proposed type-pair question.

The proponent’s better formulation is coherent, but it changes the question: agents rank counterpart *types*, criticality applies to whole types, and “edge \(e\) occurs” becomes “the type pair \((t,s)\) carries positive mass.” That is a sensible aggregate problem, but Theorems 5–6 do not establish the proposed polynomial-time route for it. This weakens the case for the specific Class A prediction; it does not show the new problem is unworthy.

That distinction leaves the negative case weak. Large placement markets with repeated preference and qualification profiles are plausible, and positive mass on a type pair is a meaningful aggregate analogue of edge membership. Theorem 5 is a genuine algorithmic result, so the anchor survives the programme’s basic gate. Replacing its identity-level construction with a mass-level one is itself a computational question of the programme’s kind. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists here.
