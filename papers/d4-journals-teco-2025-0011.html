The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(p\ge1\), take a unit-mass continuum of identical star cells with \(m\) tasks per cell, one root of cost vector \(r\) and one task-specific leaf of cost \(\ell_j\) for each task \(j\). Each task must be assigned whole to its root or its corresponding leaf in every cell. A direct mechanism receives the individual machines’ reports in each cell, assigns tasks and pays machines, and must make truthful reporting optimal for each machine holding the other reports fixed. Across cells, assignments may vary measurably, so let \(x_j\in[0,1]\) be the mass fraction of task \(j\) assigned to roots. Is \(2^{1-1/p}\) the best possible worst-case approximation ratio for deterministic truthful mechanisms, measured against \(\min_{x\in[0,1]^m}\left((\sum_j r_jx_j)^p+\sum_j(\ell_j(1-x_j))^p\right)^{1/p}\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of replicated star cells preserves each cell’s root-leaf-task links and integral assignments. Mechanisms use individual reports and payments to enforce dominant-strategy truthfulness, while performance is measured by the \(L_p\) norm of role-level average loads across cells.

The objection that survived

Role-type masses alone do not encode which root and task-specialist share a cell, or a unilateral report that leaves an atomless population distribution unchanged; the mirror must retain per-cell report and allocation structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the star \(L_p\)-scheduling results in Theorems 24 and 26 for \(p\ge1\); it leaves the graph decomposition, hyperstar, and general scheduling results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Theorem 26’s lower bound \(2^{1-1/p}\) persist when schedules can be mixed across cells?
  • Does the Hybrid \(L_p\) Mechanism achieve \(2^{1-1/p}\) against the fractional type-average benchmark?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is the paper’s truthful scheduling on stars. The paper does not give a named NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity classification for this problem. It does, however, prove named approximation results for truthful mechanisms; those are the anchors here.

Picture many identical service cells, each with one generalist machine that can process any of \(m\) tasks and one specialist for each task. Replicate the cell across a continuum. Root machines share a complete type—their vector of processing times and task eligibility—and specialists share types by task role and processing time. For fixed \(m\), the population grows while the number of types stays at most \(m+1\).

Within every cell, each task still goes wholly to one endpoint. Across cells, however, a fraction of a task type can go to the root and the rest to its specialist. That fraction records different integral assignments across a mass of copies; it does not split a task inside a cell.

For a single cell profile \((r,\ell)\), write \(x_j\in[0,1]\) for the mass fraction of task \(j\) assigned to the root. The type-average loads are

\[ L_0=\sum_{j=1}^m r_jx_j,\qquad L_j=\ell_j(1-x_j). \]

For fixed \(p\ge1\), the continuous benchmark is

\[ \mathrm{OPT}^{\mathrm{cont}}_p(r,\ell) = \min_{x\in[0,1]^m} \left( L_0^p+\sum_{j=1}^m L_j^p \right)^{1/p}. \]

A direct mechanism receives each machine’s reported costs and assigns every copy’s tasks integrally. Its assignment across the continuum is measurable, and its payments must make truthful reporting optimal for every individual machine. Performance is measured using the \(L_p\) norm of type-average loads. This is the co-modification the population limit calls for: individual bundles stay integral while type-level loads can be fractional.

Two results give a useful matched pair.

Anchor: Theorem 24, proved in this paper. For star scheduling, the Hybrid \(L_p\) Mechanism chooses

\[ S\in\arg\min_{T\subseteq[m]} \left\{ r(T)+\left(\sum_{j\notin T}\ell_j^p\right)^{1/p} \right\}. \]

It is truthful and has approximation ratio at most \(2^{(p-1)/p}\) for \(p\ge1\), and at most \(2^{(1-p)/p}\) for \(0<p<1\). I would mirror its \(p\ge1\) case with the question: does the cell-by-cell lift of this mechanism achieve \(2^{1-1/p}\) against the continuous type-average benchmark? A solution is a proof of that guarantee or a counterexample establishing a larger ratio. I expect this to be tractable in the programme’s Class A sense: the aggregate allocation has finitely many mass variables and the benchmark is convex. The discrete guarantee need not survive unchanged, because the continuous benchmark can use fractions that a single cell cannot.

Anchor: Theorem 26, proved in this paper. For every \(p\ge1\), no deterministic truthful mechanism for stars can achieve a ratio below \(2^{1-1/p}\), over the class of star sizes. Its construction uses root costs \(r_j=2^{j-1}\), leaf costs \(\ell_j=\alpha 2^{j-1}\), and weak monotonicity to force a bad allocation; the bound is approached as \(m\) grows. The continuous question is: is \(2^{1-1/p}\) still a lower bound on deterministic truthful mechanisms when they allocate mass across replicated stars and are judged by type-average loads? A solution is either a lower-bound proof for every such mechanism or a truthful mechanism with a strictly smaller worst-case ratio. My prior is that some lower bound transfers: the construction’s tension comes from the root’s \(m\)-dimensional cost vector, not from having many distinct machines. But this is only a B-like prediction, not a direct embedding—the mass model can mix integral schedules across copies and may evade the discrete argument.

The authors should recognize this as their star-scheduling problem: the same agents, eligibility structure, private processing costs, integral per-cell decisions, and dominant-strategy truthfulness remain in place. The mirror changes the population regime and evaluates the resulting type-average loads. It covers the star results of Theorems 24 and 26, not the paper’s graph decomposition, hyperstar, or general scheduling results.

The weakest point is that a machine in one cell has vanishing weight in the aggregate objective as the number of cells grows, while its private utility remains individual. Truthfulness still constrains its own assignment, but the aggregate objective may barely register that assignment. That mismatch is precisely why the continuous lower-bound question is worth asking—and why I would not claim the paper’s \(2^{1-1/p}\) bound transfers without a new proof.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that an aggregate distribution does not preserve the strategic object the paper studies. In Theorems 24 and 26, each machine privately reports its own cost vector, receives an integral set of tasks, and must prefer truth-telling when its report changes and everyone else’s stays fixed. A distribution over machine types records masses, but not a unilateral deviation by one machine: in an atomless population, that deviation leaves the distribution unchanged. A mechanism that depends only on the distribution therefore cannot express the paper’s dominant-strategy condition. To retain it, the model needs pointwise allocations that depend on an individual report, along with the task links between machines. That is a different, more detailed object than a society given by its type masses.

The best high-multiplicity story is to replicate the star into many service cells, copying each task in each cell and keeping assignments integral within cells. Under the paper’s usual individual workload objective, this mostly repeats the finite problem: the cells have no shared tasks or other coupling, so the mechanism can be run cell by cell. Theorem 24’s guarantee is then repeated across cells, while Theorem 26 remains a bound about individual star instances. Replication alone has not created a new aggregate optimization problem.

The proponent’s \(x_j\) model does create one, but it evaluates a different objective. Its benchmark takes the norm *after* averaging loads across copies, \(\|\int L(q)\,dq\|_p\), rather than aggregating individual workloads, such as \((\int\|L(q)\|_p^p\,dq)^{1/p}\), or measuring the worst individual load for makespan. For \(p\ge1\), convexity makes the former no larger; for makespan, a maximum of type-average loads can conceal heavily loaded machines. So Theorem 24’s “cell-by-cell lift” does not establish the claimed guarantee against the usual scheduling objective. It establishes a question about mean loads. That question is coherent, but it needs a separate argument that mean load is the performance measure these authors would want.

Theorem 26 has the same modeling gap. Its lower bound comes from changing one root’s report while holding the rest of the instance fixed. A mass-only model suppresses that deviation; a pointwise model restores it, but then the bound concerns individual cells, while an averaged objective may allow different cells to use different schedules. Whether the lower bound survives that aggregation is a new mechanism-design question, not a consequence of the paper’s proof. Calling the \(2^{1-1/p}\) bound a mirror without first fixing how individual truthfulness and population-level performance fit together would overstate the connection.

This is a substantial objection to these particular anchors, but not an airtight case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The weakest point in the negative case is that the programme explicitly permits type-level averages and co-modifications when population mass requires them. The authors might recognize a model that preserves pointwise incentives while measuring average load across repeated cells. If so, it could be worth studying. I would argue that the proponent has not yet specified a continuous society model that makes both anchors well-posed; I would not claim the programme has no plausible model for them.
