The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), complete rich-ad schedules \(t=(D_t,v_t,\ell_t)\), and per-capita line and slot capacities \(q,h\), define coalition welfare \(W(\nu)\) by maximizing \(\sum_{t,d}v_t(d)y_{td}\) over \(y_{td}\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_d y_{td}\le \nu_t\), \(\sum_{t,d}\ell_{td}y_{td}\le q\), and \(\sum_{t,d}y_{td}\le h\). Can one compute a welfare-maximizing mass allocation, nonnegative per-type utilities \(u_t\), and revenue \(r\) satisfying \(r+\sum_t\mu_tu_t=W(\mu)\) and \(r\ge W(\nu)-\sum_t\nu_tu_t\) for every \(0\le\nu\le\mu\), such that the core utilities are \(\epsilon\)-bidder-optimal, using \(O(\tau\log(\tau/\epsilon))\) calls to a truncated fluid-welfare oracle and \(O(\tau^2\log(\tau/\epsilon))\) additional arithmetic?

The model it lives in

A large joint rich-ad auction with \(\tau\) complete advertiser schedule types carrying rational masses, and lines and slots scaled with population. The allocation variables \(y_{td}\) assign mass of each type to integral decorations in aggregate; utilities \(u_t\) are per-member type utilities, and \(r\) is seller revenue. A truncated fluid-welfare oracle optimizes welfare using values \(\max\{v_t(d)-u_t,0\}\) under the same per-capita capacity constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's oracle-based core-pricing algorithm and its efficiency and approximation target; it does not mirror the paper's experiments, revenue comparisons, or strategic-equilibrium discussion.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the water-filling proof extend to fractional coalition masses and type-level utility coordinates with the stated \(\tau\)-dependent oracle and arithmetic bounds?
  • Can finite markets with \(N\) advertisers approximate the fluid core with a provable error?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Fluid Fast Core Pricing for Rich Ads. The paper’s strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that, given submitted bids and access to WIN-ORAC—which takes the bids and per-bidder truncations and returns a maximum-welfare allocation under the truncated bids and its winner set—Algorithm 1 returns an \(\epsilon\)-bidder-optimal core point using \(O(n\log(n/\epsilon))\) oracle calls and \(O(n^2\log(n/\epsilon))\) additional time. Here, \(\epsilon\)-bidder-optimal means that no other core point raises every bidder’s utility weakly and raises any one bidder’s utility by more than \(\epsilon\). The paper gives no comparable numbered hardness result, so I would not turn its general remarks about hard winner determination into a second anchor.

The regime I have in mind is a large batch of rich-ad placements for many advertisers using a small set of standardized campaign schedules. For example, advertisers might share the same available ad decorations, line requirements, and declared expected values because they use the same centrally managed bidding template. Let \(N\) be the number of advertisers and \(\tau\) the number of distinct schedules, with \(N\gg\tau\). A type is the advertiser’s complete schedule \(t=(D_t,\{v_t(d),\ell_{td}\}_{d\in D_t})\), where \(v_t(d)\) is the declared expected value of decoration \(d\) and \(\ell_{td}\) is its line requirement. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of advertisers with that schedule.

The batch’s inventory must grow with the population: take it to have \(Nq\) lines and \(Nh\) ad slots, for fixed per-advertiser capacities \(q\) and \(h\). This is the necessary co-modification that keeps the per-capita allocation meaningful. A mass unit is an advertiser, while mass allocations are fractions of those advertisers; each actual advertiser still receives at most one integral decoration.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Its input is rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), the type schedules above, per-capita capacities \(q,h\), and \(\epsilon>0\). For coalition masses \(0\le\nu_t\le\mu_t\), define welfare by

\[ W(\nu)= \max_{y\ge0}\ \sum_{t}\sum_{d\in D_t}v_t(d)y_{td} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{d\in D_t}y_{td}\le\nu_t,\quad \sum_{t,d}\ell_{td}y_{td}\le q,\quad \sum_{t,d}y_{td}\le h. \]

Here \(y_{td}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned decoration \(d\). The requested output is a welfare-maximizing allocation \(y\), per-member type utilities \(u_t\ge0\), and seller revenue \(r\ge0\), satisfying

\[ r+\sum_t\mu_tu_t=W(\mu), \qquad r\ge W(\nu)-\sum_t\nu_tu_t \quad\text{for every }0\le\nu\le\mu. \]

These are the efficient-allocation and no-blocking-coalition conditions for the type-mass core. The output must also be \(\epsilon\)-bidder-optimal: there is no other feasible core utility vector \(u'\) with \(u'_t\ge u_t\) for every type and \(u'_s>u_s+\epsilon\) for some type \(s\). The continuous truncated winner-determination oracle takes \(u\) and maximizes the welfare from values \(\max\{v_t(d)-u_t,0\}\), subject to the same mass and inventory constraints. Its output separates the core constraints, just as WIN-ORAC does in the paper.

The concrete question is whether this problem admits a water-filling algorithm whose cost depends on \(\tau\), rather than \(N\): \(O(\tau\log(\tau/\epsilon))\) calls to the fluid oracle and \(O(\tau^2\log(\tau/\epsilon))\) additional arithmetic. In this rich-ad specialization, each oracle call is a linear program over the type-decoration masses, so that bound would give an efficient continuous pricing algorithm. I expect Class A, conditional on proving that the paper’s water-filling argument extends to fractional coalition masses and type-level utility coordinates. The core geometry and truncated-welfare separation remain the same; the extension must establish that fractional mass participation does not break the argument that progressively freezes coordinates. This is a genuine continuous-population question, not a claim that Theorem 1 already implies the bound.

The mirror is plausible because it preserves the paper’s central object: efficient combinatorial allocation followed by core payment computation against blocking coalitions, using a truncated welfare oracle. Its continuous aspect is the society itself: coalition sizes and allocations are masses, and the algorithm’s proposed dimension is the number of distinct advertiser types. Type-level expected utilities correspond to the symmetric, ex-ante core for indistinguishable advertisers; allocation mass can be assigned among those advertisers symmetrically. Useful follow-on questions are whether finite markets with \(N\) advertisers approximate this fluid core with a provable error, and whether the same support-size compression survives richer bidding languages whose fluid winner-determination problem is harder than an LP.

My weakest point is the regime and its symmetry assumption. The paper’s Bing data has only about seven advertisers per auction on average, and real advertisers’ complete bid schedules may be nearly all distinct; near-identical schedules are separate types under the programme’s definition. The case therefore rests on a narrower but plausible market: a large batch auction with copy-exact standardized advertiser schedules and inventory scaled with the cohort. The core-pricing question itself remains the authors’ question, but if they reject this type-symmetric fluid core or the standardized high-multiplicity regime, this mirror loses its footing.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 1: it proves an oracle-call bound for computing an \(\epsilon\)-bidder-optimal core point. I can’t defeat that result as a mirror. The strongest case against is about whether its high-multiplicity regime describes a worthwhile instance of this auction problem.

In the paper’s actual setting, an advertiser’s type would have to include the full bid schedule and the expected value of every decoration. Sharing ad formats, bidding software, or a campaign template does not make advertisers the same type if their values differ. The paper’s auctions average about seven advertisers; the reported count of roughly 107 ads largely reflects multiple decorations per advertiser, not a population of repeated bidder types. So the proposed compression to \(\tau\) types needs many advertisers with copy-exact schedules, not merely standardized formats.

The proposed batch scenario does not automatically supply them. If the batch consists of independent impression auctions, pooling it changes the core: coalitions and payments are evaluated separately in each auction. If it is one joint auction, scaling the lines and slots with the number of advertisers creates a large market with replicated inventory. That is a coherent model, but it is a new market regime, not an aggregation of the paper’s time-sensitive auction with its fixed slate of ad space.

That objection weakens against the best version of the mirror. A deliberately large joint auction with repeated complete schedules and per-capita inventory is plausible enough to study. Its mass allocation is a natural aggregate of integral assignments, and the fluid welfare oracle is an LP. Nothing in the paper rules out asking whether water-filling can be compressed from \(n\) bidders to \(\tau\) types. The fact that its answer might be a straightforward extension would not make the question out of scope.

So the negative case is weak at its universal claim. It can argue that the paper does not motivate the high-multiplicity ad-auction regime with its data, and that repeated independent auctions cannot be pooled without changing the problem. It cannot show that the continuous core-pricing mirror is ill-posed or uninteresting in a large joint market. Theorem 1 remains a live anchor if that regime is accepted.
