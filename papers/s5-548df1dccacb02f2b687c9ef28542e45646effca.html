What the mirror covers

The proposed task covers evaluation of Next-Two on plurality shares; it does not computationally mirror Propositions 1–6, the divisor-method comparisons, or the Hall of Fame modification.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is that Next-Two has a natural population-continuum interpretation. Let a society be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a small support of complete voter rankings, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction holding ranking \(t\). For plurality scores, set \(s(c)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[c\text{ is ranked first by }t]\). In a large election with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings, the contender set depends on these masses rather than on individual voter counts. The decision is to output the Next-Two contender set; there is no optimization objective.

That gives a precise follow-on computational problem: given \(C\), the supported rankings and their rational masses, and a fixed tie-breaking order, compute the contender set induced by the resulting scores. Sort candidates by score; starting with the leader, include candidate \(c_h\) when all earlier candidates qualified and \(s_{h-1}\le s_h+s_{h+1}\); stop at the first failure, and never include the last candidate. This is a sensible continuous-population reading of the rule, but its direct evaluation is straightforwardly polynomial in the input size. It does not turn the paper’s contribution into a complexity result.

There is no qualifying named computational anchor in the paper. Propositions 1–6 are proved here, but they establish mathematical properties of score vectors and the rule: for example, Proposition 3 compares runoff conditions for three candidates; Propositions 4 and 5 give the candidate-count thresholds for Next-Two to select more contenders than Jefferson or Webster; Proposition 6 establishes a dropout-related property. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. The rule’s definition is an algorithm one can evaluate, but it is not a numbered complexity result. So I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem *per anchor* or assign those propositions a complexity class.

The weakness is also that the paper states its results directly in terms of scores, often vote percentages. A continuous population can ground those scores in voter types, but the propositions themselves do not need that population model. The plausible mirror is therefore narrow: continuous-population evaluation of Next-Two, not a continuization of the paper’s named results into a computational complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s contender-set computation is the strongest mirror, and the paper gives it no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–6 describe properties of the rule on score vectors; none classifies an algorithmic problem. The natural task—compute the contenders from a society of voter types—therefore adds a computational wrapper around those properties.

That wrapper does not produce a new population-level object. For plurality, the distribution \(\mu\) matters only through the score shares \(s(c)=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[c\text{ is ranked first by }t]\). For approval voting, the same holds for approval shares. The paper already formulates its criteria on real-valued scores and analyzes regions of fractional vote shares. Given type masses, one aggregates the scores, sorts candidates, and applies the stated comparisons. Changing the scenario—from a large election to a larger Hall of Fame electorate—does not change what information the rule uses. The paper’s conclusions already live on the space of those shares.

A more ambitious proposal might ask for the least mass of voters that must change ballots to alter the contender set. That could be a worthwhile new control or robustness problem, but the paper supplies no cost model or such computational question. Its mention of future strategic analysis does not anchor one either: treating voters as nonatomic makes a single voter’s ballot have zero effect on the scores, while treating candidates as strategic actors leaves a small, individually distinct set rather than a continuous population. A coalition or mass-transfer model could address this, but it would add the central problem rather than continuize a result in the paper.

So the negative case is strongest against the proposed mirror and against calling it a computational continuation of the paper. It is weaker as a universal claim: nearby control or robustness problems could be sensible research projects. But those are new questions, and the paper’s continuous score-share analysis is already the full mathematical object on which its stated results operate.
