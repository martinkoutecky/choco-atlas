The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in the required sense to anchor a continuous mirror. The paper’s numbered results are structural or axiomatic: Theorem 1 (proved here) says every noncircular hedonic game has the top coalition property; Theorem 2 (proved here) says that, among continuous rationing rules, universal existence of a stable coalition structure holds exactly when the rule is resource monotonic and consistent; Proposition 1 (proved here) says parametric rules induce games with at least one stable coalition structure. None establishes a complexity bound or algorithm.

The word “continuous” in these results describes how a rationing rule responds to changes in claims and endowment. The agents and coalition structures remain finite. The paper’s main contribution is an axiomatic characterization of core stability, so treating Theorem 2 as a computational anchor would stretch both the paper and ChoCo’s scope.

There is a plausible population-level research direction: a large municipality could have many households but few types, distinguished by claims and benefits from a public facility. Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of type \(t\), and let a coalition composition \(q\) have output \(E(q)\). A type-symmetric rationing rule assigns each member of type \(t\) a payoff \(a_t(q)\), with \(\sum_t q_t a_t(q)=E(q)\). One could ask whether there is a measurable partition of the population into such coalitions with no feasible submass coalition whose members would all strictly prefer its assigned payoffs. That makes the population continuous while preserving the paper’s core-stability object.

But this would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a named computational result in the paper. The manuscript gives no basis for predicting whether it is tractable, transfers hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 establishes a structural property of finite hedonic games; Theorem 2 characterizes which continuous rationing rules guarantee stability; Proposition 1 proves an existence result for parametric rules. None asks for an algorithm or makes a complexity claim. Here “continuous” describes how a rule responds to claims and endowment. The society and its coalition structures remain finite.

That defeats each result as a ChoCo anchor. A population version of Theorem 1 or Theorem 2 would ask for structural or axiomatic facts about stability in an atomless game. Theorem 2’s rule characterization is especially tempting, but it still characterizes which rules guarantee existence; it does not analyze the cost of deciding or constructing a stable structure. Turning it into an algorithmic question would require adding a new task.

The best population model is nevertheless plausible: finitely many household or researcher types, mass \(\mu_t\) of each type, coalition compositions \(q\), and an output function \(E(q)\). One could ask for a measurable partition into coalitions with no blocking submass. But the paper gives no composition-based \(E(q)\): it specifies outputs for each finite coalition \(C\). A computational version over continuous compositions needs a finite encoding for \(E\) and the rationing rule, plus a precise representation of feasible partitions and blocking submasses. Choosing, for example, rational piecewise-linear functions could make a well-posed problem, but those choices create a new algorithmic model rather than mirror a computational problem in this paper. The fact that its answer is unknown would be a reason to study a well-posed problem; it does not supply the missing link.

So I can defend a narrower verdict: this paper is a weak source for ChoCo’s computational programme, and its named results do not anchor a continuous complexity mirror. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no scenario is worthwhile. The typed funding or public-facility model is sensible, and its algorithmic version may merit study as a new project. The negative case is strongest on the paper-to-programme mismatch, not on the impossibility of a useful continuous-population question.
