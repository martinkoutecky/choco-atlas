The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of constituency types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a directed graph of permitted delegation edges, total preorders over incoming edges, and per-member voting weights \(w_t\), set \(W_t=\mu_t w_t\). Assuming \(W_s+W_t>W_u\) for all \(s,t,u\in T\), decide whether there is a rooted arborescence \(A\) such that every feasible arborescence \(A'\) satisfies \(\Delta_W(A',A)\leq 0\), and find one if it exists, where \(\Delta_W\) is the preference margin weighted by \(W_t\).

The model it lives in

A delegation network whose vertices are constituency types. Each type \(t\) represents population mass \(\mu_t\); all members share its delegation policy and preferences, and the bloc chooses one incoming edge. Its total influence is \(W_t=\mu_t w_t\), which weights comparisons between feasible arborescences.

The objection that survived

The mirror represents each constituency’s mass through its influence on one network node; independent member-level delegation choices and their effect on cycles are outside its scope.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the existence and finding guarantee of Theorems 19 and 13 under total-preorder preferences and the stated weight condition. It leaves unrestricted weights, partial-order preferences, and the proposed extensions to unpopularity margins, unpopularity factors, and the popular-branching polytope aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the condition \(W_s+W_t>W_u\) be weakened while retaining a polynomial-time algorithm for rational type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Rational-Mass Popular Branching for a liquid-democracy system whose constituencies delegate as units. A type \(t\) is a constituency with a common delegation policy, the same total preorder over incoming delegate types, and the same per-member voting power. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its share of the electorate, so its total influence is \(W_t=\mu_t w_t\). The constituency chooses one incoming edge for all its members. That shared decision is part of the scenario: these are coordinated blocs, not voters who happen to have similar preferences.

The regime could be a large platform with millions of represented voters organized into a few dozen constituencies, each with a stable delegation policy and access to the same delegate types. The type graph records which constituencies can delegate to which others. This is a natural fit for the paper’s liquid-democracy motivation and its vertex-weighted popularity measure.

My single anchor is Theorem 19, proved in this paper. Under the paper’s conditions—total-preorder preferences and positive integer vertex weights satisfying \(w(s)+w(t)>w(u)\) for every triple of vertices—if a popular arborescence exists, the algorithm finds one. Section 4 states a running time of \(O(mn^2)\). The companion Theorem 13, also proved here, establishes that any arborescence the algorithm returns is popular. Together these give the existence-and-finding result I am mirroring.

Precisely, an instance of Rational-Mass Popular Branching has a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a directed graph of permitted delegation edges between types, and a total preorder over each type’s incoming edges, with the root edge least preferred. Each type \(t\) has rational total influence \(W_t=\mu_t w_t\), and the instance satisfies \(W_s+W_t>W_u\) for all \(s,t,u\in T\). The question is whether there is a rooted arborescence on the type graph such that no other feasible arborescence is preferred by strictly more total influence. A solution is that arborescence; its comparison margin against \(A'\) is
\[ \Delta_W(A,A')= \sum_{t:A(t)\succ_t A'(t)}W_t- \sum_{t:A'(t)\succ_t A(t)}W_t. \]
The arborescence is popular exactly when \(\Delta_W(A',A)\leq 0\) for every feasible \(A'\). This is an undefeatedness question, not an optimization over edge costs.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the number of types and the input bit length. Multiplying all rational \(W_t\) by their common denominator preserves every popularity comparison and the weight inequality, producing an instance with integer weights to which the paper’s algorithm applies. The resulting graph has \(\tau=|T|\) vertices, regardless of how many people its constituencies represent. That is a concrete high-multiplicity payoff. The prediction about bit complexity is mine; the paper states its running time in graph-size terms.

The scope is the paper’s popular-arborescence existence and finding result, under its preorder and weight restrictions. It does not cover arbitrary weights or partial-order preferences, which the paper leaves for future work. The mirror also raises further questions: can the weight restriction be relaxed for rational constituency masses, and how does the bloc model relate to discrete elections in which constituency sizes are duplicated?

My weakest point is the bloc decision. A constituency that delegates together is a narrower regime than a population in which each voter chooses independently. I think the authors could recognize this as their problem because each bloc is a weighted vertex with preferences over incoming edges, and the shared choice is a plausible institutional rule. Still, a referee could reasonably ask for the individual-choice version. There, a continuum can make a single voter a zero-mass delegate and thereby change the role of cycles in the branching. That issue makes the bloc assumption important to state, rather than quietly treating type mass as an ordinary vertex weight.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The algorithmic anchor is real: Theorem 19 says the algorithm finds a popular arborescence whenever one exists, and Theorem 13 proves that any arborescence it returns is popular. The case against a mirror has to be about whether population mass fits the problem, not whether there is a result to mirror.

The difficulty is that a vertex in this paper is a position in a directed network, not just a voter with a preference type. It makes its own parent choice, and the identity of that parent affects whether the result is an arborescence. A type description that records only preferences is therefore insufficient: it loses the network relationships that determine cycles and reachability. Recording each vertex’s full network role preserves those relationships, but in a general instance leaves essentially one type per vertex, so the population does not become a low-support object.

The proposed constituency version is the strongest counterexample to this objection. It is coherent to treat each constituency as one vertex, let its members’ combined influence be \(W_t=\mu_t w_t\), and require the constituency to choose a single incoming edge. But this makes each bloc an indivisible decision-maker. Its mass changes the weight of its vote; it does not create a population whose members can make different delegation choices. That is a plausible institutional variant, but it does little to continuize the branching problem itself. Clearing denominators would put rational \(W_t\) within the paper’s integer-weight model when its weight condition holds. That observation alone does not make a mirror unworthy; the stronger concern is that the continuous parameter describes influence attached to finitely many network positions, while the branching decisions remain discrete and position-specific.

A more population-centered mirror would let members of each type delegate independently and record the mass choosing each parent type. Those aggregate choices do not determine whether the underlying individual-level network is an arborescence: the same type-level flows can conceal cycles among members or fail to capture how members reach the root. Preserving that information would require a richer model of the network and its individual links, rather than the paper’s finite-support population over types.

So my best case against is that Theorem 19’s computational object is a network of distinct decision positions, and ordinary type masses either fail to preserve its feasibility constraints or turn each type into a bloc that acts as one vertex. I would not call the negative case airtight: the bloc interpretation is plausible, and the authors might accept it as their problem. But it is a narrow extension of weighted vertices, not a convincing high-multiplicity relaxation of individual delegation.
