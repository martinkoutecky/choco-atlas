The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a new high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s theoretical bargaining model, but it does not meet your anchor requirement. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered Results 1–3 report experimental findings, not complexity classifications. The formulas in equations (1)–(3) give expected payoffs for the three-player game; they do not state an algorithmic result. So there is no eligible named computational anchor, and no continuous problem I can honestly present as a mirror of one.

A plausible extension would be Two-Period High-Multiplicity BF Payoff Computation. Let \(T\) be a finite set of legislator types, with positive rational population masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. A type completely specifies members’ relevant characteristics; here, members of type \(t\) share the same recognition profile. Let \(q_t^s\) be the aggregate probability that the period-\(s\) proposer comes from type \(t\), with \(\sum_t q_t^s=1\). Give the committee a total budget \(Nb\) for \(N\) members, so the per-capita budget \(b\) stays fixed as the population grows. Each member has linear utility, the discount factor is one, and proposals pass by majority vote.

The input is \((T,\mu,q^1,q^2,b)\), all rational. A solution is the exact limiting expected payoff for each type under proposer-optimal subgame-perfect equilibrium, together with an optimal type-level coalition policy. In the second period, a type-\(t\) member’s continuation value is \(v_t=bq_t^2/\mu_t\). In the first period, the proposer can choose coalition masses \(z_t\), where \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t z_t\ge 1/2\). The proposer’s optimal coalition minimizes \(\sum_t z_t v_t\), the continuation payments needed to secure a majority. This is a fractional selection problem over types; sorting by \(v_t\) gives a polynomial-time solution. The resulting type payoffs combine the chance of being selected for the coalition with the chance of being proposer.

This has a plausible regime: a large assembly with many interchangeable members across a moderate number of types, where type records recognition weight and all other relevant bargaining characteristics. The type-level recognition share \(q_t^s\) corresponds to each type-\(t\) member having probability \(q_t^s/(N\mu_t)\) of recognition. The per-capita budget scaling and the limiting majority quota of one-half are necessary co-modifications: without budget scaling, continuation values vanish as the legislature grows.

The authors could recognize this as a continuous-population version of the two-period Baron–Ferejohn model underlying their equations and coalition predictions. It could ask whether higher recognition weight changes a type’s equilibrium payoff or chance of coalition inclusion, and whether that effect persists when the number of types is small relative to the legislature. The likely computational classification is Class A for this restricted two-period model, because coalition choice reduces to a linear program.

Its weakest point is substantial: the paper studies experimental behavior in a deliberately three-player game, and the extension changes the committee size, the majority quota, and the budget scale. It mirrors the theoretical submodel, not the paper’s experimental results. The paper’s Result 1—no negative value of recognition probability in three treatments, but a negative value in Automated Period 2—could motivate the question, but it is not a computational anchor. I would therefore treat this as a credible direction for a follow-on paper, not as a result-anchored continuous mirror of this one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no eligible result in this paper to anchor a ChoCo mirror. “Results 1–3” report experimental findings about payoffs, payment choices, and coalition formation; equations (1)–(3) calculate expected payoffs in a fixed three-player game. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result. The proponent acknowledges this, so their proposed extension is not a mirror of a named result.

The strongest possible rescue is their large-committee version of two-period Baron–Ferejohn bargaining. A legislature with many interchangeable members and a small number of recognition types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But this changes the strategic object the paper studies. In a continuum, any one member has measure zero, so changing that member’s vote cannot change whether a majority accepts. The paper’s continuation-value threshold—and the proposed rule that a coalition must be paid enough to secure its members’ votes—depends on votes having strategic force. In the atomless game, that threshold does not follow from individual voting incentives. Imposing it as a coalition-feasibility rule, or deriving it through a specified finite-population limit, requires a new modeling and equilibrium-selection choice. The resulting problem may be sensible, but it is a new model rather than a continuous version of the paper’s experimental result.

Even granting that choice, the proposed coalition calculation sorts types by continuation value. That may be a neat extension, but it offers little of the computational landscape ChoCo is meant to chart. Adding richer constraints could make the optimization less immediate, but those constraints would need their own justification and would move the project further from this paper. A distribution over experimental subjects or behavioral types is another possible follow-on, but it would model a population of participants across experiments, not a continuous legislature solving the paper’s bargaining problem.

So the strongest honest negative case is narrower than “no scenario could ever make this worthwhile”: the paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and its most plausible high-multiplicity extension needs extra strategic structure before it becomes a continuous equilibrium problem. I cannot rule out a worthwhile follow-on study, but the case offered does not establish a result-anchored continuous mirror of this paper.
