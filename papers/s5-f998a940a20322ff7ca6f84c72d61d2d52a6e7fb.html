The case FOR (proponent)

By the requested anchor standard, this chapter has no eligible named computational result: it gives no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification. Definition 2 defines the optimization goal; the hypotheses and experiments concern heuristic performance. The closest claim is the unnumbered statement in §4.2(v) that finding the most-preferred criteria combination and the best-suited option combination is NP-hard, attributed to reference [18]. That is not a numbered result proved here, so it cannot honestly serve as an anchor.

The strongest plausible mirror I can offer is therefore provisional, not anchor-backed. Consider a provider choosing one common service configuration for many deployments of the same business-process family. A type is a complete CS-AHP preference model: its pairwise judgments, conditional preferences, concern weights, and any type-specific fitting scores. A type’s mass \(\mu_t\) is its share of the user or stakeholder population. The regime is many deployments but relatively few distinct preference models, so \(N\), the number of stakeholders, is large compared with support size \(\tau\).

Call the problem Continuous-Population OptSelection. The input gives the shared demands, options, QoS values, aggregation rules, logical feasibility constraints, and common qualifier-tag partition. It also gives a finite support of preference types with rational masses summing to one. Each type supplies rational CS-AHP scores \(r^t_{QT}(B)\) for each qualifier-tag cell \(B\), and the paper’s fitting-degree score \(r^t_S(z)\) for a configuration \(z\), computed from its QoS values. Let \(B^*\) be a cell maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t r^t_{QT}(B)\). The task is to return a structurally feasible configuration \(z\) whose aggregate QoS lies in a cell in \(B^*\) and which maximizes \(\sum_t \mu_t r^t_S(z)\). The configuration remains a discrete choice; the population’s preference profile is the continuous object.

This is recognizably an extension of the chapter’s optimal-selection problem: it keeps the same demands, options, QoS aggregation, conditional preferences, fitting degrees, and preferred-cell requirement, while making its plural “stakeholders” an explicit distribution. The authors might accept it as a group version of their problem, especially for selecting a shared platform configuration. It covers the optimal-selection goal of Definition 2, not AHP in general or the claimed complexity of their genetic algorithm.

I would expect hardness to transfer, if the NP-hardness claim in §4.2(v) applies to the precise underlying selection problem: a society with one type of mass \(1\) reduces this mirror to the single-stakeholder version, and the combinatorics live in the demands and options. Useful follow-up questions would be whether the hardness holds under a fixed number of preference types, and whether special structure in the demand constraints or QoS aggregation permits an exact algorithm. But those are questions suggested by the mirror, not results established by this chapter.

The weakest point is that the paper optimizes for a stakeholder’s preferences; it does not define how multiple stakeholders’ scores should be aggregated. The population-weighted objective and shared-configuration scenario are plausible choices, but they are an extension the authors would need to recognize. And because the chapter supplies no eligible named hardness anchor, this is only a candidate positive case—not a case that meets the requested anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor: this chapter supplies no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Definition 2 states an optimization goal, and §4.2(v) reports that related queries are NP-hard, citing earlier work; the chapter does not prove that claim as a named result. Its own GA analysis is parameterized by population size and generations, and its evidence for solution quality comes from simulations. So the proposed continuous question has no named computational result here to mirror.

The proposed population model also needs a collective decision rule the chapter never specifies. In its framework, a stakeholder’s AHP scores measure that stakeholder’s preferences. They do not establish that scores from different stakeholders are comparable as welfare, or that a type’s mass should weight its score. A weighted sum would impose those choices; majority, maximin, and other rules would yield different optimization problems. The proponent’s preferred cell, defined by averaging \(r_{QT}\), makes the same additional commitment.

If each stakeholder gets an individual configuration, the faithful population model can solve separately by preference type; mass records how many people use each solution but does not shape the choice. To make the population consequential, one can require a shared configuration. That is a coherent group decision problem, though it adds a common outcome and a social objective absent from the chapter.

That shared-configuration version is the strongest counterexample to a universal negative: a provider choosing one service configuration for a large user base with recurring preference types is plausible, and the chapter’s discussion of stakeholder preferences gives its authors some reason to recognize it. I cannot honestly defeat that possibility. The negative case is strongest as a challenge to calling it a mirror of a named result; it is weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from the chapter.
