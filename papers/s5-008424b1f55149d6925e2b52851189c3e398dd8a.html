The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a direct population mirror. Let each of the \(K!\) complete rankings be a type, and let \(\mu(\succ)\) be the fraction of society with ranking \(\succ\). A large electorate with many repeated ballots and relatively few distinct rankings is a natural high-multiplicity regime. Define consensus by replacing each count \(\mu_\pi(C)\) in the paper’s definition with mass \(\mu(C)=\sum_{\succ\in C}\mu(\succ)\), leaving the inversion distance and the sets’ cardinalities unchanged.

That gives a precise mathematical companion to Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every \(K>2\), there is a natural number \(c\le K(K-1)/4\) such that, for every \(n\ge1\), every \(r\in\{1,\ldots,K!/2-c\}\), every profile \(\pi\in P^n\), and every reference order \(\succ_0\), level-\(r\) consensus around \(\succ_0\) is equivalent to level-1 consensus around \(\succ_0\). Corollary 2, also proved here, says that level-\((K-1)!\) consensus implies level-1 consensus. These are structural results, not complexity results. Indeed, for rational \(\mu\), clearing denominators turns the continuous society into a finite profile, so the stated equivalence carries over directly.

There is a concrete computational question one could build from this mirror: given \(K\), \(r\), \(\succ_0\), and a finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu\), decide whether \(\mu\) exhibits level-\(r\) consensus. The input, predicate, and yes/no solution are well defined. But this is a proposed recognition problem; the paper does not analyze its algorithms or complexity, and its theorem is not a computational anchor for it. I would not assign it a tractability or hardness class from this paper.

That is the limiting point for the positive case: the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. Its numbered results concern structural properties of consensus and scoring-rule winners, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, or parameterized-tractable. The cited results from [1]—including the majority-rule and scoring-rule statements—are also not complexity results. So there is a plausible continuous-population reading of the paper’s mathematics, but no qualifying anchor on which to base the requested computational mirror. The mirror’s weakest point is also its main limitation: rational continuous societies here are just normalized finite profiles, and the paper provides no computational analysis of the compact, type-mass representation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither Theorem 1 nor Corollary 2 is a computational result. Theorem 1 proves a structural equivalence between two consensus definitions; Corollary 2 is a consequence of that theorem. Neither analyzes an algorithm, a complexity class, or a computational task. So they provide no computational result for ChoCo to mirror.

The proponent’s recognition problem—given rational type masses, decide whether the society exhibits level-\(r\) consensus—is well-defined, but it is newly added. It asks for the computational complexity of checking a property, not for a continuous counterpart to a computational problem in the paper. A better framing would still need an algorithmic task the paper actually studies; none appears here. The paper’s scoring-rule claims are structural guarantees about winners, not complexity results either.

I would not reject the population model itself. Rankings are natural voter types, and a society with repeated rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Nor is it an objection that rational masses correspond to finite profiles: that is the programme’s bridge to high multiplicity. The limitation is that this paper supplies no computational anchor on that bridge. Extending its structural claim to arbitrary masses may be mathematically natural, but it does not create the missing complexity question.

This negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper’s results*, but it cannot establish that no worthwhile computational question could ever be built from consensus. The recognition problem is the clearest counterexample to that universal claim; its weakness as a ChoCo mirror is that it has to be invented outside the paper.
