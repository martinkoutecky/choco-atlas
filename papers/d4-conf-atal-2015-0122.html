The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the market scenario: a high-volume electronic market could have many background investors who fall into a modest number of complete trader types, with each type’s mass representing its share of the investor population. That makes a population-continuum model plausible in principle. The paper itself, however, studies markets with 25 or 66 background traders, and its “continuous” double auction describes the trading institution, not a continuous population.

The paper reports computational findings, including empirical equilibrium and welfare comparisons in Tables 2–8 and Figures 3–9. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no result as an anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary asserting a computational or complexity result. Its findings are empirical comparisons of welfare and surplus in simulated equilibria. The proposed high-volume market is a plausible setting, but it is a scenario, not a continuous question tied to a named result. So no anchor survives because none was supplied.

The strongest replacement would model background traders as a distribution over complete types—valuation schedules, arrival rates, and strategies—and ask for equilibrium and welfare with a finite number of market makers. That is a coherent large-market question. But it changes the paper’s computational object: the reported equilibria are finite-player Nash equilibria, where one trader’s deviation can affect the outcome. In an atomless population, a single deviation leaves the distribution unchanged. Keeping unit-sized orders makes aggregate flow grow with the population; scaling order sizes or market-maker capacity instead creates a fluid or mean-field market model. Either route needs new market mechanics and an equilibrium notion. The result would be a computational study of that new model, not a continuization of a computational problem the paper states.

That makes the negative case strong against a direct ChoCo mirror: the paper offers no algorithmic problem to mirror, and its main contribution is empirical analysis of a finite stochastic market. It is not a proof that no scenario could yield worthwhile work. A finite-type population game for high-volume markets could be a useful adjacent project, but the supplied material does not establish it as a mirror of this paper. The universal negative is therefore weaker than the narrower conclusion.
