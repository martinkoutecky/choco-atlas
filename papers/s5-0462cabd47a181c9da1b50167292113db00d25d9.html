The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a valid positive case under the anchor requirement: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 1 constructs partition methods satisfying monotonicity, impartiality, and full influence; Theorem 2 constructs cross-partition methods satisfying monotonicity, impartiality, and full pivots. Theorem 3 says impartial rules with anonymous ballots must be constant, and Theorem 4 rules out simultaneously satisfying impartiality, positive unanimity, and negative unanimity. These are results about rules and axioms, not about complexity or algorithms. None qualifies as an anchor for the requested computational mirror.

The most tempting positive setting is a large peer-award system organized by fields: mass represents evaluators in each field, and mass nominations determine local and then overall winners, as in Theorem 1. But preserving the paper’s question is difficult. The prize goes to a particular peer, and impartiality concerns whether that person’s own ballot affects their chance of winning. In an atomless population, a single person has zero mass, so that condition risks becoming automatic. If types retain candidate identity to avoid that problem, the support may grow with the population, defeating the high-multiplicity regime. Collapsing identities changes the problem to selecting a type or representative.

So there is a plausible population-based *analogy* to the partition methods, but I do not believe it supplies a continuous computational problem the authors would recognize as their problem. Since the paper has no qualifying computational anchor, there is no honest one-per-anchor question to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s main point is decisive: Theorems 1–4 are about whether rules exist and which axioms they satisfy. Theorems 1 and 2 construct rules; Theorems 3 and 4 prove impossibilities. None states a complexity result or gives an algorithmic problem whose complexity the paper studies. Running one of the constructed rules on a profile is just counting nominations and applying its definition. A continuous input might make that a mass calculation, but it would not turn any of these results into a computational anchor.

The strongest version of the proposed setting would use a large population of repeated evaluator types and a fixed slate of possible recipients. That gives sensible mass-based nomination totals, but it changes the paper’s central feature: every evaluator is also a possible recipient, and impartiality asks whether a named person’s own ballot affects their own chance of winning. In an atomless evaluator population, one person’s ballot has zero mass, so unilateral impartiality becomes automatic. If instead types retain the identities needed to preserve the original peer-award problem, the relevant type space grows with the number of people; the high-multiplicity gain disappears. A hybrid with a fixed set of atomistic candidates and a mass electorate could be worth studying, but it is a different model whose candidate identities—not the repeated population—carry the impartiality issue.

That obstruction also defeats the strongest mass versions of the named theorems. For Theorems 1 and 2, local winners and cross-district pivots can be restated using nomination masses, but individual influence then vanishes: only a positive-mass group can move a tally. Calling that group-level influence “full influence” or “full pivots” would replace the paper’s individual axioms, and the resulting question would still be rule design rather than computational complexity. For Theorem 3, on a fixed slate, a mass-based plurality rule can be anonymous, nonconstant, and individually impartial, because no one’s unilateral ballot change alters the distribution. For Theorem 4, the same kind of rule can satisfy natural mass versions of both unanimity conditions as well. The discrete impossibility therefore either evaporates in the atomless model or requires a new group-impartiality axiom.

One could ask new computational questions about the robustness of these mass rules to coordinated changes in nomination shares. That is the strongest plausible extension, but it is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper, and the paper gives no reason to treat it as the next complexity question for ChoCo. The negative case is strongest as a reason to exclude this paper from a programme focused on computational mirrors. It cannot rule out every interesting hybrid model; those models would be adjacent new work, not a continuous version of the paper’s results.
