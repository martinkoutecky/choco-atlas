The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(M\cup\{\phi\}\), finite complete types \(t=(a,\succ)\) with masses \(\mu_t\), quota families \(Z(s)\), and per-capita bounds \(\underline q_{R,s},\bar q_{R,s}\), compute the GPS mass assignment \(x_{t,s}\) satisfying \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\), all quota constraints, and \(\sum_{t,s\ne\phi}x_{t,s}=\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\), while guaranteeing within-class envy-freeness and ordinal efficiency; realize \(x\) by assigning one indivisible school to each agent in the corresponding mass partition.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic school-assignment market with finite complete types \(t=(a,\succ)\), mass \(\mu_t\), indivisible schools per agent, and aggregate distributional quotas. GPS operates as a continuous eating process, with explicit LP extendability tests and measurable partitions realizing the aggregate assignment.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2 foregrounds mechanism properties rather than explicitly presenting a complexity theorem, so its computational status relies on reading the named theorem together with the adjacent algorithm and polynomial-time argument.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers GPS, its extendability LPs, within-class envy-freeness, ordinal efficiency, and approximate lottery implementation through exact measurable mass realization. It leaves the dynamic-menu serial dictatorship, the laminar exactness result, and the impossibility theorem unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can GPS event times and LP certificates be represented with polynomial encoding length in \(\tau\), \(|M|\), and the quota description?
  • What is the precise two-way approximation dictionary between rational finite populations and the mass problem?
  • Can the same guarantees be obtained when rankings or quota families are given implicitly rather than explicitly?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a positive case, with Theorem 2 as the lead anchor and Theorem 1 as a stronger but technically less clean secondary anchor. The paper contains no named NP-hardness theorem of its own: its NP-completeness statement about feasibility is cited from earlier work. Thus the positive case is mainly a Class A case: discrete indivisibility and population multiplicity disappear, while the allocation problem itself remains.

The natural regime is a large school-placement or refugee-resettlement market. Let \(a\) record public quota attributes such as neighbourhood, socioeconomic category, family status, or race, and let \(\succ\) be a complete ranking of the schools. A complete ChoCo type is \(t=(a,\succ)\), with population mass \(\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). In a plausible annual national market, \(N\) may be in the hundreds of thousands while only \(\tau\) combinations of public attributes and recurring ranking patterns occur, with \(N\gg\tau\).

The schools remain indivisible for every individual agent. What becomes continuous is the population: \(x_{t,s}\) means that mass \(x_{t,s}\) of type \(t\) receives school \(s\), not that an individual receives a fractional school. Since the population is nonatomic, each \(x_{t,s}\) can be realised by partitioning the agents of type \(t\). School capacities and distributional quotas are expressed per capita. If \(R\) is a set of public attribute classes, the constraint is \(\underline q_{R,s}\leq\sum_{t:a(t)\in R}x_{t,s}\leq\bar q_{R,s}\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that the generalized probabilistic serial mechanism produces an assignment that is (i) within-type envy-free, (ii) ordinally efficient, and (iii) implementable using a lottery over approximately feasible assignments. The paper also establishes immediately before the theorem that the GPS process is polynomial-time because every student switches schools at most \(|M|\) times. The theorem and its algorithm are stated in the paper itself, not imported from elsewhere. ([Paper PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~iashlagi/papers/final-paper.pdf).)

The corresponding continuous problem is GPS-\(\infty\) under distributional constraints.

An instance consists of a finite school set \(M\cup\{\phi\}\), a finite set of public classes \(A\), a finite support \(T\subseteq A\times\mathcal R\) of complete types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), lower and upper quota fractions, and the quota families \(Z(s)\). The problem first computes \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\), the maximum mass assignable to regular schools by any feasible mass assignment.

It must then output a mass assignment \(x=(x_{t,s})\) satisfying \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\), all quota constraints, and \(\sum_{t,s\neq\phi}x_{t,s}=\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\). The canonical output is generated by the continuous eating process: each type-mass consumes its most-preferred school at unit rate while the partial assignment remains extendable to an optimal feasible mass assignment; when a quota boundary is reached, the affected mass moves to its next school.

For \(\mu_t>0\), write \(p_{t,s}=x_{t,s}/\mu_t\). Within-public-class envy-freeness requires that whenever \(t=(a,\succ)\) and \(t'=(a,\succ')\), then for every school \(s\), \(\sum_{r:r\succ s}p_{t,r}\geq\sum_{r:r\succ s}p_{t',r}\). Ordinal efficiency means that there is no other feasible mass assignment whose type-level lotteries stochastically dominate \(p_t\) for every \(t\), with strict improvement for a positive-mass set of agents. A solution also includes a measurable assignment of each individual agent to one school, whose aggregate masses are \(x\).

This is recognisably the authors’ problem: the schools, preferences, distributional constraints, allocative-efficiency objective, eating rule, envy notion, and ordinal-efficiency criterion are unchanged. The only semantic change is that \(x_{t,s}\) is now a mass of distinct agents rather than an individual lottery. The finite paper’s implementation error is therefore a rounding artefact. If \(N\mu_t\) agents of each type are used as a finite approximation, the paper’s \(|T|\)-student quota error becomes at most \(\tau/N\) in population-share units; in the nonatomic model it vanishes and exact quota satisfaction is possible.

I expect GPS-\(\infty\) to be Class A. Its LP has \(O(\tau |M|)\) variables, and every extendability test is an ordinary linear program over the explicit type support. The main further questions are whether the event times can always be represented with polynomial bit complexity, whether the \(\tau/N\) finite approximation is sharp, and what happens when rankings are not explicitly enumerated but given implicitly.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here. It states that Algorithm 1, serial dictatorship with dynamic menus, outputs an allocation \(y\) and adjustment vector \(\Delta\) such that: \(y\) is feasible with respect to the adjusted quotas \([\underline q+\Delta,\bar q+\Delta]\); \(y\) is allocatively efficient; \(|\Delta_{R,s}|\leq |T|\) for every quota; \(y\) is Pareto efficient with respect to the adjusted quotas; and the mechanism induced by Algorithm 1 is strategyproof. ([Paper PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~iashlagi/papers/final-paper.pdf).)

Its continuous counterpart is Dynamic-Menu Assignment-\(\infty\). In addition to the data above, an instance contains a finite priority structure, such as a small number of admission-priority tiers. Priority is part of the complete type, so agents who differ in priority are different types. The output is a measurable assignment mechanism and a mass assignment \(x\), together with per-capita quota corrections \(\delta\), such that \(x\) satisfies the quotas \([\underline q+\delta,\bar q+\delta]\), assigns at least \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\) mass to regular schools, is Pareto efficient relative to those adjusted quotas, and is strategyproof for every infinitesimal agent. For a finite \(N\)-agent approximation, require \(|\delta_{R,s}|\leq\tau/N\); in the strict continuum limit, require \(\delta=0\).

The expected classification is again Class A. The dynamic menus are defined by the same auxiliary LP as in the paper, but over type masses rather than named students. The LP remains polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the quota description, and the encoding length. The high-multiplicity bridge is especially clear here: a finite instance with \(n_t=N\mu_t\) copies of each complete type is precisely the paper’s setting, while the continuum version removes the final integral-rounding obstruction.

This second mirror is weaker than GPS-\(\infty\). Serial dictatorship depends on an order of agents, so one must specify a meaningful high-multiplicity priority regime. A market with millions of agents but an individually unique priority position is not a convincing limit object. Priority tiers, standardised lottery bands, or repeated admission categories are plausible; arbitrary individual priorities are not. That weakness does not undermine the GPS mirror, whose simultaneous eating process is already naturally population-level.

I would not use Theorem 3 as an anchor: its impossibility result concerns the compatibility of ordinal efficiency, within-type envy-freeness, and weak strategyproofness, rather than computational complexity. The strongest positive claim is therefore narrower but solid: the paper’s GPS result admits a genuine continuous-population formulation with the same schools, quotas, preferences, and efficiency notions, and the resulting problem is a credible, polynomially solvable Class A member.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with the anchors themselves. Neither is really a complexity result in the programme’s sense. Theorem 2 is an axiomatic mechanism-design theorem; the claim that GPS is polynomial appears only as a routine observation that each student switches at most \(|M|\) times and each test is an LP. Theorem 1 is similar: its substantive claims are strategyproofness, Pareto efficiency, and quota-error bounds, not a complexity classification. If the programme requires a named theorem about the complexity of a computational problem, both anchors are weak.

Theorem 1 also resists a genuine continuum limit. Serial dictatorship requires a sequence of individually identified agents. In a nonatomic population there is no first agent, and no canonical successor. Giving agents a continuum-valued priority rank \(u\in[0,1]\) restores the order only by making \(u\) part of the type; the resulting type space is no longer the finite \(\tau\)-type high-multiplicity model. Grouping agents into finitely many priority tiers avoids that, but then there is no serial dictatorship within a tier. Any tie-breaking order reintroduces individual identity, and an aggregate eating or flow rule is no longer the paper’s dynamic-menu mechanism. The proposed per-capita correction \(\delta=\Delta/N\) therefore does not arise as a clean limit of the theorem’s mechanism: it erases precisely the finite order and one-agent-at-a-time structure on which the proof depends.

Theorem 2 is a much stronger anchor, and here the negative case is honestly limited. One can object that GPS’s \(x_{i,s}\) are lotteries for finitely many named students, whereas a continuous-population model would use mass assignment. But that is not a fundamental objection under ChoCo’s rules: with a type \(t=(a,\succ)\) of mass \(\mu_t\), \(x_{t,s}\) can be realised by partitioning the agents of that type into measurable subsets, each agent still receiving one indivisible school. Quotas become per-capita constraints, and the extendability test becomes an explicit LP over \(O(\tau|M|)\) variables. Simultaneous eating is naturally compatible with a nonatomic population.

Nor does the fact that the resulting algorithm is easy defeat it; “continuous does not help” is not an admissible objection. This is a legitimate Class A mirror: a large school-placement or refugee market with recurring public attributes and preference patterns has a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s finite rounding error disappears in the nonatomic formulation. Its private-information content may weaken when complete preference types are aggregated, but the computational mass-assignment problem remains well posed.

Thus the dynamic-menu anchor can be defeated on identity and ordering grounds, and the absence of a numbered complexity theorem weakens both anchors formally. But the universal negative claim cannot honestly be sustained: GPS supplies a credible continuous-population mirror, even if the paper’s own theorem does not present it as a complexity result.
