The continuous mirror question

Given a finite \(\omega\)-sparse template \(H=(U,F)\), with \(\tau=|U|\) and maximum matching size \(\nu(H)\), consider a continuum of copies indexed by \(r\in[0,1]\), with one agent of each role \(u\in U\) in each copy and bargaining edges between roles in \(F\) within each copy. Find measurable payoffs \(x_u(r)\ge 0\) and measurable edge-deletion sets \(B_{uv}\subseteq[0,1]\) such that \(\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{u\in U}\int_0^1 x_u(r)\,dr\le \frac{\nu(H)}{\tau}\) and \(x_u(r)+x_v(r)\ge 1\) for almost every \(r\notin B_{uv}\), while minimizing \(\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{uv\in F}\operatorname{Leb}(B_{uv})\). Give an algorithm whose objective is at most \(8\omega+2\) times the optimum.

The model it lives in

A continuum of replicated cells, each a copy of \(H\), with role mass \(1/\tau\), cell-indexed bargaining edges joining roles in the same cell, measurable payoff functions \(x_u(r)\), and measurable deletion sets \(B_{uv}\). The budget is the aggregate matching-value budget \(\nu(H)/\tau\), and the objective is the normalized mass of deleted edges.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's approximation guarantee for blocking sets in sparse graphs under replication of one finite template. It does not establish the guarantee for general connected graphings or multiple nonidentical templates, and it does not solve the exact minimum blocking-set problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the approximation guarantee extend to a finite mixture of distinct sparse templates under one aggregate allocation budget?
  • Can a finite input model for connected sparse graphings support a comparable continuous blocking-set approximation guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I would defend is a replicated-cell version of the paper’s sparse-graph blocking-set problem. Imagine many branches or campuses running the same small bargaining network \(H=(U,F)\): the same roles, the same unit-value deals, and the same sparse pattern of possible bargains in every cell. With \(N\) cells there are \(N|U|\) agents but only \(|U|=\tau\) role types. A type \(u\in U\) identifies a role and its bargaining opportunities; the cell index does not affect anyone’s preferences or costs.

In the continuum, let \(r\in[0,1]\) index cells and let \((r,u)\) be the agent in role \(u\) at cell \(r\). Give each role mass \(1/\tau\), so the total population mass is \(1\). For each \(uv\in F\), there is a unit-value bargaining opportunity between \((r,u)\) and \((r,v)\) at every \(r\). The matching capacity per unit mass is \(\nu(H)/\tau\), where \(\nu(H)\) is the maximum matching size in one cell. The allocation budget is system-wide, so payoffs can shift across cells; I do not impose a separate budget on each cell.

Call this problem Replicated Continuous Blocking Set. Its input is a finite \(\omega\)-sparse template \(H\). A solution is a measurable payoff \(x_u(r)\ge 0\) for each role and a measurable set \(B_{uv}\subseteq[0,1]\) of copies of each edge that are removed. It must satisfy
\[ \frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{u\in U}\int_0^1 x_u(r)\,dr\le \frac{\nu(H)}{\tau}, \qquad x_u(r)+x_v(r)\ge 1 \quad\text{for almost every }r\notin B_{uv}. \]
The objective is to minimize
\[ \frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{uv\in F}\operatorname{Leb}(B_{uv}). \]
The computational task is to output a feasible solution whose objective is at most \(8\omega+2\) times the optimum.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\omega\)-sparse graph \(G=(V,E)\), an efficient algorithm finds a blocking set \(B\) of size at most \(8\omega+2\) times the minimum possible, where a blocking set has a nonnegative allocation \(x\) with \(\sum_{v\in V}x_v\le \nu(V)\) and \(x_u+x_v\ge 1\) on every edge outside \(B\). This is the paper’s named algorithmic result for blocking sets. The manuscript also mentions NP-hardness of finding a smallest blocking set, citing [3], but that claim is not a numbered result here, so I am not using it as an anchor.

The approximation guarantee has a concrete route into the mirror. For a deletion pattern \(B\subseteq F\), let
\[ q(B)=\min\left\{\sum_{u\in U}z_u: z_u\ge 0,\ z_u+z_v\ge 1\text{ for every }uv\in F\setminus B\right\}. \]
This is the minimum fractional vertex-cover value of the remaining cell. A continuum policy induces a distribution \(\pi\) over deletion patterns, and its optimum is exactly the finite configuration LP
\[ \min \sum_{B\subseteq F}\pi_B|B| \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{B\subseteq F}\pi_Bq(B)\le \nu(H),\qquad \sum_{B\subseteq F}\pi_B=1,\qquad \pi_B\ge 0. \]
An optimal distribution needs at most two patterns. Each \(q(B)\) is half-integral and at most \(\tau\), so the mixing weight can be realized by some number \(N\le 2\tau\) of identical cells. The finite graph made of those \(N\) disjoint copies remains \(\omega\)-sparse and has matching number \(N\nu(H)\). Applying Theorem 2 to it gives a solution that, viewed as a piecewise-constant policy over the continuum of cells, has the same approximation factor. Trying each \(N\le 2\tau\) avoids needing to know the optimal mixture in advance. Thus I expect this approximation problem to be tractable, a Class A result.

The mirror preserves the paper’s central question: how much bargaining mass must be removed before a network can support an allocation within its matching-value budget? It continuizes the population, while retaining the network roles, the matching resource, and the edge-removal decision. The repeated-cell scenario is also a genuine high-multiplicity regime: population size grows with the number of cells, while the role set stays fixed.

The weak point is the regime’s narrowness. It models many copies of one sparse network, not one large connected network with irregular or long-range ties. The approximation result therefore supports a credible mirror of Theorem 2, but says little about whether continuous populations dissolve the hardness of the exact problem. Natural follow-up questions are whether the same guarantee extends to finitely many cell templates, whether the exact configuration LP has an efficient pricing algorithm, and whether a comparable population model works for general sparse graphings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor, so the negative case cannot rest on the paper lacking algorithmic results. Its strongest challenge is that the proposed mirror needs more structure than a distribution over types.

In network bargaining, a role \(u\) does not by itself specify who its bargaining partners are. If the cell coordinate \(r\) is omitted, the type masses do not say which copies of roles \(u\) and \(v\) can bargain together. If \(r\) is included in the type, the model has a continuum of types. The proposed construction supplies the missing information through a shared coordinate and edge relation: it is a continuum of replicated networks, or a graphing, rather than the paper’s finite-support society model. That co-modification may be appropriate, but it makes the mirror depend on a separate choice of network limit.

The replicated-cell version is also unusually narrow. Its continuous policy is exactly a distribution over finite deletion patterns for one template. Because there is one aggregate budget, an optimum needs at most two patterns; the claimed finite-copy implementation then lets Theorem 2 supply the approximation guarantee. This is a coherent high-multiplicity regime, but its result follows directly from the paper’s theorem. Extending it to finitely many templates retains that feature. A more substantial mirror would need a connected large-network regime with a finite, computable description of its incidence structure. The paper provides no such regime, and ordinary type masses cannot encode it.

That is the best case against prioritizing this anchor: the proposed example establishes a narrow replicated-network corollary, while broader mirrors require a network-limit model whose input and computational questions are not yet specified. It does not establish the requested universal negative. Repeated campuses, branches, or local markets could plausibly instantiate the regime, and the programme allows the relational structure needed to define it. Since that construction preserves a named algorithmic question, I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The negative case is weak at exactly that point.
