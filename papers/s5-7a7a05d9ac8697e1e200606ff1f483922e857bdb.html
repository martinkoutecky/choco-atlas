The case FOR (proponent)

The closest positive foothold is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: when there are three alternatives and at least four agents, a PSCC is unanimous and strategy-proof on the CEUCEP domain exactly when it is coalition-weighted. Such a rule is specified by a function \(v:2^N\to[0,1]\) that is normalized, monotone, supermodular, and satisfies partition balance. At a profile, it assigns probability \(v(N_x)\) to \(\{x\}\), probability \(v(N_x\cup N_y)-v(N_x)-v(N_y)\) to \(\{x,y\}\), and zero to \(\{a,b,c\}\).

A plausible population scenario is a large, anonymous electorate choosing among three alternatives, where a voter’s relevant type is their top alternative. If the top-alternative masses are \(p_a,p_b,p_c\), a candidate continuous analogue replaces \(v(S)\) with a function \(w(\mu(S))\). It assigns probability \(w(p_x)\) to \(\{x\}\) and \(w(p_x+p_y)-w(p_x)-w(p_y)\) to \(\{x,y\}\). One could ask for a characterization of the functions \(w\) that make these probabilities valid across all mass profiles and preserve the intended incentive condition. This is a concrete population mirror of Theorem 4’s coalition-weighted structure; the paper’s anonymous example gives the scenario some support.

The difficulty is decisive for ChoCo’s scope: that proposed question is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational problem. Moreover, in an atomless population, an individual changes no mass, so ordinary individual strategy-proofness risks becoming vacuous; requiring positive-mass deviations would change the paper’s incentive notion. Theorem 4 also covers identity-dependent rules generally, while the plausible large-electorate scenario needs an anonymous restriction.

So the honest anchor audit is that the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind: its numbered results characterize admissible rules, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The continuous coalition-weight formulation is the strongest conceptual lead, but it does not supply a qualifying computational mirror or a complexity prediction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the strongest anchor, and its population mirror is genuinely natural. In the anonymous CEUCEP case with three alternatives, the relevant types can be just the top alternatives, with masses \(p_a,p_b,p_c\). Replacing the coalition weight \(v\) by a function \(w\) gives singleton probabilities \(w(p_x)\) and doubleton probabilities \(w(p_x+p_y)-w(p_x)-w(p_y)\). The finite partition-balance condition becomes a functional equation over \(x+y+z=1\). The paper’s anonymous example supports this restriction. So I would not claim that no sensible continuous *model* exists.

The problem is what that mirror asks. Theorem 4 characterizes which rules satisfy unanimity and strategy-proofness; it does not assert the complexity of a computational problem. Characterizing the admissible functions \(w\), or their extreme points, would extend the paper’s axiomatic analysis. It would not fill ChoCo’s stated gap in computational questions. One could add a design objective and optimize over those functions, but the paper supplies no such objective, and a complexity question would also need a finite representation of \(w\). Alternatively, keeping a finite electorate makes the input finite but leaves the result a high-multiplicity discrete model; asking about positive-mass coalitions changes the paper’s individual incentive notion.

That incentive issue also limits the direct continuum lift. In an atomless society, one voter’s report changes no mass, so any rule depending only on aggregate masses gives the voter the same lottery after a unilateral misreport. Strategy-proofness then holds vacuously. Giving voters positive mass preserves strategic influence, but leaves the atomless model; treating a whole mass as the deviator asks a different, coalitional question.

Thus the best case against is narrow but solid: Theorem 4 supports a plausible axiomatic continuation, yet no proposed version yields a computational mirror of a result in this paper without adding a new computational task or changing its incentive concept. The honest weakness is that this is a scope objection, not evidence that the continuous characterization would be uninteresting on its own.
