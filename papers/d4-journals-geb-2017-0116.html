What the mirror covers

No result supplies a computational mirror; Theorem 1 concerns finite-election equilibrium structure, and Theorem 2 and Proposition 1 concern large-election limits.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large electorate in which voters share the same common-value objective and prior, while their private-signal quality comes from a small menu of classes. Let the classes be \(q_1,\ldots,q_r\), with population shares \(w_j\). A type is \(t=(\sigma,q_j)\), and its mass is the fraction of voters with that signal and quality. For example, conditional on state \(B\), the masses of \((B,q_j)\) and \((R,q_j)\) are \(w_jq_j\) and \(w_j(1-q_j)\). In a national election, the electorate could be very large while the number of distinct types remains at most \(2r\). This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It proves that a symmetric equilibrium exists and that every symmetric equilibrium has one of two cutoff forms. In Type 1, with \(1/2\le q_R^-\le q_R^+\le1\) and \(q_R^-\le p\), every voter receiving signal \(B\) votes for \(B\); a voter receiving \(R\) votes for \(B\) below \(q_R^-\), abstains between the cutoffs, and votes for \(R\) at or above \(q_R^+\). In Type 2, with \(1/2\le q_B^+\le q_R^+\le1\), voters vote with their signal at or above its corresponding cutoff and otherwise abstain. This gives a clean type-based representation that one could use to specify equilibrium computation.

There are two further nearby results, both proved in the paper. Theorem 2 says that as the electorate grows, equilibrium is either Type 1 with \(\int_{1/2}^{q_R^+}f(q)\,dq\to0\), or Type 2 with \(\int_{q_B^+}^{q_R^+}f(q)\,dq\to0\). Proposition 1 says the best candidate wins with probability one in a large election. These support the large-electorate scenario, but they are analytical results, not computational ones.

Under the paper’s strict anchor requirement, I find no qualifying computational result: none of these theorems gives a complexity classification, an algorithmic result, or a formulation that the paper uses as a computational encoding. The numerical cutoff examples do not supply a named computational anchor. So I cannot honestly provide a valid prover question per anchor.

The tempting question would be to find symmetric equilibria when the population is a distribution over \((\sigma,q_j)\) types. But in an atomless electorate, one voter’s action changes the vote mass by zero. The voter’s payoff is therefore unchanged by a unilateral action, so every strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium. That formulation loses the pivotal incentives behind Theorem 1. Theorem 2 and Proposition 1 describe what remains in the large-election limit: the behavior differences at the heart of the paper vanish in mass, and the best candidate wins almost surely. The mirror would cover only that asymptotic regime, not the paper’s small-election results or experiments; its weakest point is that the natural continuum equilibrium formulation makes the central strategic problem degenerate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is not that a large electorate with repeated types is implausible. It is plausible: voters could fall into a finite set of signal-quality classes, and the experiments themselves use a finite accuracy grid. That gives a sensible type distribution, but it does not give the paper a computational result to mirror.

For Theorem 1, the best candidate would be: given \(N\), \(p\), and a rational finite-support distribution of signal qualities, compute all symmetric cutoff equilibria and their success probabilities. That is a coherent new computational game theory problem. But the theorem supplies an equilibrium characterization, not a complexity claim or algorithmic task, and the paper gives no finite input encoding for its general density \(f\). More fundamentally, its incentives depend on whether an individual voter is pivotal. In a nonatomic electorate, one voter changes the vote mass by zero, so the strategic incentives behind the cutoffs disappear. Keeping the \(1/N\) effect preserves the finite game; letting a whole type mass deviate creates a different, coalition-level game.

Theorem 2 and Proposition 1 are asymptotic analysis, not computational results. One could ask for convergence rates or an algorithm that approximates finite-election equilibria using a continuum model. That is the strongest rescue attempt, but it either retains finite-\(N\) pivotal calculations, making it an algorithm for the original finite game, or takes the nonatomic limit and loses the individual incentives being approximated. The distinction is not that the limit’s conclusion is uninteresting; it is that the paper does not pose a computational problem whose continuous mirror would address.

The paper’s continuous distribution \(f\) is over privately drawn signal qualities, while the electorate in the main model remains finite. And its experiments produce many separate four-person elections, not one large society: combining rounds would discard the group-specific pivotal events that drive the model.

This is not proof that no useful algorithmic project can be built from the paper. Such a project is possible. The negative case is that it would introduce a new computational problem and a new strategic model together, rather than continuize a computational result the paper actually establishes.
