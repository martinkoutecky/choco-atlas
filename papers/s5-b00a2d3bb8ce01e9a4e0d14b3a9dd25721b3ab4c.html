The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a computational question about the paper’s own large-market matching model. One limitation comes first: the paper contains no numbered result asserting membership in P, NP-hardness, or another standard complexity classification. The closest anchors are equilibrium and welfare theorems. They support precise continuous problems, but they do not establish that those problems are computationally easy.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that in the two-tier large-market model a symmetric equilibrium always exists and is unique. For each doctor tier, the equilibrium is either a pure strategy or a mixture between two neighboring application choices: \(k\) high-tier hospitals and \(K-k\) low-tier hospitals, or \(k+1\) high-tier hospitals and \(K-k-1\) low-tier hospitals.

A corresponding problem is Continuum Short-List Equilibrium Computation. An instance gives rational \(r>0\) and \(v>1\), a list length \(K\), and an accuracy parameter \(\epsilon>0\). Normalize the market so each side has high-tier mass \(1/(1+r)\) and low-tier mass \(r/(1+r)\). A type includes an agent’s side, tier, and complete within-tier preference order; the preference orders follow the paper’s independent uniform distribution within each tier. Take the deferred-acceptance outcome in the paper’s \(n\to\infty\) limit. Each doctor chooses how many of their \(K\) applications go to each tier, listing their highest-ranked options within each tier. A solution is the unique symmetric equilibrium strategy for high- and low-tier doctors, represented by distributions over \(k\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\), with each distribution supported on one action or two adjacent actions. For an approximate solution, require that no representative doctor can gain more than \(\epsilon\) in expected utility by changing actions. Utilities are \(v\) for a high-tier match, \(1\) for a low-tier match, and \(0\) if unmatched.

This is a plausible Class A problem: each type has only \(K+1\) actions, and the paper’s characterization reduces equilibrium conditions to acceptance probabilities and indifference between neighboring actions. The paper even gives the large-market acceptance equation for one case, \((1-p)^k=\exp(-(1-(1-p)^k)/p)\), citing Arnosti (2015). The complexity question would be whether those equations and the equilibrium conditions can be evaluated with certified error in time polynomial in \(K\), the input bit length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\). The theorem itself does not prove that bound. Further questions include exact computation with transcendental parameters, convergence rates from finite markets to the limit, and whether uniqueness survives more general preference distributions.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here. For \(v\ge e/(e-1)\) and \(K\ge1\), the social welfare at equilibrium is at least half the full-list optimum in the paper’s balanced two-tier market. With high-tier mass \(1\) and low-tier mass \(r\), that benchmark is \(2(v+r)\).

Its mirror is Continuum Equilibrium Welfare Evaluation: given the same limit-market parameters, compute the equilibrium welfare ratio to the full-list benchmark within \(\epsilon\). The paper’s theorem supplies the guarantee that this ratio is at least \(1/2\) in its stated regime. I would also expect evaluation to be Class A if the equilibrium-computation problem is tractable, since welfare follows from the equilibrium’s matching rates. Natural extensions ask for the tight worst-case ratio, unequal supplies of doctors and positions, or additional tiers. This is a welfare anchor, not a complexity-class result.

The plausible regime is a large residency or school-assignment market: many applicants and positions, \(K\ll n\), fixed proportions of high- and low-tier agents, and exchangeable within-tier rankings. The paper’s effective strategic categories are just the two doctor tiers, and the position masses scale with the market. To respect the programme’s definition of type, however, a type must include the full preference order, not merely the tier. The ranking distribution is compactly described by the paper’s uniform model, while the number of possible complete ranking types grows with the market. A mirror using only two doctor types would therefore lose information required by the type definition; the compact distributional description is a special feature of this paper’s model.

The mirror covers only the symmetric equilibrium and welfare results for the paper’s two-tier, balanced, uniform-preference large-market model. It does not cover finite-market strategic play, arbitrary type distributions, or the simulation results. The authors would readily recognize the equilibrium problem: their analysis already studies it directly.

That is also the weakest point. The paper has already taken the population to a large-market limit and derived the equilibrium structure there. The proposed mirror is strongest as a new bit-complexity and approximation question about an existing continuous-population model. If novelty requires the continuous society itself to be new to the paper, this model is already a collision. The positive case is consequently narrow: the paper gives a clear, computable-looking limit problem, while the complexity of computing its equilibrium remains unstated.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with the anchors: neither Theorem 3.1 nor Theorem 4.1 is a computational complexity result. The first proves existence, uniqueness, and structure of equilibrium; the second proves a welfare bound. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem. The computational task proposed by the other analyst would add an algorithmic question to the paper, but it would not supply a new population continuization: the paper already studies the \(n\to\infty\) market with fixed proportions, list length, and values.

For Theorem 3.1, the best proposed task is to compute the symmetric equilibrium of that limit market to a requested precision. But the target is exactly the equilibrium the theorem characterizes. The paper reduces each doctor tier’s strategy to how many of \(K\) applications go to each tier, proves the support is one action or two adjacent actions, and expresses the large-market acceptance rates through fixed-point equations. Asking for certified bit complexity would sharpen the computational treatment of this existing continuum model; it would not make the society continuous for the first time.

There is also a mismatch with ChoCo’s stated type input. A complete type here includes a preference order over individual hospitals. As \(n\) grows, the hospital set and the space of these rankings grow too; the uniform ranking law is a succinct description of a changing random environment, not an explicit distribution over a fixed finite type set with support \(\tau\). Collapsing everyone to the two tiers would give a compact input, but tier alone is not a complete type for deferred acceptance: within-tier rankings determine proposals and rejections. The paper’s large-market approximation handles that detail analytically. Requiring an explicit complete-type distribution either loses the paper’s compact model or sends the input size growing with the market.

Theorem 4.1 has the same limitation, and its welfare question is not an independent anchor. Its equilibrium welfare is calculated from the same limiting match rates and strategies. Computing that welfare ratio to precision would add a computational specification to an already continuous analysis. Optimizing the bound over broader tier structures or supply ratios could be worthwhile, but it would ask for a generalized market model, not mirror this theorem. The finite hospital categories and per-capita capacities needed for such a model may be sensible co-modifications; the paper’s own two-tier limit already makes the corresponding aggregate move.

So the case against is that this paper is a poor choice if the programme’s contribution must be a new continuous-population *mirror*: its central equilibrium and welfare results already live in a large-market limit, while the proposed complexity questions concern computation on that existing limit. The negative case is weak against the broader claim that some worthwhile computational study remains. Certified approximate computation of the equilibrium could be a useful follow-up, and the paper does not settle its bit complexity. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario; I can only argue that such a follow-up is not a new continuization of this paper’s population.
