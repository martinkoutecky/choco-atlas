The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is plausible, but it has no qualifying named complexity anchor. The closest result is Theorem 1 (Existence), proved in this paper: for any \(\beta>0\), every course-allocation instance has a \((\sqrt{kM/2},\beta)\)-Pseudo-Market Equilibrium with Priorities. That is an equilibrium-existence bound, not a result about computing an equilibrium or the complexity of doing so. Theorem 5 proves strategy-proofness in the large, also not a complexity result. The supplementary tatonnement algorithm computes examples, but comes with no named runtime or complexity guarantee.

If I were making the positive case anyway, I would propose continuous PMP equilibrium computation. Consider a large, repeated course market—for example, recurring cohorts in a university system—with many students but a moderate set of complete types. A type \(t\) specifies a strict ranking over feasible course schedules, a priority level for each course, and a pseudo-budget. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students of that type. Course capacities scale with population: with \(D\) students, write capacity \(q_c=D\bar q_c\). Each student still receives a whole schedule; only the aggregate assignment is measured in mass.

The computational question would be: given the courses, maximum schedule size \(k\), rational type masses and budgets, per-capita capacities \(\bar q_c\), and each type’s full schedule ranking and course priorities, find priority-specific prices \(p_{c,r}\) and an assignment \(x_{t,B}\) of type mass to whole schedules \(B\). Each type must receive its most-preferred schedule affordable at its prices, and prices must obey the paper’s cutoff-priority structure. Let \(y_c=\sum_{t,B:c\in B}x_{t,B}\). Require normalized market-clearing error at most \(\sqrt{kM/2}/D\), counting under-enrollment only when the lowest-priority price is positive, as in the paper. A solution is the prices and assignment satisfying those conditions; this is an equilibrium-computation problem, not a welfare-maximization objective. Theorem 1’s bound motivates the tolerance: after scaling its aggregate error by \(D\), it vanishes as the replicated population grows.

The regime is credible where many students share standardized degree plans, priority profiles, and schedule rankings—for instance, large recurring cohorts in a consortium or a large university system. The paper itself helps the plausibility case: it studies large markets and mentions continuum replication for a fairness result. Those are supporting signs that the authors would recognize a population limit as relevant. They do not establish the computational claim.

The classification is open on the evidence here. Theorem 1 reduces the effective price dimension from \(MR\) to \(M\), which makes a tractability investigation attractive; the paper’s search algorithm supplies no polynomial-time guarantee. Further questions include whether the error can be driven to zero, how runtime depends on \(M\), support size \(\tau\), and the schedule-ranking representation, and how a continuous solution rounds back to a finite allocation.

The weakest point is the type regime. The paper’s estimated utility model includes individualized random shocks, so full preference rankings could make nearly every student a distinct type. A low-support mirror needs a different but plausible setting with recurring complete preference types; priority similarity alone is not enough. So this is a concrete continuous research lead, but it cannot be presented as a paper-anchored complexity mirror under the required anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves existence of an approximate PMP equilibrium; Theorems 2–4 establish properties of its outcomes; Theorem 5 establishes strategy-proofness in the large. None gives a complexity bound or an algorithmic guarantee. The search procedure in Appendix E and the simulations show that the authors compute examples, but do not state a worst-case computational result. Under the requested anchor standard, there is no qualifying anchor.

The proposed equilibrium-computation mirror is coherent, but it changes the question from “does an equilibrium exist?” to “how hard is it to find one?” Theorem 1’s error bound is in seats, so normalizing it by a replication size \(D\) makes the tolerance \(\sqrt{kM/2}/D\), which tends to zero. A continuous society has no intrinsic \(D\): ask for exact clearing and existence becomes an issue again; supply an \(\varepsilon\) tolerance and the problem is well-posed, but that tolerance is a new modelling choice. With atomic types, strict schedule rankings and budgets can make demand jump at price thresholds, and a type cannot be split across schedules unless the schedules are equally preferred and affordable. A richer model with a continuous budget distribution or a specified way to split mass may address this, but that is further work to define the mirror, not a computational result supplied by the paper.

The stronger version—compute an \(\varepsilon\)-PMP equilibrium for recurring preference and priority cohorts with a continuous budget distribution and per-capita capacities—could be worth studying. The authors might recognize it: they discuss large markets, and even mention a continuum replication for a fairness argument. Those points support the plausibility of the setting; they do not make the equilibrium-computation problem a mirror of a named complexity result. They also make it hard to argue that no such continuous question could be worthwhile.

Theorem 5 is a weaker anchor still. Strategy-proofness in the large already treats an individual as unable to move market prices. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass, so the analogous individual-incentive claim risks becoming automatic. Studying manipulation by positive-mass coalitions would be a different question. The empirical setting’s 6,023 students does not by itself establish a moderate-support regime either: the paper models individualized utility shocks and large choice sets. Standardized cohorts could supply a plausible regime, but the case for them is separate from the paper’s stated results.

So I can make a firm negative case against a paper-anchored complexity mirror: no qualifying computational theorem survives, and the proposed computation task is a new problem built around an existence result. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario. Continuous PMP equilibrium computation remains a plausible independent ChoCo lead; the negative case is weak precisely at that boundary.
