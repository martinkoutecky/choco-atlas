The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. It has no named result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. That matters here: the paper’s voters already form a continuum, so a continuous-population model by itself cannot supply the missing discrete-to-continuous computational result.

The closest result is Proposition 1, proved in the paper, but it is an analytical characterization, not a complexity result. It says that, when Assumption 1 holds, there is a unique optimal editorial policy with \(p_1=1\) and \(p_0\in(0,1)\), where \(p_0\) is chosen so that exactly half the voters support the government in state \(0\) after report \(1\). In the paper’s notation, it is characterized by
\[ \sum_{n=0}^{N}\gamma_n\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{S_n(p_0,1)+(n+1)\mu}{\sqrt{n+1}}\right)\right)=\frac12. \]
The newspaper’s objective at this policy is \(q+(1-q)p_0\). Proposition 1 is useful context, but it does not meet the requested anchor criterion.

The strongest adjacent computational question I can formulate is: Continuous Editorial-Policy Evaluation. Given a finite-support degree distribution \((n_j,\gamma_j)\), with binary-rational masses summing to one, rational \(q,\lambda\in(0,1)\), rational \(\mu>0\), and a precision parameter \(b\), compute a rational \(\hat p_0\) within \(2^{-b}\) of the optimal \(p_0\); return the policy \((\hat p_0,1)\). Here a type is a voter’s degree class \(n_j\), with the same preferences and signal law; \(\gamma_j\) is that class’s population mass. The objective is to maximize the probability the government wins. In the full-correlation-neglect model, the root defining \(p_0\) is
\[ \sum_j\gamma_j\left(1-\Phi\left( \frac{\frac{1}{2\mu}\ln A+\frac{n_j+1}{2\mu}\ln p_0+(n_j+1)\mu} {\sqrt{n_j+1}} \right)\right)=\frac12, \qquad A=\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\frac{1-q}{q}. \]
For a large social-media electorate with many voters but relatively few degree classes, this is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. I would expect the evaluation problem to be tractable: it reduces to finding the unique root of a monotone scalar equation. That is a proposed algorithmic question, however, not a result established by the paper; its bit-complexity analysis would need to be supplied.

This proposal covers only the computation of the optimal policy characterized in Proposition 1. It does not turn that proposition into a hardness result or establish how continuization changes the complexity of a discrete problem. The weakest point is also the central one: the proposed problem computes an optimum in a model whose population is already continuous. It is a plausible computational follow-up to the authors’ model, but not a strong, anchor-backed case for a continuous mirror under the programme’s criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only plausible anchor, and it characterizes an optimum; it does not establish a computational result. Propositions 2–9 are comparative statics and extensions of that analysis, not complexity claims. The proposed “Continuous Editorial-Policy Evaluation” takes the paper’s existing continuum of voters, its degree classes and masses \(\gamma_n\), and its optimality equation, then asks for a precision-bounded computation of \(p_0\). That is a possible computational follow-up, but it does not continuize the population: the paper already did that. Its equation identifies the unique optimum; a bit-complexity analysis of evaluating its root would be new work, but it would study computation in the paper’s continuous model, not a continuous mirror of a discrete computational result.

The strongest repair would start from a finite electorate and ask whether a high-multiplicity version of its policy-optimization problem is captured by Proposition 1. But the paper deliberately abstracts away the network structure, retaining only voters’ degrees. In a finite social network, who is connected to whom affects overlap among the messages and hence the probability of a majority. Degree alone may not be a complete type for that problem. One could specify an anonymous random-contact model where it is, but that would add a finite model absent from the paper; its limit would recover the paper’s already-continuous analysis. A convergence result for that construction might be worthwhile, but it would not turn any result here into a computational-complexity anchor.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit: the root-finding proposal is a computationalization of an existing continuum characterization, while a proper finite-to-continuous mirror needs extra modelling choices and has no computational result in the paper to anchor it. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no worthwhile project could arise. A formal complexity analysis of the root problem, or a carefully specified finite-network approximation, could be valuable independently. Their weakness as ChoCo mirrors is that neither charts the complexity landscape of a discrete problem the paper studied.
