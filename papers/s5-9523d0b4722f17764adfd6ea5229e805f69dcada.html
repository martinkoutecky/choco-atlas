The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of insurance types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), per-member expected losses \(\ell_t\), type certainty-equivalent loadings \(\vartheta_t\), and a grand-coalition outcome with per-capita reinsurer certainty-equivalent profit \(\pi\), is \(\vartheta_t<\pi/\bar\ell\) for every supported type, where \(\bar\ell=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\ell_t\), a sufficient certificate that no proper positive-mass coalition \(q\leq\mu\) can form a price-fair contract giving the reinsurer and every participating type at least their grand-coalition utility?

The model it lives in

A large standardized insurance pool with finitely many complete types carrying masses \(\mu_t\), and one reinsurer. Peer bargaining weights scale with type mass; coalition choices are mass vectors \(q\leq\mu\), and their price-fair contracts are judged by weak utility improvement over the grand-coalition outcome. The stability threshold compares each type’s loading \(\vartheta_t\) with per-capita reinsurer profit \(\pi\) divided by the pool’s mean loss \(\bar\ell\).

The objection that survived

The case defines \(\Pi\) as total certainty-equivalent profit while \(\bar\ell\) is per-capita loss; the limit needs an explicit normalization such as \(\pi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Pi_N/N\), which the case mentions but does not formalize.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result placing a problem in \(P\), proving it NP-hard, or giving another formal complexity classification. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper: under Assumption 2.5 and \(\sup_{\bm a\in\Delta^n}H(\bm a)>0\), if every peer’s certainty-equivalent loading satisfies
\[ \vartheta_i < \frac{\Pi}{\mathbb{E}[S]-\min_{j\in\mathcal N\setminus\{i\}}\mu_j}, \]
then no viable subgroup forms, assuming every subgroup must include the reinsurer. Here, viability means there is a nonempty proper subgroup and a price-fair contract under which the reinsurer and every peer in that subgroup are at least as well off as under the grand-coalition contract. The theorem also gives a sufficient condition in terms of the centralized insurer’s loading: the conclusion follows if
\[ \theta \leq \frac{\Pi}{\mathbb{E}[S]-\min_{j\in\mathcal N}\mu_j}. \]
This is a named tractability result, though not a formal complexity-class theorem.

A plausible mirror is a large digital mutual or employer-based insurance pool with one platform reinsurer and many policyholders in a small number of standardized risk classes. Let \(T\) be the finite set of classes and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of members in class \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A complete type \(t\) includes its members’ utility function \(U_t\), wealth \(w_t\), loss distribution, expected loss \(\ell_t\), outside-insurance terms, and bargaining weight. This is a high-multiplicity regime: the number of members is large while the number of distinct types \(\tau=|T|\) is moderate. To retain aggregate uncertainty as the pool grows, one reasonable risk model gives each member in type \(t\) a loss \(X_t=Z+Y_t\), where \(Z\) is a shared shock and \(Y_t\) is type-specific idiosyncratic risk.

Here is a precise problem that mirrors Theorem 4.3. Call it the Continuous Price-Fair Coalition-Stability Certificate problem. Its instance consists of the type masses and insurance data above, plus the grand-coalition utilities \(\hat u_R,\hat u_t\). The reinsurer remains a single agent. The peer bargaining weights scale per member as the pool grows, so that each type has a finite bargaining-weight density and the reinsurer retains a well-defined bargaining weight. Define the per-capita grand-pool expected loss \(\bar\ell=\sum_t\mu_t\ell_t\), reinsurer certainty-equivalent profit \(\Pi=U_R^{-1}(\hat u_R)-w_R\), and each type’s certainty-equivalent loading
\[ \vartheta_t=\frac{w_t-U_t^{-1}(\hat u_t)}{\ell_t}-1. \]
The question is whether the instance admits a certificate ruling out every viable proper subgroup of positive mass. The certificate is the typewise check
\[ \vartheta_t<\frac{\Pi}{\bar\ell}\qquad\text{for every }t\text{ with }\mu_t>0. \]
A valid certificate is the list of these inequalities; by the proposed continuous analogue of Theorem 4.3, it proves that no positive-mass proper coalition, together with the reinsurer, can use a price-fair contract to make all its members weakly better off than in the grand coalition. The corresponding outside-loading sufficient condition is \(\theta\leq \Pi/\bar\ell\).

This is a natural limit of the paper’s condition. With \(N\) members, an individual excluded from a subgroup accounts for only \(O(1/N)\) of total expected loss. When allocations and the reinsurer’s profit are normalized per capita, the theorem’s denominator converges to \(\bar\ell\). For a subgroup of type masses \(q_t\), its per-capita expected loss is \(e(q)=\sum_tq_t\ell_t\leq\bar\ell\); since \(\Pi>0\), the ratio \(\Pi/e(q)\) is at least \(\Pi/\bar\ell\). Thus the limiting threshold gives the uniform bound needed to exclude every such coalition. Checking the certificate takes \(\tau\) comparisons once the grand-coalition certainty equivalents are supplied. I expect this certificate problem to be tractable; that does not establish a complexity classification for the harder exact problem of deciding whether a blocking subgroup exists when the certificate fails.

The authors could recognize this as their problem: it preserves the reinsurer, expected-utility bargaining, the outside option, price fairness, and the same weak-improvement definition of subgroup formation. The paper already studies pool size and scales individual bargaining power with \(n\), which makes the high-multiplicity scaling plausible. Outcome contracts remain ordinary insurance contracts; the continuized object is the population and the coalition’s mass.

The weakest point is that the authors explicitly motivate their model with small pools, where each member has material bargaining influence. A large platform pool of standardized policyholders is a plausible alternative regime, but it is a narrower extension of their setting, and the certificate mirrors only their sufficient stability result. It does not claim to solve the full continuous coalition-stability problem or to turn the paper into a formal complexity-theory contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed certificate is a genuine limit of Theorem 4.3, but it is a thin computational result. The theorem gives a sufficient inequality, not an algorithm or complexity classification for deciding whether a blocking subgroup exists. In the proposed problem, the grand-coalition certainty equivalents are already supplied; the certificate then asks for \(\tau\) comparisons. Its continuous version is essentially the same bound after normalization.

The more substantial question would be whether *any* viable positive-mass subgroup exists when that certificate fails. The paper allows general dependence among individual losses, and type masses and individual loss distributions do not determine the distribution of a subgroup’s aggregate loss \(S_{\mathcal M}\). A generic mirror would need additional dependence data, potentially tied to identities or relationships rather than a finite set of agent types. That makes the proposed certificate a poor stand-in for exact coalition stability.

But this does not defeat the stronger mirror. The paper itself studies pool growth with bargaining weights scaled by \(n\), and its homogeneous pool-size model uses a common shock. Under a fixed common-shock model, coalition loss can depend on type masses, and an exact blocking-coalition question is well-defined. The reinsurer’s profit and the aggregate loss can both be normalized per capita; exponential utility, which the paper already uses for pool size, makes that scaling particularly natural. That leaves a plausible continuous computational problem, even if the paper does not solve it.

So the negative case is weak at its most important point: I cannot justify the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The best objection is that the stated certificate is almost immediate and does not resolve exact coalition search; the paper’s own large-pool model supplies a credible route around that objection.
