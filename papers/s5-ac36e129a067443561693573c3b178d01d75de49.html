Why no mirror

The proposed allocation mirror is concrete and recognizable: mass \(y_t\) represents members of an identical bid type who each receive their complete requested bundle. But the paper’s only computational anchor is labeled “Claim 3. Complexity,” which does not meet the specified numbered-result requirement, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the welfare-maximizing allocation and per-capita VM provisioning. It leaves the paper’s VCG payment and incentive-compatibility claims, along with its simulation comparisons, aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s winner-determination problem: choose which bidders receive their requested VM bundles to maximize total value under the provider’s capacity. This is a good population-level object for a cloud provider serving many standardized jobs. A type is a complete bid: a VM-demand vector and a valuation. Mass is the fraction of the workload making that bid. For example, repeated jobs in a few service classes could have the same resource request and bid, with \(n\) jobs large and the number \(\tau\) of distinct bid types much smaller. The paper’s simulations draw bids from ranges, so this scenario is not the one they study; it is a plausible regime for the same allocation problem.

There is a formal-anchor limitation: the supplied paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition. Its closest numbered computational result is Claim 3. Complexity, which I use as the anchor, with that qualification. Claim 3 says the CA-DP allocation algorithm uses \(O(nM)\) time to find the optimal winner set, by dynamic programming over users and capacity; calculating VCG payments reruns CA-DP for each winner, and the paper gives the mechanism’s overall worst-case time as \(O(nnM)\), apparently \(O(n^2M)\). This complexity analysis is given in the paper. Separately, the introduction says the WDP is NP-complete, but that assertion is not a numbered result, so I do not treat it as an anchor.

Here is the continuous problem corresponding to the allocation part of Claim 3. Let \(m\) be the number of VM types, with compute weights \(w_i\). An instance gives a finite set \(T\) of bid types, each with demand vector \(r_t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^m\), value \(v_t\ge0\), and mass \(\mu_t\ge0\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). It also gives per-capita capacity \(b\), the continuum counterpart of discrete capacity \(M\) for a population of size \(n\), so \(b=M/n\). Write \(s_t=\sum_{i=1}^m w_i r_{it}\). Choose \(y_t\), the mass of type \(t\) served, to maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T} v_t y_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le y_t\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}s_t y_t\le b. \]
A served member still receives their entire requested VM bundle; \(y_t\) is the fraction of that type served, not a fractional bundle for each user. The output can also report the per-capita quantities of each VM type to provision, \(\sum_t r_{it}y_t\).

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. The scalar-capacity model in the paper makes it a fractional knapsack LP; sorting bid types by \(v_t/s_t\), with zero-demand types handled separately, gives an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\) (and \(m\)), rather than in the number of users or the numeric capacity \(M\). This preserves the paper’s welfare objective, bundle demands, and capacity constraint. The continuous decision changes whether a type can be partly served: it means serving some members of a repeated type, each of whom still gets their whole bundle. That is the high-multiplicity relaxation at issue.

The mirror covers the optimal allocation computed by CA-DP, not the paper’s VCG payment calculation. Payments in its discrete mechanism depend on removing an individual bidder and recomputing welfare; in an atomless population, removing one person has zero mass. A continuous payment or incentive-compatibility analogue would therefore need its own formulation. Further questions include whether the allocation algorithm extends when capacity is a vector of resource constraints, and how the continuous optimum can be rounded to a finite population with an additive welfare guarantee.

The weakest point is the high-multiplicity scenario: exact equality of both requested bundles and valuations is a stronger assumption than repeated workload shapes alone. It is credible when jobs use a standardized menu of resource bundles and bid levels, but the paper’s random-bid experiments do not establish that regime. Even so, it is a recognizable version of the same WDP, and the paper’s own numbered complexity claim gives a concrete allocation result for it to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest formal objection is that the paper proves no numbered complexity theorem. But it does have “Claim 3. Complexity,” which reports \(O(nM)\) for CA-DP and \(O(n^2M)\) for the full mechanism. It is a brief, pseudopolynomial analysis rather than a theorem-level result, and the paper does not substantiate its separate claim that the WDP is NP-complete. Still, Claim 3 is plainly a computational result; its label is not enough to dismiss the anchor.

The strongest substantive objection is that the proposed mirror strips the allocation problem down to fractional knapsack. With type \(t=(r_t,v_t)\), the continuous choice \(y_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) maximizes \(\sum_t v_ty_t\) subject to one capacity constraint, \(\sum_t s_ty_t\le b\). Sorting by \(v_t/s_t\) solves it. That is a real change in the choice: CA-DP selects whole bidders, while the mirror can serve only part of a type. It removes the all-or-nothing decision that the dynamic program handles.

But this objection does not defeat the mirror under the programme’s stated standard. Serving a fraction of a repeated type means giving complete VM bundles to that fraction of its members; it does not split bundles. For standardized requests and bids, that is a sensible cloud-allocation question. With bounded bids and capacity scaled with population, the normalized gap between the discrete and fractional optima can vanish as the population grows. The fact that the resulting algorithm is simple, or that the answer is tractability, is not by itself a reason the question is unworthy.

The mirror is narrower for the full DP-VMPA mechanism. In an atomless population, removing one user changes welfare by zero, so the paper’s individual VCG externality does not carry over as a nonzero payment. A dual-price or finite-population pivotal-payment model could be proposed, but either asks a different payment question or restores the discrete individual effect. That limits the mechanism mirror; it does not dispose of the allocation anchor, which the other analyst explicitly chose.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case. There is a plausible repeated-workload regime, and the continuous allocation problem is well-posed. The strongest case against is that the mirror is modest: it exchanges the paper’s discrete winner selection for a familiar fractional knapsack problem and leaves its VCG pricing result behind. That may lower its research priority, but it does not show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
