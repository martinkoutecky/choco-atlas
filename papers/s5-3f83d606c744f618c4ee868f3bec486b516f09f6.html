What the mirror covers

The proposed distributional queue model concerns the efficiency-of-queues and coalitional-strategy-proofness requirements behind Theorem 1; it does not turn that result or Corollary 1 into a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern axiomatic compatibility, not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The nearest result is Theorem 1, proved here: no allocation rule for the paper’s sequencing problems can always choose an efficient queue and be coalitionally strategy-proof. Corollary 1, also proved here, gives related impossibilities: no rule satisfies Pareto-efficiency and coalitional strategy-proofness; no rule satisfies Pareto-efficiency, non-bossiness, and strategy-proofness; and no rule satisfies no-envy, non-bossiness, and strategy-proofness. These are meaningful results, but they do not meet the requested anchor criterion.

A plausible continuous-population translation would take types \(t=(r,c)\), where each type shares a service time and unit waiting cost, and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) gives each type’s population share. A queue could be a measurable ordering \(a:[0,1]\to T\) with distribution \(\mu\). With service workload normalized per capita, its aggregate waiting cost is
\[ \int_0^1 c(a(x))\left(\int_0^x r(a(y))\,dy\right)dx. \]
A rule would assign such an ordering and per-capita transfers to each \(\mu\), with transfers summing to at most zero. One could ask whether any such rule always minimizes aggregate waiting cost and is immune to profitable joint misreports by positive-mass coalitions.

That is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s queue-allocation model: for example, many service users could fall into a small number of shared service-duration and waiting-cost classes. But the question is still about existence of a rule with normative properties. It continuizes an axiomatic question, which the programme places out of scope; and Theorem 1 does not establish the answer for this continuum model. So it is a plausible neighboring mirror, not a defensible ChoCo computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result to mirror. Its Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 are impossibility theorems about axioms a rule should satisfy: efficiency, strategy-proofness, non-bossiness, and no-envy. They do not classify the complexity of an instance-level task. The paper’s own closest computational-looking fact—the characterization of efficient queues by ordering agents according to \(c_i/r_i\)—is a queue optimality condition, not the result the paper is built around.

The proposed mass version is coherent: many service users could share service time and waiting-cost types, and a rule could assign a measurable queue and transfers to the resulting distribution. But asking whether such a rule can be efficient and immune to profitable coalition misreports remains an axiomatic existence question. Coalitional strategy-proofness quantifies over rules, reports, and coalitions across the whole domain; it does not define an input-to-output problem whose running time or approximation can be studied. A continuum version might be worthwhile economics, but it misses ChoCo’s computational brief.

The strongest computational substitute is to give a distribution over \((r,c)\) types and ask for the queue minimizing aggregate waiting cost. That can be written as
\[ \int_0^1 c(a(x))\left(\int_0^x r(a(y))\,dy\right)dx, \]
where \(a\) orders the mass. Its optimum is obtained by sorting the \(\tau\) supported types by \(c/r\), the same structure the paper states for finite queues. This is a legitimate high-multiplicity scheduling problem, but it is not a mirror of the impossibility result: it drops the strategic and fairness requirements that make the paper’s contribution, and the paper already gives the relevant ordering rule. Asking for an algorithm to implement a rule with the paper’s axioms simply returns to the global existence question.

There are plausible high-multiplicity queueing settings, so the strongest negative case is not that sequencing cannot be continuized. It is that this paper offers no anchor for a worthwhile computational mirror: the direct translation is outside scope, while the natural optimization translation separates from the paper’s main result.
