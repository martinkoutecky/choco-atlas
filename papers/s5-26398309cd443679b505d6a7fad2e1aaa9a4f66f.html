The continuous mirror question

Given rational cohort masses \(\mu_t\), \(K\) goods categories with rational per-capita supplies \(r_g\) and \(\rho=\sum_g r_g\), reported values \(\hat v_{t,g}\), and a promise that compatible hidden values satisfy \(v_{t,g}\in[0,b]\) and \(|v_{t,g}-\hat v_{t,g}-\delta_t|\le\varepsilon\) for some \(\delta_t\ge0\), compute a sparse rational mass assignment \(x_{t,q}\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(K\), and input bit length \(L\). Bundles \(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^K\) must satisfy \(\|q\|_1\in\{\lfloor\rho\rfloor,\lceil\rho\rceil\}\), with \(\sum_q x_{t,q}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,q}q_gx_{t,q}=r_g\); for every compatible value matrix, every assigned bundle \(q\) of type \(t\), and every bundle \(q'\) assigned positive mass, require \(\sum_g v_{t,g}(q'_g-q_g)\le 2\varepsilon\lceil\rho\rceil+b\).

The model it lives in

Types are household cohorts with shared reported and true values for \(K\) categories; the society has mass \(\mu_t\) per type and per-capita supplies \(r_g\). The decision variables \(x_{t,q}\) assign cohort mass to integral bundles, and the computational objective is to find a compact assignment preserving supplies and the paper's robust maximum-envy guarantee.

The objection that survived

The theorem gives no compact mass-level implementation, and clearing rational denominators to run Round-Robin may require a population exponential in \(L\); the proponent identifies this gap as the question but supplies no construction.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the deterministic worst-case-noise Round-Robin upper bound; it leaves aside the lower bound, stochastic settings, the BTL application, and online discrepancy results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one compute a sparse rational mass assignment in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(K\), and \(L\) while preserving the Round-Robin envy bound?
  • How does tractability change when \(K\) or the per-capita supply \(\rho\) is not fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 3 (RR Upper Bound), proved in the paper. It says that for \(n\) agents and \(m\) indivisible items, if each true value \(v_{i,j}\in[0,b]\) is estimated by \(\hat v_{i,j}\), with \(\lvert v_{i,j}-\hat v_{i,j}-\delta_i\rvert\le\varepsilon\) for unknown \(\delta_i\ge0\), then the estimates suffice to compute an allocation whose envy satisfies \(v_i(A_{i'})-v_i(A_i)\le2\varepsilon\lceil m/n\rceil+b\) for every pair of agents. The paper proves this using Round-Robin’s EF1 and balancedness properties.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large distribution of households receiving bundles of staple goods. There are \(N\) households but only \(\tau\) preference cohorts: members of each cohort have the same true values and reported estimates for each of \(K\) goods categories. Each category consists of many interchangeable indivisible copies, with its supply scaling with \(N\). Write \(\mu_t\) for cohort \(t\)’s population fraction and \(r_g\) for the per-household supply of category \(g\); total supply per household is \(\rho=\sum_g r_g\). Here \(N\gg\tau\), while \(K\) and the per-household supply can remain modest. Cohort members still receive integral bundles; only the cohort-level proportions receiving each bundle are fractional.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Continuous Robust Balanced-Envy Allocation. An instance gives rational cohort masses \(\mu_t\), reported values \(\hat v_{t,g}\), per-capita supplies \(r_g\), and bounds \(b,\varepsilon\). The true cohort values are hidden but promised to satisfy \(v_{t,g}\in[0,b]\) and \(\lvert v_{t,g}-\hat v_{t,g}-\delta_t\rvert\le\varepsilon\) for some unknown \(\delta_t\ge0\). Let \(Q\) be the set of integral bundles \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^K\) with \(\sum_g q_g\in\{\lfloor\rho\rfloor,\lceil\rho\rceil\}\). Find rational masses \(x_{t,q}\ge0\), using only the reported input, such that \(\sum_q x_{t,q}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,q}q_gx_{t,q}=r_g\) for every category \(g\), and the resulting allocation has maximum true envy at most \(2\varepsilon\lceil\rho\rceil+b\) for every compatible choice of hidden true values. In the envy calculation, an agent of type \(t\) with bundle \(q\) evaluates another agent’s bundle \(q'\) by \(\sum_g v_{t,g}(q'_g-q_g)\); the maximum is over all bundles assigned positive mass.

I expect the fixed-\(K\), fixed-\(\rho\) version to be tractable: Round-Robin’s balancedness and EF1 argument carries over to mass assigned across integral bundle configurations, and the shift \(\delta_t\) still cancels up to the one-item imbalance. The computational question is whether that construction has a compact algorithm whose running time and output size are polynomial in \(\tau\), \(K\), and the rational input length \(L\), rather than in the expanded population and item counts. Further questions include how the classification changes when \(K\) or \(\rho\) varies, and whether the best robust envy guarantee can improve on the theorem’s bound.

This mirrors only the paper’s deterministic, worst-case-noise Round-Robin result. The mirror keeps the paper’s objective—worst-case envy between agents—along with noisy inputs and integral bundles. It does not replace individual envy with envy between average bundles.

The soft spot is the goods compression. The paper permits item-specific values, while this mirror assumes many copies of each of finitely many goods categories. That is a plausible instance regime, but it is a real restriction; if the authors regard item identity as essential to their problem, they may reject this mirror. The continuous population premise itself is credible here: cohort sizes and category supplies grow together, and fractional cohort allocation still assigns each individual an integral bundle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is also the hardest to defeat: Theorem 3 is a named algorithmic result, and the household-cohort mirror is a credible high-multiplicity regime. If there are \(\tau\) cohorts with identical true values and estimates, and \(K\) repeated goods categories with per-capita supplies, each household can still receive an integral bundle. A mass assignment \(x_{t,q}\) can preserve the paper’s maximum-envy objective by taking the maximum over cohort types and bundles in the assignment’s support. Neither the objective nor the population premise rules this out.

The best objection is that the proposed mirror has not yet shown a compact algorithm. Round-Robin’s guarantee comes from a finite sequence of picks: its EF1 property and balanced bundle sizes hold for each particular agent and bundle. A mass table does not encode that sequence by itself. One can obtain a finite instance by clearing the rational mass denominators and running Round-Robin, but the resulting population size can be exponential in the input bit length. Establishing a running time polynomial in \(\tau,K,L\) would require more than restating the discrete algorithm.

That gap does not defeat the mirror; it identifies the computational question it raises. The authors could ask whether the finite EF1 construction has a compact high-multiplicity implementation, or whether another mass-level construction preserves its robust envy guarantee. Restricting to repeated goods is a real limitation, but it describes a plausible instance regime, and the paper’s broader item-specific model does not make that regime artificial. The fact that the result might transfer cleanly would still be an answer to a well-posed question.

Maximum envy does make the continuous approximation fragile when a type’s share vanishes: a single outlier can matter in a finite instance even as its mass tends to zero. But the proposed mirror can restrict attention to cohorts with positive limiting shares, so this does not rule out the household scenario. I cannot make a strong universal case against this anchor. The honest negative case is that the proponent has supplied a plausible model and an open compactness question, not yet evidence that its solution would substantially improve on Round-Robin. That is a soft objection, and the anchor survives it.
