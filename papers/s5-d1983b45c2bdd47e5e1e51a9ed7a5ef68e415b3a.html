The continuous mirror question

Given two candidates, a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an initial ranking for each type, a rational symmetric influence kernel \(K\), and a normalized budget \(B\), is there a choice of directly shifted mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le B\) that makes the preferred candidate receive a strict majority? An initially opposing type \(t\) gains supporting mass \(x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)\mathbf{1}[\sum_u K_{tu}x_u\ge1]\); initially supporting mass remains \(\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

A deterministic role-block network with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rankings and shift costs fixed within each type, and a rational kernel \(K_{tu}\) describing pooled influence between types. The decision variable \(x_t\) is directly shifted mass; its total is the identity-cost objective and must fit within \(B\), while the resulting support must exceed one half.

The objection that survived

The paper floors each edge’s contribution before summing, so edges weighted \(K_{tu}/N\) contribute zero once \(N>K_{tu}\); pooled exposure has no demonstrated convergence from that finite rule.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 3.1’s two-candidate majority shift-bribery question. It leaves the paper’s other hardness results and its algorithms for particular graph classes and parameters alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the role-block problem as a function of the number of types τ and input bit length?
  • Can the continuous optimum approximate finite typed block networks, and under what scaling or rounding conditions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a large membership election with repeated network roles: for example, a platform or national association with hundreds of thousands of voters, grouped into a few dozen cohorts. People in a cohort share a ranking, the cost of moving the preferred candidate up one place, and the same influence profile toward every other cohort. Let \(N\) be the population size and \(\tau\) the number of these types, with \(N\gg\tau\).

A distribution over ballots alone would lose the network structure that drives this paper’s problem. I would therefore make each type’s network role part of its description and include a symmetric interaction kernel \(K_{tu}\). The entry \(K_{tu}\) is the influence a member of type \(t\) receives from one unit of directly shifted mass of type \(u\). This is a deterministic block-network limit: in an \(N\)-person version, each relevant pairwise edge has weight \(K_{tu}/N\), so aggregate exposure stays finite as the population grows. This co-modification keeps the population continuous while retaining the paper’s one-step propagation mechanism.

My lead question is Continuous Role-Block Shift Bribery. An instance has two candidates, a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), an initial ranking for each type, a rational symmetric kernel \(K\), and a rational normalized budget \(B\). A solution chooses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) directly shifted one place toward the preferred candidate. With identity cost per unit mass, its cost is \(\sum_t x_t\). Every unbribed member of type \(t\) receives exposure \(E_t(x)=\sum_u K_{tu}x_u\). If type \(t\) initially opposes the preferred candidate, its supporting mass after propagation is \(x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)\mathbf{1}[E_t(x)\ge1]\); initially supporting mass remains \(\mu_t\). The question is whether some \(x\) of cost at most \(B\) gives the preferred candidate a strict majority. A feasible rational vector \(x\) is a witness.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper by a reduction from Dominating Set. It states that Shift Bribery over Social Network is NP-complete on general undirected graphs under majority voting, even with two candidates and identity cost functions. In full, the underlying decision problem receives a voter profile, a social network, a preferred candidate, identity shift costs, and a budget, and asks whether a within-budget shift vector makes that candidate win after network influence propagates. The continuous question keeps the two-candidate shift, direct bribery budget, propagation threshold, and majority objective; it replaces individually specified voters and edges with masses and role-level interactions.

For the continuous problem, my provisional expectation is continuum-specific hardness when \(\tau\) is unrestricted. Choosing which types cross the exposure threshold creates a threshold-coverage problem over roles, even though the amounts bribed are divisible. That combinatorics depends on the number of types, not on the number of people. This is a conjectural direction, not a consequence of Theorem 3.1: each proposed activation pattern yields a linear feasibility problem, so enumerating patterns gives an exact \(2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) algorithm. The open question is whether the pattern choice is NP-hard in \(\tau\), or whether the structure of the kernel permits a polynomial algorithm. Other natural questions are whether the continuous optimum approximates finite typed block networks as \(N\) grows, and what approximation guarantees hold when \(\tau\) is large.

I would claim this mirrors only Theorem 3.1, not the paper’s bounded-treewidth, complete-graph, or other graph-class results. I think the authors would recognize the question as theirs in a high-multiplicity block-network regime: the campaign still pays to shift preferences, and network effects still determine which additional voters switch.

The weakest point is that Theorem 3.1’s arbitrary-graph reduction can give every vertex a distinct neighborhood. Its NP-hardness therefore says nothing by itself about populations with \(N\gg\tau\). The kernel also deliberately removes within-role neighborhood differences. That prevents claiming hardness transfer, but it leaves a well-posed question about whether network-role diversity alone can keep the continuous problem hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no “nothing computational to mirror” objection here: Theorem 3.1 is a named NP-completeness result, and the paper contains several other algorithmic results. The serious negative case is that its hardness depends on a feature the proposed continuum does not preserve: the individual graph neighborhoods.

For two candidates, a direct shift is binary, \(s_j\in\{0,1\}\), and voter \(i\)’s propagated shift is \(\sum_j\lfloor s_j w_{ji}\rfloor\). This makes the proposed \(K_{tu}/N\) scaling problematic. Once \(N>K_{tu}\), each edge contributes zero under the paper’s rule, however many shifted voters there are. Keeping edge weights at least \(1\) has the opposite effect: one shifted voter can influence every connected member of a block, while that voter’s normalized cost tends to zero as \(N\) grows. Thus the paper’s rule has no nondegenerate limit under the proposed per-edge scaling.

The role-block model repairs this by using \(\mathbf{1}[\sum_u K_{tu}x_u\ge1]\): it pools exposure across a type before applying the threshold. That is a coherent model, but it changes the propagation rule from per-edge integer contributions to aggregate fractional exposure. It also replaces individual neighborhoods with a type-level interaction kernel. Theorem 3.1’s Dominating Set reduction relies on those neighborhoods; compressing its hard instances into a small number of roles generally loses the information that encodes the reduction. So its NP-hardness does not support the proposed continuous problem. More importantly for this negative case, the proposed problem is better described as a new weighted threshold-coverage campaign model than as the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s formal model.

That is the strongest objection I can make, not a decisive one. The programme allows co-modifications forced by a continuum, and the authors might regard pooled, normalized exposure as a natural version of network persuasion for large cohorts. If they do, the role-block question survives: it is well-posed, has a computational objective, and offers a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. The honest negative conclusion is therefore limited: this paper provides no canonical continuization of its influence mechanism, and the proponent has not shown that their kernel model is the paper’s limit. I cannot make the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
