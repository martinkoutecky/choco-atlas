What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest case with a qualifying anchor: this paper contains no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Its named results are algebraic characterizations and existence statements.

The closest candidates are Theorem 6, proved here, which says that for fully ranked profiles a positional rule is recoverable from pairwise data exactly when its weighting vector or its negative is equivalent to Borda; and Theorem 9, also proved here, which characterizes the corresponding recoverable rules for top-\(k\) rankings. A natural continuous-population question would take ballots as types, their fractions as masses, and ask whether a linear decoder can recover the positional scores from pairwise totals for every society. But that is a rule-characterization question, not a computational result of the paper—and continuizing an axiomatic or algebraic question while the society stays finite is outside ChoCo’s scope.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime behind that formulation: many voters choosing among a small number of ballot types. The paper’s rational-valued profile spaces make the algebraic connection natural. But those spaces also include signed profiles, and the theorems’ claims over them do not automatically establish the same statements for normalized, nonnegative population distributions. Making the latter the subject would require additional work.

So the strongest honest positive-side observation is that the paper offers a natural population model to build on. It does not offer a named computational result to mirror, and I would not present Theorem 6 or 9 as qualifying anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper states no computational problem whose complexity it classifies. Its theorems describe linear maps, kernels, module structure, and which voting rules can be recovered from other data. There is no decision, optimization, or parameterized problem with an algorithmic result to continuize.

The proponent’s nearest candidates, Theorems 6 and 9, do have natural population readings: ballots are types, and a society is a distribution over them. But asking whether one fixed linear decoder recovers positional scores from pairwise totals for *every* society gives exactly the paper’s question. The point masses on individual ballot types are themselves societies, so a decoder that works on all distributions must work on every ballot type and hence on the whole profile space. The same holds for the top-\(k\) result. Asking about a single given society instead would be a different, pointwise question; it would not test whether the voting rule is recoverable. Neither reading supplies a computational problem from the paper.

The high-multiplicity regime itself is entirely sensible here: many voters can share one of finitely many rankings or partial rankings. That supports using population distributions, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor. A possible new project would ask which tuples of results under several rules are realizable by a normalized distribution. That is a population-feasibility question, but it changes the subject to a new input-output problem; Theorems 1 and 2 establish existence over rational profile spaces, not the complexity of deciding such feasibility.

So none of the proposed anchors survives as a computational mirror. The negative case is strong under ChoCo’s remit, though I cannot establish the broader claim that no worthwhile follow-on question could be built from this paper. The realizability problem is a plausible adjacent direction; it is not a computational result this paper gives to mirror.
