What the mirror covers

Propositions 1–3 establish welfare losses, a lower bound, and a replica construction; none provides a computational result for a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its Propositions 1–3, proved here, establish welfare claims about stable matching schemes: Proposition 1 gives an example with losses under every stable scheme; Proposition 2 gives a lower bound on those losses; and Proposition 3 constructs, by cloning, examples whose average losses are at least \(3/8 - 3/(4\kappa n)\) in magnitude under every stable scheme. None says that a computational problem is in \(P\), hard, or belongs to a parameterized complexity class. The clone construction is used to prove an existence result; it is not a complexity result.

The strongest positive indication is that the paper already studies large markets, replicas, and normalized ranks. That makes a continuous-population interpretation natural: mass could represent shares of men and women with given community and preference types, with matching represented by flows between types. But turning that into a precise computational problem would go beyond the paper’s named results. Proposition 3’s construction also orders individual clones in ways that may require growing numbers of distinct preference types, so it does not by itself establish a bounded-support continuum result.

So there is a plausible population-level research direction here, but no eligible anchor for the requested mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continue. Propositions 1–3 establish welfare claims about stable matchings: losses can occur, have a lower bound, and can remain large under replication. The “algorithm” in Proposition 3 constructs replicas; it does not solve a decision or optimization problem or establish its complexity. So the paper supplies no computational anchor.

The best population-level reformulation is still coherent. Put mass on complete preference types, represent matchings as flows between types, and measure spouse ranks by the mass of partners a type ranks at least as highly. One could then ask whether the losses in Propositions 1 and 3 persist for stable flows. The paper’s large-market and replica setting makes this a sensible regime; lack of multiplicity is not a convincing objection.

But that reformulation continues the paper’s welfare theorem, not a computational result. The clone construction’s identity-sensitive preference orders also mean that faithfully preserving its details can require growing support. Replacing those orders with preferences over a fixed set of types may repair the model, but it changes the theorem one would need to prove. One could add a computational task—say, optimizing losses over stable type flows—but that would be a new problem selected by the next researcher, not a question posed or resolved by this paper.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a source of computational mirrors: the plausible continuous question is about welfare structure, while a computational version has to be supplied from outside the paper’s results. The broader claim that no such matching problem could ever be worthwhile for ChoCo would go too far; a stable-flow computation might be a worthwhile project on its own. The defensible objection is that it is not a computational mirror of this paper.
