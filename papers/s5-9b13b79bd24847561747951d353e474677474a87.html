The case FOR (proponent)

On the programme’s literal anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results establish convergence, monotonicity, polarization, and probability-one asymptotics; none asserts NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), fixed-parameter tractability, an approximation guarantee, or a computational formulation with a complexity claim. Theorem 4 is proved here, but it is an analytic phase theorem, not a computational result. Thus the following is the strongest substantive mirror, but it is formally provisional rather than a compliant ChoCo case.

The best anchor is Theorem 4, proved in the paper’s Appendix C. It considers a deterministic two-island network with \(n\) individuals in each island, within-island interaction parameter \(p_s\), cross-island parameter \(p_d\), \(p_s>p_d\), unit edge weights, no self-weight, common bias \(b>0\), and initial opinions \(x_0\in(1/2,1)\) in \(V_1\) and \(1-x_0\) in \(V_2\). The theorem states that if \(b\ge1\), opinions in \(V_1\) converge to \(1\) and those in \(V_2\) to \(0\); if \(1>b\ge 2/(h_G+1)\), where \(h_G=p_s/p_d\), opinions converge to a unique pair \(\hat{x},1-\hat{x}\) with \(\hat{x}\in(1/2,1)\); and if \(b<2/(h_G+1)\), every opinion converges to \(1/2\). The surrounding argument shows that the first regime is polarizing, the intermediate regime may increase or decrease disagreement depending on \(x_0\), and the last regime is depolarizing.

A natural continuous problem is therefore:

Continuous Two-Island Polarization Verification. An instance consists of two voter types \(A,B\), with masses \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\). Every member of \(A\) has initial opinion \(x_A(0)=x_0\), every member of \(B\) has \(x_B(0)=1-x_0\), and both types have bias \(b\). Their aggregate exposure is \(p_s\) to their own type and \(p_d\) to the other type. Thus \(S_A(t)=p_sx_A(t)+p_dx_B(t)\), \(S_B(t)=p_sx_B(t)+p_dx_A(t)\), and the type-level update is \(x_r(t+1)=\frac{x_r(t)^bS_r(t)}{x_r(t)^bS_r(t)+(1-x_r(t))^b(D_r-S_r(t))}\), with \(D_A=D_B=p_s+p_d\).

The solution is the limiting pair \((x_A^\infty,x_B^\infty)\), together with the decision whether the normalized network disagreement \( \eta_\infty=p_d(x_A^\infty-x_B^\infty)^2 \) exceeds its initial value \( \eta_0=p_d(2x_0-1)^2 \). Equivalently, one may give the instance a rational threshold \( \rho \) and ask whether \( \eta_\infty\ge\rho \). There is no external intervention variable here: the decision variable is the induced type-level opinion trajectory, and the objective is to classify its limiting polarization.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Think of two large online or geographic communities whose members share the same initial stance, assimilation bias, and within- versus cross-community exposure rates. The number of individuals is \(2n\), while the number of complete behavioral types is only \(2\). The replacement of \(n\) repeated agents by mass \(1/2\) is especially defensible here: because all members of an island have the same opinion at every time, individual neighbor identities are irrelevant to Theorem 4; only the aggregate exposure rates matter. The normalization also respects the paper’s scaling, since \(p_s\) and \(p_d\) are per-capita interaction rates as \(n\) grows.

Writing \(h=p_s/p_d\), the continuous problem has exactly the theorem-backed solution:

  • If \(b\ge1\), \((x_A^\infty,x_B^\infty)=(1,0)\), and the process polarizes.
  • If \(1>b\ge2/(h+1)\), \((x_A^\infty,x_B^\infty)=(\hat{x},1-\hat{x})\), where \(\hat{x}\) is the unique fixed point in \( (1/2,1) \) of \(F(x)=\frac{x^b(p_sx+p_d(1-x))}{x^b(p_sx+p_d(1-x))+(1-x)^b(p_s(1-x)+p_dx)}\). Polarization occurs exactly when \(x_0<\hat{x}\).
  • If \(b<2/(h+1)\), both types converge to \(1/2\), so disagreement vanishes.

I would classify this restricted problem as tractable, or Class A, because the continuum instance collapses to a one-dimensional monotone fixed-point problem with an explicit phase boundary. It is not a continuum-specific hardness candidate, and it does not demonstrate a discrete hardness result disappearing; the source theorem is already analytically easy.

I would not use Theorems 5 or 6 as additional anchors. They concern recommender algorithms in limits \(n\to\infty\) and \(T\to\infty\), with a pre-existing continuous distribution \(g\) of user opinions. A population mirror there would risk merely restating the paper’s own asymptotic model, while still lacking a computational result.

The weakest point is decisive: this is a mathematically natural continuous-population analogue, but the paper does not formulate or solve a computational problem. A referee could reasonably call it a mean-field restatement of Theorem 4 rather than a continuous computational-social-choice contribution. What survives that objection is the regime itself—the two-island model is genuinely high-multiplicity and the mass dynamics are faithful—but a proper ChoCo paper would need to add a computational layer, such as choosing a minimum mass of users whose bias or exposure must be changed to force consensus, and then prove a named algorithmic or hardness result for that intervention problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is stronger than “Theorem 4 is not continuous.” Under ChoCo’s admission criterion, this paper has no qualifying anchor at all: Theorems 1–6 establish convergence, monotonicity, phase behaviour, and asymptotic probabilities, but none gives a complexity claim, algorithm, approximation guarantee, or computational optimization problem. The proponent’s “Continuous Two-Island Polarization Verification” therefore is not a computational mirror of a paper result. It is an analytic restatement.

The two-island construction makes this especially clear. By symmetry, the finite \(2n\)-agent system is exactly governed by two scalar variables \(x_A(t)\) and \(x_B(t)\). The parameter \(n\) disappears from the recurrence; it only enforces that certain degrees are integral. Replacing the two islands by masses \(1/2\) does not relax the finite problem or expose a high-multiplicity algorithmic structure. It simply writes the quotient already used in the proof. Adding a threshold \(\rho\) to the limiting disagreement changes the output predicate, not the computational nature of the problem: there is still no intervention, search space, input-size issue, or complexity question.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer happens to equal the discrete answer. That would be a mistake. The objection is that there is no computational question whose answer could become easier, harder, or algorithmically different. A scalar fixed-point calculation with a theorem already giving all parameter regimes is an opinion-dynamics analysis, not yet a continuous computational-social-choice result.

The best rescue would be to introduce control: for example, find the minimum mass \(\alpha\) whose bias or exposure rate must be changed to force consensus. But that is a new problem, not a mirror of Theorem 4. In the faithful two-island model, splitting off an \(\alpha\)-fraction merely creates one or two additional homogeneous types, leaving a constant-dimensional nonlinear recurrence. It does not turn population multiplicity into the source of a meaningful computational problem. If interventions instead target particular edges, neighbourhoods, or users, then the mass vector is no longer sufficient: the outcome depends on network correlations and identities. One must add a mixing kernel or graphon, together with a new control model. That may be worthwhile dynamics research, but it is no longer the paper’s stated result being continuized.

The same problem affects any attempt to rescue the recommender results. The paper already assumes a distribution \(g\) of user opinions and takes limits over users and books. A continuous population formulation would either restate that asymptotic mean-field calculation or add a new design objective—such as optimally changing recommendation probabilities or user masses. Again, the latter could be interesting, but it would be an invented computational layer rather than a mirror of a named computational result.

The two-island regime is genuinely a sensible high-multiplicity regime; I would not use that as an objection, and existing high-multiplicity work would support rather than undermine it. But naturalness of the regime is only a prerequisite. Here the prerequisite is present while the required computational target is absent. Against the strict ChoCo brief, that is decisive. The honest weakness is that a separately proposed mass-control problem might become a worthwhile project; it simply cannot be credited as this paper’s continuous mirror without substantially changing what the paper is about.
