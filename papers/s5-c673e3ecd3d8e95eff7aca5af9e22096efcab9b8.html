What the mirror covers

The proposed extension applies Proposition 1 to two delegates with population-average utilities; the paper's other implementation and equilibrium results likewise provide no computational anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. The closest is Proposition 1, proved here: Price & Choose subgame-perfect implements the efficient options—every subgame-perfect equilibrium selects an option maximizing the two players’ total utility, and every such option is selected in some equilibrium. That is an implementation theorem, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The other propositions are also mechanism or equilibrium results. Calling any of them a computational-complexity anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

The strongest population-based extension I can honestly offer is a proposed problem, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of the paper. Call it Mass Price & Choose. Consider two equally sized, large organizations negotiating over an explicitly listed finite set of options \(A\). Each organization is represented by a delegate whose objective is the average utility of its members. Members share a small set of complete types: each type specifies utilities over \(A\) and the same quasi-linear treatment of money. Organization \(r\)’s population is a distribution \(\mu^r\) over those types, with rational masses summing to one. The number of members is large while the support sizes \(\tau_1,\tau_2\) remain modest. Equal organization sizes let a per-member transfer between them remain budget balanced.

Given these distributions and the type utility tables, the problem is to compute an equilibrium price vector and an option selected in a subgame-perfect equilibrium. Define \(U_r(a)=\sum_t\mu^r_tu^r_t(a)\). A solution is a price vector \(p\) and an option \(a\) such that
\[ \sum_{a'\in A}p_{a'}=0,\qquad p_{a'}=U_2(a')-\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{b\in A}U_2(b), \]
and \(a\in\arg\max_{b\in A}\bigl(U_1(b)+U_2(b)\bigr)\). Proposition 1 supplies the implementation argument once the two population distributions are aggregated into utility functions: the chooser is indifferent at these prices, the proposer’s best options are exactly the efficient ones, and every efficient option can occur in equilibrium. For an explicit option list and explicit rational utility tables, the computational problem is tractable by aggregating the utilities and scanning \(A\), in time polynomial in \(|A|,\tau_1,\tau_2\), and the input bit length.

The positive case is that this retains the paper’s finite options, zero-sum prices, proposer/chooser timing, and utilitarian efficiency objective, while making the populations themselves distributions over types. The main weakness is that the paper’s players are individual decision-makers; treating a delegate as maximizing average member utility adds a collective objective. More fundamentally, the resulting computation is elementary and has no named complexity result in the paper to mirror. If \(A\) is given succinctly instead, the hard part may come from optimizing over the option encoding, not from population multiplicity. So this is a plausible extension of the mechanism, but not a computational mirror supported by the paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper contains no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes the subgame-perfect equilibria of Price & Choose; it makes no claim about the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 2–5 likewise concern implementation and equilibrium payoffs, not algorithms or complexity. One can define a task of computing an equilibrium from the model, but that task is an extension built around the paper’s result, not a computational result established by it.

The proposed Mass Price & Choose model is coherent: two delegates could represent large organizations whose members’ utilities are summarized by average utility. But after that modelling choice, each population enters only through \(U_r(a)=\sum_t\mu^r_tu^r_t(a)\). Distributions with the same averages induce the same game, and Proposition 1 gives the equilibrium prices and efficient options directly. The members themselves do not act in the mechanism; the strategic players remain two delegates. Adding member-level constraints or disagreement would make the population matter, but would also create a different collective mechanism.

A richer option representation could make finding an efficient option computationally demanding. That would be a legitimate new question, but the computational challenge would come from the representation of the options, and this paper supplies no complexity result to carry over or re-examine. The same issue arises if one tries to use the many-player or bidding extensions: their results are implementation statements, not computational anchors.

So the negative case is strong against the submitted anchor as a *computational mirror of this paper*: its named result is not computational, and the proposed population collapses to aggregate utility before play. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built. The organization scenario could motivate a useful new model; I cannot honestly rule that out.
