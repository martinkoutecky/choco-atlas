The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), binary rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and a strict partner-type order \(\succ_t\) for each \(t\in T\), partition the unit interval into type blocks of those masses and let \(\theta(a)\) denote agent \(a\)'s type. Decide whether there exists a measurable, measure-preserving involution \(\pi\) with \(\pi(a)\ne a\) almost everywhere such that, for every feasible rival pairing \(\sigma\), \(\lambda\{a:\theta(\pi(a))\succ_{\theta(a)}\theta(\sigma(a))\}\geq\lambda\{a:\theta(\sigma(a))\succ_{\theta(a)}\theta(\pi(a))\}\). Agents of the same type are indifferent between partners of that type.

The model it lives in

Types are complete roommate preference profiles with rational population mass \(\mu_t\), and each profile ranks partner types. The decision object is a measurable perfect pairing of the nonatomic population; it must have a nonnegative population-mass vote margin against every rival pairing.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's existence complexity result. It does not separately mirror Observation 1's odd-size stability result or the gadget lemmas.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can existence and head-to-head comparison be represented exactly by finitely many coupling variables over \(T\)?
  • What is the complexity of the decision problem in \(|T|\) and the binary input length of the masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror I believe is nonatomic popular roommates. Imagine a large housing or team-matching cohort in which people rank partner profiles—say, combinations of role, location, or work style—and every profile is acceptable. Each type is a complete agent profile, including its ranking of partner types. Members of a type share that ranking; there may be hundreds of thousands of people but only a few dozen types. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort of type \(t\).

There is one co-modification: agents rank profiles rather than named individuals, so people in the same profile are tied. That seems plausible in a large anonymous market, and it preserves the paper’s central question: can a pairing avoid losing a head-to-head vote among the people whose partners change?

Formally, an instance has a finite set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of all partner types for each \(t\). Represent the population by a unit interval partitioned into blocks of lengths \(\mu_t\), and let \(\theta(a)\) be agent \(a\)’s type. A matching is a measurable, measure-preserving involution \(\pi\) on the population, with \(\pi(a)\ne a\) almost everywhere. It pairs every agent with exactly one other agent. For two matchings \(\pi,\sigma\), define
\[ V(\pi,\sigma)=\lambda\bigl(\{a:\theta(\pi(a))\succ_{\theta(a)}\theta(\sigma(a))\}\bigr). \]
The continuous matching is popular if \(V(\pi,\sigma)\ge V(\sigma,\pi)\) for every feasible \(\sigma\). The decision problem is whether such a \(\pi\) exists. Its type-to-type mass flow \(x_{ts}\) satisfies \(x_{ts}=x_{st}\) and \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\); the matching itself, rather than just this summary matrix, determines which people vote which way.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for a complete graph on an even number \(n\) of vertices, deciding whether a popular matching exists is NP-complete. The paper proves this by reduction from 1-in-3 SAT. The nonatomic problem asks the corresponding existence question when the electorate is a distribution over preference types and votes are measured as population mass. I would expect continuum-specific hardness, though with modest confidence: the popularity condition still compares a proposed pairing against every rival pairing, and those comparisons depend on how the same people are paired across the two outcomes. But the paper’s hardness alone does not establish this. Its reduction uses many gadget roles, while fractional mass may allow divisions unavailable to a finite matching. The first follow-up is whether the comparison can be reduced to a finite type-level characterization, and whether the reduction survives when each type has positive nonatomic mass.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: the objects are still complete-preference roommate matchings, and popularity is still the same head-to-head vote condition. The interpretation is strongest in anonymous, large cohorts; it would be much weaker for a small committee whose members rank one another individually. This mirrors only Theorem 1. I would not carry over the paper’s odd-\(n\) result—Observation 1 says every popular matching is stable for odd \(n, and the paper attributes that observation to Huang’s personal communication—because its parity argument has no direct nonatomic counterpart. The soft spot is also the model’s main cost: ranking partner types ties agents within each type, and the aggregate flow x\) does not by itself preserve individual-level comparisons between matchings. If a finite type-level representation cannot retain those comparisons, this remains a natural continuous-population question but a less convincing computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has one clear computational anchor: its theorem proves that deciding whether a complete roommates instance with even \(n\) has a popular matching is NP-complete. So the negative case cannot say there is no result to mirror.

The strongest objection is that the theorem’s hard instances are built from individually specified gadget vertices and their rankings of other named vertices. The reduction gives no evident regime with many interchangeable agents of each type. A direct continuous lift would give each gadget role its own type, so support grows with the construction; giving each role positive mass also lets it split across matching states. A large-cohort model with a small number of types would instead rank partner profiles. That is a substantial change to the preference structure, and the paper’s reduction gives no evidence that its hardness captures this regime.

The proponent’s model can be made precise, though. Let types have rational masses and strict rankings over partner types, and represent a matching by a measurable, measure-preserving pairing. A type-to-type mass flow alone does not determine the votes between two matchings: for each type, one must also track how agents’ current partner types are coupled with their partner types under a rival matching. Maximizing over those couplings gives a well-defined population-level popularity question. It is a genuine computational problem, and large anonymous cohorts that rank partner profiles are plausible. This repair answers the formal objection; it does not restore the paper’s particular hard instances.

That leaves a case for lower priority, not a sound veto. The source theorem does not itself establish that its problem has a useful high-multiplicity regime, and the natural profile-based mirror changes the model enough that its relevance to these authors is uncertain. But the programme explicitly treats such fractional changes as potential results, and a changed answer would still be valuable. I cannot honestly defeat the strongest mirror in every plausible scenario; the negative case is weak precisely at that point.
