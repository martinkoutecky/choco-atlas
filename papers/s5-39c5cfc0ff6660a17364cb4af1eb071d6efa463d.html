The continuous mirror question

Given \(d>9\), rational \(B,\sigma>0\), \(\varepsilon>0\), and rational masses \(\nu_{ij,y}\ge 0\) summing to \(1\) over \(1\le i<j\le d\) and \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\), with positive-mass pairs forming a connected graph, compute a rational \(\hat w\) satisfying \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\hat w=0\) and \(\|\hat w\|_\infty\le B\) whose mass-weighted BTL negative log-likelihood is within \(\varepsilon\) of its minimum.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is narrow: mirror the pairwise-comparison estimation problem behind Theorem 2.C, using the MLE’s convex optimization as the algorithmic hook. The paper’s crowdsourcing setting makes a high-multiplicity regime plausible: many worker-task responses about a modest catalogue of items, with relatively few distinct comparison types. Treat each completed response as an agent. Its type is the compared pair and reported outcome; its mass is that type’s fraction of all responses. With \(d\) items, there are at most \(2\binom d2\) such types, so a large response population can have small support.

Here is a precise continuous problem. The input is \(d>9\), known rational \(B,\sigma>0\), an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), and binary rational masses \(\nu_{ij,y}\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), for \(1\le i<j\le d\) and \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\). The positive-mass comparison pairs must form a connected graph. Each mass is the share of responses recording outcome \(y\) for pair \(\{i,j\}\). Output a rational vector \(\hat w\) with \(\mathbf 1^\top\hat w=0\), \(\|\hat w\|_\infty\le B\), whose weighted BTL negative log-likelihood is within \(\varepsilon\) of its minimum:

\[ \sum_{i<j}\sum_{y\in\{-1,+1\}} \nu_{ij,y}\log\!\left(1+\exp\!\left[-y(\hat w_i-\hat w_j)/\sigma\right]\right). \]

The underlying question is how to estimate item qualities from pairwise judgments; the mass-weighted objective is the continuous population version of the MLE computation. I would expect this problem to be tractable to requested accuracy through convex optimization.

The anchor is Theorem 2.C, proved in this paper. For connected pairwise comparisons with \(d>9\), the BTL model has \(y_i\in\{-1,+1\}\) and
\[ \Pr(y_i=1\mid x_i,w^*)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-w^{*\top}x_i/\sigma)}, \]
where each \(x_i\) is a difference vector, \(1^\top w^*=0\), \(\|w^*\|_\infty\le B\), and \(B,\sigma\) are known. Writing \(\hat\Sigma=L/n\) for the standardized comparison-graph Laplacian, if \(n\ge 0.04467\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(\hat\Sigma^\dagger)/B^2\), the minimax risk for estimating \(w^*\) in squared \(\hat\Sigma\)-seminorm satisfies
\[ 0.001\,\frac{d\sigma^2}{n} \le M_n^{\hat\Sigma}(\mathrm{BTL}) \le 1.37\left(e^{B/\sigma}+e^{-B/\sigma}\right)^4\frac{d\sigma^2}{n}. \]
The paper proves the upper bound using the maximum-likelihood estimator, whose objective is convex. The continuous question uses that estimator as its computational core; it does not claim the theorem’s risk bounds automatically extend to arbitrary fractional weights.

The paper’s scenario itself supports this mirror: it studies pairwise judgments collected from crowdsourced workers. A large campaign comparing a fixed catalogue could have many response episodes but comparatively few pair-and-outcome types. The authors would plausibly recognize weighted BTL estimation as the high-multiplicity form of the estimator they analyze in Section 4.

The weakest point is also decisive: Theorem 2.C is a statistical minimax-rate result, not a named polynomial-time or hardness classification. Convexity is stated and used in the paper, but it gives no bit-complexity bound for the weighted continuous input. Under a strict reading of the anchor rule, this is only a borderline algorithmic anchor, not a clean computational-complexity result; the paper has no named hardness result to mirror. Further questions would be the bit complexity of \(\varepsilon\)-approximate weighted BTL estimation, and whether a continuous allocation of mass across comparison pairs can optimize worst-case estimation error.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not establish a computational result. Theorem 2.C bounds the statistical minimax risk for estimating \(w^*\); it does not classify the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The paper already identifies the BTL maximum-likelihood estimator as a convex optimization problem. Recasting its realized data as masses and asking for an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate minimizer is a coherent computational task, but it is a new generic convex-optimization question, not the continuous version of a named complexity result in this paper.

The proposed types, \((\{i,j\},y)\), also describe realized response records rather than respondent characteristics. Their masses preserve the empirical loss, but they do not by themselves preserve the theorem’s statistical uncertainty: normalized frequencies do not say how many responses produced them, and the BTL outcome remains random conditional on the compared pair. Adding the sample count can retain the finite-sample setting, but then the mass formulation is a weighted representation of the same data and the optimization is still just the empirical MLE.

A stronger mirror would make the comparison pair the type and continuously allocate a large workforce’s responses across pairs, retaining BTL randomness conditional on each pair. That could support a worthwhile question about choosing a comparison design to minimize worst-case estimation error. But the continuous object there is the experiment allocation, not the population. To make the population itself continuous, one would need to model worker types such as differing response reliability; this paper assumes a common BTL noise parameter and explicitly leaves richer worker-ability models for future work. Such an extension may be interesting, but it is not a mirror of Theorem 2.C’s model.

So the negative case rests on a mismatch: the paper’s central result is statistical, while the proposed continuous problem either optimizes a deterministic empirical loss or continuizes measurement allocation. Neither supplies the population-continuous computational question ChoCo is seeking. I would not call the case airtight, though: large crowds responding to a fixed catalogue are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a follow-up on continuous comparison design could be valuable. The point is narrower: that plausibility does not rescue the proposed anchor as a continuous computational-social-choice mirror of this paper.
