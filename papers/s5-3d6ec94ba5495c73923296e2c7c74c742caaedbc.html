The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for a *follow-on computational question*, not for a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s named results.

A plausible question is Finite-Type Power-Sharing Equilibrium. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) on ideal points \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_\tau\in[0,1]\), rational party ideal points \(\theta_A<1/2<\theta_B\), and a shared vote-motivation weight \(\lambda\). Each party chooses a platform \(x_i\in[0,1]\). Voters choose the platform they prefer under \(U(x,\theta)=-|x-\theta|\), splitting their vote equally when indifferent; if the platforms coincide, each party receives half the votes. The implemented policy is \(x=\rho_Ax_A+\rho_Bx_B\), and party \(i\)’s payoff is \(\lambda\rho_i+(1-\lambda)U(x,\theta_i)\). The decision problem is whether this game has a pure-strategy equilibrium; its search version asks for a pair satisfying both parties’ best-response inequalities.

A credible regime is a large electorate whose members share one of a modest number of ideal points—for example, categories on a policy-position survey grid—with many voters per category. The mass \(\mu_j\) is the fraction at ideal point \(\theta_j\); \(\tau\) can be small relative to the number of voters. This preserves the paper’s policy competition, vote shares, proportional power sharing, and mixed policy-and-vote motives. The authors would likely recognize it as their game with a high-multiplicity population and an algorithmic question added. A first guess is that the finite ordered support may make the problem tractable by analyzing the payoff changes at voter-indifference thresholds, but that is a conjecture, not a result of this paper.

The paper’s closest named results do not meet the requested computational-anchor test. Theorem 1, proved here, says that under Assumptions 1–6, if the two parties have equal vote-motivation weights \(\lambda_A=\lambda_B=\lambda\in[0,1]\), the proportional power-sharing game has a pure-strategy equilibrium. It establishes existence, not a complexity bound or an algorithm.

Proposition 3, also proved here, gives a particularly explicit equilibrium characterization: with a uniform electorate on \([0,1]\), utility \(U(x,\theta)=-|x-\theta|\), and equal motivation weight \(\lambda\), there is a unique pure-strategy equilibrium. For \(\lambda<1/2\), the platforms are \(x_A^*=\lambda/[2(1-\lambda)]\) and \(x_B^*=1-\lambda/[2(1-\lambda)]\); for \(\lambda\ge 1/2\), both equal \(1/2\). For every \(\lambda\in[0,1)\), each party receives half the votes and the implemented policy is \(1/2\). This is an explicit solution for a special case, but the paper does not state a computational-complexity result. More importantly, it concerns the paper’s already continuous electorate, not the finite-support high-multiplicity problem above. I would not present it as a qualifying anchor.

The scope of this candidate is equilibrium computation for proportional power-sharing elections; it does not cover the paper’s broader equilibrium-existence analysis or its comparative statics. Further questions include whether symmetric motivations guarantee equilibrium with atomic finite support, the complexity in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, and what changes under asymmetric motivations.

The weak point is fundamental: Saporiti’s model already takes the electorate to be a continuum with distribution \(F\). So it supports the plausibility of studying this game with continuous population masses, but it does not provide a discrete-to-continuous result or a named computational result for a new mirror. Under the prompt’s anchor requirement, this paper supplies no eligible named computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves pure-strategy equilibrium exists under symmetric motivations; Proposition 3 characterizes equilibrium in a uniform, Euclidean-preference example. Neither asks for an algorithm or classifies complexity, and the paper gives no finite input representation for its distribution \(F\) or utility \(U\). The other named results are also analytic game-theory claims. Their being numbered theorems does not make them computational anchors.

The proposed Finite-Type Power-Sharing Equilibrium is the best follow-on candidate, but it starts from a different place: the paper already models the electorate as a continuum. Replacing its smooth distribution with finitely many ideal points gives an atomic distribution. A positive mass can then be indifferent between distinct platforms, while the paper’s assumptions and equilibrium arguments rely on a smooth distribution and a unique indifferent voter. That extension may be sensible, but it is not a high-multiplicity mirror of a discrete computational problem or result in this paper.

One could instead keep a smooth distribution and specify a computable family of densities and utilities. That would make an equilibrium-computation question possible, but the encoding and restrictions would be newly chosen; they would determine the computational problem. The paper supplies neither such a specification nor an algorithmic task to inherit. So this is a proposal for a new computational study of an already continuous economic model, not a continuous mirror of the paper’s contributions.

The negative case is strongest on that distinction. It cannot rule out a worthwhile finite-support or encoded-density follow-on as a separate project; it does show why neither Theorem 1 nor Proposition 3 provides the requested anchor.
