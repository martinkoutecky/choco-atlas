What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors are Proposition 2’s allocation characterization and Proposition 4’s decentralization thresholds; neither yields a computational mirror. The paper’s other propositions likewise characterize welfare allocations, transfers, or incentives in its already-continuous model.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is that this paper already models society as a continuum. Its adult population has mass \(1\), divided between two preference types; fertility probabilities induce ex post type masses \(x\pi\), \(x(1-\pi)\), \((1-x)\varepsilon\), and \((1-x)(1-\varepsilon)\). The policy variables are allocations and transfers, and the objective is to maximize the minimum equivalent consumption subject to resource constraints. That is a concrete continuous-population model, not merely a paper with fractional outcomes.

But it is not a continuous *mirror*: it is the paper’s existing model. And none of its numbered results is an admissible computational anchor. For example, Proposition 2 characterizes the ex post egalitarian optimum and how consumption rankings vary with the reference fertility and the balance of adoption demand and supply. Proposition 4 gives wage thresholds for decentralizing that optimum under quasi-linear preferences. These are economic characterization results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified.

So the honest positive case is limited: the paper could motivate computational questions about finding fair allocations or decentralizing transfers, but those would be new questions about an already continuous model. There is no named computational result here to anchor the required mirror, and I can’t give one problem per anchor without misrepresenting the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchors themselves: Proposition 2 characterizes a maximin allocation, and Proposition 4 gives wage thresholds for decentralizing it. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm. The paper assumes a continuum population from the outset, so these are results about an existing continuous planner model—not discrete computational results awaiting a continuous mirror.

The best algorithmic version of Proposition 2 would ask for an optimal allocation given the model’s parameters and utility functions. But the paper has only two preference categories and four realized groups. Its general utility functions are not given a finite input representation; in the quasi-linear cases, the planner’s problem has fixed dimension and the paper derives the allocation conditions directly. Expanding to a support of \(\tau\) types with different preferences, wages, fertility risks, and costs could define a worthwhile optimization problem. It would also require specifying how utilities are encoded and how the discrete and continuous problems relate. That is a new multi-type welfare-optimization model, not a mirror of Proposition 2.

For Proposition 4, the strongest computational question is to calculate or check the stated wage thresholds. Those thresholds are already explicit functions of the parameters. A richer problem involving many types and incentive-compatible transfers might be interesting, but it would add a type space and constraints absent from the proposition; it would not supply the missing computational result.

So the negative case is strong for the anchors actually raised: there is no computational result here to mirror. The paper’s continuous population is not itself a novelty collision, since it does not answer a computational question. The weak point is the universal claim: a carefully specified, richer multi-type version could be a worthwhile computational project. I cannot honestly rule out that extension; I can only say it would need to be developed as a new problem rather than credited as a mirror of this paper’s results.
