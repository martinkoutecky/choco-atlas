The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about one thing: this paper has no numbered theorem about computational complexity, so there is no theorem-to-theorem mirror. But that does not establish that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror. The paper’s own findings raise computational questions about how IRV winners change when ballot mass is moved or divided, and the authors explicitly discuss both those changes and partitions of the electorate.

For the downward monotonicity paradox, take rankings as voter types and their population shares as masses. Given a society \(\mu\), ask for the least mass of ballots that must be changed by lowering a losing candidate to make that candidate win. The Ward 2 example supplies a concrete witness: changing 80 ballots from Arab–Gordon–Worlobah to Gordon–Arab–Worlobah makes Arab win. Computing the minimum share needed, across societies and candidates, is a continuous winner-robustness problem. It directly quantifies the kind of paradox the paper documents; it is not a question about fractional outcomes or noise. Large elections with many voters sharing ballot types are a natural high-multiplicity setting, and the answer in proportions is meaningful without individual voter identities.

The consistency paradox gives an even more direct population-mass question. Given \(\mu\), can it be split into two sub-societies whose IRV winner is \(c\), even though \(c\) loses in the combined society? Formally, ask whether there are \(0<\lambda<1\) and societies \(\mu_1,\mu_2\) such that
\[ \mu=\lambda\mu_1+(1-\lambda)\mu_2,\qquad \operatorname{IRV}(\mu_1)=\operatorname{IRV}(\mu_2)=c,\qquad \operatorname{IRV}(\mu)\ne c. \]
The paper provides exactly such a split and says that many others can be found. It also says the frequency of this paradox in American elections is unknown. Finding such decompositions—or minimizing how much the electorate must be repartitioned to obtain one—turns that explicit observation into a computational question about a continuous society. Ballot mass can be divided without preserving precinct or voter identities, as the paper’s discussion of alternative partitions invites.

Neither question is guaranteed to be easy, and the paper does not formulate either one as an algorithmic problem. That makes it a less direct anchor than a paper with a stated complexity theorem. But IRV’s elimination rounds make winner regions depend on comparisons of linear functions of ballot masses, so searching over mass changes or decompositions has real computational structure. The evidence here supports a narrower criticism—this paper offers no ready-made complexity result to mirror—not the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
