The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(d\in[0,1]\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t=(x,p)\), where \(x\in[0,1]\), \(p\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\), and the masses sum to \(1\), construct a randomized direct mechanism over feasible placements \(Y_d=\{(y_1,y_2)\in[0,1]^2:|y_1-y_2|\ge d\}\). Agents report locations while preferences remain public; for a finite realization with population size \(n\), a report changes mass in increments of \(1/n\). Is there a group-strategyproof mechanism whose worst-case ratio of optimal to achieved per-capita expected social utility is at most \(2\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a rational finite-support measure \(\mu\) on location-preference types \(t=(x,p)\); a type contributes mass \(\mu_t\) and utility \(p_1|x-y_1|+p_2|x-y_2|\). For feasible placements \(Y_d\), the mechanism returns the equal lottery on \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\), independently of the reported measure. If \(q\) is the mass with preference \((1,1)\), this gives expected per-capita welfare \((1+q)/2\); the paper’s bound becomes \(\mathrm{OPT}\le 1+(1-d)q\), yielding ratio at most \(2\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the randomized general minimum-distance upper bound in Theorem 7; it leaves the other upper and lower bounds, including Theorem 8, unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the best worst-case approximation ratio for randomized group-strategyproof mechanisms on these supported population measures equal to \(2\)?
  • Does the lower bound in Theorem 8 persist in the high-multiplicity regime?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high multiplicity population version of the randomized mechanism design problem, anchored on Theorem 7. The theorem is proved in this paper. It says that, for every minimum separation \(d\in[0,1]\), Mechanism 4 returns \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\) with probability \(1/2\) each; this mechanism is group strategyproof and has approximation ratio at most \(2\) for total social utility. Its guarantee follows from the paper’s upper bound \(\mathrm{OPT}\le n+(1-d)|N_1\cap N_2|\).

A plausible regime is siting two harmful facilities along a populated corridor. Many residents may share a neighborhood location and the same public information about which facilities affect them. A type is \(t=(x,p)\), where \(x\in[0,1]\) is the location and \(p\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\) records the affected facilities. The type is complete for this model: residents in a type have the same location and preferences. There may be \(n\) residents but only \(\tau\) distinct types, with \(\tau\ll n\).

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. Call it High Multiplicity Two Obnoxious Facilities. An instance has rational \(d\in[0,1]\), a population size \(n\), and a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\), where \(n\mu_t\) is integral. A resident has mass \(1/n\); changing their reported location changes the empirical measure by \(\frac1n(\delta_{(x',p)}-\delta_{(x,p)})\), with \(p\) public. A randomized direct mechanism maps reported measures to lotteries over
\[ Y_d=\{(y_1,y_2)\in[0,1]^2: |y_1-y_2|\ge d\}. \]
For true measure \(\mu\), its per capita expected welfare is
\[ \mathbb E_{y\sim F(\mu)} \left[\sum_{t=(x,p)\in T}\mu_t\bigl(p_1|x-y_1|+p_2|x-y_2|\bigr)\right]. \]
The problem is to construct a group strategyproof mechanism and bound its worst case ratio to the optimal feasible placement. This asks for the paper’s underlying mechanism, not a continuous encoding.

The mirror of Theorem 7 has the same explicit solution: ignore the reported measure and use the equal lottery on \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\). It is group strategyproof because no report or coalition can change the outcome lottery. If \(q\) is the mass with preference \((1,1)\), its expected welfare is \((1+q)/2\): each resident affected by one facility gets expected utility \(1/2\), and each resident affected by both gets utility \(1\). The paper’s bound becomes \(\mathrm{OPT}\le 1+(1-d)q\), so the ratio is at most
\[ \frac{2(1+(1-d)q)}{1+q}\le 2. \]
Thus this anchor goes in the tractable direction: it yields a constant size mechanism and the same guarantee for normalized population mass. Facility locations are already continuous in the paper; the mirror’s continuization is the population representation.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this covers the general minimum distance setting and the randomized group strategyproof upper bound in Theorem 7. I would not add Theorem 8’s randomized lower bound as an anchor. Its proof uses a two agent instance, and that lower bound does not automatically survive when each individual has only \(1/n\) of a high multiplicity population. The paper has named algorithmic mechanism results, but no named running time, NP hardness, or parameterized complexity result; the mirror therefore supports the programme’s computational angle only weakly. Further questions include whether the best possible ratio changes in this population model, and whether deterministic guarantees or other welfare objectives behave differently.

The weak point is that the strongest anchor’s mechanism ignores the population entirely. Its extension to mass distributions is clean and faithful, but it does not reveal a new complexity landscape. More generally, an atomless limit can make individual deviations negligible; specifying the finite population realization above keeps the strategyproofness claim meaningful. That makes this a defensible, modest mirror rather than a strong example of continuization unlocking new computation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is a scope mismatch: Theorem 7 proves that a strategyproof mechanism has a certain welfare approximation ratio. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem or give an algorithmic result whose running time, parameter dependence, or tractability changes with the population representation. An approximation ratio for a mechanism is not, by itself, a complexity result.

The proposed mass model is coherent. With types \(t=(x,p)\) and masses \(\mu_t\), utility becomes an integral (or finite weighted sum); a finite population with \(n\mu_t\) integral preserves individual deviations as changes of size \(1/n\). Theorem 7’s guarantee carries over. So I would not reject the mirror because high multiplicity is implausible, or because the theorem’s answer stays the same. If one instead takes an atomless model where an individual has zero mass, strategyproofness can become vacuous—but the finite-population formulation avoids that problem.

A stronger proposed mirror might ask for the best truthful welfare guarantee across all such mass distributions, or for the cost of computing an optimal strategyproof mechanism from a supported type set. The first remains a mechanism-existence and approximation question; the second adds a mechanism-synthesis problem that this paper does not formulate or analyze. Theorem 7’s own mechanism is independent of the reported society, and the paper gives no complexity question to carry over. Those could be worthwhile questions in mechanism design, but they do not fill ChoCo’s stated gap in computational complexity over continuous societies.

This is a deliberately narrow negative case: it does not show that a continuous population model is unsensible for this facility-location setting. If the programme includes mechanism-design approximation as a target in its own right, this objection weakens substantially.
