What the mirror covers

Theorems 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, and 5.1 characterize the core or families of solutions by axioms; none supplies an eligible computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that cooperative games have a natural high-multiplicity regime. Imagine a large workforce with a fixed set \(T\) of skill and contract types. A coalition is described by its mass vector \(q\), its worth by \(V(q)\), and an allocation by per-capita payoffs \(x_t\). A continuous core would require
\[ \sum_{t\in T} q_t x_t \ge V(q) \]
for every feasible coalition \(q\), with equality for the whole population. This is a plausible population mirror of the paper’s core: the number of workers can be enormous while the number of types stays small. Reduced games could likewise be defined after removing specified masses of players.

But this paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its numbered results are axiomatic: for example, Theorem 4.1, proved here, says that on convex games the core is the unique inclusion-wise maximal solution satisfying individual rationality and weak Hart-Mas-Colell consistency. Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 give axiomatic characterizations of the core, and Theorem 5.1 characterizes a family of solutions. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to a parameterized complexity class. So there is no eligible anchor, and I cannot honestly state a continuous problem *per anchor*.

One could propose computing or optimizing over the continuous core for such a typed workforce model. That would be a concrete computational question, but it would be a new extension, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The soft spot is therefore decisive for this task: the population interpretation is plausible, but the paper’s contribution is axiomatic, while ChoCo’s scope asks for computational questions. I would not present the proposed extension as an anchored positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.1 characterizes the core as an inclusion-wise maximal solution; Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 give axiomatic characterizations; Theorem 5.1 classifies solutions satisfying a package of axioms. None defines an encoded computational problem or makes a complexity claim. There are no anchors in the case for, and I don’t see an eligible one elsewhere in the paper.

The workforce example is the best candidate for a new question: with type masses \(p\), coalition masses \(q\), worth function \(V(q)\), and per-type payoffs \(x\), one could ask whether \(x\) is in the continuous core, defined by \(p\cdot x=V(p)\) and \(q\cdot x\ge V(q)\) for every feasible \(q\). One could also study the corresponding reduced games. That is a coherent population model, but it is an added computational agenda, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

For that agenda to have a complexity landscape, \(V\) needs a finite representation or an oracle with specified costs. An arbitrary game on a continuum of coalition masses does not provide one. Choosing, say, a piecewise-linear representation could make core separation a concrete computational question, but the chosen representation would supply structure absent from the paper. And a finite set of types is itself a substantive restriction: anonymity in the paper means invariance under relabeling players; it does not mean that players can be merged whenever they share a few attributes. In a general cooperative game, worth can depend on which players form a coalition.

So I would argue against presenting this paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror. The obstacle is the absence of a computational target, not that a continuous model would be trivial or that its answer is already known. But I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: a carefully specified computational study of a typed continuous core could be worthwhile as a separate extension. The negative case is strong on fit to this paper and weak on ruling out that broader possibility.
