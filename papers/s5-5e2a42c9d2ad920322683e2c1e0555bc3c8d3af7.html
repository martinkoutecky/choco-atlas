The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant anchored case from this paper: none of its named formal results asserts a complexity classification or a running-time guarantee for a computational problem. The closest candidates are the Proposition in §5.2 establishing pure-strategy equilibrium existence and a complete lattice, and the Theorem in §6.1 comparing family-driven and caseworker-driven equilibrium outcomes. Those are analytic results. Section 8 iterates a fixed-point map to compute approximate equilibria, but gives no complexity bound. The supplied text also omits result numbers, so I can’t quote numbers as printed without inventing them.

There is a plausible *unanchored* mirror: in a large agency or statewide placement pool, let child and family types be complete observable profiles, including their valuations, costs, and discount factors. Let \(\mu_C\) and \(\mu_F\) give their population shares; strategies specify which counterpart types each type is interested in, and matching consumes mass while replacement keeps the population stationary. One could ask whether pure-strategy equilibria exist, whether they form the same lattice, and whether the paper’s Pareto comparison between search methods survives. The paper’s model makes this extension recognizable to its authors, but its high-multiplicity regime is uncertain: complete preference profiles may leave nearly every agent as a distinct type. Since this candidate has no qualifying named computational anchor, it should not be presented as satisfying the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that there is no qualifying anchor here. The existence-and-lattice proposition in §5.2 uses Tarski’s fixed-point theorem; the §6.1 theorem is a Pareto comparison; the parameter propositions are comparative statics. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Section 8 iterates a fixed-point map to produce numerical results, but states no running-time guarantee or complexity result. The empirical hazard estimates are also not computational results of the kind ChoCo studies. Under the programme’s stated test, that is decisive: there is no computational result in this paper to mirror.

The strongest repair would be to give complete child and family preference profiles positive population masses, then ask for the complexity of computing extremal equilibria or testing Pareto dominance under the two search methods. But this is a new computational problem, not a continuous version of a result the paper proves. The paper’s game has one player per type, draws one child type uniformly, and makes each family type present with probability \(\lambda\). Replacing those players with masses requires specifying how arrivals and encounters scale, and whether a deviation is made by an individual or by an entire type group. Those choices determine the game and its equilibrium concept; the paper supplies no canonical mass model or computational question that resolves them.

There is a further obstacle to making that repair faithful. A type here includes a full valuation profile, costs, and discount factors. With idiosyncratic preferences, exact profiles may have little or no repeated mass. Grouping agents with different profiles would change the strategic problem. A setting with many agents sharing exact profiles is possible, but the paper does not establish that this is a natural regime for its adoption model. Even there, asking for equilibrium computation would launch a new algorithmic agenda rather than continue a computational contribution already present in the paper.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as submitted: none of the proposed results supplies a ChoCo-style computational anchor. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. A plausible high-multiplicity search game could be formulated, but it would be an extension of this analytical paper, not a continuization of one of its computational results.
