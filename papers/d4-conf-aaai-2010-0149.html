The continuous mirror question

Given population scale \(N\), a finite report domain of single-minded bidder types \(t=(x_t,v_t)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), a kernel and penalty parameter \(\lambda\), and per-capita item supplies scaled to \(N\), determine the maximum unilateral incentive regret of the paper’s one-run kernel auction with price-based payments. A report from type \(t\) to \(t'\) changes the distribution by \(\mu' = \mu + \frac{1}{N}(e_{t'}-e_t)\); decide whether the maximum regret is at most \(\eta\), and return a maximizing report or a certificate.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity combinatorial auction with support types \(t=(x_t,v_t)\), masses \(\mu_t\), and explicit population scale \(N\). Each bidder receives an integral bundle, while type-level allocations are aggregate counts bounded by \(N\mu_t\); feasible selections respect supplies scaled with population. The kernel auction uses the paper’s welfare-minus-penalty objective over type counts, then computes prices and type-specific payments \(r\).

The objection that survived

With fixed supply, at most \(m\) bidders receive bundles, so their population share vanishes as \(N\) grows; the proposed model needs per-capita supply scaling to retain a meaningful aggregate allocation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its supporting Propositions 1–3 on stability, approximate universal competitiveness, and payment error. It leaves the complexity of solving the allocation program and the paper’s broader auction-design discussion without a complexity classification.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the maximum regret or its certificate be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), input bit length, and the relevant auction parameters?
  • Can the paper’s regret bound be sharpened to depend on \(\tau\), or remain useful as \(N\) grows under per-capita supply scaling?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Given a kernel price structure for which the auction’s optimization returns an efficient allocation, and a penalty parameter \(\lambda\), it says that charging the paper’s price-based payments \(r\) makes the auction \(\epsilon\)-incentive compatible, with \(\epsilon=2(n-1)\kappa^2/\lambda\), where \(\|\phi(x)\|\le\kappa\). Each \(r_h\) is the price revenue from a revenue-maximizing allocation among the other bidders, minus the price revenue those bidders receive in the chosen allocation. The theorem is a named approximation guarantee, not a result that the problem is in P or has a particular running time. The paper sketches delayed column generation, but gives no complexity classification.

A plausible mirror is a high-multiplicity kernel auction with a one-agent incentive certificate. Let a type \(t=(x_t,v_t)\) specify a bidder’s desired bundle and value, and let \(\mu_t\) be its fraction of the population. Take a large population of \(N\) bidders with \(N\gg\tau\): for example, a licensing or procurement market where many bidders fall into a small number of standard bundle and valuation classes. A single bidder then has mass \(1/N\). The instance also specifies the kernel, \(\lambda\), and a regret threshold \(\eta\).

Aggregate the paper’s auction over types. Its allocation variables \(\alpha_t\) represent the allocated amount of each type, bounded by \(N\mu_t\); its columns represent feasible selections of bidders, summarized by their type counts. The objective is the paper’s welfare term minus the same kernel penalty, now written using type totals:
\[ \sum_t \alpha_t v_t- \frac{1}{2\lambda} \left\| \sum_t\left(\alpha_t-\sum_z z_t\bar\alpha_z\right)\phi(x_t) \right\|^2. \]
Here \(z\) ranges over feasible type-count selections and \(\bar\alpha_z\) weights those selections. A solution gives the aggregate allocation, the kernel price vector, and a common payment for each type, calculated by the paper’s price-based rule after removing one bidder of that type. The question is whether this one-run mechanism has worst-case regret at most \(\eta\) over every one-bidder report change in the given type domain; a solution includes the allocation, prices, payments, and either a certificate or a violating report. This is a direct type-aggregated version of the problem in Theorem 1, rather than a continuous-outcome reformulation.

I would expect exact computation to retain hardness as the number of items varies: its feasible-selection columns still encode combinatorial winner determination. That is an inference from the formulation, not a complexity result in this paper. With a fixed item set or a restricted bundle menu, type aggregation might make the computation practical in \(\tau\), but Theorem 1 does not establish that. Further questions include whether the pricing step can be done in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and whether one can prove an incentive bound that depends on support size rather than total population.

This mirror covers Theorem 1 and its stability-to-payment argument, including Propositions 1–3 as supporting steps. It does not mirror a complexity result for allocation: the paper states none. Nor does aggregation alone guarantee the advertised computational saving: computing \(r\) may still require revenue maximization for every bidder.

The weakest point is that the result protects an individual bidder’s incentive, while a truly atomless population has no positive-mass individual to remove. Keeping an atom of size \(1/N\) preserves the paper’s question, but makes this a high-multiplicity, finite-agent mirror rather than a strict atomless limit. The bound also grows with \(N\), so the theorem does not promise a useful population-uniform guarantee as the market grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only plausible anchor, but it does not establish the kind of computational result ChoCo is set up to mirror. It bounds the incentive regret of an auction solution, assuming the optimization has already been solved. The paper gives no complexity classification or algorithmic guarantee for solving that optimization; delayed column generation is suggested as a possible method. A continuous question about computing the auction would therefore be a new question, rather than a mirror of Theorem 1.

The best attempt at a mirror is to aggregate bidders by type \(t=(x_t,v_t)\), with mass \(\mu_t\), and ask whether the resulting price-based payments remain approximately truthful. But Theorem 1 compares the economy with bidder \(h\) to the economy without \(h\). The distribution \(\mu\) does not specify that counterfactual: it does not even specify whether a given mass represents ten bidders or ten million. Include a population size \(N\), and removal changes the finite type counts by one; the mirror has retained an individual atom and is a high-multiplicity finite auction. Take the atomless limit, and the report change leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. The theorem’s individual marginal-contribution question is then absent from the model.

That loss is especially sharp with the paper’s fixed supply of indivisible items. Every nonempty bundle consumes at least one item, so at most \(m\) bidders can receive bundles. In a population of \(N\), the served fraction is at most \(m/N\); in the atomless limit, the allocation to selected individuals has zero population mass. Aggregating the types may still describe who is available to win, but it cannot represent the individual allocation and removal that the theorem evaluates. Keeping individual removals restores that information only by retaining the finite population scale.

The strongest repair is to scale item supplies with the population, so a positive mass of each type can receive bundles. That is a sensible co-modification and could support a computational study of aggregate allocation or price sensitivity. But an individual report then does not change the population distribution; replacing the individual with a positive mass perturbation asks about cohort manipulation, a different strategic property. Alternatively, retaining a tagged bidder in a continuous background makes a hybrid model whose key incentive question still depends on that finite bidder.

So the proposed anchor faces a three-way choice: keep the individual and the finite population scale, take an atomless limit in which the individual counterfactual disappears, or change the strategic question to a mass deviation. That is a strong case against this as a worthwhile continuous mirror of the paper’s named result. It does not prove that no adjacent continuous market problem could be worth studying, but such a problem would no longer mirror Theorem 1.
