The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see a qualifying anchor for a positive case. The paper presents §3.1’s named algorithm, TPSMA, and calls it “quicker and efficient,” but it gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity result. It provides no running-time bound or correctness result for the algorithm.

One could define a new high-multiplicity problem: group people into preference types, let mass represent the population share of each type, and choose a perfect fractional matching into triples to maximize aggregate satisfaction. But that would be a fresh formulation, not a continuous mirror of a named result in this paper. The paper also does not clearly formalize its global optimization objective or show that TPSMA finds an optimum. I would not offer that formulation as an anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor, so there is no continuous question from their case that survives to defeat. The paper’s named item, TPSMA, is an algorithm, but the paper gives no theorem establishing its correctness or running time. Its final claim that it is “quicker and efficient” has no bound behind it. That leaves no stated computational result for a continuous mirror to explain or extend.

The strongest salvage is to define one: let types be preference rankings over roommate categories, score each triple by the members’ average satisfaction, and maximize total satisfaction over a perfect fractional partition. With \(\tau\) types, a variable for each triple of types gives an LP with \(O(\tau^3)\) variables and mass-balance constraints. This could describe a large housing pool with repeated categories of residents. But it is a new model, not a result of this paper. The paper ranks people against named individuals; replacing those rankings with category preferences discards information. Keeping the original rankings instead makes types encode identity-specific orders over \(3n-1\) people, so the support need not stay small as the population grows.

Nor does the LP establish TPSMA’s claim. The paper’s procedure uses assignments to generate candidate pairs and triples, then says to choose mutually exclusive, exhaustive triples with maximum satisfaction. It does not show that its generated candidates include an optimal partition, or prove that the procedure finds one. A correct aggregate LP would answer a well-defined high-multiplicity roommate question, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a result the paper established.

That is a strong negative case for this paper under the requested anchor standard. It does not justify the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous roommate model exists: the category-based housing problem is plausible, and might merit study on its own. The honest weakness is that ruling it out as a worthwhile *new* question would require a judgment about novelty and significance that this paper alone cannot settle.
