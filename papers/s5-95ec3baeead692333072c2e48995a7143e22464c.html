None classifies the complexity of a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest results are Proposition 6, which characterizes the \(2+1\) rule’s equilibrium in the symmetric binomial model, and Proposition 8, which gives a mixed equilibrium in a large asymmetric Poisson game. They are analytical equilibrium results, not complexity results. There is also a boundary wrinkle in Proposition 6: its statement says the strict equilibrium exists iff \(V\le3\), while its proof says the equilibrium is strict for \(V<3\) and weak at \(V=3\).

A natural population regime would have many voters sharing just two types, \(L\) and \(R\), so their masses are \(\mu_L\) and \(\mu_R\). But turning that into a continuous equilibrium-computation problem would be a new question, not one of this paper’s named results. In a deterministic continuum, an individual voter has zero mass and cannot affect the outcome; preserving the paper’s pivotal incentives requires stochastic population fluctuations, which the paper already studies analytically. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The attached “case for” raises no positive anchor. Its central point supports the negative verdict: Propositions 1–9 and Lemmas 1–2 characterize equilibria, pivotal events, and welfare; none states the complexity of a computational problem. There is no named computational result here to mirror.

The closest candidate would be: given voter-type masses and the paper’s parameters, compute an equilibrium for the \(2+1\) rule. But in the paper’s model there are only two voter types and four candidates, and Propositions 6 and 8 already characterize the relevant equilibria. Turning those characterizations into an algorithm would restate the paper’s analysis, not open a complexity question. Enlarging the candidate or type space enough to make equilibrium computation a new algorithmic challenge would be a new model and result, not a mirror of one in this paper.

The population continuum also sits awkwardly with the paper’s central mechanism. Its strategic incentives depend on discrete pivotal events: one voter’s ballot can change who wins a seat. With deterministic masses, each voter has measure zero and cannot be pivotal. Retaining pivotality means keeping finite stochastic populations and their rare-event comparisons, which the paper already studies through binomial and large Poisson games. A continuum game could be posed, but it would either lose those individual incentives or change the strategic unit to a bloc or coalition.

So the strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is not that the paper’s large-population setting is implausible, nor that its analytical limit work counts as computational prior art. It is that the paper’s contributions are analytical equilibrium results, with no computational question for a continuous-population complexity programme to take up. This supports a strong no-mirror verdict for this paper; it cannot rule out a worthwhile algorithmic voting model built from different results or assumptions.
