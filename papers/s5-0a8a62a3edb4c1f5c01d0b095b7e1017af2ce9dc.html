The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive evidence is that a plausible high-multiplicity regime is right in the paper: a large school or housing allocation population with finitely many payoff-relevant types, where agents of the same type share the same utility and signal distribution. The type shares \(q(\theta)\) are population masses, and the per-capita supplies of high- and low-quality objects are part of the model. That is a sensible continuous society. But it is already the paper’s model, so it cannot itself be presented as a new continuization.

The closest result to an anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that an optimal allocation rule can be chosen to be ordered: each type gets a lower and an upper signal threshold; the upper threshold weakly decreases with type, the lower threshold weakly increases, and each type is indifferent between truthful reporting and reporting the next higher type. It also says every optimum agrees almost everywhere with such an ordered optimum for all but the lowest type, and gives the lowest type the same probabilities of receiving each object. This is a substantial structural characterization, but it does not classify a computational problem or give an algorithmic complexity bound.

Proposition 4, also proved there, is another near-anchor: for every \(\delta>0\), the authors construct feasible, incentive-compatible finite-\(N\) allocation rules, with supplies \(\lfloor N\alpha_H\rfloor\) and \(\lfloor N\alpha_L\rfloor\), whose welfare approaches within \(\delta\) of the continuum optimum as \(N\) grows. It is an asymptotic bridge between finite and continuum populations, not a complexity result.

So the paper has named structural and approximation results, but no named result saying an underlying problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. I can’t honestly supply a continuous problem per qualifying anchor: there is no such anchor here. A computational study of finding an optimal threshold vector from finitely encoded type, utility, supply, and signal data could be a worthwhile follow-up, but it would be a new question about a population the paper already makes continuous—not an anchored continuous mirror of a discrete result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper already makes the population continuous. Its society is a mass distribution over finitely many payoff-relevant types, with per-capita supplies of objects. That is exactly the population move ChoCo studies. The paper’s remaining agent-level uncertainty is about signals, not about a discrete population waiting to be continuized.

Theorem 1 is a substantial structural result, but it characterizes optimal mechanisms in that continuum model; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 4 is likewise a convergence result: finite-agent allocations approach the continuum optimum. It gives no runtime bound or complexity classification for computing those allocations. Neither supplies the discrete computational result a continuous mirror would need to reflect.

The best stronger candidate is to ask how hard it is to compute an optimal mechanism from finitely encoded type, utility, supply, and signal data. That could be worthwhile research. But the paper allows general continuous signal densities, so a complexity question would first need a finite representation or a restricted parametric family. It would then be a new computational study of an already continuous population model, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

So no anchor raised by the proponent survives as a continuous mirror. The negative case is strong on that point, but it cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile ChoCo work could grow from this paper: a suitably formalized computational study might be valuable. What the paper does not support is calling that study a continuous mirror.
