Why no mirror

Theorem 1 and Theorem 1a give leximin characterizations, not a computational guarantee or complexity result. The displayed LP stages provide a way to execute ESR, but the paper's named results do not assert a computational result under the stated gate.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror extends Theorems 1 and 1a's cutoff-share characterization to a distribution over preference types; it leaves the tower procedure and the assignment-domain identity in the Corollary outside its computational coverage.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this weighted-quantile leximin problem be solved in time polynomial in the number of alternatives, support size, and input bit length?
  • Does the maximizing lottery set depend only on the positive support, or can positive type masses change it?

The case FOR (proponent)

The literal anchor requirement exposes a real limitation: this paper has no named theorem asserting a complexity result such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that, for every fixed preference profile \(R\), ESR returns exactly the lotteries that leximin-maximize the vector \(t=(t_i(k))_{i,k}\), where \(t_i(k)\) is the lottery’s probability on agent \(i\)’s top \(k\) indifference classes. The paper also gives a staged algorithm using linear programs, but Theorem 1 is a characterization, not a stated complexity classification. So this can only be a conditional positive case, if an algorithmically used characterization counts as an anchor.

The lead mirror would be Mass-Leximin Random Social Choice. An instance has a finite alternative set \(A\), an explicitly listed support \(T\) of complete weak preference orders, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. A type is one such preference order; its mass is the fraction of society with that order. The decision variable is a common lottery \(p\in\Delta(A)\), as in the paper. For type \(t\), write its indifference classes as \(E_t^1,\ldots,E_t^{K_t}\), and define
\[ v_{t,k}(p)=p\!\left(\bigcup_{r\le k}E_t^r\right). \]
Form the weighted distribution of cutoff shares
\[ \nu_p=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{k=1}^{K_t}\delta_{v_{t,k}(p)}. \]
The problem is to return an exact lottery \(p\) whose increasing quantile profile is lexicographically maximal. To compare two lotteries, merge the finitely many breakpoints of their quantile profiles and compare the profiles interval by interval from the lowest quantile upward; at the first interval where they differ, the lottery with the higher share is preferred. Any maximizing lottery is a solution.

This is a faithful high-multiplicity version of Theorem 1: if \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), then multiplying \(\nu_p\) by \(N\) gives precisely the paper’s finite vector of cutoff shares, with the coordinates for type \(t\) repeated \(n_t\) times. Thus the mirror replaces an explicit list of \(N\) agents with binary-encoded type masses, while keeping the paper’s preference information, shared lottery, and egalitarian objective. A plausible regime is a large public vote over a modest number of alternatives, with millions of voters but relatively few distinct weak rankings. The paper itself allows the lottery to represent either a random choice or time-shares; the continuous object here is the population.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The paper’s ESR procedure uses LPs over lottery probabilities and constraints for indifference classes. Repeated agents of the same type give repeated constraints, so the procedure appears compressible to the \(\tau\) listed types and their classes, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). That is an extrapolation from the described algorithm, not a complexity theorem proved in the paper. The resulting questions include proving that bit-complexity bound and determining whether the outcome depends on the positive masses or only on which types appear.

The main weakness is twofold: there is no qualifying named complexity anchor, and the algorithm’s duplicate constraints suggest that changing positive type masses may leave the outcome unchanged. The proposed problem is still a plausible continuous form of the paper’s leximin characterization, but it may yield a high-multiplicity compression result rather than a new mass-sensitive social choice rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes ESR: it says which lotteries leximin-maximize the cutoff shares. It gives no complexity classification for a computational problem. The staged procedure uses linear programs, but the paper’s contribution is the characterization, not a result about worst-case complexity or algorithms. That makes Theorem 1 a weak anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme.

The proposed mass-quantile mirror is nevertheless coherent. A large public vote can have a finite set of weak-ranking types with rational population shares, and the society can choose a common lottery over alternatives. Writing \(\phi_{t,k}(p)=p(U_{t,k})\) for each type’s top-\(k\) share gives a faithful population version of the theorem. I would not dismiss it because voters are interchangeable or because lotteries are already fractional.

The deeper problem is that population shares do not affect this mirror’s optimization. There are at most \(m\tau\) cutoff coordinates, each a linear function of \(p\), and the feasible set remains the simplex \(\Delta(A)\). The masses \(\mu_t>0\) only weight those coordinates in the quantile profile. In this convex setting, progressive leximin fixes the coordinates that must remain at each level; any other coordinates that can rise can be raised together by averaging feasible lotteries. Positive weights change the widths of quantile intervals, but not which coordinates can be raised. Thus the maximizing cutoff shares depend on which types are present, not on their positive masses. A type’s mass matters discontinuously at zero: for example, adding any positive mass of voters with the reverse of a strict ranking can force a compromise lottery, however small that mass is.

That leaves a clean optimization over at most \(m\tau\) linear shares, expressible through a polynomial-size sequence of LPs. It may be a reasonable follow-up, but it looks like a routine algorithmic consequence of the characterization and the LP procedure already described. The continuous input has compressed duplicate types; it has not created a new population-scale complexity question.

The best version that makes masses affect feasibility is the random-assignment subdomain, with object supplies scaling with population. That co-modification is legitimate under the programme’s scope, and the paper explicitly connects ESR there to Serial rule. But the resulting type-level problem is leximin optimization over a fractional assignment polytope, with a polynomial number of type-object variables and cutoff constraints; the paper already supplies the Serial-rule interpretation. This could merit a tractability note, but it still does not provide a missing complexity result from this paper.

So I would argue against prioritizing this paper as a ChoCo mirror: the stated anchor is a characterization, the strongest public-vote mirror loses the population proportions, and the assignment version appears to reduce to a small LP formulation. I cannot make the universal negative airtight. A study of these continuous fairness models could be worthwhile in its own right, but that would be a new optimization project rather than a computational mirror of a result in this paper.
