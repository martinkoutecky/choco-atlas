What the mirror covers

The proposed finite-role question covers only Theorem 4.1's outcome classification for connected, aperiodic, symmetric soft-opposition networks; it leaves Theorem 4.2's influence results and the other persistence and multipolarization results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under the anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational result: none of its named results asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

The nearest foothold is Theorem 4.1, proved here, but it is an analytic classification, not a complexity result. It says that for a connected, aperiodic, symmetric network with no self-loops and soft opposition \(\mathfrak{D}(x)=-x\), the dynamics converges to a polarization that is not always zero exactly when the network is opposition bipartite; fails to converge for some initial opinions exactly when it is reverse opposition bipartite; and converges to neutral consensus exactly when it is neither. I can’t honestly present it as the required computational anchor.

The strongest positive follow-on I can make is a finite-role continuum classification problem based on that theorem. Consider a large population with a fixed, small set \(T\) of roles. A role completely specifies an agent’s interaction intensities and follow/oppose relation to every role. Let \(\mu_a\) be the mass of role \(a\), and let \(q_{ab}=q_{ba}\geq 0\) be the interaction intensity, with symmetric sign \(\sigma_{ab}\in\{+1,-1\}\) for following or opposing. With \(d_a=\sum_b\mu_b q_{ab}>0\), the type-level opinions evolve by

\[ z_a(t+1)=\frac{1}{d_a}\sum_{b\in T}\mu_b q_{ab}\sigma_{ab}z_b(t). \]

A precise problem is: given rational masses and intensities, and the signed interaction pattern, classify the dynamics as polarization, divergence, or convergence to neutral consensus; return the corresponding signed partition or other certificate. Assume the induced interaction operator is connected and aperiodic, as in Theorem 4.1.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: millions of people in a few stable social or institutional roles, with role-level interaction rules shared across each role. The population is the continuous object: mass determines how strongly each role contributes to the average. The opinion is the evolving state, not the thing being continuized. The authors could reasonably recognize this as a population-level version of their update model.

I would expect this classification to be tractable, by checking signed-balance conditions on the \(\tau\)-role graph and using rational linear algebra for the limiting influence weights. Its scope is just Theorem 4.1’s outcome classification. It does not establish that the paper’s own result is computational, and it offers no hardness-transfer anchor.

The weak point is substantial: the mirror restricts arbitrary individual networks to a symmetric type-level interaction structure, and the resulting computation may be routine. It is a credible follow-on question, but under the stated anchor requirement, it does not make this paper a positive computational-mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the missing computational anchor. Theorem 4.1 gives an analytic trichotomy for a supplied signed network: its structure determines whether opinions polarize, diverge, or converge to neutral consensus. Theorem 4.2 likewise characterizes influence, and Proposition 3.1 establishes fixed points. None defines a computational problem and gives a complexity result. The paper studies opinion dynamics, not a choice rule or a computational task such as manipulation, control, or robustness. Theorem 4.1 is a named result, but it cannot anchor the kind of computational mirror ChoCo is seeking.

The finite role model is still a coherent population model, and the proponent is right that large groups sharing interaction rules are plausible. But for an arbitrary network, an agent’s relevant type must capture its interaction pattern; the number of types may then grow with the population. Keeping only a few roles and a role to role interaction matrix instead restricts the paper’s networks to a block structured regime. That restriction may be reasonable, but it does not turn the paper’s analytic classification into a computational result.

The strongest repair would be to study continuum network dynamics, or to add an optimization problem that changes role masses or interaction links to induce a desired outcome. The first remains an analytic dynamics question; the second introduces a new intervention objective absent from the paper. Neither supplies a computational mirror anchored in one of its results.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated anchor requirement, but the universal claim is too strong: a finite role continuum is a plausible follow on, and may be worthwhile as a separate project. What does not survive is the claim that this paper provides a worthwhile *computational mirror* of Theorem 4.1.
