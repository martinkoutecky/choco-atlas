Why no mirror

Theorem 4.4 is a computational result, but its steps are unilateral actions by finite agents, including actions that can displace another agent's match. In an atomless population those actions have zero aggregate effect; making a cohort move together adds a collective-action rule, and arbitrary mass transfers do not define a finite update bound. Theorem 3.1 gives a natural mass-flow endpoint characterization, but the paper does not use its LP to compute, so it is not a second computational anchor.

What the mirror covers

The proposed flow mirror describes Theorem 3.1's static endpoint, but no computational anchor survives; the convergence, cycling, and randomized convergence results are not mirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-volume market for a generic unit commodity: many buyers and sellers, with a small set of willingness-to-pay and opportunity-cost classes. A type records an agent’s value or cost and, when trade is restricted, its compatibility neighborhood. Each type’s mass is its fraction of the population. Matching becomes a flow of mass between types; prices and the DOA’s action rules remain the objects being studied. This continuizes the population, while the paper’s price grid remains part of the mechanism.

Lead — Mass-DOA-Complete Convergence, anchored in Theorem 4.4, proved in the paper. The theorem says that for a complete bipartite trading graph, Mechanism 1 with parameter \(\epsilon>0\), started from any valid state, reaches an \(\epsilon\)-stable state within \(n^3/\epsilon\) steps. The paper’s result concerns finitely many individual agents; it does not already make the population continuous.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite set \(I\) of buyer types and \(J\) of seller types, rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one across both sides, rational values and costs \(v_i,c_j\in[0,1]\), and a price grid with \(\epsilon=1/K\). Every buyer type can trade with every seller type. Start with all buyer mass bidding \(0\), all seller mass offering \(1\), and no matches. A state records unmatched mass by type and price and matched mass by buyer and seller type and their current prices. A legal update selects any active unmatched type-and-price cohort, applies Mechanism 1’s activity, increment, matching, and unmatched-first rules, and transfers the amount of mass that the move matches; any unmatched remainder stays as a separate cohort.

Ask whether every legal arbitrary-recognition sequence reaches an \(\epsilon\)-stable aggregate state after \(\operatorname{poly}(\tau,K,L)\) updates, where \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) and \(L\) is the input bit length. Stability means every compatible buyer–seller pair of positive-mass cohorts has bid minus offer at most \(\epsilon\); matched mass trades at equal prices; and any unmatched mass submits its true value or cost. A proof of that bound, or a legal counterexample sequence, is a solution. I expect this complete-market version to be tractable, but the bound is a new claim about the aggregate process, not a consequence already established by Theorem 4.4.

Supporting anchor — Mass-Assignment Equilibrium, from Theorem 3.1, also proved in the paper. It says a discrete market state is stable exactly when its matching is optimal for the primal maximum-surplus matching LP and its price-wise utilities are optimal for the dual. The underlying continuous problem is precise: given rational type masses, values, costs, and a compatibility graph \(E\), find a flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(v_i-c_j)x_{ij}\), subject to each type’s matched mass being at most its population mass, and return type prices \(p_i,q_j\). Require \(p_i\le q_j\) on every compatible edge, \(p_i=q_j\) wherever \(x_{ij}>0\), and \(p_i=v_i\) or \(q_j=c_j\) whenever the corresponding type has unmatched mass. The weighted primal and dual LPs have polynomial size in the type support, so this mirror is tractable in \((\tau,L)\). The theorem’s discrete formulation is proved in the paper; extending its LP certificate to type masses is the mirror. Fractional type-level trade is the natural aggregate of integral trades by individuals.

The population regime is plausible here: think of a commodity exchange with many participants but relatively few recurring valuation classes. A discrete market with counts \(n_i\) maps to masses proportional to \(n_i\); conversely, rational masses can be expanded to a finite market using a common denominator. The central convergence mirror is narrower than the paper: it covers the complete-market DOA result, not the arbitrary-graph deterministic cycling example. The paper’s prices being continuous-valued does not itself create a collision; its population is still finite.

My weakest point is the dynamic aggregation. The paper’s proof counts individual matches and price-grid changes. In a mass process, updates can split cohorts and move arbitrarily small quantities, so those counting arguments may fail or permit very long runs. Applying each recognition to a whole behaviorally identical cohort makes the proposed mirror well-defined, but it adds an aggregate-action convention the paper does not prove. The static equilibrium mirror remains strong even if the dynamic convergence mirror fails. Further questions include whether type-level equilibria are reachable from the zero-information state, and whether randomized recognition gives polynomial convergence on restricted compatibility graphs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a split one: the static equilibrium mirror is already the ordinary type-capacitated assignment problem, while the paper’s distinctive convergence result depends on individual actions that vanish in an atomless population.

The high-volume commodity market is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Give buyer type \(i\) mass \(\mu_i\), seller type \(j\) mass \(\nu_j\), and let \(x_{ij}\) be the mass they trade. The equilibrium mirror is the flow LP maximizing \(\sum_{ij}(v_i-c_j)x_{ij}\), subject to the type-mass capacities and the compatibility edges. Its dual gives the type-level prices and the stability conditions. This is exactly the primal-dual assignment formulation in the paper, compressed from individual agents to types; the proof of Theorem 3.1 carries over to mass flows. The resulting continuous computation is already a standard, polynomial-size network optimization problem. It is a sound model, but it leaves no new computational question for ChoCo to chart.

Theorem 4.4 is a stronger anchor, but its main object does not carry over with the population. Its steps are unilateral actions by individual buyers and sellers. In particular, one buyer can overbid and take another buyer’s match. In an atomless population, a single agent has zero mass: their action cannot change the aggregate matching or displace positive mass. If a whole type-price cohort acts together, it can change the aggregate state, but that is coordinated block behavior, not the paper’s strategically uncertain individual response.

The proposed mass-update version exposes the problem. If a legal update may transfer any positive amount of mass, the same aggregate adjustment can be divided into arbitrarily many smaller updates; an infinite sequence can approach a stable state without reaching one in finitely many updates. Then “polynomially many updates” depends on an arbitrary choice of update size. Requiring each update to match the whole available cohort would remove that ambiguity, but it adds a batch-action convention and changes what arbitrary recognition means. Keeping individual actions instead preserves the original mechanism, but its step count can depend on the population size \(n\), which the continuous input is meant to compress.

A more ambitious mirror could define a continuous-time or cohort-level auction dynamic and ask whether all fair trajectories converge. That may be worth studying, but it needs a rule for how mass acts and a schedule or rate model. Those choices supply new economic assumptions; they do not follow from the paper’s mechanism. Meanwhile, the static endpoint remains available directly from the flow LP.

So the negative case is strongest against the paper’s central computational contribution: the continuous society either has no aggregate dynamics under individual actions, or gets dynamics whose action unit must be specified anew. I would not claim this proves that no collective-action variant could interest the authors. That is the weak point in the universal claim. But such a variant would be a new mean-field auction model, while the paper’s faithful static mirror is already solved by the type-flow formulation.
