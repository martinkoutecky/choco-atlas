The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: this paper has no named NP-hardness, \(\mathrm{P}\), FPT, or related complexity theorem. Its named results concern the frequency of manipulation. So it does not provide a standard ChoCo complexity anchor. Nevertheless, its main theorem supports a credible continuous-population mirror of manipulation.

I would use Theorem 2 as the lead anchor. Proved in this paper, it says that if a voting rule \(r\) is homogeneous, anonymous, non-imposing, satisfies the canceling-out condition, and has a profile \(P\) such that both \(P\) and \(D_m(P)\) are stable, with \(r(P)=c_1\) and \(r(D_m(P))=c_2\), then there is an \(\epsilon>0\), independent of \(n\), such that \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} M_{i,m,n}(r)>\epsilon\) for every \(n\). Here \(D_m\) uniformly redistributes the position of one alternative while preserving the rankings of the other \(m-1\) alternatives, and stability means that sufficiently small relative perturbations do not change the winner. Equivalently, for at least one voter, a uniformly random truthful preference and uniformly random strategic report succeed with probability \(\Omega(1/n)\).

The natural mirror is a high-multiplicity electorate. Let \(T=L(C)\) be the finite set of complete rankings and let \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) give the mass of each ranking type. For rational \(\mu\), anonymity and homogeneity let us define \(\bar r(\mu)\) by scaling \(\mu\) to an ordinary finite profile. At stable real-valued societies, the same extension is locally well defined; ties require a fixed tie-breaking convention.

A plausible regime is a large referendum, platform election, or shareholder vote with millions of participants but only a few dozen or few hundred recurring complete ballots: party-line rankings, standard user segments, or coordinated organizational blocs. The number of agents is then much larger than \(\tau=|T|=m!\). A type is a complete ranking, not merely a preference label: agents of one type are indistinguishable to the rule and have the same strategic possibilities.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Continuous Mass-Frequency Manipulation. Its instance consists of a rule \(r\), a rational background society \(\mu\), a rational block mass \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), and a rational threshold \(q\). For true type \(t\) and reported type \(t'\), define

\[ \mu^{\mathrm{true}}_{\alpha,t}=(1-\alpha)\mu+\alpha\delta_t \]

and

\[ \mu^{\mathrm{dev}}_{\alpha,t,t'}=(1-\alpha)\mu+\alpha\delta_{t'}. \]

The block succeeds when

\[ \bar r\!\left(\mu^{\mathrm{dev}}_{\alpha,t,t'}\right) \succ_t \bar r\!\left(\mu^{\mathrm{true}}_{\alpha,t}\right). \]

The problem asks whether the fraction

\[ F_r(\mu,\alpha)= \frac{1}{\tau^2} \sum_{t,t'\in T} \mathbf 1\!\left[ \bar r\!\left(\mu^{\mathrm{dev}}_{\alpha,t,t'}\right) \succ_t \bar r\!\left(\mu^{\mathrm{true}}_{\alpha,t}\right) \right] \]

is at least \(q\), and returns the exact value together with the successful type/report pairs. The decision variable is the report \(t'\); the objective is the mass, or equivalently the probability, of successful manipulations.

This is genuinely the paper’s problem with the indivisible voter replaced by an \(\alpha\)-mass block. For a finite profile of \(n-1\) other voters, take \(\alpha=1/n\) and let \(\mu\) be their empirical type distribution. Then the displayed societies are exactly the truthful and manipulated \(n\)-voter profiles. Averaging \(F_r(\mu,1/n)\) over the random background profile recovers the paper’s \(M_{i,m,n}(r)\). Thus the bridge is not merely verbal: the finite model is a rational special case of the mass model.

I would expect this fixed-\(m\), explicit-type problem to be Class A. One can enumerate the \(\tau^2\) type/report pairs. For positional scoring rules, the winner conditions are linear in the mass variables, and a minimum-mass version becomes a small LP; for fixed \(m\), STV, Copeland, maximin, and ranked pairs can plausibly be handled by enumerating their finite winner or elimination regions. If \(m\) is unbounded and rankings are represented implicitly, ordinary manipulation hardness may transfer instead, giving a Class B version. This paper itself does not establish either complexity classification.

The paper’s Theorem 3, proved here, gives the concrete scope: every nontrivial positional scoring rule with at least four alternatives, Copeland with at least five alternatives, and STV, maximin, and ranked pairs with at least three alternatives are frequently manipulable in the paper’s sense. The same continuous mass problem therefore applies to all of those rule families. The fractional profiles used in the proof of \(D_m\) are not themselves a prior continuous-population model; they are a proof device, so there is no novelty collision.

The authors should recognize this as their problem rather than as an unrelated relaxation. The voting rule, complete-ranking types, strategic reports, and preference-based success criterion are unchanged. Only the unit of manipulation changes from one named voter to a positive mass block, which is forced by an atomless population. The paper itself identifies coalitional manipulation as an important next question, making this co-modification especially defensible.

The weak point is substantial: \(\Omega(1/n)\) success for an individual voter does not imply that a positive fraction of a continuum can manipulate. At a stable society, every sufficiently small mass perturbation may fail, and the interesting behaviour may live only on instability boundaries. Moreover, the paper’s theorem is probabilistic rather than a standard computational-complexity result. So this is a strong mirror of the paper’s manipulation question, but not a strong Class A/B/C result unless the mass problem is studied independently.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are structural and probabilistic statements about the frequency of successful manipulation; they do not define an input, an optimization problem, or a complexity measure. The computational discussion in the introduction concerns other papers. Thus any continuous problem must be invented by the analyst, rather than extracted from a computational contribution here.

The deeper problem is that Theorem 2 measures finite-granularity pivotality. A random profile has empirical distribution \(\mu_n\), a single voter changes it by

\[ \frac{\delta_{t'}-\delta_t}{n}, \]

and the proof obtains its \(\Omega(1/n)\) phenomenon from lattice boundaries together with \(O(\sqrt n)\) sampling fluctuations. The static continuum society retains neither scale. Under the paper’s product distribution, \(\mu_n\) converges to the uniform distribution over rankings; the fluctuations disappear, while the individual perturbation has zero mass. At a stable limiting society, manipulation vanishes. At a boundary, one must retain a tangent direction, a fluctuation scale, and a tie convention—effectively a local-limit or noise model, not a continuous-population model.

The proposed \(F_r(\mu,\alpha)\) does not repair this. For fixed \(\alpha>0\), it studies a positive-mass coalition changing from one type to another. That may be a worthwhile new problem, but it is not the paper’s individual-manipulation result; the paper explicitly leaves coalitional manipulation for future work. The choice of \(\alpha\) is also external: there is no continuum analogue of “one voter” except \(\alpha\to0\), and that limit is precisely where the effect disappears around stable societies.

Taking \(\alpha=1/n\) merely restores the finite model in normalized notation. With \(\mu\) equal to the empirical distribution of \(n-1\) voters, the two displayed societies encode exactly the original \(n\)-voter profiles. This is a valid high-multiplicity representation, but the continuum has not become the object of the theorem: the relevant parameter is still the atom-sized \(1/n\) perturbation. Conversely, taking a genuine positive-mass limit changes the question to coalition manipulation.

The same issue defeats Theorem 3. Its propositions only verify that positional scoring rules, Copeland, STV, maximin, and ranked pairs satisfy Theorem 2’s hypotheses. In a continuum these hypotheses may describe two stable mass vectors with different winners, but that says nothing about a positive measure of successful mass transfers. Small transfers are blocked by stability; fixed transfers are coalitional; and the intermediate \(1/n\) regime is the finite pivotality regime again.

A more ambitious repair could study a Gaussian tangent process around a limiting society, or optimize the fraction of society benefiting from a mass transfer. Either could produce a good new social-choice paper. But the former continuizes the sampling noise and the latter continuizes coalitional manipulation. Neither is a continuous mirror of the named results here. The negative case is therefore strongest as a claim about mismatch: every faithful version loses the phenomenon, while every nondegenerate version changes the problem.
