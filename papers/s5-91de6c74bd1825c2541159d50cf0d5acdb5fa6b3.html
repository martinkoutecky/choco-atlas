The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of roommate types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compatibility between types, type-level rankings of compatible partner types, and prescribed unmatched masses \(u_t\) whose positive support is independent, decide whether a measurable reciprocal matching of an atomless population with type masses \(\mu_t\) exists that leaves mass \(u_t\) of each type unmatched and is popular. Popularity means that for every alternative feasible matching on the same population, the mass preferring the proposed matching’s partner type is at least the mass preferring the alternative’s; agents indifferent between partner types contribute to neither side. Restrict to instances with no stable matching, as in the setting of Theorem 2.

The model it lives in

An atomless population \(\Omega\) partitioned into cohorts of masses \(\mu_t\), with type-level compatibility and preferences; feasible solutions are reciprocal measurable matchings with prescribed unmatched masses \(u_t\). Compare matchings agent by agent on the same \(\Omega\), counting the mass preferring each assignment, and ask whether a popular matching exists.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s prescribed-unmatched-set existence problem. It leaves aside Theorem 3’s odd-\(n\), high-minimum-degree bound, the experiments, and the paper’s other matching results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can popularity and feasibility be characterized using a finite formulation over type masses and the cross-matching assignments needed to compare the same agents?
  • For fixed \(|T|\), what is the complexity in the input bit length, and does a tractable algorithm exist?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For a roommates instance with no stable matching and a specified nonempty independent set \(U\) of vertices, let \(Z=V\setminus N(U)\setminus U\). The theorem says the algorithm either finds a popular but unstable matching that leaves exactly \(U\) unmatched, or proves none exists, in \(O(n^{|Z|}(m+|Z|^3))\) time. This is polynomial when \(|Z|\) is fixed; it is not a polynomial-time claim for unrestricted \(|Z|\). The paper’s cited NP-completeness results motivate the problem, but they are not numbered results here, so I would not use them as anchors.

A natural mirror is Mass-Popular Roommates with Prescribed Unmatched Mass. An instance has a finite set of roommate types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a compatibility relation between types, a strict ranking for each type over compatible partner types, and a prescribed unmatched mass \(u_t\leq\mu_t\) for each type. Types are complete descriptions for this model: agents of type \(t\) have the same compatibility and preferences over partner types. Agents are indifferent among partners of the same type.

Represent the population as an atomless set \(\Omega\), with mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\). A feasible solution is a measurable matching of agents: each matched agent has exactly one compatible partner, and each pair is reciprocal; exactly mass \(u_t\) of type \(t\) is unmatched. For two matchings \(\phi\) and \(\psi\), count the mass of agents who strictly prefer their partner’s type under \(\phi\) to their partner’s type under \(\psi\), and vice versa. A matching \(\phi\) is popular if, for every feasible \(\psi\), the mass preferring \(\psi\) is no greater than the mass preferring \(\phi\). The problem is to find such a matching or establish that none exists. If the prescribed unmatched types include compatible pairs, the answer is immediately no: those unmatched agents could prefer matching with one another.

The regime is a large housing or roommate pool in which preferences and compatibility depend on a small set of shared attributes. There may be hundreds of thousands of people but only a modest number \(\tau\) of types. The action is still an ordinary one-partner assignment for each person; only the mass of agents assigned along each type-to-type pairing is divisible. That makes population mass, rather than fractional partners, the continuous object. The prescribed unmatched vector is the mass counterpart of specifying \(U\), while the types with no compatibility path to unmatched types play the role of \(Z\).

I would expect the fixed-\(\tau\) version to be tractable, perhaps through a finite-dimensional mass-flow formulation combined with a continuous analogue of the paper’s decomposition into a popular part and a stable remainder. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 2: its algorithm enumerates discrete matchings, and the theorem does not show that this enumeration becomes an LP over type masses. The useful follow-up questions are whether popularity can be characterized using only type-level masses, whether the popular/stable decomposition survives when cohorts can split, and how the complexity changes as \(\tau\) grows.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it preserves roommate compatibility, pairwise majority comparisons, and the existence of a popular matching, while making the population a distribution over types. The main caveat is that the paper assumes strict rankings over individual neighbors. Grouping agents by archetype naturally introduces indifference among same-type partners, so this mirror is a weak-preference extension. If the authors regard those individual distinctions as essential, the type compression will not faithfully mirror their model. Also, the paper’s Theorem 3 result for odd \(n\) and minimum degree at least \(n-c\) is not part of this mirror: its parity condition and bounded number of missing edges do not carry over cleanly to an atomless population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational result, so the negative case cannot rest on a lack of something to mirror. The trouble is that its tractable parameter is the number of individual vertices in \(Z\), not the number of voter types. Its algorithm enumerates ways to match those vertices. If a rational type mass is expanded into \(q\) identical agents, the running time can grow as \(q^{|Z|}\); with masses encoded in binary, \(q\) can be exponential in the input bit length. Thus the theorem’s polynomial bound does not give a high-multiplicity or continuous algorithm.

The proposed mass version is coherent, but it changes the key object in the proof. In the paper, each named vertex has one partner, and its strict vote between two matchings depends on those partners. A type-to-type mass matrix does not say which members of a cohort receive which partners in each matching. Pairwise popularity comparisons therefore need joint assignments—or couplings—between the candidate matching and each challenger. The alternating paths and blocking-edge decomposition used by Theorem 2 do not transfer directly to those mass variables. An algorithm for the proposed model would need a new characterization; the paper gives little evidence for the suggested fixed-\(\tau\) tractability.

The paper’s headline high-degree result narrows the target further. With minimum degree at least \(n-c\), any set \(U\) of unmatched vertices has size at most \(c\). Its required unmatched population therefore has mass at most \(c/n\), which vanishes as the population grows. Requiring positive unmatched mass instead means leaving that high-degree regime. This is a genuine degeneracy of the result’s tractable setting, not just a problem with translating odd parity literally.

Still, the strongest version of the mirror survives these objections as a research question: a large roommate pool with a small set of shared preference and compatibility types, and a definition of popularity over measurable matchings that accounts for cross-matching assignments. The authors could plausibly recognize that as their problem. But it is a new type-level popularity problem, not a continuous lift of the algorithm in Theorem 2. The honest negative case is therefore a case for low priority, not a universal veto: it cannot establish that no worthwhile scenario exists.
