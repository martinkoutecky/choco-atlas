What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for a model-level extension, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results. The paper has no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification such as polynomial time, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability.

The closest named results are Theorem 6 and Theorem 17, both proved in the paper. Theorem 6 says that for a random geometric graph with expected average degree \(\delta=o(\sqrt n)\), the expected fraction of monochrome edges after one FSP round is at least
\[ \frac12+\frac9{800} \left(\frac12-\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\lfloor\delta/2\rfloor}}\right)^2 \left(1-e^{-\delta/2}\left(1+\frac\delta2\right)\right)(1-o(1)). \]
Theorem 17 says that for an Erdős–Rényi graph with expected average degree \(\delta=o(\sqrt n)\), the expected fraction is at most \(\frac12+o(1)\). These are substantial results about probabilities and graph structure, but neither classifies the computational complexity of a problem.

The continuous question I would defend as a *new extension* is a one-round FSP on a repeated block template. Let \(H=(R,F)\) describe a fixed set of residential block roles and which roles are adjacent. The instance gives rational masses \(\mu_{i,+},\mu_{i,-}\) for each role \(i\in R\), summing to one. A type is a resident’s block role and group; mass is the fraction of residents of that type. Each unit of mass updates according to the majority group mass in neighboring roles, with ties split equally between the two resulting groups. The question is: what fraction of adjacent resident pairs have the same group after the update, and is it at least a given rational threshold \(q\)? This describes many residents sharing a small number of complete types, with population size much larger than the support.

The core model is recognizable: binary groups, local majority decisions, one simultaneous update, and monochrome edge fraction as the outcome measure. On this specified one-round problem, the update and fraction can be computed in polynomial time from the role graph and rational masses. That is a tractable continuous population problem, but it is not a complexity result from this paper, and it does not explain Theorems 6 or 17.

The weak point is decisive: those theorems rely on random individual types and random graph neighborhoods. A deterministic mass profile smooths away the finite-sample fluctuations and shared-neighborhood correlations that produce the geometric graph’s segregation advantage. Preserving those effects would require carrying local random environments into the type description, weakening the high-multiplicity case. So I would offer the block-template question as a plausible extension of the FSP model, while reporting plainly that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result in ChoCo’s sense to mirror. Theorem 6 and Theorem 17 are named, substantial results, but they bound an expected outcome on random graph ensembles. The paper poses no algorithmic input-output problem and proves no complexity classification or algorithm for one. Its central contribution is probabilistic analysis of a one-round process. Turning its theorems into decision problems about whether an edge fraction exceeds a threshold would add a computational task the authors did not study.

Theorem 6 is the stronger anchor, and it exposes the main modelling problem. Its segregation effect comes from random finite neighborhoods: adjacent vertices share randomly colored neighbors, and the majority fluctuation in that shared neighborhood can determine both vertices’ updates. In a continuous block-template model, residents of each role are replaced by group masses. With balanced masses, those local majorities tie; the finite-sample fluctuations that drive the theorem have disappeared. Giving the roles imbalanced masses can make the update interesting, but then the model starts with structured group densities rather than producing the paper’s effect from uniform random initial types.

A more faithful proposal would use a continuum spatial population with a contact kernel and ask for the monochrome edge mass after one update. That is a coherent continuous dynamical model. But its deterministic mass update still suppresses the fluctuations in Theorem 6. Restoring them requires extra randomness at the local-environment or fluctuation level. One can study that limit, but the object doing the work is then a law of random neighborhoods or a stochastic limit, rather than a high-multiplicity population of interchangeable agents. In the paper’s sparse regime, local neighborhoods remain finite even as the city grows, which makes replacing them with masses especially ill-suited.

Theorem 17 has the same category problem from the other direction. Its upper bound follows because adjacent vertices in an Erdős–Rényi graph are unlikely to share a neighbor, so their updates are driven by disjoint random sets. A continuum density alone records neither those finite random neighborhoods nor their overlap. A distribution over neighborhood environments could retain them, but that would recast the paper’s random-graph probability model; it would not give a computational population problem already present in the paper.

The proposed repeated-block mirror is therefore an extension, not a mirror of either theorem. It adds a role graph, treats each role as a mass of residents, and asks for an edge-fraction threshold after a deterministic mass update. That may be a useful study of density-level dynamics, but it changes the source of the behavior and supplies a new decision question. Its connection to this paper is the choice of process and statistic, not a continuous version of a computational result.

This is a strong reason to reject the proposed mirrors as ChoCo targets. It does not prove that no worthwhile continuous study of the FSP exists: a continuum spatial model or a stochastic local-environment limit could be valuable on its own. The negative case is weakest precisely there. But those possibilities do not repair the missing computational anchor or make the block-template problem a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
