The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. It has no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, and it gives no algorithm or complexity analysis to mirror. Its named results concern power-index formulas and axioms.

The closest main result, Theorem 2, is proved here: for \(n\ge 3\), any power index \(\Phi\) satisfying efficiency, the null-player property, homogeneous increments sharing (HIS), and discretizability (D) on interval simple games must equal \(\Psi\) on every approximable interval simple game. That is a uniqueness characterization, not a computational result. Proposition 7, also proved here, shows that a finite \((j,k)\)-simple game and its natural embedding as an interval simple game have the same Shapley–Shubik power index. That bridge continuizes the decision levels, not the population.

The model is \(v:[0,1]^n\to[0,1]\): each of the \(n\) voters has a continuous approval level, and the group decision is continuous. The population remains finite. A large committee with repeated voting roles might motivate a follow-up about aggregate power by type, but a continuum population would require defining power when no individual is pivotal in the finite-permutation sense. This paper neither defines that problem nor establishes a result about it.

So there is no honest per-anchor continuous computational problem to hand to a prover. The potential follow-up may be worthwhile, but it is not a computational mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes \(\Psi\) by axioms; Proposition 7 proves that a finite \((j,k)\)-game and its interval embedding have the same index. Neither states an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a computational problem. A question about computing an index could be worthwhile on its own, but without an input representation or complexity claim in the paper, it would be a new project rather than a computational mirror.

The two results also continuize the decision levels, not the population. In Theorem 2, discretizability concerns approximating a game on \([0,1]^n\) while \(n\) stays fixed. In Proposition 7, the grid of possible inputs and outputs becomes intervals, while the voters remain the finite set \(N\). This distinction is not itself a reason to reject a population mirror; it shows that neither result supplies the population-complexity anchor the programme is looking for.

The best repaired proposal is a society with type distribution \(\mu\), an aggregation rule \(V\) over type-level actions, and a power measure for each type. But that changes what the paper measures. Its index assigns power to individual coordinates and uses permutations of those individuals. In an atomless population, a single person has zero mass and cannot change a rule that depends only on the population distribution, so individual influence degenerates. Summing the influence of many identical individuals might remain meaningful, but treating a type’s whole mass as one player instead makes it a coordinated bloc. Those are different power concepts. There is no uniform random ordering of an atomless electorate built into the paper, and its axioms do not select a unique type-level extension from the possible alternatives.

That leaves a plausible new question about type-level power, but not a surviving mirror of either result. The negative case is weakest at the universal edge: such a population model could be worth studying in power-index theory. Its connection to this paper’s results, though, would be a newly chosen index and computational problem, not a continuous computational question those results already pose.
