The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: this paper gives a plausible mirror for the *structure* of stable assignments, but it has no numbered result that qualifies as a computational-complexity anchor under your rule. Its closest result is Theorem 4.1, proved here: for every SPA-S instance, the stable matchings under student-oriented dominance form a distributive lattice. The meet assigns each student their better project from two stable matchings; the join assigns their worse project. The student-optimal and lecturer-optimal stable matchings are the lattice’s extremes.

A credible high-multiplicity setting would be a large, centralized university project-allocation system: perhaps \(10^5\) students choosing among a comparatively small number of project offerings, with students grouped by their full project rankings and eligibility or priority band. Project and lecturer capacities would scale with cohort size. A type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); capacities are fractions of the cohort. Each person still receives one whole project, while \(x_{t,p}\) records the mass of type \(t\) assigned to project \(p\). This captures the paper’s two-sided capacity constraints and its stability question. The type assumption does require lecturers to rank types, tying students within a type; that is an extension toward SPA-ST, which the paper itself discusses.

I would name the lead question the Type-Continuum SPA-S Lattice Problem. An instance consists of finite project and lecturer sets, lecturer-to-project assignments, rational type masses, each type’s strict project ranking, each lecturer’s priority order over types, and rational per-capita project and lecturer capacities. A feasible allocation satisfies \(\sum_p x_{t,p}\leq\mu_t\), the project capacities, and the lecturer capacities. Stability rules out any positive mass of a type moving to a preferred project under the paper’s blocking conditions: entry where both capacities have slack; entry to an undersubscribed project at a full lecturer through an internal move or a priority substitution; or replacement of lower-priority mass at a full project.

The question is whether, whenever \(x\) and \(y\) are stable allocations, the stable allocations form a distributive lattice under typewise student preference. More precisely, order each type’s assignments by the cumulative mass receiving its top \(j\) projects. Does taking the coordinatewise maximum of those cumulative masses produce a stable meet, and taking the minimum produce a stable join, with both distributive identities holding? A proof for all such instances, or a valid counterexample, would answer the question. The model has no scalar objective: its solutions are stable allocations and, if the lattice claim holds, their meet and join.

I would tentatively expect the computational follow-up—finding extremal stable allocations for rational, finite-support instances—to be tractable, because the number of type-project variables is polynomial in the explicit input size. That is a conjecture, not a result of this paper, and the proposed structural question does not itself fit the A/B/C complexity trichotomy. There is no hardness anchor here whose population-driven combinatorics might dissolve.

The mirror covers only Theorem 4.1. Theorem 2.1, the Unpopular Projects Theorem, is cited from [9, 35] and could motivate further invariants for masses, but I would not count it as another anchor. Further questions include whether stable allocations always exist under these type-level priorities, whether student- and lecturer-optimal allocations can be computed efficiently, and how the answers change across weak, strong, and super-stability notions.

The soft spot is central: Theorem 4.1 is structural, not a complexity result, and the paper establishes nothing about continuous populations or algorithms over them. Also, grouping many students into a type erases the strict individual lecturer rankings used in SPA-S; ties among a type’s members may break the lattice property. That is why this is a real, testable extension the authors could recognize—not an established continuation of their theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is Theorem 4.1, but it establishes a structural property, not a computational result. The paper proves that finite SPA-S stable matchings form a distributive lattice; it gives no complexity theorem for computing, optimizing, counting, or enumerating them. The lattice may support future algorithms, but those would be new questions, not continuous mirrors of a computational result in this paper. The cited Unpopular Projects Theorem is inherited from earlier work, and is structural too.

The proposed university-cohort model makes the population story plausible, but it faces a choice. In SPA-S, lecturers rank students strictly. A student’s rank in a lecturer’s list is part of their type, so active students cannot be grouped into repeated, indistinguishable types: the support grows with the cohort. To obtain high multiplicity, the model instead ties students within a type. That may be a sensible SPA-ST extension—the paper itself discusses ties—but it changes the blocking relation and the stability model. It is therefore a mirror of a related open structural question, not a continuization of Theorem 4.1 as stated.

The best rescue is the proponent’s cumulative-mass order and a theorem about whether its meet and join preserve stability and distributivity. That is a coherent mathematical question, and failure of the lattice property would still be informative. But it remains a structural classification question. Turning it into a computational one would require adding a task such as finding extremal stable allocations; that task is not established by this paper’s theorem, and enumeration becomes a different problem when the allocation space may be continuous.

So the negative case is strongest on fit with ChoCo’s computational programme, not on the claim that no continuous matching model could be worthwhile. If the programme included structural extensions of stable matching to type-level priorities, the SPA-ST version could merit study. Under the stated scope, though, this paper supplies no computational anchor for a continuous mirror.
