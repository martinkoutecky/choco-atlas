The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a follow-on direction, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results: the paper contains no named result about a problem being in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its named results concern economic implementability.

The nearest candidates do not meet that requirement. Theorem 1, proved here, says that if an SCF is fully implementable under a solution concept satisfying WSC, then it is BIC; conversely, an implementable BIC SCF makes that concept WSC for its implementing mechanisms. Corollary 1, derived here from the Myerson–Satterthwaite impossibility result, says that an individually rational, ex-post efficient SCF is not fully implementable under any WSC solution concept. Both are substantive implementation results, but neither classifies computational complexity.

There is a possible population-level setting nearby: many replicated buyer–seller pairs, with each pair’s complete type recording its valuations, preferences, beliefs, and other relevant parameters; mass records the fraction of pairs of each type. A planner could choose a mechanism or outcome schedule. But the paper’s strategic agents are the individual members of a finite set \(I\). Treating the pairs—or the traders themselves—as a continuum changes how an individual deviation affects outcomes, so the paper’s WSC and full-implementation results do not directly supply the mirror. That mismatch is the weakest point, and it is decisive here: I cannot state a prover-ready continuous problem *per computational anchor* without inventing an anchor the paper does not have.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes when BIC is necessary for implementation; Corollary 1 extends an economic impossibility result. Neither defines an algorithmic problem with an encoded input, objective, or complexity measure. The paper even allows broad classes of mechanisms and measurable spaces, so turning its implementability questions into complexity questions would first require choosing a finite representation and restricting the mechanism class. That could produce worthwhile research, but the computational problem would come from those new choices, not from a result in this paper.

Neither anchor becomes a computational mirror just by putting types into a distribution. The \(p_i\) in the model are beliefs about other agents’ types, not population shares. Theorem 1’s key claim is about whether each agent and type has incentives to report truthfully across all solutions. Asking for its analogue with a continuum of agents would be a legitimate economic question, but still a characterization of incentives unless a separate algorithmic task is added.

Corollary 1 has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many replicated buyer–seller markets, with mass over market types. But if each market remains a bilateral trade problem, the Myerson–Satterthwaite impossibility applies market by market; averaging across markets does not create a new computational landscape. If instead the model has a continuum of buyers and sellers in one market, the agents become negligible and the strategic problem changes: it is no longer bilateral trade with two pivotal agents. Either version may be interesting, but neither yields a computational mirror of the corollary as stated.

The proponent’s candidate therefore needs a substantial extra step. One could specify a finite-support distribution of market types, restrict the available mechanisms, and ask for an algorithm to find an implementable rule. That is a possible new project, but the paper neither poses nor classifies that problem. And the paper’s references to continuous implementation do not fill the gap: continuity of an implementation requirement is different from a continuous population.

I would not defend the literal universal claim that no population scenario could make related mechanism-design work worthwhile. Large populations of bidders or trading cohorts can be sensible high-multiplicity settings. The negative case is narrower but firm: the proposed anchors do not support a worthwhile *computational mirror of this paper*; making them computational would require importing the central problem from elsewhere.
