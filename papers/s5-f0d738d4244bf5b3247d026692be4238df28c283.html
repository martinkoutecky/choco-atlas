The paper’s interval games do not fill that gap. Their approval inputs and outcomes vary continuously, while the game still has \(n\) player coordinates and its power index still averages over player orderings. That is continuity on the input and outcome side, not a continuous society.

The proponent’s best alternative—a large organization with many interchangeable members in each of finitely many roles—is coherent. Asking for each role’s *total* limiting power would avoid the vanishing-per-person problem, and symmetry can make those totals well-defined. But to determine pivotality, a role distribution needs to come with a rule specifying how role members’ approvals combine. For arbitrary games, that interaction structure can distinguish nearly every player; restricting it to a compact role-based family is an additional modelling choice. The resulting question would be about computing power in that chosen family, not a computational result established by this paper.

Encoding makes the gap concrete. If the whole game table is explicit, the average-game formula can be evaluated in time polynomial in the table size. To get a nontrivial complexity question, one would need a succinct representation—say, a restricted class of weighted or role-based games—and then study its algorithms. That could be a worthwhile project, but its model and computational target would need to be supplied independently.
