The case FOR (proponent)

A municipal participatory budget has a plausible continuous-population mirror: let each type be an approval set \(t\subseteq P\), and let \(\mu_t\) be the share of residents with that ballot. The decision remains a feasible project set \(X\), with \(\sum_{p\in X}c(p)\le L\). A mass version of the paper’s U-JR condition would require that every group of types with total mass at least \(1/L\) and a commonly approved project has some project in \(X\) approved by positive mass within that group. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime when a large population shares relatively few ballot types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no named computational result to continuize. Its numbered items are definitions and algorithms; Table 2 reports empirical rates, and the prose mentions polynomial time and co-NP-hardness without establishing either as a theorem. The proposed setting itself is plausible: a large municipal consultation can have many residents sharing approval ballots, and a distribution over approval sets is a natural type model. The threshold \(1/L\) remains meaningful, and a mass version of U-JR can be defined. That answers whether high multiplicity makes sense for participatory budgeting. It does not supply a result from this paper to mirror.

The strongest version would define weighted variants of SCC, Monroe, or STV and ask whether they satisfy mass-U-JR, or how often they do under a specified distribution over societies. Those are coherent new questions, but the paper neither proves a guarantee for its algorithms nor studies voter-population growth. Answering them would require choosing the continuous algorithms, the axiom’s precise mass semantics, and a new experiment or complexity problem. That could be worthwhile research on participatory budgeting; the paper alone does not make it a worthwhile continuous mirror of its results.

So I would reject the proposed anchor for this paper, while keeping the negative verdict narrow: the case against is strong on the absence of a computational result to translate and weak as a claim that continuous participatory budgeting has no worthwhile questions.
