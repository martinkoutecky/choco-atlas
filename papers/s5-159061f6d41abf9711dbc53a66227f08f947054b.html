What the mirror covers

The proposed mass model would extend the anonymous, propositionally independent rule classification in Theorem 17 and the normal-pair classification in Theorem 13; it leaves the paper's other results untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive direction I can see is a mass-based version of the paper’s anonymous, propositionally independent judgment aggregation. It has a plausible regime: a large public consultation on a fixed agenda, where participants’ complete, logically consistent judgments repeat across many people. A type is a complete truth assignment to the agenda’s symbols; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of respondents with type \(t\). The support size \(\tau\) counts distinct judgment types, which could be small relative to the population.

Call the prospective problem Quota-Continuous Judgment Aggregation. Given a symbol-complete, symbol-connected agenda \(\mathcal A\), characterize the anonymous aggregation rules \(F\) that map every finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over consistent judgments to a consistent collective judgment. Require unanimity preservation, and require the output on each proposition \(x\) to depend only on its supporting mass \(p_x=\sum_{t:t(x)=T}\mu_t\): \(F(\mu)(x)=h_x(p_x)\), with \(h_x(0)=0\) and \(h_x(1)=1\). A solution is a family of such functions that produces a logically consistent judgment for every \(\mu\). This preserves the paper’s agenda and its anonymous, issue-wise aggregation question. OR-like and AND-like rules have natural mass versions; XOR has no obvious one, since the same proportions can come from electorates of different sizes and parities.

The nearest named result is Theorem 17, proved in this paper: for a symbol-complete, symbol-connected basis, an anonymous, propositionally independent judgment aggregation rule in normal form must use OR, AND, or XOR (or a negation) across all judges, and each compound proposition must use the same function type (or its negation) over a subset of its symbols. Theorem 13, also proved here, classifies nontrivial normal pairs: both functions must be AND, both OR, or each must be XOR or NXOR. These are meaningful anchors for an axiomatic continuization, but neither asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper has no qualifying named computational result, so neither can serve as an anchor under the stated requirement.

I would expect the quota-rule classification to illuminate which finite anonymous rules survive when population size disappears, with parity as a likely casualty. But that is a new axiomatic research question, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper; it has no warranted Class A, B, or C prediction. The weakest point is therefore decisive for ChoCo: the continuous-population model is plausible and the authors could recognize its connection to their work, but it misses the programme’s computational gate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive for ChoCo: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 13 classifies normal pairs of Boolean functions; Theorem 17 classifies anonymous, propositionally independent aggregation rules. Neither studies an algorithmic problem with an input and a computational objective. The paper proves no complexity bound, hardness result, or exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm. Fourier analysis is the proof technique, not an algorithmic contribution.

The proposed quota model is a reasonable population continuization. A large consultation can have many people sharing complete judgment types, and replacing judge counts with rational masses is sensible. But classifying the mass rules \(h_x\) that preserve unanimity and logical consistency is a new axiomatic question. It may be interesting in its own right; it does not supply the missing computational anchor. The same goes for Theorem 13: translating its function identities into a continuum functional classification would continue the paper’s mathematics, not its computational agenda.

One could invent a decision problem around these results—for example, asking whether an agenda admits a non-dictatorial rule, or computing a collective judgment from a given distribution. But those would be newly posed problems, not computational results of this paper being continued. Making the input explicit does not turn the paper’s classification theorems into complexity results.

I would not argue that repeated judgment types are implausible, or that losing finite-population parity makes the mirror unworthy; either could be a legitimate consequence to study. The honest limit of the negative case is that an axiomatic mirror might be worthwhile outside ChoCo. For the programme as defined, though, neither proposed anchor clears its computational gate.
