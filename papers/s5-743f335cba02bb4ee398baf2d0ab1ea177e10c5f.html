The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational anchor to defeat: the case for names none, correctly. The numbered propositions establish equilibrium existence, characterize asymptotic offers and payoffs, and compare voting rules. None gives a complexity result or an algorithmic classification. Recasting equilibrium existence as “compute an equilibrium” would require a new problem: the paper specifies no computational input format for its distributions and payoffs, output precision, or equilibrium-selection task. That may be a worthwhile question, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The strongest attempted mirror is to give a mass distribution over voter signal types and ask for the proposer’s equilibrium offer. The creditor setting makes a large population plausible, so lack of multiplicity is not the objection. The structural problem is that a voter’s signal drives a decision whose strategic incentive depends on being pivotal. In an atomless electorate, one voter’s action changes zero mass, so the pivotal conditioning in equations (2)–(6) disappears. Under majority rule, adoption follows the aggregate accepting mass; under unanimity, any positive mass of rejectors blocks adoption. Neither gives the paper’s pivotal incentives for choosing a cutoff. Keeping finite-\(n\) pivotal probabilities preserves those incentives, but leaves a finite-agent game; using the paper’s sequence of finite games and its limiting pivotal probabilities is already its analytic method, not a continuous computational model.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a source of continuous computational mirrors. Its honest limit is that a new equilibrium-computation problem, perhaps with finitely many signal types and compressed voter counts, could still be worth studying. I can’t establish the universal claim that no project inspired by the paper could be worthwhile; that would go beyond what the paper supports.
