The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: the paper has a very natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor in the strict sense of the programme. None of its numbered results states NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), fixed-parameter tractability, approximation, or a comparable complexity classification. The closest anchors are welfare and equilibrium theorems. If those are admissible, the following is the best positive case.

The natural regime is a large federation, jury pool, or expert committee with many members but few behavioural classes. Class \(j\) contains mass \( \rho_j \) of the population, all sharing the same private-value probability \(p_j\), cautiousness \(c_j\), and signal technology. Members independently observe \(s_Q\) or \(s_R\). Thus the number of agents \(N\) is large while the number of classes \(J\) is fixed or small. This is plausible for a large jury pool, a federation of many similar jurisdictions, or a standards body containing many repeated organizational roles.

A realized voter type is \(P\), for a private-value agent, or \((j,s,c_j)\), for a common-value agent from class \(j\) who observed signal \(s\). The state-dependent population masses are

\[ \mu^q_{j,P}=\rho_jp_j,\qquad \mu^q_{j,s}=\rho_j(1-p_j)\Pr(s\mid q). \]

The action variable is a type-dependent strategy

\[ \sigma_\theta\in\Delta(\{r,q,v\}), \]

where \(r\) supports the reform, \(q\) opposes it without vetoing, and \(v\) vetoes it. A quorum \( \kappa\in[0,1] \) implements \(R\) when the \(r\)-mass is at least \( \kappa \) and the veto condition is satisfied; otherwise it implements \(Q\). The objective is to maximize aggregate ex ante utility among admissible mechanisms, equivalently in the homogeneous-cautiousness case to implement the paper’s ex ante-efficient decision rule \(M^*\).

There is one necessary co-modification. In an ordinary atomless game, an individual has zero mass and therefore cannot be pivotal, whereas the paper’s central institution gives every individual veto power. I would therefore define the continuous problem as a singular high-multiplicity limit: quorum decisions depend on masses, but the veto operation retains a Boolean veto flag, or equivalently the solution must be the limit of symmetric equilibria in finite populations obtained by replicating each class. This preserves the paper’s strategic content rather than silently replacing individual veto by a mass threshold.

My lead anchor would be Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that for every tuple \( (f_Q,f_R,n,p) \), there exists a \(k\)-Veto voting system that is ex ante efficient. Here \(f_Q\) and \(f_R\) are the two signal likelihood ratios, \(n\) is the odd group size, and \(p\) is the vector of private-value probabilities. The result is proved for the paper’s baseline homogeneous-cautiousness model, with admissible mechanisms required to respect the veto constraint. The proof uses Lemma 1 to select a quorum \(k\) inducing pure signal-revealing behaviour, so that the resulting voting rule implements the efficient mechanism \(M^*\).

The corresponding problem is Continuous Efficient Quorum Design:

Given rational class masses \( \rho_j \), private-value probabilities \(p_j\), cautiousness parameters \(c_j=1/2\), signal likelihoods, and a prior over \(q_Q,q_R\), find a quorum fraction \( \kappa \) and a limit equilibrium strategy \( \sigma \) for the replicated finite societies such that the induced continuum mechanism maximizes aggregate ex ante utility among admissible mechanisms. Return \( \kappa \), the type strategies, and the optimal value; return “none” if no quorum limit implements the optimum.

This is recognizably the same problem: which approval threshold should a large decision body use when every member retains veto power, signals are private, and the rule must aggregate information without violating participation? The continuous object is the population distribution over repeated voter classes, not a fractional outcome.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A in the homogeneous finite-support regime. The quorum is one scalar, the signal space is binary, and the paper’s own proof identifies the relevant posterior thresholds. A natural computational programme would ask for an exact algorithm in \(J\) and the input bit length, approximation guarantees for irrational masses, and convergence rates between the finite-\(N\) optimum and the continuum optimum. With arbitrary heterogeneous cautiousness and equilibrium selection, continuum-specific difficulty could arise.

A useful secondary anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here. It states that, for every \( (f_Q,f_R,n,p) \), Veto interim-dominates—and therefore ex ante-dominates—Unanimity, strictly unless

\[ f_Q\ge (f_R)^{n-1}. \]

The comparison is made with the paper’s equilibrium notion: there is an equilibrium under Veto that gives every agent at least as much interim utility as every equilibrium under Unanimity, with strict improvement for some type except in the stated case.

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Veto-Dominance:

Given a finite-support population distribution and signal structure, compare the limiting information-aggregation equilibrium of majority-quorum Veto with every limiting equilibrium of Unanimity. Decide whether every positive-mass type receives weakly higher interim utility under Veto, and whether the inequality is strict for at least one type. The output is the dominance verdict, the minimum interim-utility gap, and a witnessing equilibrium-limit strategy.

This should also be tractable for homogeneous finite-support populations: the paper already gives the finite equilibrium structure through Proposition 6, and the large-population limit can be expressed through type masses and likelihood ratios. A natural extension would compute finite-\(N\) error bounds or the smallest population size at which the continuous dominance gap exceeds a prescribed \( \varepsilon \).

The paper’s Propositions 3–5 strengthen the regime justification: Veto is asymptotically optimal, remains so with heterogeneous cautiousness, and for sufficiently large \(n\) interim-dominates Unanimity. These are not additional anchors I would use, because they are limit theorems rather than computational results, but they make the high-multiplicity interpretation especially defensible. They show that the authors themselves regard large populations with repeated information and preference types as a meaningful limiting object.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper already studies asymptotics and does not contain a named computational theorem. Moreover, individual veto power is singular under continuization, so the mirror must explicitly retain a veto flag or be defined through finite replicated equilibria. If the programme’s anchor rule is enforced literally, this paper should not receive a fully positive computational-mirror verdict. If welfare and equilibrium theorems are accepted as anchors, however, Continuous Efficient Quorum Design is a precise, plausible, and potentially tractable continuous-population problem directly descended from Theorem 2.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is stronger than the proponent admits: under ChoCo’s stated gate, this paper has no eligible anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are equilibrium and welfare results, not results about complexity, algorithms, approximation, or computation. “Find a quorum implementing the efficient mechanism” is a newly invented computational problem, not a computational problem established by the paper. The paper’s numerical grid and asymptotic arguments do not change that.

Theorem 2 is nevertheless the strongest rescue, so it is worth testing carefully. Its content depends essentially on finite-agent pivotality. An agent’s choice among \(r,q,v\) matters because it can change the probability of being pivotal, and the proof compares the expected payoffs of those pivotal events. In an atomless society represented only by masses, one agent’s action changes no mass. If the rule depends on \(\mu_r\) and \(\mu_v\), then an individual cannot alter the outcome by switching actions. The strategic problem that Theorem 2 solves has disappeared; arbitrary action assignments become compatible with the same aggregate outcome.

The proposed Boolean veto flag does not repair this. If the flag is triggered by a positive mass of vetoes, it is no longer individual veto power: it is a mass threshold, and private-value agents no longer have the paper’s guarantee that one of them can enforce \(Q\). If the flag is triggered by even one measure-zero agent, it is not a function of the population distribution \(\mu\); it reintroduces identity-sensitive information outside the continuous society. A finite replicated-game limit can retain that flag, but then the object is a sequence of finite Bayesian games together with an equilibrium-selection rule, not a standalone continuous-population problem.

That limit is also singular. If the continuum contains positive mass of private-value agents, then finite replications contain at least one such agent with probability tending to one, so the status quo is enforced almost surely. If private-value mass is zero, the aggregate signal frequencies identify the state in the usual law-of-large-numbers limit. The limit may be mathematically meaningful, but the paper’s individual veto and pivotality mechanism is no longer the object doing the work. Studying convergence rates for the finite games would be a new asymptotic game-theory project, not a continuization of a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 1 fares no better. A type-level version of Veto interim-dominating Unanimity can certainly be written, but it has the same fork. In the ordinary atomless model, individual actions are non-pivotal, so the strategic distinction between the rules is lost and the strict interim comparison has no natural basis. In a finite-replication model, the answer depends on which equilibrium branch is selected and on how \(k\), \(n\), and the signal parameters approach their limits. The proponent’s “limit equilibrium” is therefore an additional modelling choice, not something supplied by Theorem 1. Asking for the minimum utility gap is a new equilibrium-comparison problem with no computational theorem behind it.

Theorem 2 can also be converted into a mass-veto rule or a quorum-design problem, but that changes the institution. The paper studies a quorum combined with veto power held by each individual. A positive veto-mass threshold instead studies a different collective rule. Keeping finitely many veto-holders as atoms makes the continuous population irrelevant to the decisive part of the mechanism. Allowing a genuinely continuous distribution of cautiousness introduces a new representation problem for the distribution and its equilibrium, outside the paper’s finite-type model.

Propositions 3–5 do support the claim that large repeated populations are a sensible regime; I would not misuse them as a novelty collision. But they provide evidence for high multiplicity, not a missing computational question. They make the proposed mirror more plausible as asymptotic economics, while doing nothing to supply the computational anchor ChoCo requires.

The honest weakness is that a mathematically interesting continuous study could be built here. A large committee with repeated signal and preference classes is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and finite-to-continuum convergence might be worth analysing. But that would be a new computational programme about limiting Bayesian voting games. It would not mirror a named computational result of this paper, and preserving the paper’s individual veto mechanism either destroys the atomless model or imports a finite identity-sensitive object back into it. Under the programme’s standard, that is a negative verdict.
