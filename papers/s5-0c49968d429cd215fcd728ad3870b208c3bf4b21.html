The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow. Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, says that for its three-way assortative matching process with threshold \(k\), the finite-state Markov chain has a unique stationary distribution. Its proof uses irreducibility, a positive probability of staying in each state, and finiteness. The paper then works out the distribution for \(k=2\) and reports solving it for larger \(k\). This is the closest usable anchor, but it is not a named complexity result: the paper gives no result about polynomial time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. If the anchor rule requires one of those, the paper has no qualifying anchor.

My lead candidate is Three-Way Assortative Fluid Stationary Law. Take a large healthcare or hiring market with three roles and many agents of each role. An agent’s type is their role and whether they are high or low type; within a role and skill class, agents are interchangeable. Each period, \(N\) triplets arrive, with one agent of each role per triplet. Each is high type independently with probability \(p\), so there are only eight possible triplet compositions, with mass approaching \(\rho_a=p^{h(a)}(1-p)^{3-h(a)}\) for composition \(a\), where \(h(a)\) counts its high types. The regime is \(N\gg 8\). Scale the paper’s high-type threshold to \(k_N=\lfloor \kappa N\rfloor\), and normalize queued counts by \(N\). The continuous state lies on \(\mathcal S_\kappa=\{x\in[0,\kappa]^3:\min_i x_i=0\}\), the scaled version of the three surfaces in the paper’s state space.

The matching rule is the paper’s threshold and high-type priority rule, applied sequentially to the triplets in each period. The continuous object is the fluid limit of this process as \(N\) grows; time can remain discrete. The problem is: given rational \(p\), \(\kappa\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), determine whether the resulting fluid map \(F_{p,\kappa}\) has a unique invariant probability measure and, if so, return a finitely supported rational measure \(\hat\pi\) on \(\mathcal S_\kappa\) with \(W_1(\hat\pi,\pi)\le\varepsilon\), where \(\pi=(F_{p,\kappa})_\#\pi\). The action is fixed by the matching rule; there is no optimization objective in this anchor. The requested computation is the long-run distribution of normalized unmatched mass, not a fractional matching or an outcome-space relaxation.

This preserves the paper’s question about stationary behavior while making the *population* continuous. I would tentatively expect the approximation task to be tractable if the fluid dynamics are piecewise simple in this low-dimensional state space and have a quantitative convergence bound. The finite-chain theorem itself establishes neither condition. Further questions are whether the fluid map exists under the paper’s precise matching rule, whether \(\pi_N\) converges to its invariant measure, and at what rate.

The weakest point is substantial: scaling the arrivals and threshold changes the regime, and the randomness of individual triplet arrivals washes out in the fluid limit. The theorem’s uniqueness for every finite \(k\) does not imply a unique fluid invariant measure. Also, the paper’s transition and matching descriptions would need a fully specified tie-breaking rule before the fluid map could be handed to a prover. Authors might recognize this as a continuous-population analogue of their matching process, but they could reasonably regard it as a new fluid-limit analysis rather than a computational question already present in their paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole strong anchor is Theorem 3.1: for each fixed threshold, the three way assortative process has a unique stationary distribution. That is an existence and uniqueness result for a finite Markov chain. The paper states no complexity bound, algorithm, hardness result, or approximation guarantee. Its later statement that programming was used to solve the stationary equations does not supply one. The proponent recognizes this gap, but their proposed task—decide uniqueness for a fluid map and approximate its invariant measure in Wasserstein distance—is a new computational problem, not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed fluid model also needs a substantive change to the process. With one arriving triplet per period and \(k_N=\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor\), a normalized state changes by at most \(O(1/N)\) per period. A nontrivial path limit therefore needs an additional time scaling; depending on the drift, it may be a diffusion rather than the deterministic map the proponent proposes. Batching \(N\) triplets into each period gives a plausible fluid model, but changes the arrival regime. Either route needs new limit and stationary convergence analysis. The finite chain’s uniqueness does not establish uniqueness for the limiting object, nor that its stationary laws converge to it.

The best repaired mirror would define a high volume arrival regime precisely, derive its fluid or diffusion limit, and ask for an algorithm to approximate that limit’s stationary law. That could be a worthwhile applied probability problem. It still takes the paper’s stationary behavior and adds an algorithmic question the paper never poses. Optimizing thresholds is a weaker repair: the three way model fixes \(k\), and does not define a welfare objective for choosing it. The optimal threshold result in the two way section is attributed to prior work, not established for the paper’s three way model.

The negative case is weakest on whether the regime makes sense: a market with many interchangeable agents in each role and skill class is a credible high multiplicity setting, and type-level masses are natural. The case rests on the narrower point that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. If the programme accepts an analyst-created complexity problem built from a paper’s dynamics, the stationary-law proposal remains live; on the stated anchor standard, it does not qualify.
