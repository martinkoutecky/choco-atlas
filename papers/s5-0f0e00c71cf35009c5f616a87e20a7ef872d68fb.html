What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror is anchored in a named computational result; the paper’s convergence and regularity results are not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying anchor in this paper. The numbered Theorem 1 states weak convergence of empirical measures to the underlying distribution; Lemma 1 establishes regularity properties of Walrasian demand correspondences. Neither studies the computational complexity of a problem.

The paper’s central claim in §8.7 is that its 22-step mechanism implements Walrasian equilibrium in a subgame-perfect Bayesian equilibrium with truthful reporting and compliant supply strategies. That is a substantive result, but it is unnumbered and concerns mechanism design, not computational complexity. The paper also already models a continuum of potential traders.

A computational study of how to calculate or execute that mechanism for a finite-support distribution of trader types could be a plausible adjacent project. It would need its own precise input and computational question, though; it cannot serve as a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no named computational result here to continuize. Theorem 1 is a convergence result for empirical measures; Lemma 1 establishes regularity of demand correspondences. The central claim in §8.7 is an equilibrium and incentive-compatibility result. None asks for, or establishes, the complexity of a computational problem. That leaves no result for a ChoCo mirror to take as its anchor.

The most charitable computational proposal would be to ask how hard it is to calculate or execute the mechanism for a society with finitely many preference-and-endowment types. But the mechanism already represents society through distributions over trader types and demand strategies. So this would not continuize the population; it would add a computational question to an already continuous model. It would also need substantial new choices: a finite encoding for utilities and demand correspondences, a computational model for the market equilibrator, and a specified equilibrium-selection and approximation rule. Those choices could define an interesting project, but the paper does not supply a particular computational problem whose continuous mirror they would be.

One could instead study finite-population approximations to the mechanism. The paper’s empirical-measure convergence gives that idea some footing, but a computational study would still need to introduce a target algorithmic task, an input representation, and an error criterion. It would be a new approximation project built around the paper, not a mirror of a computational result in it.

So the negative case is strong as a verdict about *this paper’s mirror*: there is no computational result to mirror, and its main mechanism already uses a continuous population model. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile computational project could arise in any related scenario. Such a project is conceivable; the paper simply does not furnish the anchor for calling it a continuous mirror.
