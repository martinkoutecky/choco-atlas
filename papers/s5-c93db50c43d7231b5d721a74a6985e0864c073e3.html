The continuous mirror question

Given rational feature vectors \(x_t \in \mathbb{Q}^d\) with population masses \(\mu_t \ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and fixed \(m \ge 3\), \(p \ge 2\), choose \(\lambda_S \ge 0\) for each multiset \(S\) of \(m\) types, subject to \(\sum_S a_t(S)\lambda_S=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), to maximize \(\sum_S \lambda_S \sum_{\{r,s\}\subseteq S}\|x_r-x_s\|_p^p\).

The model it lives in

Types are recurring feature vectors \(x_t\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\). The decision variable \(\lambda_S\) is the mass assigned to groups with multiset composition \(S\); each group has \(m\) members and its cost is the sum of within-group pairwise \(p\)-distances.

The objection that survived

Related multi-marginal optimal-transport work already studies closely related coupling formulations and algorithms, limiting the mirror's novelty; the proponent does not address that overlap, though it does not undermine the question's legitimacy.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(p\)Max-\(m\)DWM hardness result in Theorem 4 for repeated feature profiles. Theorem 5's minimizing analogue collapses, and the paper's stable-roommate results are outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(m\) and support size \(\tau\), how does the continuous optimum's additive gap from the best discrete grouping scale with cohort size?
  • What is the complexity when \(m\) is part of the input, or when the type-composition LP is represented implicitly?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s hardness result for equal-sized clustering. Take a large school or employer forming teams of a fixed size from recurring skill profiles. A type is an exact feature vector \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), complete for the clustering objective: people with the same vector have the same cost to every other type. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) profiles. In a large cohort, \(n\) can be much greater than \(\tau\). This is especially plausible for standardized profiles with many repeated values; the paper itself points to forming student groups as an application.

The lead anchor is Theorem 5 (Kondor [2022]), reported in this paper but credited to that cited work rather than proved here. It says that, for every fixed \(m\ge3\) and \(p\ge1\), the \(p\)Min-\(m\)DWM optimization problem is NP-hard: given \(n\) points \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathbb{R}^d\), with \(m\mid n\), partition them into groups of size \(m\) to minimize the sum of within-group pairwise \(p\)-distances, \(\sum_j\sum_{\{a,b\}\subseteq C_j}\|x_a-x_b\|_p^p\). The dimension \(d\) is unrestricted. In particular, \(p=2\) covers the paper’s balanced minimum sum-of-squares clustering case.

Here is a continuous version of that same optimization problem. For each multiset \(S\) of \(m\) types, let \(a_t(S)\) be the number of members of type \(t\) in \(S\), and let \(c_p(S)=\sum_{\{r,s\}\subseteq S}\|x_r-x_s\|_p^p\), counting repeated types in separate group slots. Choose \(\lambda_S\ge0\), the mass of groups with composition \(S\), subject to \(\sum_S a_t(S)\lambda_S=\mu_t\) for every \(t\). The Continuous Minimum \(p\)-Distance Grouping problem asks for a feasible \(\lambda\) minimizing \(\sum_S\lambda_S c_p(S)\). Each group still has exactly \(m\) members; the population is continuous because mass of a type can be distributed among group compositions. For a discrete population, any partition gives this model with \(\lambda_S\) equal to the number of such groups divided by \(n\); the mirror allows these normalized group counts to be real.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A) for fixed \(m,p\). There are \(\binom{\tau+m-1}{m}=O(\tau^m)\) group compositions, so the stated problem is a linear program of polynomial size in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. It preserves the paper’s distances, group size, and objective. The change is the one made by continuizing the population: aggregate mass can be split across group compositions. The natural follow-up questions are when the optimum is integral, how large the discrete-to-continuous objective gap can be, and how well the continuous solution rounds as cohort size grows.

A second, related anchor is Theorem 4 (Kondor [2022]), also reported here and credited to that work rather than proved in this paper. For every fixed \(m\ge3\) and \(p\ge2\), it says that \(p\)Max-\(m\)DWM is NP-hard: for the same input points and equal-size partition requirement, maximizing the same sum of within-group pairwise \(p\)-distances is NP-hard. Its mirror, Continuous Maximum \(p\)-Distance Grouping, uses the same types, masses, feasible \(\lambda\), and costs, but maximizes \(\sum_S\lambda_Sc_p(S)\). I expect this too to be Class A by the same finite LP formulation. Here the plausible application is forming deliberately diverse teams from recurring profiles. Further questions include the integrality gap and rounding quality for the maximizing objective, and whether useful guarantees hold when \(m\) is not fixed.

These are good mirrors of the selected results because the paper already formulates weighted matching and clustering as ways to form groups of size at least three, and explicitly discusses both similar and varied student groups. The continuous questions retain those groups and the original distance objective. The scope is the higher-dimensional weighted grouping and clustering hardness results; it does not extend to the paper’s stable-roommate existence and stability-complexity results.

The weakest point is that this scenario requires repeated complete feature profiles. The paper’s hardness results allow arbitrary point sets, potentially with nearly every point distinct, and do not establish that their hard instances have the repeated-profile structure of a large cohort. So this is a credible mirror for a specific high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that every instance covered by the theorems has a useful population interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5’s minimum-distance anchor collapses in the proposed continuous model. For each type \(t\), let \(t^m\) be the group of \(m\) copies of that type and set \(\lambda_{t^m}=\mu_t/m\). These group masses use exactly all of \(\mu\), and every group has cost zero. Since distances are nonnegative, the optimum is always zero. The same collapse holds for an atomless distribution: the diagonal coupling \((x,\ldots,x)\) has every marginal equal to \(\mu\) and zero pairwise cost. The discrete problem’s interesting remainder is caused by indivisible counts; with fixed support, those remainders are negligible as the cohort grows. Avoiding the collapse would require an extra restriction, such as banning same-type groups or imposing diversity quotas, which changes the clustering problem.

That defeats both the \(p\)Min-\(m\)DWM anchor and its balanced MSSC special case. It does not defeat Theorem 4’s maximizing anchor. Its type-mass formulation is a genuine continuous question: it is equivalent, up to a factor of \(m\), to maximizing a pairwise-distance cost over symmetric \(m\)-way couplings with all marginals equal to \(\mu\). Multi-marginal optimal transport already studies this continuous coupling model and its computational complexity, including LP formulations and algorithms ([Altschuler and Boix-Adserà](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-022-01868-7), [Lin et al.](https://www.jmlr.org/beta/papers/v23/19-843.html)). That makes the mirror less novel as optimization, but under the programme’s stated rule, outside work does not erase a well-posed mirror when the paper itself remains discrete. And the fixed-\(m\) LP is an immediate tractability result, which still fits the programme.

So the negative case is strong against the minimizing anchor, but weak against the maximizing one. I can’t honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: Theorem 4 survives as a credible population-continuous problem.
