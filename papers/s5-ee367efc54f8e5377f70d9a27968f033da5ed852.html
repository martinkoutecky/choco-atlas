What the mirror covers

Propositions 1–4 provide structural characterizations; Proposition 5 gives the reserve-and-quota policy guarantee. No continuous computational question is established for any of them.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Propositions 1–4 establish structural facts about \(r\)-targeting diversity and the selection frontier; they do not assert a complexity classification or analyze an algorithm’s running time.

The closest result is Proposition 5, proved here. It shows that the reserve-and-quota policy selects a group whose type distribution is maximally \(r\)-diverse, and that every alternative group is either strictly less \(r\)-diverse or priority-dominated by the policy’s choice. That is an implementation and optimality result, not a result that computing the choice is in P or is hard.

A continuous type-mass version of that selection problem might be a plausible new research question, but it would not mirror a named computational result of this paper. Under the requested anchor rule, there are no anchors—and therefore no anchor-based continuous problems to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no qualifying computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–4 characterize a diversity order and its finite selection frontier; Proposition 5 proves that the reserve-and-quota policy is diverse and merit-optimal. None gives a complexity classification, an algorithmic guarantee, or a running-time result.

The best computational reformulation would ask: given \(x,q,r\), and the priority order \(P\), how efficiently can we compute \(\mathcal{F}_r(x,q)\), its reserve and quota bounds, or the policy’s selected group? That could be a useful new question, but the paper does not study it. Turning a structural theorem into a compute-this problem does not create a named computational result for this programme to mirror.

There is also a fidelity problem with a population-mass version of Proposition 5. Its merit guarantee depends on the strict ranking of named agents. Preserve that information in the type, and each agent’s distinct rank can force a distinct type, so support grows with the population. Aggregate agents by diversity category instead, and the model loses the priority comparisons needed for the theorem. A plausible repair is to represent applicants by category and merit score, then select the highest-scoring mass subject to diversity constraints. But that replaces the paper’s strict order over individuals with a score distribution and a mass-selection rule. It is a recognizable extension of its setting, not a faithful continuous mirror of its result.

The paper’s motivating cases include small committees, where many agents per complete type are not a natural assumption. Large-scale hiring or admissions could support a mass model, but it does not resolve the priority issue. So I would reject a continuous mirror *of this paper’s computational results*: there is no computational anchor, and the strongest candidate cannot retain both high multiplicity and its merit semantics. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous extension exists in any scenario is weaker; a score-distribution model could be worth studying, but this paper does not establish it as a mirror.
