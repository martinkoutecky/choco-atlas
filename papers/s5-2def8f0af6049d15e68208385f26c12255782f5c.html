The continuous mirror question

Given finite firm and worker type sets \(P,Q\), rational type masses \(\alpha_p,\beta_q\) in compatible market units, integer capacities \(r_p\), nonnegative surpluses \(a_{pq}\), and a rational linear objective on type payoffs \((\pi,w)\), compute an optimal payoff in the type-anonymous continuous core and a supporting aggregate assignment of firm mass to integral worker bundles.

The model it lives in

A firm of type \(p\) has mass \(\alpha_p\), capacity \(r_p\), and surplus \(a_{pq}\) for each worker of type \(q\), whose mass is \(\beta_q\). Let \(k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{Q}\) range over bundles with \(\sum_q k_q\le r_p\), and let \(\lambda_{p,k}\ge 0\) be the mass of type-\(p\) firms assigned bundle \(k\); impose \(\sum_k\lambda_{p,k}=\alpha_p\) and \(\sum_{p,k}k_q\lambda_{p,k}\le\beta_q\). The type-anonymous core uses per-type payoffs \(\pi_p,w_q\ge0\), with \(\pi_p+\sum_q k_qw_q\ge\sum_q k_qa_{pq}\) for every \(p,k\), and total payoffs \(\sum_p\alpha_p\pi_p+\sum_q\beta_qw_q\) equal to the maximum aggregate surplus. The task is exact optimization of a supplied linear objective over these payoffs, with a supporting feasible \(\lambda\).

The objection that survived

One wage per worker type captures only the symmetric slice of finite cores; Proposition 2 permits unequal salaries for otherwise identical workers, so the proposed mirror does not establish preservation of the full finite core.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers type-level core payoffs, competitive salaries, and their optimization. It leaves the kernel and bargaining-set separation, nucleolus, tau-value, manipulation results, and the reverse buyer-seller model outside this formulation.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact running time in \(|P|,|Q|\), the mass and value bit lengths, and the output representation?
  • Does a type-level min-max order capture every vertex of the continuous type-anonymous salary core when firm cohorts split across bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

My best case is narrow: this paper has no numbered NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem. It does, however, give named results used to compute the core and its extreme competitive salaries. Those support a real continuous-computation question, though not a claim that the paper has already classified its complexity.

A plausible regime is a large labor market with many repeated firm and worker profiles: for example, large employer cohorts with the same hiring capacity and valuations, and large worker cohorts with the same qualifications and reservation values. A type must include all the market data that matters: a firm’s capacity and values for every worker type, or a worker’s relevant values across firm types. The number of agents can be very large while the number of such types, \(\tau\), stays moderate.

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be the firm and worker types, with rational masses \(\alpha_p\) and \(\beta_q\), and let \(a_{pq}\) be their per-worker surplus and \(r_p\) a firm’s capacity. A bundle for a firm of type \(p\) is a vector \(k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{Q}\) with \(\sum_q k_q\le r_p\). Let \(\lambda_{p,k}\) be the mass of type-\(p\) firms choosing bundle \(k\). Then
\[ \sum_k\lambda_{p,k}=\alpha_p,\qquad \sum_{p,k}k_q\lambda_{p,k}\le\beta_q, \]
and total surplus is \(\sum_{p,k}\lambda_{p,k}\sum_q k_q a_{pq}\). Each firm still hires an integral bundle; it is the mass assigned to different bundles that can be fractional.

For the payoff question, I would use a type-anonymous market: every worker of type \(q\) receives the same wage \(w_q\), and every firm of type \(p\) receives the same payoff \(\pi_p\). The continuous core is the set of nonnegative \((\pi,w)\) satisfying
\[ \pi_p+\sum_q k_q w_q\ \ge\ \sum_q k_q a_{pq} \quad\text{for every firm type $p$ and bundle $k$,} \]
with total payoff equal to the maximum aggregate surplus. This is the dual of the bundle-allocation LP. It keeps the paper’s market and core question, while making mass, per-capita payoffs, and fractional aggregate assignments explicit.

The lead anchor is Theorem 16, proved in this paper. For any capacity-balanced many-to-one market and optimal matching, every extreme competitive salary vector belongs to the set of max-min salary vectors indexed by an order of the workers and a choice, at each position, to minimize or maximize that worker’s salary subject to the salaries already fixed. The paper’s Proposition 14, also proved there, supplies the converse filter: any such candidate that lies in the competitive-salary set is extreme. Together they give an exhaustive procedure: generate the order-and-choice candidates and retain the feasible ones. The theorem does not claim that this procedure is efficient.

Its continuous mirror is Type-Mass Extreme Salary Enumeration. The input is the rational type masses, capacities, and surplus matrix above. The question is to return every vertex of the worker-wage projection of the type-anonymous continuous core, exactly. A solution is the complete list of those wage vectors, with supporting core payoffs. I expect the underlying optimization to be tractable, but the full enumeration problem is open: the direct type-order analogue has \( |Q|!\,2^{|Q|}\) candidates, and may do much more work than the number of vertices requires. My working expectation is Class A in an output-sensitive sense; I would not claim polynomial delay from Theorem 16. The central follow-up is whether a sharper enumeration procedure exists, and whether a min-max order on types still captures every vertex when cohorts can split across firm types.

The other anchor is Proposition 2, derived in this paper from the core description; the paper notes its relationship to Sotomayor’s earlier characterization. For a capacity-balanced market and an optimal matching \(\mu\), it says that a payoff vector \((x,y)\) is in the core exactly when: (i) each worker \(j\) has \(0\le y_j\le a_{j^\mu j}\); (ii) for workers \(j,k\) assigned to different firms, \(y_k-y_j\ge a_{j^\mu k}-a_{j^\mu j}\); and (iii) each firm’s payoff is \(x_i=\sum_{j\in\mu(i)}(a_{ij}-y_j)\). Thus the full core reduces to salary constraints and determined firm payoffs.

Its mirror is Type-Mass Core Optimization. The input is a typed market as above plus a rational linear objective on \((\pi,w)\). The task is to return a core payoff maximizing that objective, together with a supporting aggregate assignment. This is an underlying market question, not a request merely to print an LP encoding. I expect Class A: the bundle inequalities have an efficient pricing check, since a firm’s most profitable bundle is found by selecting up to \(r_p\) workers with the greatest positive values of \(a_{pq}-w_q\). The aggregate assignment can likewise be optimized over finitely many types. A useful next result would give the precise runtime in \(|P|,|Q|\), the bit lengths of masses and values, and the output representation.

These anchors are computational characterizations, not complexity classifications. I see no basis for claiming hardness transfers or continuum-specific hardness from this paper. Conversely, the continuous questions are not collision claims: the society itself is now a distribution over types, and the object is the computational structure of its core. Fractional matching as an outcome is only the bookkeeping needed to aggregate a population whose individual workers and firm bundles remain discrete.

The strongest weakness is the type-anonymous wage assumption. In the finite paper, workers with identical input data can still receive different salaries in some core allocations, especially when they are hired by the same firm; Theorem 16’s vectors are indexed by named workers. Aggregating them to one wage per type can merge or discard extreme points. Standardized wage scales or cohort contracts make the mirror a plausible market regime, but the paper does not establish that its full set of extreme allocations survives that restriction. The next formal bridge should compare the continuous core with the type-anonymous slice of finite cores at masses \(n_t/n\), and determine which finite extreme salaries survive aggregation.

This case covers the paper’s core and competitive-salary results, especially its characterization and enumeration of extreme salary vectors. I would leave the kernel/bargaining-set separation, nucleolus, \(\tau\)-value, and manipulation results outside this mirror unless a separate type-mass question is formulated for them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper gives structural descriptions, not a complexity result. Proposition 2 gives a quadratic-size description of the finite market’s core, and Theorem 16 gives an exhaustive characterization of its extreme salary vectors. The latter’s order-and-choice procedure has \(n!2^n\) candidates; the theorem establishes completeness, not an efficient enumeration algorithm. So neither result itself points to a difficult algorithmic bottleneck that continuization might resolve.

The difficulty with mirroring Theorem 16 is that its vertices are vectors of salaries for named workers. If identical workers are assigned one wage per type, the model keeps only the type-anonymous slice of the finite core. That slice can discard asymmetric extreme allocations: averaging over permutations of identical workers preserves core feasibility, but the average need not be extreme. If instead the mirror keeps all individual wage differences, the wage distribution becomes part of the state, and the number of represented categories can grow with the population.

The better mirror is to ask for every vertex of the type-anonymous continuous core. That is a legitimate question, but it is no longer a direct lift of Theorem 16: the finite proof uses a fixed optimal matching and constraints between individual workers, while a type’s mass can be allocated across several firm types. An order on worker types therefore does not inherit the theorem’s completeness argument. One can enumerate candidate orders over the types, but that gives \(q!2^q\) candidates for \(q\) worker types, with no output-sensitive bound from the paper. The mirror remains possible; this paper supplies little basis for expecting its extreme-point enumeration to be efficient.

Proposition 2 has a stronger mirror, and it is the hardest anchor to defeat. For repeated firm and worker types, the aggregate market can be written as a configuration LP: each firm type chooses integral worker bundles, and mass is assigned across those bundles. Its dual uses one payoff per firm type and one wage per worker type. Pricing a bundle for firm type \(p\) amounts to selecting up to \(r_p\) workers with the largest positive values of \(a_{pq}-w_q\). Thus core optimization has a straightforward polynomial-time route in the number of types and the input bit lengths. This is a coherent large-market question, though its tractability appears to follow directly from standard LP and pricing machinery rather than from a new structure in this paper.

That leaves the negative case resting on a narrow standard for “worthwhile”: if it requires a new complexity phenomenon or a nontrivial algorithmic obstacle, neither anchor supplies one. The extreme-point question loses its direct connection to the theorem, and the core question looks like an immediate Class A configuration-LP application.

I cannot honestly make the universal case the prompt asks for. A market with large cohorts of firms and workers sharing complete types is plausible, and Proposition 2’s type-mass core question survives as a well-posed continuous-population computation problem. Its likely solution is easy, which weakens the case for prioritizing it, but under the programme’s stated scope an easy Class A result still counts. The negative case is strongest against Theorem 16 and weakest against Proposition 2.
