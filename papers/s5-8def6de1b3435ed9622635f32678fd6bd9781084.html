The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(K\), budget \(B\), and class valuation bounds \(\theta_j\), an instance is an ordered finite stream of cohorts \((q_s,j_s,v_s,r_s)\), where \(q_s\) is positive rational mass and each cohort profile is \((j_s,v_s,r_s)\). The policy learns each cohort only on arrival, chooses a per-unit allocation \(x_s\in[0,r_s]\) irrevocably, and must satisfy \(\sum_s q_s x_s\le B\). Its group utility is \(U_j(x)=\sum_{s:j_s=j}q_s v_s x_s\). Characterize the Pareto frontier of achievable pairs \((\alpha,\beta)\), where \(\alpha\) is the worst-case ratio of offline optimal total utility to the policy’s total utility, and \(\beta\) bounds \(\frac{1}{K}\sum_j U_j(w)/U_j(x)\) for every feasible alternative \(w\) on every completed stream. Determine the minimum feasible \(\beta\), the minimum \(\alpha\) for each feasible \(\beta\), and policies attaining the frontier, including how the answers depend on finite profile support.

The model it lives in

An ordered stream of atomless cohorts with profiles \((j,v,r)\) and rational masses \(q\). Each revealed cohort receives a per-unit allocation \(x\), consuming \(qx\) resource and adding \(qvx\) to its group utility. The fixed budget is \(B\); future cohorts and the stopping point are unknown. The completed profile masses can be normalized to a distribution after the stream, while the order remains part of the online instance.

What the mirror covers

Covers the \(\beta\)-PF guarantee and fairness–competitiveness results in Theorems 3–6; leaves GFQ, \((\gamma,\beta)\)-fairness, and the caching experiments outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a fixed finite profile support of size \(\tau\), how do the optimal fairness guarantee and competitive frontier depend on \(\tau\) and the input bit length?
  • Do the paper’s bounds remain tight when cohort masses are arbitrary positive rationals and the policy observes each mass only upon arrival?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a fluid version of McORA’s online \(\beta\)-PF problem. One caveat up front: the paper has no named NP-hardness, FPT, or polynomial-time classification theorem. Its named results are about online competitive guarantees and lower bounds. Those are useful algorithmic anchors, but they do not by themselves establish the kind of computational-complexity landscape ChoCo usually seeks.

Consider a cloud provider receiving a large stream of small jobs. A job type is its complete profile \((j,v,r)\): its user group \(j\), value \(v\) per unit of resource, and allocation limit \(r\). A type’s jobs are indistinguishable for the model’s purposes. Suppose there are \(K\) groups and \(\tau\) profiles that occur, with many jobs per profile. At arrival step \(t\), a cohort of mass \(q_t\) and profile \((j_t,v_t,r_t)\) is revealed. The total number of steps and future mass are unknown. The provider immediately chooses a per-unit allocation \(x_t\in[0,r_t]\); the cohort consumes \(q_t x_t\) resource and contributes \(q_t v_t x_t\) utility to its group. With budget \(B\), feasibility requires \(\sum_t q_t x_t\le B\).

For a completed stream, the type distribution is the cohort mass aggregated by profile and normalized by total mass. The ordered cohort arrivals must also be part of the instance: the final distribution alone cannot express the online uncertainty the paper studies. The population is the continuous object here; the arrival order remains an input feature. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime when the number of jobs is far larger than the number of group, value, and capacity profiles. It also preserves the paper’s central choices and objective: irrevocable online resource allocation, group utilities, and fairness guarantees. Since utility and resource use are linear, allocating a common amount per unit of a homogeneous cohort represents any allocation with the same cohort average.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6, proved in the paper’s Appendix B.4. It states that for any \(\beta\ge \frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^K\alpha_j\), where \(\alpha_j=1+\ln\theta_j\), no \(\beta\)-PF online algorithm can have competitive ratio smaller than
\[ \alpha_K\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\alpha_j}{K\beta}\right)^{-1}. \]
The paper also proves that SAM-Threshold attains this bound, making the competitiveness–fairness trade-off Pareto-optimal. Here \(\beta\)-PF means that for every feasible alternative allocation \(w\), the algorithm’s allocation \(x\) satisfies \(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^K U_j(w)/U_j(x)\le\beta\). In the mirror, the question is: given \(K\), \(B\), group fluctuation bounds \(\theta_j\), and a requested \(\beta\), what is the smallest competitive ratio achievable by an online policy on every admissible mass-cohort stream, and what policy attains it? The expected answer is the same frontier, with the weighted SAM-Threshold policy attaining it. That is an exact online-performance claim, not an NP-hardness claim.

A second, cleaner anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here, in Appendix B.2. It states that no online McORA algorithm can guarantee \(\beta\)-PF for \(\beta<\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^K(1+\ln\theta_j)\). Its matching upper bound is Theorem 3, proved in Appendix B.1: U-Threshold is \(\beta\)-PF at \(\beta=\frac{1}{K}\sum_j\alpha_j\), using class-specific thresholds that are \(1\) up to utilization \(B/\sum_i\alpha_i\), then rise as \(\exp(K\beta u/B-1)\) up to \(B\alpha_j/\sum_i\alpha_i\). The corresponding mirror question is: what is the minimum \(\beta\) for which a deterministic online policy can guarantee this proportional-fairness condition for every mass-cohort stream, and can a policy attain it? The expected answer is the same \(\frac{1}{K}\sum_j(1+\ln\theta_j)\), attained by the fluid version of U-Threshold.

These are the best anchors in the paper for this case, though neither is a classical complexity result. Their lower bounds are based on adversarial arrivals with increasingly fine valuation levels and utilization arguments. Those arguments have a natural mass-stream interpretation; weighted threshold decisions can act on cohort mass instead of unit arrivals. For finite type support, a useful further question is whether the exact bound depends on the spacing of the valuation levels and converges to the paper’s logarithmic expression as the support is refined. Another is the running time, in \(K\), \(\tau\), and input bit length, for computing the optimal thresholds and frontier when the type support is explicitly given.

The mirror’s weakest point is that online order is indispensable. A static distribution \(\mu\) over profiles does not say which jobs have arrived when, so the authors would need to accept an ordered mass stream as part of the continuous model. There is also a genuine scope limitation: the paper already allows fractional resource allocations, and its theorems analyze online competitive performance rather than the computational complexity of a finite optimization problem. I would therefore present this as a credible continuous-population extension of their online guarantees—not as a direct contribution to the programme’s usual P-versus-hardness trichotomy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is a case about fit. The paper’s named results are competitive guarantees for online policies: Theorems 3 and 4 give the optimal \(\beta\)-PF guarantee, and Theorems 5 and 6 give the competitiveness–fairness frontier. None classifies running time or supplies an optimization problem whose tractability turns on a support-size parameter. A mass-stream version would still ask how well a policy performs against unknown arrivals and stopping time. Its input must retain the ordered stream of mass increments; the final type distribution alone cannot encode that uncertainty. The resulting question sits outside ChoCo’s main complexity agenda.

That case does not defeat the mirrors. A plausible model is an ordered stream of cohorts with type \((j,v,r)\) and mass \(q\). Allocating \(x\) per unit of a homogeneous cohort consumes \(qx\) resource and gives its group \(qvx\) utility. Repeated cloud jobs with shared profiles make this a credible population model. It preserves the paper’s decisions and fairness criteria, and the authors could reasonably recognize it as their problem.

For Theorems 3 and 4, the mirror asks for the smallest \(\beta\) that guarantees proportional fairness over every mass-cohort stream. The paper’s lower-bound streams already use increasingly fine valuation levels, and cohort mass can represent those arrivals. A fixed finite support of valuation types may change the exact bound, so a careful mirror would ask for the support-dependent guarantee in \(K\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length. That is still a legitimate question; the changed bound does not invalidate it.

Theorems 5 and 6 admit the same stronger mirror: ask for the best competitive ratio achievable by a policy satisfying a given fairness guarantee on every ordered mass stream. Rational cohort masses can be scaled to integer multiplicities, with the resource budget scaled accordingly, so the paper’s high-multiplicity connection applies directly. That scaling does not erase online order or stopping uncertainty. It does mean the mirror offers no evident new computational bottleneck: the paper’s results concern policy performance, and their threshold rules already operate on aggregate utilization.

So the honest negative case is that these anchors are poor fits for a programme centered on complexity classifications and optimization methods such as separation or column generation. The continuous stream does not expose that kind of problem. But I cannot make the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The population can coherently be continuized while preserving the online model, and both anchors survive that formulation. Treating their close connection to the discrete results as an objection would confuse a real high-multiplicity bridge with a novelty collision.
