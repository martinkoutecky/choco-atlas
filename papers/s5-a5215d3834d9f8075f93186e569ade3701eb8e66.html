The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) goods, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) normalized, monotone submodular valuation types, and per-good capacity \(1/\rho\), find a common price menu over integral bundles and masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) summing to \(\mu_t\) for each type. Require each type's assigned mass to use only bundles maximizing \(v_t(S)-p(S)\), and each good's total assigned mass to respect its capacity. Can a value-query algorithm achieve a \(\rho/10\)-approximation to the maximum welfare over all feasible mass assignments, with running time stated in \(m,\tau,L,\rho\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic posted-menu auction: types \(t\) carry masses \(\mu_t\), each agent receives an integral bundle \(S\), and \(x_{t,S}\) may split a type's mass among tied utility-maximizing choices. The common menu \(p(S)\) must respect per-good capacity \(1/\rho\); welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\), compared with the best feasible mass assignment.

The objection that survived

The theorem's \(n\) counts strategic bidders, while \(\rho\) sets inverse per-capita supply and \(\tau\) counts types; the proposed \(\rho=\tau\) regime has no direct parameter correspondence, and the common menu omits report-conditioned menus.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the combinatorial-auction setting of Theorem 3.1; it leaves the flexible combinatorial public-project result and the exact public-project truthful-in-expectation result unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial value-query approximation algorithm exist for the stated common-menu problem?
  • Can the structured-submenu lower-bound ideas survive when a type's mass can split across tied bundles?
  • How should the approximation guarantee depend on \(\rho\) and \(\tau\), given that neither is the theorem's strategic-bidder count \(n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is a high-multiplicity submodular auction. Imagine a large buyer population purchasing bundles from a catalog of \(m\) goods. Many buyers share the same submodular valuation: \(\mu_t\) is the fraction with valuation type \(t\), and the number of actual buyers is much larger than the support size \(\tau\). If each good has \(N/\rho\) copies for \(N\) buyers, its capacity in the normalized society is \(1/\rho\). Each buyer still receives an integral bundle; fractions describe how much of a type receives each bundle.

Here is the continuous problem I would give a prover. The input is \(M\), a capacity parameter \(\rho\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) normalized, monotone submodular valuation functions \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb R_+\), available through value oracles. Find a common menu of integral bundles and prices \(p(S)\), together with masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), such that
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}\le \frac1\rho. \]
Every type may be assigned only to bundles maximizing \(v_t(S)-p(S)\); mass may split across tied choices. The objective is to maximize
\[ W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S), \]
with approximation measured against the maximum welfare over all feasible mass assignments, whether or not they are induced by a truthful menu. Call this the High-Multiplicity Submodular Auction Menu Approximation problem. The menu is chosen from the society distribution; an individual has zero mass and cannot change that distribution by deviating. Choosing a utility-maximizing bundle from the posted menu therefore gives the truthful choice. A polynomial value-query algorithm for a \(\rho/10\)-approximation, with complexity stated in \(m,\tau,L,\rho\), would be a natural positive result to seek.

My anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It states that a deterministic truthful \(n/10\)-approximation mechanism for combinatorial auctions with normalized monotone submodular valuations needs at least
\[ \frac{e^{m/n^2}}{10n^2m^6} \]
value queries. In particular, when \(n=m^{1/2-\epsilon}\) for constant \(\epsilon>0\), the required number of queries is superpolynomial, despite the approximation factor being only \(n/10\). This is a value-query lower bound, not an NP-hardness claim. The theorem’s menu argument makes it a good anchor: the continuous question still concerns submodular valuations, integral bundles, truthful choice from prices, and the difficulty of finding a useful menu over the \(m\)-item space.

For the mirror, I expect hardness to transfer (Class B) when \(\rho=\tau=m^{1/2-\epsilon}\): the exponential menu search is over bundles of the \(m\) goods, and the population is represented by masses over valuation types. A concrete supporting regime has \(N\gg\tau\) buyers, roughly equal masses on \(\tau\) types, and \(N/\rho\) copies of each good. Its continuous society is the high-multiplicity version of a finite profile with one representative of each type and one copy of each good. The paper’s theorem does not prove the corresponding continuous lower bound. The questions it generates are whether its structured-submenu argument survives this replication, whether splitting a type’s mass across tied bundles enables a column-generation or LP algorithm, and how the query complexity changes when \(\tau\ll\rho\).

The weak point is that Theorem 3.1 allows bidder-specific menus conditioned on the other bidders’ reports, while this anonymous continuum market gives agents a common menu. Mass splitting can also make allocations more flexible. So this is a plausible, recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s auction problem, not a claimed transfer of its theorem. The co-scaling of item supply preserves scarcity per person, and the central computational object—the truthful menu over integral bundles—remains.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is a real computational result, and repeated buyers with the same complete submodular valuation are a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The objection is not that types suppress individuality. It is that the theorem’s strategic object changes at the continuum.

In the paper, bidder \(i\)’s menu is induced by the other bidders’ reports. Truthfulness must hold across every possible report \(v_i\), with \(v_{-i}\) fixed. The lower bound exploits that structure: it constructs a large, carefully priced submenu for a particular bidder, then makes finding that bidder’s preferred bundle require many value queries. In the proposed continuum, one buyer’s report leaves the population distribution unchanged. The common menu is chosen from \(\mu\), and each buyer simply selects a utility-maximizing bundle from it. That is a coherent price-mediated allocation problem, but the report-conditioned menu problem from Theorem 3.1 has disappeared. Its structured-submenu argument gives no reason to expect the same query barrier.

There is also a parameter mismatch in the proposed bridge. The theorem’s \(n\) is the number of strategic bidders, and its \(n/10\) approximation guarantee is tied to that count. The mirror sets \(\rho=\tau\), although \(\rho\) controls inverse item capacity and \(\tau\) counts valuation types. If instead \(\rho\) tracks the number \(N\) of buyers before the limit, the proposed capacity \(1/\rho\) per item tends to zero as \(N\) grows. Keeping per-capita supply positive requires scaling item supply with \(N\), which gives a sensible large market but changes the scarcity regime. Neither choice recovers the theorem’s parameters automatically.

A finite replicated market could preserve strategic influence: repeat each valuation type many times, scale item supply with the replication, and let menus depend on the other reports. That is a plausible model, yet taking its population limit returns to the common-menu problem. Keeping the market finite retains the paper’s strategic constraints but leaves a discrete population. A result for that replicated model might be valuable, though it would need its own formulation and lower bound; it does not follow from the stated continuous mirror.

So the best case against this anchor is that the proposed mirror replaces a truthful-mechanism query lower bound with a nonatomic menu-and-demand allocation problem. Its computational interest is possible, but it would need an independent case that the authors regard that market-design question as their problem. I cannot honestly make the universal negative airtight: the menu problem is coherent, and an author could reasonably find it worth studying.
