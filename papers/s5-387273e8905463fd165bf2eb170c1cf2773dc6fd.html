The case FOR (proponent)

The closest results are:

  • Theorem 1.1, proved here: the Quantum Gibbard–Satterthwaite Conjecture is false. The paper’s counterexample is QCV with the Natural Extension: it is quantum incentive compatible, onto alternatives, and not a quantum dictatorship.
  • Theorem 3.2, proved here: Quantum Condorcet Voting is quantum incentive compatible under the paper’s definitions of strong positive, strong negative, and weak quantum preference.
  • Theorem 2.1, cited from Sun et al. [48]: QCV satisfies sharp and unsharp unanimity and independence of irrelevant alternatives, while satisfying neither sharp nor unsharp dictatorship; it therefore bypasses their quantum analogue of Arrow’s theorem.

These are substantive mathematical results, but none is a computational-complexity result. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no continuous problem per anchor to state or classify.

A large electorate with many repeated quantum ballot types is an imaginable setting for a new extension. But turning the paper’s individual-voter manipulation question into a continuum question would require choices the paper does not make: arbitrary voter entanglement is not captured by a distribution over ballot types, and an individual deviation has zero mass in a nonatomic population. Giving a positive mass of voters the right to deviate instead asks about coalition manipulation. That could be a research lead, but it changes the problem and does not provide an honest continuous mirror of a named result here. The missing computational anchor is the decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the decisive eligibility problem: none of the three named results classifies a computational problem. Theorem 1.1 gives an axiomatic counterexample to the paper’s Quantum Gibbard–Satterthwaite conjecture; Theorem 3.2 proves a truthfulness property; Theorem 2.1, from earlier work, concerns Arrow-style axioms. None specifies an algorithmic problem, its input representation, or a complexity measure. A population distribution alone would not turn any of them into the kind of computational result ChoCo mirrors.

The best attempted mirror still runs into a semantic obstacle. These results define manipulation by a *single voter’s* alternate ballot. In an atomless society, changing one voter’s ballot changes no mass, so any rule determined by the population distribution is insensitive to that deviation. Letting a positive mass change ballots instead asks about a coalition. Adding one distinguished voter preserves unilateral influence only by restoring an atom and making that voter’s identity part of the model. Those are different strategic questions, not continuous versions of the paper’s individual-voter results.

The quantum model adds a further constraint: its input may be a joint state entangled across voters. A distribution over individual ballot types does not, in general, retain those correlations. Restricting to independent ballots could give a mass model, but narrows the input domain; encoding the full relational state in each voter’s type would defeat the repeated-type population model.

The strongest alternative is a new problem: for a restricted, independent-ballot version of QCV, find the minimum mass or cost of a coalition that changes the outcome. That could be worthwhile research, and might fit ChoCo as a bribery or robustness problem. But it adds a cost objective and coalition-deviation semantics that the named results do not study. It does not rescue a continuous mirror of Theorem 1.1 or 3.2; Theorem 2.1 is an even less direct anchor.

So none of the three anchors survives as a continuous *computational mirror*. The honest limit of this negative case is that “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” is too broad to establish: the coalition problem could be valuable in its own right. The supported conclusion is narrower: this paper offers no qualifying mirror of its results for ChoCo.
