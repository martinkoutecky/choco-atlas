The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), acceptable pairs \(E\), strict rankings of \(J\)-types for each \(i\), and rankings with ties of \(I\)-types for each \(j\), find in time polynomial in the input size a stable mass matrix \(x\) with \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\ge (9/13)\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\). Require \(x_{ij}\ge0\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\), and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\); for every acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(R_{ij}\) and \(S_{ij}\) be the masses on each side unmatched or assigned to strictly worse types, and require \(R_{ij}S_{ij}=0\), so no positive masses on both sides can block.

The model it lives in

An atomless population on each side is partitioned into finitely many types with common acceptability and rankings over partner types. A feasible mass matrix assigns each unit of population to at most one partner; its objective is total matched mass, and stability rules out any acceptable pair with positive mass on both sides preferring that pair to their assignments.

The objection that survived

The \(13/9\) charging argument uses individual proposal graphs, and the case supplies no mass analogue; this makes the tractability forecast speculative, though it leaves the continuous question intact.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the maximum stable-matching approximation guarantee, targeting \(13/9\) over type masses; it leaves the tightness example, proof-specific charging analysis, and cited prior hardness bounds unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the proposal algorithm’s finite-state structure be weighted by cohort mass while retaining the \(13/9\) approximation guarantee?
  • If not, is there another polynomial-time algorithm with that guarantee, or what approximation ratio is achievable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s maximum-cardinality stable matching problem with one-sided ties. Consider a large centralized placement round: there may be hundreds of thousands of applicants and individual placements, but only a few dozen recurring preference profiles on each side. A type is a cohort with the same acceptable partners and the same preferences over partner types; mass is the fraction, or normalized count, of that cohort. This is a credible regime for studying cohort-level placement outcomes, rather than a claim that every small matching instance has a useful continuum limit.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that Huang and Telikepalli’s algorithm returns a stable matching \(M\) whose size is within a factor \(13/9\) of a maximum-cardinality stable matching \(\mathrm{OPT}\), or equivalently \(|M|\ge 9|\mathrm{OPT}|/13\). The paper supplies the refined analysis; the algorithm itself is due to Huang and Telikepalli. The introduction mentions discrete NP-hardness as prior work, but that is not a numbered result of this paper, so I am not using it as an anchor. ([Theorem 2 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.05391))

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: \(13/9\)-Approximation for Continuous One-Sided-Ties Maximum Stable Matching. An instance gives finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\ge 0\), acceptable type pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\), strict rankings of acceptable \(J\)-types for each \(i\), and rankings of acceptable \(I\)-types for each \(j\), with ties permitted only on the \(J\)-side. A feasible solution is a nonnegative mass matrix \(x_{ij}\) on \(E\), with row sums at most \(\mu_i\) and column sums at most \(\nu_j\). Its size is \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\).

Stability means that no acceptable type pair has positive mass on both sides who would strictly prefer to match across that pair. Formally, let \(R_{ij}\) be the \(i\)-mass that is unmatched or matched to a type strictly worse than \(j\), and let \(S_{ij}\) be the \(j\)-mass that is unmatched or matched to a type strictly worse than \(i\), counting a tied tier as not strictly worse. Require \(R_{ij}S_{ij}=0\) for every \((i,j)\in E\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}\) be the maximum size of a stable mass matrix. The task is to compute a stable \(x\) with \(\sum x_{ij}\ge 9\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}/13\), in time polynomial in the numbers of types, acceptable pairs, and input bit length. The object being computed is the underlying stable mass matching, not an encoding.

I expect this approximation question to be tractable, even if exact optimization is harder. The paper’s proof is driven by the structure of stable matchings and augmenting paths, and its proposal algorithm already has a finite set of proposal states and bounded proposal capacity. A weighted version that moves cohort mass through analogous states is a concrete route to investigate. A central follow-up is whether that structure gives the same \(13/9\) guarantee directly for rational masses, or whether a better bound is possible. This mirrors the paper’s approximation result without claiming its proof already works in the continuum.

The weak point is that the paper ranks named individuals, while this mirror ranks cohorts. If identity-specific preferences matter, splitting cohorts into complete types may make the support large and remove the high-multiplicity advantage. And if members of a cohort are treated as interchangeable even by agents on the strict-preference side, the finite expansion can introduce ties there too. The mirror is strongest for placement settings where cohort identity is the meaningful preference unit and within-cohort distinctions have no role in stability; it is not a faithful compression of every one-sided-ties instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The \(13/9\) theorem is a genuine computational anchor, so the negative case cannot start by dismissing the paper as purely axiomatic. Its strongest objection is that the proposed type matrix may not preserve the preference information this particular problem uses. On the strict-preference side, agents rank *individual partners*. If several partners are collapsed into one cohort, the matrix \(x_{ij}\) forgets their different positions in those rankings; that can change which pairs block and which stable matchings exist. Keeping those distinctions may require types that grow with the population, undermining the small-support high-multiplicity premise.

The proponent’s better version—agents rank partner *categories*, with members of each category interchangeable—is coherent. But it changes the object from one-to-one matching among individually ranked people to matching mass across categories or capacities. That may be a worthwhile computational problem, but it does not preserve the paper’s named-person stability question or automatically mirror its \(13/9\) result. The paper’s proof relies on a graph of accepted proposals with degree at most two and charges among individual augmenting-path components; the category model has no immediate counterpart for those objects. The failure of that proof to transfer is not, by itself, a reason the new question is unworthy.

So this is a conditional case against the proposed mirror, not a convincing case against every scenario. If matching within cohorts is genuinely interchangeable, the authors might recognize the category-level problem as theirs. Closely related work already studies hard stable-matching variants with groups of similar agents and preferences over types; as the programme brief emphasizes, that kind of high-multiplicity work supports the regime rather than counting as a continuous collision. [Bredereck et al., “Solving hard stable matching problems involving groups of similar agents”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109). I therefore can’t honestly sustain the universal negative claim: the strongest objection is loss of individual-rank information in a faithful compression, and the cohort-level reformulation leaves a plausible mirror standing.
