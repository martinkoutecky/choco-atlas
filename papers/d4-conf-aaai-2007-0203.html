The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational complexity result: no numbered theorem, proposition, lemma, or corollary says a problem is tractable, hard, or belongs to a complexity class. The closest relevant results are semantic. Proposition 16, proved here, says that for any two interpretations, preference under every compatible scaling is equivalent to the paper’s Pareto-like preference: the interpretation being displaced must violate at least one base, and the preferred interpretation must be strictly better for every base it violates while both interpretations satisfy any base they both satisfy. Proposition 19, also proved here, says the Pareto fusion satisfies majority independence: repeating a collection of bases any number of times does not change the result. These are useful foundations for a mirror, but they are not eligible computational anchors under your criteria.

The strongest candidate I can make is Continuous Pareto-Fusion Membership. An instance consists of a finite propositional language, a constraint \(\chi\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete ranked-base types \(B_t\), with positive rational masses summing to one, plus a queried interpretation \(\omega\). The question is whether \(\omega\) is in the fusion result: whether \(\omega\models\chi\) and there is no \(\omega'\models\chi\) that Pareto-dominates it according to the ranking functions \(\kappa_{B_t}\). The solution is a yes/no answer. Equivalently, the full-fusion version returns exactly the constrained Pareto-minimal interpretations.

A plausible regime is a very large population of offices or deployed expert systems using a small number of shared ranked knowledge-base templates: many agents, few distinct types, and each type’s mass is its population fraction. The authors could recognize this as their fusion problem with repeated source types grouped together. Its expected classification is hardness transfers (Class B) if the finite-source version is hard: every finite multiset can be represented by rational masses over its distinct bases, with exactly the same fusion result. For the membership question, there is also a direct coNP upper bound: a counterexample to membership is a feasible interpretation that Pareto-dominates the query. The paper establishes neither hardness nor a complexity classification, however, so these are properties of the proposed problem, not results to attribute to the authors.

The soft spot is decisive: Proposition 19 makes the actual mass values irrelevant. Only which types have positive mass matters. So this is a clean high-multiplicity packaging of the paper’s semantics, but it offers no mass-driven relaxation or obvious continuous-optimization gain. It covers only computing the Pareto fusion, not the paper’s rational-postulate results. I would present it as a plausible mirror, while being explicit that it does not satisfy the requested named-computational-anchor test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is not that any result fails after continuization. It is that this paper’s fusion rule discards population proportions by design. None of its named results is a computational complexity result: Proposition 16 equates two semantic preference relations, and Proposition 19 establishes majority independence. The proposed membership problem is therefore a new computational question about the paper’s operator, not a computational result the paper already supplies for a continuous mirror.

Proposition 19 makes the membership proposal’s mass coordinates redundant. Representing \(\mu\) by its support gives the same fusion result for every assignment of positive rational masses to that support: repeating a base any number of times cannot affect the outcome. Proposition 16 reinforces the point: preference is determined by how each distinct base ranks interpretations, not by how many people hold that base. A query about whether \(\omega\) is Pareto-minimal may be worth studying, but it is a problem about distinct ranked views and propositional interpretations. The distribution’s fractions do no work. Calling the input a continuous society does not make the computation population-based.

There are conceivable ways to make the fractions matter, but each changes the paper’s central choice. A mass-weighted score would compare dissatisfaction across sources, despite the paper’s motivation being that their ranks are incommensurable. An \(\varepsilon\)-Pareto rule could allow some mass to lose in exchange for broad gains, but it would replace the paper’s protection of every distinct view with a tolerance for ignoring small groups. A robustness question based on moving mass between bases would require an intervention and a cost model the paper does not provide. These may be sensible new fusion problems; there is no evident reason the authors would regard one as the continuous version of this operator.

The negative case is strongest against a *continuous-population* mirror, not against studying the computational complexity of Pareto fusion. Many systems could share a ranked-base template, so a high-multiplicity setting is plausible. But majority independence already removes the multiplicities, and changing the fractions leaves the result fixed. That makes the proposed mirror a reasonable complexity question with a weak claim to ChoCo’s population-continuization programme. I cannot rule out the authors finding that support-based computation valuable; I can argue that its value would come from analyzing their logical fusion operator, not from making the society continuous.
