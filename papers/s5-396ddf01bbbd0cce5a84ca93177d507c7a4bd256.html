The case FOR (proponent)

Under the required anchor rule, I can’t make a fully compliant positive case: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. “Statement 1” gives necessary conditions for a Hamilton apportionment to fully favor small beneficiaries; “Statement 2” gives additional conditions. Neither classifies a computational problem. The paper does present the named AHS algorithm and examples, but its guarantee that it finds a solution when one exists is unnumbered, and the paper gives no running-time classification.

The strongest plausible mirror is the inverse problem behind AHS. Consider many identical beneficiary units in each of a small number of exact administrative classes—for example, standardized local service areas. Let \(T\) be the classes, \(\mu_t\) the fraction of beneficiaries in class \(t\), and \(a_t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}\) the desired number of indivisible entities per beneficiary. Set the per-beneficiary supply to \(r=\sum_t\mu_t a_t\). Given a gap threshold \(\delta\), ask for positive relative weights \(v_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t=1\), such that Hamilton apportionment with quotas \(q_t=rv_t\) gives every beneficiary of class \(t\) exactly \(a_t\) entities, while fully favoring smaller beneficiaries. A solution is the weights \(v_t\) and a Hamilton remainder ordering witnessing that allocation, with \(\frac{a_s}{v_s}-\frac{a_t}{v_t}\ge\delta\) whenever \(a_t>a_s\). This keeps the entities indivisible; mass represents repeated beneficiaries, and supply scales per beneficiary.

If treated as an exploratory mirror, I would expect this inverse problem to be tractable when the number of classes \(\tau\) is modest: AHS’s construction follows ordered allocation and remainder constraints, which may admit a continuous optimization formulation. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result proved by the paper. It raises the questions of whether the construction is polynomial in \(\tau\) and input bit length, whether fractional class masses change feasibility, and whether the discrete AHS guarantee survives as an exact continuous characterization.

The regime is plausible for large populations of standardized beneficiaries, with \(n\) much larger than \(\tau\). The weakest point is that AHS constructs beneficiary weights to realize a chosen apportionment; it does not study a fixed population distribution with a computational intervention over it. The proposed mirror preserves that inverse-design task, but a reviewer could reasonably question whether it is a continuous-population problem in ChoCo’s intended sense. And because the paper supplies no qualifying numbered anchor, this remains a candidate mirror rather than an anchored positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own caveat is the strongest point against a mirror: the paper has no numbered result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Statements 1 and 2 give conditions for full favoring, while AHS is presented as a construction algorithm with examples and simulations, not a running-time or complexity result. So there is no qualifying complexity result here for ChoCo to chart.

The proposed inverse problem is a plausible new question, but it does not preserve the paper’s input regime. AHS takes a strict sequence of beneficiary-specific allocations, \(x_1>\cdots>x_n\), and constructs corresponding weights. In that problem, the requested allocation distinguishes beneficiaries: grouping them into identical types either leaves \(\tau=n\), or requires changing the target to a common class allocation and generalizing the strict-sequence conditions. The proposed gap threshold and population-scaled supply add further choices. That may be a worthwhile extension, but it is not a direct continuous mirror of a complexity result in this paper.

The stronger cohort version also runs into Hamilton’s remainder rule. Members of a type have the same quota and remainder, so a remainder cutoff can split a cohort: some identical beneficiaries receive an extra entity and others do not. A continuum can represent that split as fractional mass, but then full favoring must be defined on type averages or allocation distributions rather than on each beneficiary’s integer allocation. Insisting on identical integral awards instead restricts the model to cases where each cohort is selected wholly or not at all. Either formulation may be studied, but each changes what the paper calls an apportionment and what its full-favoring condition evaluates.

There is a possible scaling that avoids the simplest degeneracy: grow the number of entities with the number of beneficiaries to keep supply per beneficiary positive. But it does not resolve the cohort and target-allocation issues. Conversely, keeping the paper’s total supply fixed as the population grows makes the share of beneficiaries receiving entities vanish, leaving little of its apportionment question in the limit.

So I would not count the proposed inverse problem as a surviving anchor for this paper. The honest limit of the negative case is that it cannot establish that *no* mass-based variant could interest the authors: a class-level inverse feasibility problem could be formulated. The stronger and better-supported conclusion is narrower: this paper offers no named computational-complexity result to mirror, and the best proposed continuization changes the instance structure and the property being tested.
