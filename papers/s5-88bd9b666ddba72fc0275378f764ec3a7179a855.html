The continuous mirror question

Given rational referee ability types \(\alpha_1<\cdots<\alpha_\tau\) in \([\ell,1]\), where \(\ell=3-\sqrt{7}\), rational masses \(\mu_i\ge 0\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and a fixed editor ability \(e\in[\ell,1]\), choose nonnegative pair masses \(y_{ij}\) for \(i\le j\). Require \(2y_{hh}+\sum_{i<h}y_{ih}+\sum_{j>h}y_{hj}=\mu_h\) for each \(h\). Maximize \(\sum_{i\le j}y_{ij}q(\alpha_i,\alpha_j,e)\), where \(q(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{32}\left(4(4+x+y)+\frac{4(x-y)^2}{(4-xy)z}+(4-xy)z\right)\). Return an optimal mass pairing \(y\); the total article mass is \(1/2\).

The model it lives in

A referee population of total mass \(1\), divided into ability types with masses \(\mu_i\), is assigned to article mass \(1/2\). Each article receives two referees and is evaluated by an editor of fixed ability \(e\). The variables \(y_{ij}\) give the article mass assigned to each referee-type pair, and the objective is total expected correct evaluations under the paper's reliability function \(q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 1's multi-article pairing objective and reverse-order optimum. It leaves aside Theorem 1's single-jury role choice, the numerical larger-jury results, and the broader sequential-scheme discussion.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the reverse-order result extend to arbitrary rational cohort masses as an optimal anti-monotone mass pairing, and what additive loss suffices to round an optimal mass solution to an integral referee assignment?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. Its named computational results are optimization theorems instead. The two anchors below are both proved in the paper; the continuous complexity claims are questions raised by the mirrors, not results the authors already establish.

The strongest anchor is Corollary 1. For an editor of ability \(e\in[\ell,1]\), where \(\ell=3-\sqrt7\), and \(n=2m\) referees with distinct abilities \(\ell\le a_1<\cdots<a_n\le1\), the paper considers pairing the referees to review \(m\) articles. It proves that the unique pairing maximizing the expected number of correctly evaluated articles pairs referee \(i\) with referee \(n-i+1\): the lowest-ability referee with the highest, the next-lowest with the next-highest, and so on. This is proved here, using the paper’s Proposition 2.

That suggests the Continuous Referee Pairing problem. Consider a high-volume review programme with article mass \(1/2\) and a normalized referee population of mass \(1\). The input is a finite support of ability types \(\alpha_1<\cdots<\alpha_\tau\) in \([\ell,1]\), with rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), and one editor type of ability \(e\in[\ell,1]\) for each article. A type completely specifies the referee’s ability and hence the paper’s signal distribution; agents of the same type are interchangeable. Choose nonnegative pairing masses \(y_{ij}\), for \(i\le j\), where \(y_{ij}\) is the mass of articles reviewed by types \(i,j\). Require \(2y_{ii}+\sum_{j<i}y_{ji}+\sum_{j>i}y_{ij}=\mu_i\) for each type \(i\). Maximize \(\sum_{i\le j}y_{ij}q(\alpha_i,\alpha_j,e)\), where \(q\) is the paper’s probability of a correct evaluation for two early referees and an editor with the casting vote. On this ability range the paper gives \(q(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{32}\left(4(4+x+y)+\frac{4(x-y)^2}{(4-xy)z}+(4-xy)z\right)\). A solution is the pairing-mass vector \(y\).

This is a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s referee-assignment problem. The article and review process are already the paper’s own example, and Corollary 1 itself studies assignments across many articles. A plausible regime is a large conference whose many referees fall into a few calibrated expertise cohorts; the paper notes that review systems already collect expertise ratings. There, the number of referees can be large while \(\tau\) remains small. A finite pairing maps directly to \(y\) by recording the fraction of articles assigned each type pair. The continuous problem is an LP with \(O(\tau^2)\) variables, so its expected direction is Class A, tractable. The paper’s reverse-order result also points to an anti-sorted mass pairing: pair the lowest remaining ability mass with the highest remaining ability mass, splitting cohorts as needed.

A second useful anchor is Theorem 1. It proves that, under the casting-vote scheme with three honest jurors, equiprobable states \(A\) and \(B\), and abilities \(a\le b\le c\), reliability is maximized when the median-ability juror \(b\) has the casting vote. The theorem and proof appear in this paper.

Its batch mirror is the Continuous Casting Assignment problem. Take a normalized population of jurors, with ability types \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), and \(3\)-person panels. Each type specifies the paper’s signal density \(f_{\alpha_i}(s)=(1+\alpha_i s)/2\) in state \(A\) and \(g_{\alpha_i}(s)=(1-\alpha_i s)/2\) in state \(B\); signals are independent, the prior is \(1/2\), and voting is honest as defined in the paper. Choose nonnegative masses \(x_{ijk}\) of panels with early-voter types \(i,j\) and casting-voter type \(k\). Require \(\sum_{i,j,k}(\mathbf 1_{i=h}+\mathbf 1_{j=h}+\mathbf 1_{k=h})x_{ijk}=\mu_h\) for every type \(h\). Thus the panel mass totals \(1/3\). Maximize \(\sum_{i,j,k}x_{ijk}R(\alpha_i,\alpha_j,\alpha_k)\), where \(R\) is the exact reliability under the paper’s signal and voting model. A solution is the allocation \(x\), including which type occupies the casting role.

This asks who should cast the vote across many independent three-person panels assembled from large juror cohorts. For any fixed triple, Theorem 1 says the best casting type is the median; the remaining decision is how to form panels from the available type masses. The formulation has \(O(\tau^3)\) variables and a linear objective with coefficients computed from the paper’s reliability model, so this mirror also points to Class A. Its further question is how well an optimal mass allocation can be rounded for a fixed finite batch when the cohort counts do not divide evenly.

Both mirrors continuize the population: a mass records a fraction of referees or jurors of a complete signal type. The paper’s signals are already continuous, but that is not the proposed continuization. The states and the voting outcomes stay binary; what becomes continuous is the population assigned across repeated decisions.

My weakest point is that Theorem 1 concerns role assignment within a fixed three-person jury, while Continuous Casting Assignment also chooses how to group jurors into panels. That extra allocation is a natural high-volume extension, but it is additional structure. The paper’s own multi-article referee assignment in Corollary 1 makes the batch setting easier to defend. More broadly, neither anchor is a hardness result, so these mirrors do not test whether population continuization removes discrete hardness. They do provide precise, plausible aggregate optimization questions, and the referee-pairing mirror is especially close to a problem the authors already study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that neither anchor is a computational-complexity result in the paper. Theorem 1 is an analytic result about role choice for one fixed trio. Corollary 1 proves a structural pairing rule; it does not analyze complexity or give an algorithmic treatment of populations grouped by type. The proposed mirrors add a mass-allocation layer, so they ask new questions rather than continue a computational analysis the paper began.

That objection is much weaker against Corollary 1 than it first appears. The paper already considers assigning referees across many articles, and a large review pool with repeated ability cohorts is a plausible high-multiplicity version of that setting. The finite theorem’s distinct abilities do not rule out cohorts in another instance regime. The continuous pairing question is therefore close to the paper’s own assignment problem, and I cannot make a sound case that no worthwhile mirror exists for it.

Theorem 1 is a better target for the negative case: a single three-person jury has no population axis to continuize while keeping the problem intact. But the proposed batch of panels is a credible extension, and the paper’s multi-article referee assignment makes repeated decisions a natural setting. The panel allocation adds structure, but it still uses the paper’s signal model, reliability objective, and casting role. That is enough for the authors plausibly to recognize it as their problem.

So the negative case is weak at its strongest point. Neither paper result supplies the complexity classification ChoCo seeks, but that does not invalidate a new computational mirror. The proposed formulations are well-posed, and the batch settings are plausible. The fact that their continuous versions may be readily expressible as optimization problems is not a reason to reject them; I cannot honestly defeat both anchors on the grounds given.
