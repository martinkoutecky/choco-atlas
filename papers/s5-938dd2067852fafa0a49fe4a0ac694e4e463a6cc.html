The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is a large electorate in which voters are grouped into cohorts with the same ideal policy \(x\) and the same private valuation \(s\in\{-d,d\}\) of candidate quality. A society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with the ideal policies approximating the uniform distribution and mass \(\rho\) on voters who favor candidate \(A\)’s image. Candidates choose platforms \(a,b\in[0,1]\); each type votes according to the paper’s utility rule, and each candidate’s payoff is whether their vote mass reaches a majority. One could ask for the pure or mixed Nash equilibria of this continuous population game. A plausible regime is many voters in a relatively small number \(\tau\) of policy-and-image cohorts.

The paper’s closest named result is Proposition 2, proved here: for \(0<d\le 1/4\), \(\rho\in[1/2+2d,1]\), and \(n\ge 1/[4d(2\rho-1)]\), the game has a unique Nash equilibrium. Candidate \(A\) chooses \(1/2\), while candidate \(B\) mixes equally between \(1/2-\sqrt{d(2\rho-1)}\) and \(1/2+\sqrt{d(2\rho-1)}\). That result makes a continuum-voter extension plausible, but it is an equilibrium characterization, not a computational complexity result. Proposition 1 and Proposition 3, and Lemmas 1 and 2, likewise characterize equilibria or best responses; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

So I cannot give a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem per anchor under the requested standard. There is also a modeling weakness in the proposed mirror: with the paper’s i.i.d. continuously distributed ideal points, exact voter types almost surely do not repeat. The cohort regime requires grouping or discretizing those ideal points, and the continuum removes the paper’s finite-\(n\) uncertainty about election outcomes. It is a possible extension, but not a strong, anchored positive case for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names Proposition 2, but it characterizes a Nash equilibrium; it does not state the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 1 and 3 and Lemmas 1 and 2 have the same character. There is no decision or optimization problem with an input encoding, output, and algorithmic complexity for a continuous version to mirror. Under ChoCo’s brief, that is the strongest objection: the paper offers equilibrium analysis, not a computational result about electoral intervention or winner robustness.

The proposed mass model is plausible as an analytical extension. But its direct question—what equilibria arise when voters are represented by a distribution over \((x,d)\)—continues the paper’s equilibrium analysis. To make it computational, one would need to choose a new task, such as deciding whether a pure equilibrium exists or computing an equilibrium from a rational finite-support electorate. That could be interesting on its own, but the paper neither poses nor motivates that computational problem. And a finite-support version discretizes the paper’s continuous ideal-point space; the natural distribution over ideal points has continuum support. Neither choice supplies the missing computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong against counting this as a ChoCo mirror, but it cannot establish the universal claim that no useful algorithmic follow-on exists. If equilibrium computation itself is within the programme’s intended scope, the proposed mass game remains a plausible starting point; the paper simply does not provide a named computational result for it to mirror.
