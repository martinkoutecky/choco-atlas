An unanchored positive candidate is still plausible. Consider a large regional carrier pool in which many carriers run recurring, standardized service patterns. A type would include the carrier’s depot, delivery requests, vehicle capacity, and cost data; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of carriers of type \(t\). For each carrier-type coalition pattern \(S\) of size two or three, let \(g(S)\) be the carriers’ separate routing costs minus their minimum joint routing cost. Let \(\lambda_S\) be the mass assigned to pattern \(S\), with \(\sum_S a_{tS}\lambda_S\leq\mu_t\), where \(a_{tS}\) counts how many members of type \(t\) the pattern needs. The problem is to choose feasible \(\lambda_S\) maximizing \(\sum_S\lambda_S g(S)\), and allocate each coalition’s gain among its members using its Shapley shares. Its solution specifies coalition mass and type-level payments. With bounded-size routing instances, the coalition-mass decision is a finite LP with \(O(\tau^3)\) patterns; with larger routing instances, the routing subproblem can remain hard.

A plausible regime is many carriers but relatively few exact, recurring service templates. This asks about a continuous carrier population and preserves the paper’s joint concerns of partner choice, routing gain, and compensation. The authors might recognize it as a high-multiplicity version of their underlying problem, though it does not preserve their decentralized bargaining protocol. The soft point is substantial: exact carrier types may be numerous in real data, and limiting coalition size to three follows the paper’s evaluated setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no result in this paper to anchor a mirror on: it proves no theorem, lemma, or corollary about the complexity of a computational problem. The NP-hardness of VRP and the \(O(2^n)\) cost of evaluating coalitions are motivation and background. The paper’s own results are empirical MARL performance in a three-carrier setting. So the proponent has raised no qualifying anchor; its coalition-mass LP is a proposed new problem, not a continuous version of a named result here.

That LP also changes the paper’s central task. It maximizes aggregate gain using precomputed \(g(S)\), imposes a coalition-size cap, and allocates gains by Shapley shares. The paper instead studies decentralized bargaining without explicit access to the characteristic function. If the cap is removed, the coalition patterns proliferate; if it is kept, the model’s tractability partly comes from that restriction. The LP may be useful, but it does not preserve the paper’s distinctive computational question.

A more faithful continuous bargaining model faces a modeling gap. A carrier’s type must include the delivery geometry, costs, assets, and relevant bargaining characteristics for carriers of that type to be interchangeable. With exact types, recurring multiplicity may be scarce; with coarser route templates, coalition routing gains may depend on information the types discarded. And the paper’s random-proposer process does not directly extend to a nonatomic population: there is no uniform random draw from a continuum, while each individual’s bargaining action has vanishing aggregate weight. A block-based or mean-field formulation might repair this, but it would need to define a new strategic model before there is a computational question to analyze.

This is a skeptical case, not a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. Its weak point is the exact-template regime: if many carriers genuinely repeat a small catalog of complementary service patterns, the proposed coalition-mass LP could be a sensible high-multiplicity problem. That would be a plausible research direction, but the paper’s missing complexity result and the LP’s changed objective and information assumptions make it difficult to call it a mirror of this paper’s contribution.
