What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers weighted-Borda ranking only; the paper’s Markov forecasting and interval TOPSIS approaches are not anchored by named computational results either.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. It gives three methods and works through numerical examples, but makes no complexity claim. Example 2 is the best foothold for a positive case: for a four-candidate election, its voter tiers turn 30 ballots into 41 effective ballots and change the Borda winner from \(b\) to \(a\). That is an illustration, not an eligible anchor under your requirements. I would not present it as one.

The most plausible mirror is the paper’s weighted-Borda ranking problem. Consider a large association election with many members but relatively few distinct combinations of reported ranking and importance tier. A type is a pair \(t=(\pi,\ell)\), where \(\pi\) is a complete candidate ranking and \(\ell\) is the member’s importance tier. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the membership. This is plausible when rankings and tiers are shared across sizable groups; the regime of interest has many more members than supported types.

Call the problem Continuous Weighted-Borda Ranking. Given candidates \(C\), a finite support of \(\tau\) types with rational masses summing to one, and a rational weight \(q_\ell\) for each tier, compute the ranked winners. For each candidate \(c\), calculate
\[ W_\mu(c)=\sum_{t=(\pi,\ell)}\mu_t q_\ell\bigl(m-\operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)+1\bigr), \]
where \(m=|C|\) and \(\operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)\) is \(c\)’s position in ranking \(\pi\). Return the candidates in score order, with ties reported. The decision is the social ranking; the objective is to maximize weighted Borda score. This is a direct continuous version of the method the paper computes, and its ranking-and-tier types preserve the distinctions that method uses.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: summing the scores takes \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations, with bit complexity polynomial in the input length. It covers only the weighted-Borda contribution. The Markov example’s probabilities describe transitions between candidate-preference states, and the TOPSIS example’s intervals describe evaluations; neither, by itself, makes the population the continuous object.

The weakest point is also decisive for grading: there is no valid named anchor, and weighted-Borda winner computation is already straightforward for a finite electorate. So this is a plausible continuous formulation, but it does not establish a hardness result that continuization could dissolve or a meaningful complexity boundary. A natural follow-on, beyond the paper, would ask for the minimum cost of transferring mass between ranking types to make a specified candidate win.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the missing anchor. The paper states no theorem, lemma, corollary, or other named computational result. It proposes three procedures and illustrates them with numerical examples. That is not a claim about the complexity of a computational problem, so there is no such claim for a continuous mirror to preserve.

The proponent’s Continuous Weighted-Borda Ranking is the best candidate for a mirror. A society supported on ranking-and-tier types is sensible for a large association, and the formula \(W_\mu(c)\) faithfully aggregates the paper’s weighted scores. But it formalizes a method in the paper; it does not mirror a named computational result. That distinction matters under the requested anchor test. The formula’s direct evaluation in \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations does not itself disqualify it—an easy answer can still be worthwhile—but the paper makes no computational claim for that task.

The stronger rescue is to ask how much mass must be moved between ranking types to make a chosen candidate win. That is a coherent high-multiplicity problem, but it adds an intervention objective and a cost model absent from the paper. With those additions it may be a worthwhile ChoCo problem, especially as a weighted variant of Borda bribery. It still cannot serve as a mirror of a result this paper proves: the article neither studies voter conversion nor claims anything about its complexity.

The Markov and TOPSIS examples do not supply alternative anchors. The Markov example already computes with a probability distribution over candidate states, but it makes no computational-complexity claim; adding costs for changing transition behavior would introduce a new control problem. TOPSIS ranks candidates from a finite criteria matrix. One could construct a society of evaluator types to generate such data, but the paper’s example does not state a computational result about that construction.

So the negative case is strong under the paper’s named-result anchor requirement, but weak as a universal claim about research value. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous study built around weighted-Borda manipulation. I can say that such a study would extend this paper’s method into a new computational problem; I cannot call it a mirror of a computational result the paper contains.
