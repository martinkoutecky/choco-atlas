The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible angle is the bicameral result, because it has a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large legislative body with many interchangeable members in each of two chambers. A type is chamber membership; its mass is that chamber’s share of the legislature.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that, under simple-majority quotas, if the smaller chamber has \(m_s<m_r\) members, then a member of the smaller chamber has more critical coalitions of every relevant size—and therefore ranks above a member of the larger chamber under any power index—when the chambers are both odd, both even, the smaller is even and the larger odd, or the smaller is odd and the larger is even with \(m_r>2m_s\). This is a good structural result to motivate a mirror. It is not, however, a qualifying computational anchor: the theorem does not assert a complexity classification, and the paper does not analyze the running time or complexity of a named computational problem. Under the requested anchor rule, there are no eligible anchors in this paper.

A possible continuous question, offered as a prospective analogue rather than an anchor-backed result, is Continuous Bicameral Pivotal-Density Comparison. Let the society have two types, \(S\) and \(R\), with rational masses \(\alpha_s<\alpha_r\) summing to \(1\), and let each chamber require half its mass to pass a bill. A coalition is a mass vector \(x=(x_s,x_r)\); it wins when \(x_s\ge\alpha_s/2\) and \(x_r\ge\alpha_r/2\). Give coalition sizes a shared nonnegative, piecewise-rational weight density \(w\). Define the two types’ pivotal densities by
\[ \Phi_S(w)=\int_{\alpha_r/2}^{\alpha_r}w(\alpha_s/2+y)\,dy,\qquad \Phi_R(w)=\int_{\alpha_s/2}^{\alpha_s}w(x+\alpha_r/2)\,dx. \]
The question is: given the masses and \(w\), which type has greater pivotal density? A solution returns the exact comparison. In this model, the smaller chamber’s integration interval contains the larger chamber’s, so \(S\) weakly outranks \(R\) for every \(w\), with strict inequality when \(w\) assigns positive weight to the additional interval. Exact integration makes this version tractable.

The weakness is substantial: this pivotal-density definition replaces the paper’s counts of discrete coalitions, \(\,c_i(k)\), with geometric measure on coalition-mass boundaries. The resulting comparison removes the paper’s exceptional parity regimes, so it is not clearly a high-multiplicity limit of the authors’ power indices. Legislatures with many interchangeable members make the population scenario plausible; whether the authors would recognize this particular power semantics as their problem is doubtful. Thus there is a sensible continuous object to investigate, but the paper itself supplies no named computational result to anchor the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a strong mathematical result, but it does not give ChoCo a computational problem to mirror. It compares critical-coalition counts in a parameterized bicameral game; its proof reduces the comparison to binomial inequalities. The paper gives no complexity classification, algorithmic bottleneck, or exact-versus-approximation question. The US-system calculations using Mathematica do not supply one either. So the proponent is right that there is no qualifying computational anchor here.

The proposed pivotal-density comparison does not repair that gap. Its ordering follows because both integrals use the same \(w\) over total coalition size, and the smaller chamber’s interval contains the larger chamber’s. But the finite counts in the paper are not lengths of those intervals: they include type-specific numbers of coalitions, such as \(\binom{m_s-1}{q_s-1}\binom{m_r}{k-q_s}\) for a senator and \(\binom{m_r-1}{q_r-1}\binom{m_s}{k-q_r}\) for a representative. A common density on total mass discards that composition information and imposes a new power semantics. It also needs a chosen boundary measure, since removing one person from a continuum population changes no mass. The clean result is therefore a consequence of the proposed definition, not a continuous version of Theorem 1.

There is a more faithful repair: take growing bicameral games with chamber sizes and quotas converging to fixed proportions, and ask for the limiting Banzhaf, Shapley–Shubik, or semivalue rankings. That is a sensible continuous-population question, and the legislature scenario makes high multiplicity plausible. But it is an asymptotic analysis of power indices, not a complexity question of the kind this programme is designed to chart. For the paper’s two-chamber model, the critical counts already have closed binomial formulas; a computational challenge would need extra structure beyond the result being mirrored. Allowing the number of chambers or the game description to grow could create such a challenge, but that would be a newly designed problem rather than a mirror grounded in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest as a fit judgment: this paper may support worthwhile continuous voting theory, but it offers no named computational result whose high-multiplicity version opens a compelling ChoCo complexity question. The universal claim that no scenario could produce one is less secure; the plausible legislature regime is real, and a limiting power-index theory could interest its authors. The case against is that this interest appears to lie outside the programme’s computational gap.
