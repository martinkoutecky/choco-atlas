The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or other complexity classification. Its strongest named result relevant to computation is Appendix C’s “Theorem”—printed without a number, and proved in the paper. It is a robustness result, not a complexity claim.

In full, the theorem says that for a connected assessor–object graph and the fixed degeneracy-breaking condition \(\sum_{(a,o)} c_{ao}(v_o-b_a)=C v_{\mathrm{ref}}\), changes to the fitted values and biases are bounded by
\[ \|(\Delta v,\Delta b)\|_{\mathrm{results}} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_2}}\|\Delta s\|_{\mathrm{scores}}. \]
Here the score norm squares and sums changes weighted by \(c_{ao}\), while the results norm weights \(\Delta v_o^2+\Delta b_a^2\) by the total confidence attached to each object and assessor. The quantity \(\mu_2\) is the second-smallest eigenvalue of \(M=\begin{bmatrix}I&D\\D^\top&I\end{bmatrix}\), where \(D_{ao}=c_{ao}/\sqrt{C_oC'_a}\), \(C_o=\sum_a c_{ao}\), and \(C'_a=\sum_o c_{ao}\). Thus the theorem connects the stability of the calibration output to the connectivity of the assessment graph.

My lead mirror is Type-Population Calibration with Confidence. Consider a large review programme in which many assessors work under recurring roles: for example, assessors share a discipline, expertise band, rubric training, and confidence scale. A type \(t\) is a cohort with the same stable additive bias and the same confidence and assignment characteristics used by the model. The type mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the assessor population. The objects remain individual proposals or outputs, since recovering a value for each object is central to the paper’s task. A plausible regime has \(N\) assessors but only \(\tau\) such cohorts, with \(N\gg\tau\).

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite object set \(O\), a finite support \(T\) of assessor types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational assessment masses \(q_{to}\), rational confidence weights \(c_{to}>0\), and rational mean scores \(\bar s_{to}\) for each pair with \(q_{to}>0\). The mass \(q_{to}\) is the number of assessments of object \(o\) per unit assessor population supplied by type \(t\); the mean score summarizes those assessments. Assume the resulting type–object graph is connected. Find object values \(v_o\) and type biases \(b_t\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t,o}q_{to}c_{to}(\bar s_{to}-v_o-b_t)^2, \]
with the gauge fixed by
\[ \sum_{t,o}q_{to}c_{to}(v_o-b_t) = \left(\sum_{t,o}q_{to}c_{to}\right)v_{\mathrm{ref}}, \]
for a specified reference value \(v_{\mathrm{ref}}\). A solution is the fitted values and biases, together with the spectral-gap stability certificate obtained from the corresponding weighted matrix \(M\). This is the underlying calibration problem, not an encoding of it.

The connection to the theorem is direct: replace each individual assessor’s confidence-weighted edge by the assessment mass \(q_{to}\) times its confidence \(c_{to}\). The resulting bound measures how sensitive calibrated object values and cohort biases are to changes in the type-level mean scores. I would expect this finite-support problem to be Class A: after fixing the gauge, it is a rational weighted least-squares system, and the stability quantity comes from a finite matrix. A polynomial-time bit-complexity analysis in \(\tau\), \(|O|\), the number of supported pairs, and input bit length \(L\) looks attainable. The paper does not establish that complexity bound; it is the natural question raised by this mirror.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime. It keeps their additive-bias model, confidence weighting, incomplete assignments, and calibrated object values, while aggregating assessors who are genuinely interchangeable for those purposes. The mirror covers the CWC calibration and its stability analysis. It does not claim to continuize the selection of funded proposals, Bayesian model comparison, or the paper’s other case studies.

The soft spot is the shared-bias cohort assumption. If assessor-to-assessor idiosyncrasy is substantial, grouping assessors by role may discard exactly the variation the original method seeks to infer; and if confidence profiles or assignments differ widely, \(\tau\) may grow until the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. There is also no discrete hardness result here whose dissolution the mirror could test. The positive case is therefore deliberately narrower: a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s calibration-and-robustness problem, with an expected tractability result, rather than a claimed complexity-theoretic breakthrough.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about the complexity of a computational problem. Its one plausible anchor is the Appendix C stability theorem, which bounds how score perturbations affect fitted values and assessor biases. That is a mathematical robustness bound, not a complexity result.

The proposed type-population calibration model is coherent: if assessors in a cohort really share a bias, and their assessment and confidence patterns can be aggregated, the cohort means give a weighted least-squares system. But that system is not the theorem’s computational problem. The theorem bounds sensitivity for a given assessment graph; replacing individual assessors with cohort masses changes the graph weights and repeats the same spectral calculation. The paper already formulates the finite calibration problem as a linear system. The mirror introduces no mass-transfer choice, combinatorial feasibility question, or pricing problem whose complexity the programme could chart.

A stronger winner-robustness version would ask how much assessor mass must change to alter which proposals are funded. The paper gives no model of permissible assessor changes, score bounds, intervention costs, or assignment constraints. Those choices determine the problem. Adding them could produce a worthwhile new project, but it would be a new intervention model rather than a mirror of the stability theorem.

There is a real limitation in the cohort construction: the paper estimates assessor-specific biases, so grouping by role only works if role is a credible proxy for a shared bias. Including each assessor’s actual bias and full object-by-object assignment and confidence profile in their type may leave little aggregation. That weakens the proposed scenario, though it does not rule out a separate setting where shared cohort biases are plausible.

So the strongest negative case is that this paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to mirror, and its robustness theorem does not become one merely by replacing assessor counts with masses. I would not claim the universal conclusion that no continuous assessment model could be worthwhile: the cohort model is a sensible high-multiplicity object. The case against is that it is a thin fit for this programme’s computational questions, not that every such scenario is impossible.
