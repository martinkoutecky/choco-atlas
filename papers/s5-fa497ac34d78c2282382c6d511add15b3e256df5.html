What the mirror covers

The proposed model extends the fixed-agreement drought-resilience result in Proposition 2 and its threshold corollary; it leaves the core characterization, satiation extension, contingent and proportional agreements, and Aral Sea calculations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact separation over coalition masses and their release choices admit a polynomial-time algorithm for rational piecewise-linear benefits?
  • Can fixed-floor feasibility, including efficiency, continuous-core constraints, and self-enforcement, be solved in polynomial time in the input bit length and number of types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible scenario is a basin with many irrigation districts or water cooperatives signing annual release agreements. A district’s type would include its position on the river, benefit curve, controlled-flow distribution, and contract terms. If thousands of districts fall into a small number \(\tau\) of such complete types, \(\mu_i\) can represent each type’s share of normalized service capacity, with water supplies scaling with that mass. This is a credible high-multiplicity setting: the paper itself discusses farmers, cities, and industries as river users, though it studies sovereign countries as the contracting parties.

There is a strict anchor problem. The paper contains no named result asserting a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Its strongest nearby result is Proposition 2, proved in this paper in Appendix A. It says that among all core fixed water sharing agreements, the upstream incremental agreement is self-enforced at lower water flows than any other core agreement. That is a substantive optimization result about treaty design, but it is not a computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires.

As an exploratory mirror of Proposition 2, I would define Continuous Fixed-Agreement Sustainability. Its input is an ordered set of \(\tau\) district types with rational masses \(\mu_i\), \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\); a finite set of joint flow states \(\Omega\) with rational probabilities \(\pi_\omega\); per-capita inflows \(e_i(\omega)\); concave benefit functions \(b_i\); and a drought-floor vector \(\ell\). A contract chooses aggregate fixed releases \(W_i\) from type \(i\) to \(i+1\), and transfers \(P_i\) received by type \(i\) from downstream. With \(W_0=W_\tau=P_0=P_\tau=0\), type \(i\)’s per-capita consumption in state \(\omega\) is

\[ q_i(\omega)=\frac{\mu_i e_i(\omega)+W_{i-1}-W_i}{\mu_i}. \]

The question is whether there exists an efficient contract that is in the continuous core and self-enforcing at the specified drought floor; a solution is such a contract, or a certificate that none exists. “In the continuous core” means that every coalition of submasses \(a\in[0,\mu]\) gets at least its standalone worth. Define that worth as the maximum expected benefit the coalition can obtain by choosing its own fixed releases \(Y_i\), subject to nonnegative water balances \(a_i e_i(\omega)+Y_{i-1}-Y_i\ge 0\) in every state; disconnected parts of a coalition are evaluated separately. If \(u_i\) is type \(i\)’s per-capita expected welfare under the basin-wide contract, the core constraints are \(\sum_i a_i u_i\ge v(a)\) for every \(a\in[0,\mu]\).

Self-enforcement requires, for each type, that the per-capita compensation cover the benefit it gives up by releasing water. At its drought floor this is

\[ \frac{P_i}{\mu_i}\ge b_i\!\left(\ell_i+\frac{W_{i-1}}{\mu_i}\right) - b_i\!\left(\ell_i+\frac{W_{i-1}-W_i}{\mu_i}\right). \]

Efficiency requires \(W\) to maximize expected total benefit for the whole society. A natural next question is to find the most severe floor at which any such contract remains sustainable, and whether the continuous upstream incremental contract attains it.

For finite \(\tau\), finite flow support, and explicitly represented concave piecewise-linear benefits, I would expect the fixed-floor feasibility problem to be tractable. Efficiency and coalition-worth calculations have convex optimization formulations, while a violated core constraint can be sought by optimizing over the finite-dimensional coalition-mass vector and its release choices. That suggests a separation-oracle route. This is a forecast for the proposed model, not a result of this paper. Its existing flow uncertainty is probabilistic; the proposed mirror continuizes the population, so it is not already supplied by the paper’s use of random flows.

The soft spot is substantial: Proposition 2 concerns a finite game among countries, while this mirror lets coalitions contain arbitrary fractions of each type. That change may alter the core and the incentive structure. And because there is no qualifying computational anchor, this is a plausible extension of a named treaty-design result, not a fully compliant anchor-backed case under your rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is the anchor. Proposition 2 is a characterization of treaty design: among core fixed agreements, the upstream incremental agreement withstands lower flows than any other. It gives no computational problem, algorithm, or complexity result. The paper’s optimization programs support its economic analysis, but the proposition does not ask how efficiently to compute an answer. So the proposed mirror would begin by inventing a computational task, rather than continuizing a computational result from the paper.

The strongest version of that invented task would ask for a most drought-resilient agreement from rational, piecewise-linear benefit and flow data. But Proposition 2 already narrows the calculation: find the efficient releases, evaluate the relevant follower-coalition worths, and check one self-enforcement condition per position on the river. In the proposed finite-type model, those are a small collection of optimization problems, not a search over the continuum of submass coalitions used to define its core. Adding that continuum of coalition constraints would be a new modeling choice, not something required to compute the agreement characterized by Proposition 2. This does not make an easy answer worthless; it does make the case for a ChoCo complexity mirror much less direct.

The proposed high-multiplicity scenario has a deeper fit problem. On a single river, a country’s position determines its predecessors, followers, and water transfers. Position is therefore part of its complete type, and the positions are structurally distinct. Treating many districts at the same position as one mass requires them to share a water right and negotiate as a group; treating them as separate negotiators changes the flow and coalition structure. The proposed per-capita endowment also assumes that total controlled water scales with mass. That could fit replicated catchments, but independent copies of the river problem have separate water and transfers, so their coalition worths add and the treaty calculation separates by basin. A model that preserves richer interaction among numerous users would need a different hydrologic and institutional structure.

I would not claim that no plausible population model exists. The paper itself mentions farmers, cities, and industries, and a study of many comparable irrigation cooperatives could be sensible. But to preserve the paper’s river-chain structure, location tends to make the relevant agents distinct; to get repeated types, one must either group users or replicate largely independent basins. That is a weak basis for saying this paper has a worthwhile continuous *computational* mirror. The negative case is strongest on the missing computational anchor and weaker on the universal claim: a thoughtfully redesigned model of many water users could still be worth studying, though it would be an extension rather than a mirror of Proposition 2.
