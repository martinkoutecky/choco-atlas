The continuous mirror question

Given weak-ranking types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and a finite allocation cross-section of positive masses \(\eta_{t,\mathbf b}\) receiving integral bundles \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\), decide whether every positive-mass recipient’s bundle SD-dominates every other recipient’s bundle according to the evaluator’s type. For SD-EF1, allow deletion of one good from each compared recipient bundle before testing dominance.

The model it lives in

Types are weak rankings over \(m\) indivisible good categories, and population mass \(\mu_t\) is split among positive type-bundle atoms \(\eta_{t,\mathbf b}\), with per-capita supplies \(q_g\). There are no optimization variables: the allocation is fixed, and the output is whether all cross-section comparisons satisfy SD-EF or SD-EF1.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is Theorem 1, the paper’s own main result. It states that, given an allocation of \(m\) items and \(n\) agents with weak total-order preferences, verifying both SD-EF and SD-EF1 takes \(\mathcal{O}(nm)\) time. The paper proves this result here. Its algorithm checks each agent’s preference list in one pass, using counters and lazy initialization to avoid checking every pair of agents. The paper gives no named hardness result, so this is the natural anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large public distribution of standardized, indivisible goods: for example, aid kits or household supplies distributed to many residents. Let the goods be \(m\) categories of copies, and let a type \(t\) be a weak ranking of those categories. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu_t\) over a modest number \(\tau\) of such rankings. Each category has a rational per-capita supply \(q_g\), so its total stock grows with the population. A resident still receives an integral bundle; only the share of residents receiving each bundle is fractional. This keeps the goods indivisible and makes population, rather than a lottery over outcomes, the continuous object.

Here is the mirror question, SD-EF Mass Verification. An instance gives the types and masses \(\mu_t\), the per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and a finite list of positive rational masses \(\eta_{t,\mathbf b}\). Each \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^m\) is an integral bundle, and \(\eta_{t,\mathbf b}\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\) receiving it. Require
\[ \sum_{\mathbf b}\eta_{t,\mathbf b}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,\mathbf b}\eta_{t,\mathbf b}b_g=q_g \]
for each type and good category. Given such a feasible allocation, decide whether it is SD-EF. For every two positive-mass atoms \((t,\mathbf b)\) and \((s,\mathbf d)\), require
\[ \sum_{h\succeq_t g} b_h\ \ge\ \sum_{h\succeq_t g} d_h \qquad\text{for every category }g. \]
Thus each resident’s integral bundle must SD-dominate every bundle received by anyone else, according to that resident’s type.

The paired question, SD-EF1 Mass Verification, uses the same instance and asks whether, for every such pair of atoms, there is a category \(h\) with \(d_h>0\) such that \(\mathbf b\) SD-dominates \(\mathbf d-\mathbf e_h\) under type \(t\). The deletion is one good from an individual bundle, not a unit of population mass. With weak rankings, it suffices to test removing a most-preferred category present in \(\mathbf d\), as in the paper’s Lemma 2.

These questions preserve the paper’s universal comparisons: they do not average away envy within or between types. The allocation record is a population cross-section, not a lottery that gives an individual a fractional bundle. There is also a direct finite-instance dictionary. A discrete allocation with \(n\) agents maps to masses equal to cohort counts divided by \(n\), and to per-capita supplies equal to stock divided by \(n\). Conversely, rational masses and supplies can be scaled by a common denominator to obtain a finite allocation with integral bundles.

Both verification questions look tractable. Let \(K\) be the number of positive type-bundle atoms. For each evaluator type and each preference prefix, compute the minimum count in a bundle assigned to that type and the maximum count in any population bundle. SD-EF holds exactly when every such minimum is at least the corresponding maximum. For SD-EF1, compute the maximum after removing the most-preferred good from each comparison bundle. These scans take \(\mathcal{O}(\tau K m)\) prefix-count operations, plus input and bit-complexity costs. The run time depends on the listed support, not on expanding rational masses into individual residents.

The regime is credible when the population is large, \(\tau\) is modest, and bulk allocation gives residents a modest number \(K\) of type-bundle outcomes. Many residents share the same ranking, while the bundle support records any differences in what they receive. A qualification is that the paper’s theorem concerns a finite set of goods; scaling copies and supplies per capita is a co-modification needed for a growing population.

My weakest point is that this mirror may look like a compressed high-multiplicity restatement of finite verification: its exact rational instances scale back to finite ones, and the proposed support-based checks are straightforward. The per-capita supply model also extends the paper’s fixed inventory. Those are real limits on how novel the mirror is. They do not erase the case that it asks the authors’ own question—whether a specified allocation is SD-EF or SD-EF1—over a population distribution, while preserving integral bundles and the original one-good relaxation. The next questions would be whether SD-EF or SD-EF1 allocations can be *found* under per-capita supplies, and how their existence and computation depend on the type and bundle supports.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the mirror only becomes a genuine population limit after changing the resource side too. With the paper’s fixed set of \(m\) indivisible goods, a continuum of people cannot receive nonempty bundles: at most \(m\) people can do so, and they have zero mass. Almost everyone receives the empty bundle, so verification on the positive-mass population becomes vacuous. If one instead keeps track of those zero-mass recipients, the population distribution no longer contains enough information to verify fairness.

The proponent’s per-capita supply model repairs that degeneration, but it changes the setting to growing stocks of replicated good categories and rankings over those categories. That is a substantial extension of the paper’s model. It is not, however, a decisive objection under ChoCo’s stated scope, which explicitly allows per-capita supplies when continuization requires them.

The sharper concern is that, in the proposed SD-EF and SD-EF1 mass-verification questions, the fairness test uses the *support* of the type-bundle distribution, not the mass values. Once an atom has positive mass, its exact weight does not affect whether it must be compared with another atom. So this is not a problem where the continuous weights themselves drive a new optimization question; it is a support-based verification problem with feasibility constraints involving masses. That makes the mirror look less distinctive than the programme’s mass-transfer examples.

Still, support-based verification is a legitimate high-multiplicity question. Complete types and the recorded bundles preserve the information the pairwise comparisons need; SD-EF1’s removal of one good remains meaningful for each individual bundle. And the paper does have a named computational result: Theorem 1 proves an input-linear verification algorithm. Calling that result too simple or merely input-bound would be an argument about the answer’s interest, which does not defeat the mirror under the stated standard.

So the negative case is weak. Fixed inventory makes a direct continuum limit degenerate, and the repaired version makes the masses largely irrelevant to the fairness predicate. But the allowed per-capita extension answers the first problem, and the second does not make the resulting verification question incoherent or unfaithful.
