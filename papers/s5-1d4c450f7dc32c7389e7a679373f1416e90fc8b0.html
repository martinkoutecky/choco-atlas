Why no mirror

The paper's named anchors are \(\mathrm{BPoA}\) efficiency bounds for finite Bayesian auctions, not computational results under the stated anchor rule: no algorithm, hardness theorem, or computable formulation is asserted. The proposed mass model with per-capita supplies and a nonatomic equilibrium is a plausible new research direction, but the paper neither defines nor computes it, and its Appendix D leaves the relevant equilibrium-limit issues unresolved. Therefore the computational-result gate fails even though a high-multiplicity auction interpretation may be sensible.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only the first-price and second-price \(\mathrm{BPoA}\) bounds, with Lemmas 1 and 3 as supporting deviation inequalities; it leaves the examples, correlated-valuation lower bound, no-overbidding discussion, and equilibrium-existence appendix without a computational mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the finite \(N\)-buyer, \(Nq_j\)-copy replication converge to a well-defined nonatomic equilibrium and \(\mathrm{BPoA}\)?
  • Can such an equilibrium or an \(\varepsilon\)-welfare certificate be represented and computed from finite type data?
  • Does the \(\mathrm{BPoA}\le 2\) bound survive the chosen rationing and limiting equilibrium concept?

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrower than a standard computational mirror: this paper contains no named \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or approximation-complexity theorem. Its strongest named anchors are efficiency theorems. They nevertheless support a credible continuous-population problem, because the paper’s proofs already operate through type distributions, expected prices, and type-by-type deviations.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Section 4. It states: in simultaneous first-price item auctions with subadditive bidders, the Bayesian price of anarchy is at most \(2\). Equivalently, for every product distribution of bidder valuations and every Bayes–Nash equilibrium, expected equilibrium welfare is at least one half of expected optimal welfare.

A natural continuous mirror is the following. Let \(M=[m]\) be item kinds and let \(T\) be a finite set of bidder types. Type \(t\) has a monotone subadditive valuation \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\). The society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers of type \(t\). To avoid the degeneracy of having only one copy of each item while the buyer population grows, let \(q_j\) be the supply of item \(j\) per unit population. This is the natural high-multiplicity regime: with \(N\) buyers there are \(Nq_j\) copies of item \(j\).

An aggregate allocation is \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), the mass of type-\(t\) buyers receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\), satisfying \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\). Its welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). Thus

\(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,q)=\max_x\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).

The variables \(x_{t,S}\) describe fractions of the population receiving whole bundles; they do not give any individual a fractional bundle.

A strategy is a type-contingent bid distribution \(\sigma_t\) over vectors \(b\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^m\). For each item \(j\), the highest-bidding mass up to \(q_j\) receives that item. In the first-price version, each winner pays their own bid. A continuum Bayes–Nash equilibrium is a collection \(\sigma=(\sigma_t)_{t\in T}\) such that no positive-measure type can improve its expected quasi-linear utility by replacing \(\sigma_t\), with the aggregate bid and price field held fixed. Formally, one can define this directly as a nonatomic Bayesian game, or as a weak limit of finite games with \(N\mu_t\) buyers and \(Nq_j\) item copies.

The continuous problem is therefore:

\(\textsc{First-Price-BPoA}_\infty\): given \(T\), \(\mu\), the valuation functions \(v_t\), supplies \(q\), and a tie-breaking rule, determine

\(\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{FP}}_\infty(\mu,q)} \frac{\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,q)}{W(\sigma)}\),

where \(\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{FP}}_\infty(\mu,q)\) is the set of continuum Bayes–Nash equilibria and \(W(\sigma)\) is their expected aggregate welfare. A solution is an equilibrium strategy field, its induced allocation and welfare, and either the exact ratio or an \(\varepsilon\)-certificate for it. The theorem one would seek is \(\mathrm{BPoA}^{\mathrm{FP}}_\infty\le2\).

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. The action remains a simultaneous vector of item bids, the objective remains social welfare, and the strategic issue remains that bidders cannot express bundle values directly. Only the population representation changes: many buyers share one of finitely many complete valuation types. The paper’s Lemma 1, also proved here, is exactly the bridge: against any distribution of item prices, every subadditive bidder has a bid yielding utility at least one half of the value of the full bundle minus expected total prices. In the continuum, the price distribution is generated by the type-mass strategy field rather than by named opponents. The paper’s virtual-profile argument likewise becomes sampling a virtual population from \(\mu\).

I would expect this mirror to remain on the tractable side for its central efficiency guarantee. The proof is linear in population masses and survives finite-\(N\) replication and passage to a limit. That is not a claim that computing an equilibrium is already known to be polynomial; the paper proves an efficiency bound, not an equilibrium-computation theorem. The further questions are whether the constant \(2\) is tight in the continuum, whether finite-population BPoA converges to the continuum value, and whether equilibrium existence and certification are computationally manageable.

A second, independently credible anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper in Section 5. It states: in simultaneous second-price auctions with independently drawn subadditive valuations, if bidders satisfy either strong or weak no-overbidding, the Bayesian price of anarchy is at most \(4\). Thus every such equilibrium has expected welfare at least one quarter of the expected optimum.

The corresponding problem, \(\textsc{Second-Price-BPoA}_\infty\), uses exactly the same \((T,\mu,v,q)\), but each item is allocated to the highest bidder and its winners pay the item’s clearing bid. Strong no-overbidding requires every bid \(b\) in the support of type \(t\)’s strategy to satisfy \(b(S)=\sum_{j\in S}b_j\le v_t(S)\) for every \(S\subseteq M\). Weak no-overbidding requires, for each such bid, expected value of the won bundle to be at least expected total winning bids under the induced price distribution.

The problem is to determine

\(\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{SP}}_\infty(\mu,q)} \mathrm{OPT}(\mu,q)/W(\sigma)\)

over equilibria satisfying either restriction, with the target theorem \(\mathrm{BPoA}^{\mathrm{SP}}_\infty\le4\). The paper’s Lemma 3 supplies the relevant continuous deviation: against any price distribution, a strongly no-overbidding bid achieves the same half-value-minus-price guarantee. The final factor \(4\) comes from combining that deviation bound with the no-overbidding inequality controlling total bids by welfare. Those statements are naturally type-level and therefore pass plausibly to a mass model.

The regime is plausible in a recurring procurement or retail platform with \(N\gg m\) buyers and a large stock of each item category. Buyers may be households, firms, or departments drawn from a small collection of repeated demand templates: same subadditive valuation, same risk policy, and same bidding environment. Here \(N\) is large while \(\tau=|T|\) is fixed or modest. The high-multiplicity interpretation is not “a distribution is merely a prior”; \(\mu\) is the actual population composition, and the supply constraints and welfare objective are imposed on its mass.

The weakest point is the necessary supply scaling. If one insists on exactly one copy of each item while \(N\to\infty\), the winners form zero population mass and the per-capita welfare problem degenerates. The mirror therefore uses per-capita item supplies, which is a co-modification forced by continuizing the population. A referee could regard that as a substantial extension of the auction model. The answer is that it preserves the paper’s underlying strategic question—simultaneous item bidding with subadditive preferences—and makes the high-multiplicity regime economically meaningful. If that co-modification is accepted, Theorems 1 and 2 give a strong basis for a continuous mirror; if it is rejected, this paper’s single-copy auction model has no nondegenerate population-continuum limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case turns on eligibility, not on claiming that a high-multiplicity auction model is incoherent. The paper contains no named computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are universal efficiency bounds, \( \mathrm{BPoA}\le 2 \) and \( \mathrm{BPoA}\le 4 \); they provide neither an algorithm, a complexity classification, an approximation scheme, nor even a finite input-output computational problem. The paper explicitly leaves equilibrium existence in continuous strategy spaces unresolved. Thus the proposed \(\textsc{First-Price-BPoA}_\infty\) and \(\textsc{Second-Price-BPoA}_\infty\) are new research problems motivated by the paper, not continuous mirrors of computational results proved in it.

Theorem 1 does not acquire computational content merely because its proof uses distributions over valuations. In the paper, those distributions are priors over named bidders in a finite auction with one copy of each item. The proposed mass model must either keep one copy per item, in which case winners have vanishing population mass as the buyer population grows, or introduce supplies \(Nq_j\). The latter is a sensible repair, but it creates a multi-unit, per-capita auction with a new allocation rule and a new limiting equilibrium concept. The paper never studies that game.

The strongest rescue is to define finite auctions with \(N\mu_t\) repeated valuation types and \(Nq_j\) item copies, then take a limit. But this leaves a dichotomy with no computational anchor. Keeping finite \(N\) gives a family of ordinary finite Bayesian games; taking the limit gives a nonatomic or price-taking equilibrium problem. A unilateral deviation in the latter has zero effect on aggregate clearing prices, unlike the strategic environment the paper quantifies over. One must additionally specify tie rationing, price fields, equilibrium convergence, and a finite representation of strategy distributions over \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^m\). The paper supplies none of these, and its Appendix D warns that equilibria need not survive limits of discretizations. A supremum over an possibly empty or infinitely represented equilibrium set is not yet an algorithmic problem.

Theorem 2 has the same defect, with an additional complication. Strong no-overbidding can be lifted to a type-level condition \(b(S)\le v_t(S)\), and weak no-overbidding can be defined relative to an aggregate price distribution. That makes the proposed model formally imaginable, but does not make it computational. Theorem 2 gives no procedure for finding, representing, or certifying a continuum equilibrium satisfying either restriction. In the finite-replication interpretation, one has merely a sequence of Vickrey games; in the nonatomic interpretation, one has a new multi-unit Vickrey/Wardrop model whose equilibrium and approximation theory are absent from the paper.

The proponent’s use of Lemmas 1 and 3 does not repair the gap. Those lemmas are pointwise deviation inequalities against a price distribution. They may remain valid in a continuum market, but their survival is a proof-transfer observation, not a continuous-population computational result. No exponential-variable LP, separation problem, or population-dependent complexity question appears. The proposed task “return an equilibrium strategy field and an exact ratio or \(\varepsilon\)-certificate” is precisely the new work; it is not what either theorem establishes.

So the fairest negative verdict is not that auctions cannot have a meaningful high-multiplicity interpretation. They plainly can: repeated buyer types in retail or procurement are plausible. The point is that this paper supports a new mean-field auction programme, not a worthwhile ChoCo mirror under the stated anchor rule. Its finite repetition is only a reformulation or limiting family, while its genuinely continuous version changes the auction and introduces an unstudied infinite-dimensional equilibrium problem. If ChoCo accepts any new computational question inspired by a BPoA theorem, the negative case is weak; under the programme’s requirement of a named computational result to continuize, both proposed anchors fail.
