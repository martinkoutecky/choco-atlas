What the mirror covers

The proposed construction targets only Proposition 4's limit of the optimal quota fraction; it leaves the finite-population expected-welfare characterization, cost comparative statics, and majority-quota welfare-loss analysis unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result: none of its propositions says a problem is in P or NP-hard, and it gives no complexity classification. The closest anchor for a positive case is Proposition 4, proved in Section 5.2. It says that, when per-capita cost tends to \(\gamma_0\ge 0\) and the limiting mean intensity of opposition is positive, the optimal quota grows proportionally with population size; the optimal quota fraction tends to
\[ \frac{\mu_-^0}{\mu_+^0+\mu_-^0}>0, \]
where \(\mu_+^0\) and \(\mu_-^0\) are the limiting conditional mean intensities of support and opposition. This is an asymptotic optimization result, not a stated complexity theorem. With a finite rational input encoding, however, its formula gives a polynomial-time computation.

My lead mirror is Asymptotic Mass-Quota Calibration. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of rational gross valuations \(x_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a rational limiting per-capita cost \(\gamma_0\), and a fixed setup cost \(K\ge0\). Type \(t\) means an agent has valuation \(x_t\); all agents pay the same per-capita cost. For a population of size \(n\), let \(C(n)=K+n\gamma_0\), so each agent pays \(\gamma_n=\gamma_0+K/n\). Draw \(n\) agents independently from the type distribution \(\mu\). A quota \(\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) provides the good if at least \(\alpha\) agents have nonnegative net valuation. Its expected welfare is
\[ W_n(\alpha)= \mathbb E\!\left[ \mathbf 1\{K_n\ge\alpha\} \sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\gamma_n) \right], \]
where \(K_n\) is the number of supporters. The problem is to return the limiting optimal quota fraction \(q^*=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_n^*/n\), for \(\alpha_n^*\in\arg\max_\alpha W_n(\alpha)\). A solution can give \(q^*\) and certify it from the input’s conditional means.

Specifically, let \(a_0\) be the mean of \(x_t-\gamma_0\) among types with \(x_t>\gamma_0\), and \(b_0\) the mean of \(\gamma_0-x_t\) among types with \(x_t<\gamma_0\). Assuming positive mass on both sides and no type exactly at \(\gamma_0\), the answer is
\[ q^*=\frac{b_0}{a_0+b_0}. \]
For rational, finite-support input, computing these two means and their ratio takes time polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\). The expected classification is Class A, tractable. This is a narrow algorithmic mirror: it covers the optimal quota’s asymptotic fraction, not the paper’s welfare-loss analysis or all of its cost comparative statics.

The regime is a large local referendum—say, a municipality or homeowners’ association—with many residents, equal cost sharing, and relatively few distinct valuation types for the same proposed project. Here \(\mu_t\) is the limiting fraction of residents of type \(t\), while finite electorates sample from that type distribution. This fits the paper’s equal-cost quota model, and the authors already define and study the optimal quota as a proportion of \(n\); they would likely recognize this as a direct high-multiplicity question. In the finite-support encoding, the type count can remain small while the population grows.

The weakest point is also the key modeling caveat: the paper’s uncertainty is over finite i.i.d. electorates, whereas a continuous society has exact type masses. As \(n\) grows, sampling noise disappears. The question above retains the paper’s welfare objective by defining the continuous quota through the limit of its finite-electorate optimizers; it is not simply the optimization of welfare in one fixed, deterministic mass distribution. If the mirror must have a direct deterministic-mass objective, this is not yet one. Also, Proposition 4 already supplies the asymptotic formula, so this is a well-motivated computational restatement, not an unsolved hardness-dissolution result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here: a large homeowners’ association with a small number of valuation types is a natural regime. The negative case is that Proposition 4’s quota limit does not give a nondegenerate optimization problem on a continuous society.

The proposed mirror uses the rational masses \(\mu_t\) as probabilities for drawing \(n\) discrete agents. Its objective therefore depends on finite-population sampling noise. In a continuous society, those masses are the exact fractions of people of each type. Let \(v_t=x_t-\gamma_0\), let \(p=\sum_{v_t\ge0}\mu_t\) be the supporter mass, and let \(S=\sum_t\mu_t v_t\) be aggregate net welfare per person. A quota fraction \(q\) then provides the good exactly when \(p\ge q\), giving welfare \(S\) if it is provided and \(0\) otherwise.

That direct mass problem does not select the paper’s \(q^*=b_0/(a_0+b_0)\). If \(S>0\), every \(q\le p\) provides the good and is optimal; if \(S<0\), every \(q>p\) rejects it and is optimal. The paper’s ratio lies on the welfare-maximizing side of \(p\), but the continuous objective does not single it out. Its finite-\(n\) selection comes from differences between nearby probabilities of particular supporter counts. Those differences disappear from the limiting mass objective. So computing \(q^*\) from the conditional means is a valid computation of the paper’s asymptotic statistic, but it is not an optimization over the exact continuous society.

One could retain a meaningful threshold tradeoff by making the *composition* of the continuum society random—for example, by adding uncertainty about which valuation distribution a community faces. That is a plausible question, but it changes the paper’s independent, individual-level uncertainty into uncertainty over aggregate societies. With finitely many possible societies, the resulting quota objective is a one-dimensional threshold calculation over their supporter masses. A richer version may be worth studying, but it is not the Proposition 4 mirror.

Thus the strongest objection is not that the continuous regime is unnatural, or that the quota formula is easy. It is that continuizing the population removes the sampling variation that makes the paper’s quota calibration distinctive; retaining that variation means keeping the finite random electorate. This makes the negative case real, though not airtight.
