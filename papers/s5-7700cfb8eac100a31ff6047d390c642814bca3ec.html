Its numbered results are structural claims about stable matchings: for example, Theorem 3.4 states the Rural Hospitals Theorem, and Theorem 3.5 states that sincere students receive the same college allocation across Boston-mechanism Nash equilibria. One could then ask whether the Rural Hospitals invariants persist or how to compute such assignments. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against starts with the paper’s clearest limitation: it contains no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 2.4–3.5 establish structural facts about stable matchings and equilibria. The paper mentions deferred acceptance, but does not classify its complexity or pose an algorithmic task. That leaves no computational claim here for continuization to reclassify.

The strongest Rural Hospitals mirror is coherent: give student preference types masses, scale college capacities with the population, and ask whether unmatched mass and college enrollment remain invariant across stable mass assignments. But that asks whether a structural theorem survives, not how the complexity of a computational problem changes. Continuum matching work already studies Rural Hospitals analogues and DA-style computation, though under particular model assumptions; for example, Arnosti gives a continuum model with a Rural Hospitals theorem under a no-atoms condition and an analogue of deferred acceptance.[Arnosti, *A Continuum Model of Stable Matching With Finite Capacities*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881) A complexity classification for a specific finite-support, rational-input variant could still be new, but it would be a further question, not a computational result in this paper’s theorem.

The strongest Pathak–Sönmez mirror would define a nonatomic Boston game with masses of sincere and strategic student types, proportional capacities, and ask whether sincere types’ assigned mass is invariant across equilibria—or how to compute that assignment. Again, the theorem gives an invariance claim, not a computational method or complexity bound. Computing a continuum equilibrium would require formulating a new strategic problem; the paper’s reduction to a tiered stable-matching economy does not itself provide a complexity question for that continuum game.

I cannot make the stronger claim that these population regimes are implausible: large college admissions markets are natural candidates. The negative case is strongest on fit to this paper. Its contributions are proofs of structural results, and turning them into algorithmic questions would begin a new computational project rather than continue a computational result the paper actually made.
