The case FOR (proponent)

My lead, and only anchor, is Theorem 17 (LP formulation for DRL), stated and proved in this paper. It says that when there are \(K\) subpopulations with margin matrices \(M^{(1)},\ldots,M^{(K)}\), and plausible population mixtures lie in the total-variation ball \(\mathcal W(w_0,\rho)\) around a reference mixture \(w_0\), the distributionally robust lottery problem has a linear-program formulation of size \(O(mK)\). The paper’s formulation maximizes \(t\) over a lottery \(p\in\Delta(A)\) and auxiliary variables, subject to \(t\le \beta_a-2\rho\lambda_a+\sum_k w_{0,k}\gamma_{a,k}\), \(\beta_a+\gamma_{a,k}\le p^\top M^{(k)}e_a\), and \(-\lambda_a\le\gamma_{a,k}\le\lambda_a\), with \(\lambda_a\ge0\), for every alternative \(a\). Here \(\beta_a\) renames the paper’s auxiliary variable \(\mu_a\) to distinguish it from population mass. The theorem is a formulation result; standard polynomial-time LP algorithms make it a tractability result for rational input.

The continuous mirror I would ask the authors to study is Type-Population TV-Robust Maximal Lottery. An instance has \(m\) models and a finite support \(T\) of \(K\) annotator-preference types. A type is a complete pairwise preference pattern over those models; its matrix \(M^{(t)}\) records its pairwise margins. The input also gives a rational nominal population distribution \(\mu^0\in\Delta(T)\) and a radius \(\rho\). Mass \(\mu_t\) means the fraction of the evaluation population with type \(t\), and the admissible populations are \(W(\mu^0,\rho)=\{w\in\Delta(T):\tfrac12\|w-\mu^0\|_1\le\rho\}\). The question is to find a model lottery \(p\in\Delta(A)\) maximizing \(\min_{w\in W(\mu^0,\rho)}\min_{q\in\Delta(A)}p^\top(\sum_t w_tM^{(t)})q\). A solution is an optimal lottery, with its robust value.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for crowdsourced model evaluation: many judgments come from recurring annotator-task preference types, while the number \(N\) of judgments is much larger than the number \(K\) of relevant types. For a finite dataset with \(n_t\) judgments of each type, the empirical population has mass \(\mu^0_t=n_t/N\). The continuous instance treats those masses as population proportions and permits reweighting within the stated uncertainty set. The lottery over models is already part of the paper’s decision; the continuization here is of the evaluation population.

Theorem 17 applies directly when the type matrices and masses are supplied explicitly: set \(M^{(k)}=M^{(t)}\) and \(w_0=\mu^0\). Thus this mirror is tractable by an \(O(mK)\)-size LP, with running time polynomial in the input bit length under standard rational encoding. It covers the paper’s robust-lottery computation, not its axiomatic results or experiments. It also suggests further questions: what if the possible type set is exponentially large and types are given implicitly? Can a pricing oracle optimize over preference types? Which structured uncertainty sets beyond total variation retain an efficient formulation?

The paper has no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer question, so I would not manufacture one. The weakness in this positive case is also clear: Theorem 17 already continuously varies weights over a simplex around subgroup matrices. That is very close to a continuous-population model. The mirror is most distinct when it opens up each coarse subgroup into complete preference types, but the robust-lottery objective and its computational structure remain essentially the paper’s own. So this is a strong case that the authors’ problem has a sensible continuous-population formulation, and a weaker case that the paper has left that formulation unstudied.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not leave a continuous mirror to build: Theorem 17 already solves that problem. Set \(T=[K]\), \(\mu^0=w_0\), and \(M^{(t)}=M^{(k)}\). Then the proposed objective and TV ambiguity set are exactly the paper’s DRL objective and \(\mathcal W(w_0,\rho)\). The theorem supplies the \(O(mK)\)-size LP, so replacing \(K\) groups with \(K\) preference types adds no new computational question. In a high-multiplicity instance, \(N\) observations with type counts \(n_t\) are already represented by masses \(n_t/N\); the algorithm depends on the explicit support and input bit length, not on listing each observation separately.

Calling the categories “complete preference types” does not create a gap. The rule uses only pairwise margin matrices and makes no transitivity assumption. Any explicit finite collection of type matrices fits Theorem 17’s arbitrary \(M^{(k)}\) inputs; refining groups into more types simply increases \(K\). If a type records information beyond its pairwise matrix, that information cannot affect this paper’s robust-lottery objective without changing the problem.

One could ask about an exponentially large, implicitly described type space and a pricing oracle. But then the open issue is the succinct representation and optimization over types, not the continuous population mirror proposed here. The paper has already made population weights continuous, optimized over a TV neighborhood of population mixtures, and given the LP; its finite-sample result also studies how to choose that neighborhood from data. The proposed mirror is therefore a relabeling of a named computational result, rather than a new continuization question.
