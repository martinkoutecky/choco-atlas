The continuous mirror question

Given \(q\) good classes with common values \(w_j\), a per-person capacity \(H\), and types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) and initial integral bundles \(b_t^0\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^q\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and supply per unit population is \(s_j=\sum_t\mu_t b^0_{t,j}\), does there exist a finite rational distribution \(x_{t,b}\ge 0\) over integral final bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^q\), \(\sum_j b_j\le H\), such that \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=s_j\), and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\sum_j\max\{b^0_{t,j}-b_j,0\}\le K\), while every pair \(b,c\) used with positive mass satisfies \(w\cdot b\ge w\cdot c-\max_{j:c_j>0}w_j\), taking the maximum as \(0\) when \(c\) is empty?

The model it lives in

A continuous population with type masses \(\mu_t\), common additive values \(w\), and integral per-person bundles drawn from \(q\) good classes. The decision variables assign mass \(x_{t,b}\) from each initial type to each final bundle, conserving per-capita supplies; the cost is per-capita goods whose owners change, and EF1 must hold between every pair of positively represented bundles.

The objection that survived

With a fixed catalogue and bounded bundles, one can enumerate bundle patterns and test compatible sets, which may make continuous optimization less central than the mirror suggests.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.6's identical-valuation EF1 reallocation result; it leaves the paper's other fairness notions, valuation classes, and parameterized results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does compatibility among EF1 bundle patterns yield an efficient algorithm for the mass problem, or a continuum-specific hardness result?
  • Does the discrete 3-Partition obstruction survive when mass from each initial type can split across final bundle patterns?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a mass version of the paper’s EF1 reallocation problem for identical additive valuations. Call it *Mass-EF1-Reformability*. Goods remain indivisible for each person; what becomes continuous is the population and the supply of replicated goods.

An instance has \(q\) good classes, common nonnegative integer values \(w_1,\ldots,w_q\), a per-agent capacity \(H\), and finitely many agent states \(t\in T\). State \(t\) records both the common valuation and an initial integral bundle \(b_t^0\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q\), with \(\|b_t^0\|_1\le H\). It has rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The supply of class \(j\) per unit population is \(s_j=\sum_t\mu_t b^0_{t,j}\). Given a rational budget \(K\), ask whether the population can be reassigned integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q\), \(\|b\|_1\le H\), within that budget.

A solution is a finite rational distribution \(x_{t,b}\ge0\): mass \(x_{t,b}\) of state \(t\) ends with bundle \(b\). It must satisfy \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=s_j\) for each good class. The moved-good mass is \(R(x)=\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\sum_j\max\{b^0_{t,j}-b_j,0\}\), and must be at most \(K\). This counts goods leaving their initial owners; conservation matches those losses with recipients’ gains. Finally, every pair of bundles \(b,c\) used by positive mass must satisfy EF1: \(w\cdot b\ge w\cdot c-\max_{j:c_j>0}w_j\), with the maximum taken as zero for an empty bundle. Thus every person receives an integral bundle and must meet the fairness criterion individually; only the mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

A plausible regime is a large, replicated resource-sharing system with homogeneous users and a standardized catalogue of equipment or course types. For instance, agents may share the same additive values while belonging to one of two initial-state cohorts: a stocked cohort and an empty cohort. With \(N\) large, there can be many agents in both cohorts even though the number of states is \(\tau=2\). The stock of each good class scales with population, while each person’s final bundle remains integral. Initial holdings belong in the state because they determine the cost of changing ownership.

My anchor is Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper. It establishes that Good-Bounded EF1-Reformability is NP-complete even when all agents have identical additive valuations; the reduction gives strong NP-hardness from 3-Partition. In the reduction, a 3-Partition instance has \(3r\) positive integers \(a_j\) summing to \(rB\), each strictly between \(B/4\) and \(B/2\). The construction sets \(\lambda=3r+1\), creates partition goods of values \(\lambda a_j\) and \(\lambda B+1\) helper goods of value \(1\), and uses \(r+1\) agents with the same valuation. One source agent initially holds every good, the other \(r\) agents are empty, and the reallocation budget is \(3r\). The paper proves that an EF1 allocation within that budget exists exactly when the numbers have a valid 3-partition. The proof is in this paper; it relies on the standard 3-Partition hardness result.

The mass question mirrors that underlying problem directly: can we move goods among agents to reach EF1 while minimizing the amount of goods whose owners change? I would provisionally expect Class C for the general bounded-capacity mass problem. Once the allowed integral bundle patterns are fixed, mass assignment and moved-good cost are linear. But EF1 restricts which patterns may coexist, and finding or pricing those patterns may retain hard bundle combinatorics. The decisive follow-up is whether that pricing step admits an efficient algorithm. Theorem 3.6 does *not* settle it: its discrete 3-Partition argument assigns named items to named bins, while a mass solution can distribute agents fractionally among different integral bundle patterns. The reduction may therefore lose its force in the continuum. Its own gadget slice is a useful test case: the construction forces target bundles to have three partition goods, so those patterns can be enumerated, which is evidence that this slice might instead be tractable.

This case covers only the identical-valuation EF1 result in Theorem 3.6. My weakest point is the regime behind the reduction: it has a stocked source cohort and many empty recipients, and the source cohort’s fraction shrinks as \(r\) grows. A replicated deployment with many source agents is possible, but the paper’s reduction does not establish that this is the most natural high-multiplicity setting. More broadly, fractional assignment of mass across bundle patterns may erase precisely the 3-Partition obstruction that makes the discrete result strong. That is also the main question the mirror should put to a prover: does the continuous EF1 reallocation problem admit tractable pricing, or is there a new hardness reduction that survives population continuization?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.6, and it clears the programme’s basic bar: it proves NP-completeness for a named reallocation problem. There is no honest way to dismiss it as merely axiomatic or to say that the paper has no computational result to mirror.

The best objection is about how well that result motivates a *population* continuum. The reduction’s combinatorics sit in the goods: \(3r\) distinct partition values, plus a number of helper goods depending on \(B\). The population is one source agent and \(r\) empty recipients. In the proposed mirror, identical recipients can divide their mass across different integral bundle patterns. That may dissolve the reduction, as the proponent acknowledges. But if we preserve every item as its own class so the construction remains faithful, the continuum leaves the item-packing structure intact and mostly changes how recipient agents can be distributed across patterns. This is a reason to doubt the mirror’s payoff, not a reason it is ill-posed: a continuum-specific hardness result would still matter under ChoCo’s stated goals.

The more substantial negative case concerns which version is natural. With a fixed catalogue of good classes, bounded per-agent bundles, and a small set of initial cohorts, there are only finitely many bundle patterns. One can enumerate candidate sets of mutually EF1-compatible patterns and solve the mass-allocation constraints for each. That may leave a useful problem, but it makes the continuous machinery look less central. To retain Theorem 3.6’s construction, the number of good classes grows with the input, the source bundle grows, and the source cohort has mass \(1/(r+1)\). The construction therefore gives little evidence that its hard instances resemble a stable, replicated resource-sharing system.

A better mirror can answer some of those concerns. It can retain singleton good classes, include each initial bundle in the agent’s type, and use positive-mass source and recipient cohorts. That is a coherent high-multiplicity model, and I cannot show that no plausible setting supports it. But those choices weaken the link to the paper’s reduction: they make the mirror worth asking as a new problem, while leaving Theorem 3.6 itself with limited force as evidence that the continuous question will be rich.

So the negative case is weak where it must be strongest. The reduction is a poor guide to a natural high-multiplicity regime, but that does not defeat the mirror; the faithful version remains well-posed, and the fixed-catalogue version remains a legitimate question even if it proves easy. I do not see an honest argument that *no* scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror of this result.
