The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I would defend is Continuous-Mass Gerrymandering. The paper has no numbered result that classifies a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. Its closest named anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for an even \(n\), an \(n\times n\) grid, and \(K=2\) equal-population connected districts, a party controlling the districting can win both districts whenever its vote share is at least \(1/2+1/n\). The theorem covers the worst case over how that party’s voters are distributed across the grid. It is a structural result about districting feasibility, rather than a complexity classification.

In the mirror, each type is a pair \((v,P)\): precinct \(v\) and party \(P\). A large precinct population has mass \(1/n^2\); if \(q_v\in[0,1]\) is the fraction supporting the target party, set
\[ \mu_{(v,P)}=\frac{q_v}{n^2}, \qquad \mu_{(v,\bar P)}=\frac{1-q_v}{n^2}. \]
The \(q_v\) are binary rationals, and the support has at most \(2n^2\) types. The scenario is a large election with many voters grouped into a much smaller number of precinct-party types. For example, millions of voters may be represented by a few hundred precincts, with each precinct’s party proportions recorded as masses.

Given this distribution and \(K\mid n^2\), the problem is to partition the grid’s vertices into \(K\) connected districts of \(n^2/K\) vertices each, maximizing the number won by the target party. A district counts as won when its target-party mass is at least \(1/(2K)\), using the paper’s tie-breaking convention. The solution is the partition and its win count; the associated gerrymandering power is the maximum win fraction minus the party’s population share. The map and the assignment of precincts to districts remain those in the paper. Only voter counts within each precinct become fractional.

Theorem 2 anchors the \(K=2\) case: in this model, it suggests that the same \(1/2+1/n\) guarantee holds even when the precinct-party masses are rational. The authors’ proof uses the grid’s structure and exchanges between strips, so this is a close match to their districting question. For the full optimization problem, I would expect hardness to transfer: the connected-partition choices remain, and discrete instances with equal precinct populations embed by setting each \(q_v\) to the corresponding fraction of party voters. This is a prediction, not a result established by the paper; a transfer claim would need hardness for the matching discrete grid model. Further questions include the exact worst-case vote-share threshold for each \(n\), and the complexity of maximizing wins for general \(K\).

The scope is the paper’s worst-case districting analysis and its gerrymandering-power objective. It does not claim a mirror for the empirical urban-rural simulation or the heuristic algorithm’s performance. Its weakest point is that continuity leaves the main graph-partition choices intact, so this mirror may not yield a tractability gain. That does not make the population model implausible: precinct and party are complete types for the paper’s purposes, and many voters can share each type.

The case is therefore positive about the mirror’s plausibility, with one important limit: Theorem 2 is the closest named anchor, but the paper supplies no named complexity-classification result of the kind your anchor rule asks for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Its Theorem 1, Proposition 1, and Theorem 2 are structural guarantees about which districtings exist; the algorithmic contribution is a heuristic evaluated on experiments, not a complexity or approximation theorem. That matters here because the proposed mirror of Theorem 2 is another existence claim, not a computational question.

The proposed type \((v,P)\) is sensible: many voters can share a precinct and party, and their mass can be a rational proportion. But for fixed \(n\), Theorem 2’s strip-exchange proof uses inequalities, not integrality, so it extends directly to those fractional masses. And the paper already gives a continuous-measure treatment in Theorem 1 for the large-grid limit. Neither result classifies an algorithmic problem. Theorem 2 therefore does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The stronger alternative is to ask for the complexity of maximizing the number of districts won when precincts carry rational party masses. That is a coherent weighted graph-partition problem, and related computational work already studies graph districting with aggregated voter weights. That literature supports the plausibility of the high-multiplicity regime; it is not a reason to reject the mirror. But this optimization question is a new computational study, not a continuous version of a computational theorem in this paper. [A related weighted graph-gerrymandering model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09889) illustrates that distinction.

So I would reject Theorem 2 as a surviving computational anchor. The negative case is weaker against the broader optimization question: the precinct-party masses are a plausible population model, and I cannot honestly rule out that studying its algorithms would be worthwhile. My case against is narrower: this paper’s named results do not establish that it has a computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
