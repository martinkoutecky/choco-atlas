Why no mirror

The repeated-cohort LP is a coherent extension of the thesis’s aim of balancing agents across jobs. But the cited figure is an empirical comparison, and the thesis gives no numbered computational result anchoring that mirror; its described coalition-formation algorithms are implementations of prior work. The required computational-result gate therefore fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed model covers aggregate assignment of repeated job cohorts to agent teams; it leaves the thesis’s stateful MAPC simulation, algorithm integration, C-Link comparison, and TA-1 results outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • If the recipe set is implicit and team sizes or the type catalogue vary, can the highest-value feasible team recipe be found efficiently, or does pricing become hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The thesis has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result for one of its problems. Its computational evidence is algorithm descriptions and simulation experiments, so I cannot honestly supply the kind of formal anchor you require. The closest favorable result is Figure 5.1(a): with the job rate at 10% of maximum, after 1,000 steps the O-CF approach using the exact DC algorithm completed more jobs on average over 50 simulations than H-CF using C-Link or TA-3 using task allocation on batches of three jobs. That is an experimental comparison, not a complexity theorem.

The mirror I would pursue is Mass Coalition Formation for Repeated Job Cohorts. Consider a large urban service fleet and a large batch of jobs. An agent type records every feature the decision uses: vehicle capabilities, tools, capacity, costs, availability, and location or route profile. A job type records its required items and tools, destination, deadline band, and reward. The instance gives each agent type \(a\) a rational population share \(\mu_a\), with \(\sum_a \mu_a=1\), and each job type \(j\) a rational demand \(d_j\), measured per reference population.

A team recipe for job type \(j\) is an integer vector \(k\), where \(k_a\) is the number of agents of type \(a\) in one team. It must meet the job’s role and tool requirements. Let \(R_j\) be the admissible recipes, and let \(w_{jk}\) be the rational value of assigning that team to that job type, using the thesis’s reward, cost, marginal-contribution, and coalition-size considerations. The decision variable \(x_{jk}\geq 0\) is the mass of job instances served with recipe \(k\). Unused agent mass \(u_a\) and unserved job mass \(z_j\) are allowed. The problem is:

\[ \max \sum_j\sum_{k\in R_j} w_{jk}x_{jk} \]

subject to

\[ \sum_j\sum_{k\in R_j} k_a x_{jk}+u_a=\mu_a \quad\text{for every agent type }a, \]

\[ \sum_{k\in R_j}x_{jk}+z_j=d_j \quad\text{for every job type }j, \]

with all variables nonnegative. A solution is an optimal rational allocation of job mass to integral team recipes. Each actual team still contains whole agents in whole roles; only the share of repeated job instances assigned to each recipe is fractional.

The plausible regime is a metropolitan fleet with thousands of largely standardized service units and recurring jobs, spread across a fixed catalogue of vehicle roles and service zones. The number of units can greatly exceed the number of complete operational types. The thesis itself considers heterogeneous vehicle types and balances agents across jobs; this mirror scales that same decision to repeated cohorts. Its Section 2.2.4 also discusses agent-type representations of coalition values, citing [AdK11 and UKIY11]. Under ChoCo’s criterion, that is evidence that the high-multiplicity regime makes sense, not a novelty collision.

For a fixed role catalogue and bounded team sizes, there are finitely many recipes, and the stated problem is an LP. I would expect Class A in that regime. The harder follow-up is whether the recipe catalog can be kept implicit: can the pricing problem find the best team composition from a compact description of requirements and contributions without enumerating all recipes? With the number of types varying, that question could expose hardness in the recipe-selection structure. The thesis does not answer it. This mirror covers its optimal coalition-structure generation before task allocation, along with the job-balancing objective; it does not claim to continuize the whole MAPC simulator or C-Link.

The thesis’s favorable comparison is also sharply limited. In Figure 5.3, at the same 10% job rate, O-CF completes fewer jobs than TA-1, which starts task allocation as soon as each job arrives. The positive case is therefore for a large, repeated, batch-planning regime where balancing teams across a cohort is the object of interest—not a claim that coalition formation beats the thesis’s strongest task-allocation baseline in its contest setup. That is my weakest point: the paper’s own best comparison depends on waiting for three jobs, and its one-job-at-a-time comparison goes the other way. The large-cohort scenario is plausible and close to the authors’ stated motivation, but the thesis supplies no complexity result establishing that its continuous version would be tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to mirror. Figure 5.1(a) reports average completed jobs across 50 runs of a modified, 12-agent contest setup. The thesis gives no complexity theorem for its coalition-formation problem; it implements existing algorithms and compares their performance in simulation. The figure supports a claim about that setup, not a result about worst-case complexity or algorithms for a new problem.

The mass recipe LP is a sensible way to model repeated teams serving repeated jobs, but it changes what the thesis measures. In the experiments, agents have individual routes and changing loads and inventories; jobs have particular requirements and destinations; coalition formation is followed by sequential task allocation. A type definition that preserves those details may have to distinguish nearly every agent or job, so the proposed compact type catalogue disappears. Grouping them into zone and job classes with average values restores a small catalogue, but then the LP models static aggregate assignment rather than the thesis’s stateful simulation and its completed-job comparison.

That is a case against treating Figure 5.1(a) as an anchor, not a decisive case against every mirror. The thesis explicitly aims to balance agents across jobs, discusses type-based coalition values, and points toward domains with many concurrent tasks. A repeated-cohort optimization model could be close enough to that broader aim for the authors to recognize it as their problem. Its fixed-catalogue LP is coherent, and existing high-multiplicity work would support the regime rather than count against it.

So my negative case is narrow and ultimately weak: this thesis offers no complexity result to anchor directly, and the proposed LP abstracts away several features of the reported experiment. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
