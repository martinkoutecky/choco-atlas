The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no computational result for a continuous population to mirror. Theorems 3.3 and 3.4 bound the price of anarchy of PS and CPS; Theorem 3.5 gives a lower bound. These are welfare guarantees, not complexity classifications or algorithms for a computational problem. The lower bound is not a hardness result: it says the ratio can be large, not that computing anything is hard.

A large course-allocation population grouped by unit-sum valuation vectors is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. One could scale course capacities per capita and ask about continuum-agent PS or CPS. But the best computational-sounding proposal—given rational type masses, compute worst-equilibrium welfare or decide whether the price of anarchy exceeds a threshold—introduces an equilibrium-computation problem the paper does not study. Asking whether the stated bounds still hold over mass distributions returns to an analytic price-of-anarchy theorem. Neither gives the programme’s intended complexity mirror.

So the case against a mirror does not rest on there being no sensible types, or on probabilistic allocations making the population continuous. It rests on the missing computational target. That is a strong reason to exclude this paper from the programme, though it cannot establish that no worthwhile algorithmic question could ever be built around its models.
