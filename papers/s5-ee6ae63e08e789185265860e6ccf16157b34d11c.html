The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a group map \(g:T\to\{1,\ldots,k\}\), nonnegative rational contact intensities \(\lambda_{st}\), a deadline \(D\), and a seed-mass budget \(b\), choose \(y_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t y_t\le b\). Set \(a_t^0=x_t^0=y_t\), and for \(r=0,\ldots,D-1\) define \(a_t^{r+1}=(\mu_t-x_t^r)(1-\exp(-\sum_s\lambda_{st}a_s^r))\) and \(x_t^{r+1}=x_t^r+a_t^{r+1}\). For \(F_i(y)=\sum_{t:g(t)=i}x_t^D\), find a rational feasible seed-mass vector whose \(\sum_i\sqrt{F_i(y)}\) value is within additive \(\varepsilon\) of the maximum.

The model it lives in

A sparse typed contact network with \(n\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), where each directed type-\(s\)-to-type-\(t\) contact succeeds with probability \(\lambda_{st}/n\). Choose seed masses \(y_t\) under a population-scaled budget and maximize the sum of square-root group influence reached by deadline \(D\), using the stated type-level limit recurrence.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the budgeted fair surrogate \(P4\) and its greedy optimization question; it leaves the coverage formulations \(P2\) and \(P6\), the hard disparity constraint, and the empirical results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-level objective admit diminishing returns or another structure that yields an approximation guarantee polynomial in the input size, \(|T|\), \(D\), and \(1/\varepsilon\)?
  • Can a continuous seed-mass solution be rounded to useful seed sets in finite typed networks, with a quantified error?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of FAIRTCIM-BUDGET, the budgeted fair time-critical influence problem in Section 5.1.2. The paper’s anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Appendix A. It says that if \(\hat S\) is the greedy output for \(P4\), and \(S^*\) is an optimal solution to ordinary TCIM-BUDGET problem \(P1\), then
\[ f_\tau(\hat S;V,G)\ge (1-1/e)\,H\!\left(f_\tau(S^*;V,G)\right). \]
Here \(P4\) maximizes \(\sum_i H(f_\tau(S;V_i,G))\) subject to \(|S|\le B\), with \(H\) nonnegative, monotone, and concave. The theorem is an algorithmic guarantee for that fairness surrogate, not a guarantee that group utilities are equal. The paper asserts NP-hardness for \(P1\) and \(P2\) in prose, citing prior work, but gives no numbered hardness theorem to use as an anchor.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. Call it Mass-FAIRTCIM-BUDGET. Its input is a finite set \(T\) of agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), a group assignment \(g:T\to\{1,\ldots,k\}\), a deadline \(D\), a rational seed-mass budget \(b\), and nonnegative rational contact intensities \(\lambda_{st}\). A type records both the agent’s salient group and their contact profile: agents of type \(s\) have the same successful-contact intensity into every type \(t\). Interpret \(\lambda_{st}\) as the limit of a sparse network in which each directed \(s\)-to-\(t\) contact succeeds with probability \(\lambda_{st}/n\). Thus the society has \(n\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) in a finite population of size \(n\), while \(T\) stays modest.

A decision is a seed-mass vector \(y\), where \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_t\le b\). Define \(a_t^0=x_t^0=y_t\), with \(a_t^r\) the mass newly influenced at time \(r\) and \(x_t^r\) the cumulative mass influenced by then. For \(r=0,\ldots,D-1\), set
\[ a_t^{r+1}=(\mu_t-x_t^r)\left(1-\exp\!\left(-\sum_{s\in T}\lambda_{st}a_s^r\right)\right), \qquad x_t^{r+1}=x_t^r+a_t^{r+1}. \]
For group \(i\), let \(F_i(y)=\sum_{t:g(t)=i}x_t^D\), the mass of that group reached by the deadline. The problem is to find a feasible \(y\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{i=1}^k H(F_i(y)). \]
One fixed, fully specified choice is \(H(z)=\sqrt z\), which the paper also uses. A computational version asks for a rational seed-mass vector whose objective is within additive \(\varepsilon\) of optimum.

This is a population mirror: it keeps the paper’s group-sensitive, deadline-limited diffusion and seed-selection question, while replacing individual seed identities with chosen mass in each type. A plausible regime is a large platform distributing urgent job or public-health information, with many users but relatively few group-and-contact-profile types. The programme’s high-multiplicity premise is doing real work here: within this network model, agents of a type are interchangeable for propagation and seeding.

I expect the approximation version to be Class A. The type-level recurrence has only \(|T|\) state variables per time step, so evaluating an allocation is finite-dimensional; the natural next claim to prove is diminishing returns in the mass variables, which would put continuous submodular maximization methods in reach. I would ask the prover to establish an approximation guarantee and its dependence on \(|T|\), \(D\), input bit length, and \(\varepsilon\). The discrete theorem’s displayed bound should not simply be carried over: the continuous limit and the normalization from counts to mass need their own proof. Further questions include whether rounding a mass solution gives useful seed sets in a finite typed network, and whether the model admits stronger guarantees under restrictions on the contact matrix.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the problem underlying Theorem 1, not every result in the paper. It does not replace the network by fractional outcomes or random profiles; it makes the population the continuous object and keeps time-critical influence as the computational objective.

The weakest point is the network restriction. If one must preserve an arbitrary fixed graph, agents with the same demographic group can have very different influence positions; making their type complete may require a separate type for nearly every person. The mirror is most convincing in an exchangeable or block-mixing regime where group-and-contact types really are few. Also, \(P4\) is a surrogate for disparity, not a hard parity constraint, so this mirror does not by itself promise equal normalized coverage across groups.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole named anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 1, but its displayed bound does not follow from the valid greedy guarantee. For \(P4\), greedy gives a \((1-1/e)\)-approximation to its own objective, \(\sum_i H(f_\tau(S;V_i,G))\). The proof then turns that into a bound on total influence. That step is false for the stated class of \(H\): with one group and \(H(z)=2z\), which is nonnegative, monotone, and concave, the theorem demands \(f_\tau(\hat S;V,G)\ge 2(1-1/e)f_\tau(S^*;V,G)\), even though \(S^*\) maximizes total influence. The proof also treats the sum of transformed group utilities as though it were the transform of total utility. So the theorem’s particular total-influence guarantee is not a sound result to mirror. The valid computational anchor is the greedy approximation for the surrogate objective itself.

The proponent’s strongest model still survives that objection. In a sparse block-mixing network, agents sharing a group and contact profile can be interchangeable, and a type-mass seed decision can be meaningful. But this buys a continuum by changing what the problem is about. In the paper, seed selection acts on a fixed graph: where a node sits, its neighbors, and its centrality can determine its value. A finite contact-intensity matrix retains those features only under a random-mixing assumption. If preserving them requires giving agents distinct types for their local network positions, the type count may grow with the population. Otherwise, the optimization is over how to allocate seeds among blocks, rather than which nodes to seed in the given network.

There is also a scaling choice the mirror must defend. With sparse contacts \(p=\lambda/n\), a fixed seed budget and finite deadline produce vanishing influenced mass as \(n\) grows. The proposed model avoids that degeneration by scaling the budget to a positive fraction of the population. That is a plausible campaign regime, but it shifts the question from choosing a small set of influential nodes to allocating a large seed mass across types. It leaves open whether the resulting finite-dimensional optimization is a substantial computational-social-choice question, rather than a useful mean-field resource-allocation model.

A nearby “continuous influence maximization” treatment I found optimizes node-level controls on a finite network and uses a heterogeneous mean-field approximation; under ChoCo’s stated scope, that is not a continuous-population collision. [“Shadowing and shielding”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8213046/) The prior-art objection therefore does not defeat the mirror either.

So the strongest negative case is narrower than the requested universal claim: the proponent’s exact theorem anchor is defective, and its repair changes the graph and the scale of seeding. But the block-mixing regime is coherent, the population is genuinely the continuous object, and the question remains computational. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in that regime; the negative case is weak at precisely the point where it must defeat the best version.
