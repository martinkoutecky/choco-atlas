The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Its results characterize voting-game equilibria. So I cannot give a fully compliant positive case anchored in the kind of computational result the brief requires.

The strongest candidate is Corollary 1 (Groseclose and Milyo, 2010), which the paper cites and restates; its proof here points to Proposition 2. In a reduced, narrow, equal-weight simultaneous vote with quota \(k\), both \(N^+\) and \(N^-\) nonempty, and \(n_- = |N^-|\), if \(n_- \notin \{k-1,k\}\), the unique pure-strategy equilibrium has exactly \(N^-\) voting Yes. If \(n_- \in \{k-1,k\}\), no pure-strategy equilibrium exists. This is an equilibrium characterization, not a complexity classification.

A plausible continuous question is Atomless Narrow-Vote Equilibrium. Picture a very large one-member-one-vote union referendum, where members fall into a small number of recurring types because their issue preferences and costs of defying the union line are shared. Let \(T\) be a finite set of types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type records its benefit \(G_t\) and voting cost \(c_t\); restrict the instance to the paper’s reduced narrow-vote types, \(0<c_t<G_t\) or \(G_t<c_t<0\), with positive mass of each. A proposal passes if the Yes-vote mass is at least a rational quota \(q\). Each infinitesimal member chooses Yes or No and receives \(G_t\) if the proposal passes, minus \(c_t\) if they vote Yes. The question is to return the pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, as type-level Yes masses \(x_t\), and whether the proposal passes. This is an equilibrium problem, so it has no optimization objective.

In the atomless game, an individual cannot change the outcome with their vote. With strict costs, the equilibrium has \(x_t=\mu_t\) for \(c_t<0\) and \(x_t=0\) for \(c_t>0\); the proposal passes exactly when \(\mu_- \ge q\). This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many members, few payoff types, and the population represented by their shares. The problem is directly solvable by comparing rational masses, so I expect it to be tractable. It covers only Corollary 1’s reduced, equal-weight simultaneous-vote case; it does not cover the paper’s general decision rules, weighted examples, or sequential voting.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a continuous-population version of their simultaneous voting problem: it retains the Yes/No decision, voting costs, benefits, quota, and free-riding outcome. The serious weakness is that atomlessness removes individual pivotality—the strategic feature behind the finite equilibrium characterization. At the boundary \(\mu_-=q\), the continuous game has a unique equilibrium that passes the proposal, while the corresponding finite case \(n_-=k\) has no pure-strategy equilibrium under Corollary 1. Thus this is a credible extension to study, but not a clean limit identity for the paper’s result. The natural next questions are how finite equilibria behave near that boundary and whether a useful continuous version survives for weighted or multiple-majority rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not meet the programme’s target: Corollary 1 characterizes pure-strategy equilibria; the paper has no named result about computational complexity or algorithms. That is a decisive mismatch for a programme about the computational landscape.

The proposed atomless version is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, but its continuum limit removes the strategic feature that makes the paper’s result interesting. An individual has measure zero, so changing their vote cannot change whether the Yes mass reaches \(q\). Their action only affects their own voting cost: types with \(c_t<0\) vote Yes, and types with \(c_t>0\) vote No. The equilibrium calculation is just a comparison of rational masses with \(q\). At \(\mu_-=q\), this remains true, even though the corresponding finite game has no pure-strategy equilibrium. That gap is not a technicality to repair with a different quota convention: individual pivotality has disappeared. This is a continuum degeneration of the game’s central object.

Making each type a coordinated bloc could preserve pivotality, since an entire type’s mass could swing the outcome. But then the strategic players are blocs, rather than the individuals in the paper. With a small number of blocs, the population multiplicity no longer supplies the model’s central structure; with a complex rule over blocs, the computational difficulty would come from encoding that rule. The paper offers no computational result to anchor such a new problem.

The same problem reaches the stronger candidates. Weighted and multiple-majority rules can be extended naturally as thresholds on type masses, but in the atomless individual-voting game each voter is still non-pivotal. Sequential voting relies on an ordered list with a last voter for backward induction; a continuum has no such last individual. Retaining a finite set of pivotal voters or blocs may produce a legitimate hybrid model, but it would preserve those actors as atoms rather than continuize the population whose strategic behavior the paper studies. I see a plausible adjacent question about large finite electorates and their limiting equilibria, but that is an asymptotic game-theory question, not a worthwhile continuous computational mirror of this paper.
