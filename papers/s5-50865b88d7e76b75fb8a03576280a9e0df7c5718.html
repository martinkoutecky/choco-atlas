The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets on both sides, rational type masses, compatible contracts, and type-level valuations and thresholds, with each type represented by an atomless cohort, find a γ-popular matching whose matched mass is at least ¾ of the maximum possible. Popularity is tested against every feasible alternative matching on the same cohort members: a vote is zero exactly when the same individual contract pair is retained, and otherwise follows the paper’s thresholded vote rule. The matching and challenger must be represented by a joint coupling that records exact pairs retained and makes both matchings jointly feasible.

The model it lives in

A two-sided market of atomless cohorts with finitely many complete agent types and rational masses. A matching assigns mass along compatible contracts; a joint coupling of current and alternative assignments records exact pairs retained, so votes and matched mass are defined without naming individuals.

The objection that survived

The proposed transition tables do not enforce a jointly realizable pair of matchings or track the mass of exact individual pairs retained; a coupling that does both still needs a finite type-level formulation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

This mirrors Theorem 2’s γ-popularity guarantee and its ¾ comparison with the largest γ-popular matching; it leaves the paper’s hardness, exact computation, super-popularity, and experimental results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The affirmative case is strongest for one narrow target: a maximum-\(\gamma\)-popular matching over a population of repeated agent types. That keeps the paper’s central question—maximize the number of matched agents subject to popularity—and makes matched mass, rather than a count of named individuals, the objective.

My numbered anchor is Theorem 2, proved in §3.2. It says that the authors’ algorithm outputs a \(\gamma\)-popular matching \(M\) such that (i) for every matching \(N\), \(|M|\ge \tfrac23|N|\); (ii) for every \(\gamma\)-popular matching \(N\), \(|M|\ge \tfrac34|N|\); and (iii) for every \(\gamma\)-min stable matching \(N\), \(|M|\ge \tfrac45|N|\). The theorem also asserts that \(M\) itself is \(\gamma\)-popular. These guarantees are proved here. The adjacent §3.1 statement says that finding a maximum-size weakly popular matching is NP-hard even with ties on only one side, but its restatable theorem is shown without a numeral in the supplied text, so I do not invent one for it.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Mass-\(\gamma\)-Popular Matching, with a \(3/4\)-approximation guarantee. An instance has finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\) on the two sides, rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\), and allowed contracts \(E\subseteq A\times B\). The masses on each side sum to one. A type records everything the matching problem uses: its acceptable contracts, valuation \(p_a(e)\) or \(p_b(e)\), and threshold \(\gamma_e^a\) or \(\gamma_e^b\). Each mass unit receives at most one whole contract; the mass is divisible because it represents a cohort, not a fractional contract for one person.

A mass matching is a flow \(x_e\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{e\ni a}x_e\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_{e\ni b}x_e\le\nu_b\). Its size is \(\sum_e x_e\). To define popularity without discarding who changes assignment, an alternative matching \(y\) comes with transition tables for each type: \(z^a_{e,f}\) records the mass of type \(a\) moving from current assignment \(e\) to alternative assignment \(f\), where either may be unmatched, and likewise for type \(b\). Their row sums are the assignment masses under \(x\), their column sums the assignment masses under \(y\), and both sides must describe the same contract mass \(y_e\). A transition \(e\to f\) votes \(-1\) if the new contract improves by at least its threshold, \(+1\) otherwise, and \(0\) if \(e=f\). The mass matching \(x\) is \(\gamma\)-popular if every feasible alternative and transition plan has total vote mass at least zero.

The computational question is: given the rational type masses, contracts, valuations, and thresholds, return a \(\gamma\)-popular \(x\) whose size is at least \(3/4\) of the maximum size of any \(\gamma\)-popular mass matching. Measure running time in the number of types, \(|E|\), and the binary input length \(L\). This asks about the matching problem itself; the transition tables make the comparison well-defined, rather than changing the objective.

The natural regime is a large centralized assignment market with repeated profiles: for example, a public placement system with many applicants sharing preference lists over program or contract types, and many positions whose priorities are the same over applicant types. There might be hundreds of thousands of people but only hundreds or thousands of complete preference-and-threshold types. The assumption is not that all residents or applicants are interchangeable; it is that the market contains large cohorts that are interchangeable for every feature the matching problem uses. That seems plausible in some markets, and the paper’s own focus on matching many agents makes the mass objective natural. An individual still receives an integral assignment; only the type-level totals are fractional.

I would expect the \(3/4\)-approximation question to be tractable in that regime. Theorem 2 constructs an auxiliary matching instance, finds a stable matching, and projects it back. A type-mass version could use capacitated matching among types; the theorem’s size comparisons might then become inequalities over mass rather than counts. That is a research direction, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 2. A separate question is whether the *exact* maximum-size popular mass matching is tractable. The paper’s hardness result makes that a worthwhile target, but I would not claim the approximation theorem settles it. A mass formulation may remove hardness that depends on indivisible population counts, while hardness encoded in the type and contract structure could remain.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves two-sided assignments, their thresholded head-to-head votes, and the objective of maximizing matched agents. It mirrors Theorem 2’s guarantee directly and can also ask whether the \(4/5\) comparison with \(\gamma\)-min stable matchings survives when size means mass. The scope is that optimization result and its weak-popularity special case, not the paper’s super-popular existence question or its experimental averages.

The weakest point is identity. Weak popularity rewards an agent for keeping an equally valued partner when the alternative partner is different. Type-pair flows do not, by themselves, say whether the same two individuals remain paired. The transition tables retain assignment changes between types, but they treat contracts as type-level outcomes. That is faithful where counterpart class or contract type is the relevant outcome; where named counterpart identity matters, identities must enter the types, and the support may grow until there is little high-multiplicity gain. The positive case therefore depends on a real, but defensible, type-level market regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives a \(3/4\)-approximation for a defined optimization problem. The negative case has to be about whether that problem survives as a population model, not whether its guarantee would carry over.

The obstacle is the paper’s vote rule. In weak popularity, an agent gets \(0\) for keeping the same partner and \(+1\) for switching to a different but equally valued partner. In \(\gamma\)-popularity, the comparison likewise distinguishes the same edge from a different assignment that falls short of the improvement threshold. But a type-level matching records mass on edges between types, not which individuals are paired. If the alternative preserves the same type-level edge mass, it could preserve every actual pair or reshuffle all of them. Those cases produce different votes under the paper’s definitions. So the proposed flow \(x\), even with transition tables between type-level assignments, does not by itself define whether \(x\) is popular.

There are two ways to repair that, and neither cleanly preserves this mirror. Treating “same counterpart type” as “same partner” makes popularity a category-level switching rule. That may be a useful new model, but it discards the paper’s explicit distinction between staying with a partner and changing to an equally valued one. Preserving the distinction requires tracking which individual pairs persist across the current and alternative matchings. That information is absent from a distribution over a small set of types; encoding it at individual-pair resolution gives up the finite-support compression that motivates continuization. This is a modeling problem, not an objection that the continuous answer might differ from the discrete one.

The paper’s \(3/4\) proof also uses the structure of finite matchings: their symmetric differences decompose into alternating paths and cycles, and the approximation bounds follow from counting edges and path endpoints. Fractional mass matchings need a new comparison argument. That alone would not make the question unworthwhile, but it means the proposed theorem is not yet a well-specified mass analogue of Theorem 2.

This makes the negative case meaningful but not airtight. A market where one side consists of repeated cohorts and the other side’s partner categories are themselves the relevant real-world partners might support a type-level popularity rule that the authors would recognize. I cannot honestly rule out every such scenario. My case is that the particular weak- and \(\gamma\)-popularity anchor depends on individual pair identity, and the proposed finite-type flow does not retain it.
