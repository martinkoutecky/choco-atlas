The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(F\), committee size \(k\), fixed non-increasing weights \(w^{(k)}\) with \(w_i=0\) for \(i>\lambda k\), a finite support \(T\) of complete cost-vector types, and rational masses \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), choose \(W\subseteq F\) with \(|W|=k\) minimizing \(L_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}w_\ell c^\uparrow_\ell(W,t)\); determine its approximation complexity as a function of \(|F|\), \(|T|\), and the encoding length.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity OWA committee-selection model in which \(T\) contains complete client or approval types, \(\mu_t\) is type mass, \(W\) remains an integral \(k\)-committee, and the objective is the mass-weighted OWA cost.

The mirror covers Theorem 21's truncated-OWA hardness and Theorem 5's Harmonic \(k\)-median approximation, including Corollary 6 for PAV; it leaves the metric OWA result, \(p\)-geometric hardness results, and the negative-association theory without separate mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a defensible continuous mirror here. My lead anchor is the paper’s hardness result, because it gives a clean Class B case: the population can be replaced by mass, but the combinatorics remain in the candidates and committee choice.

The common mirror is this. There is a finite candidate/facility set \(F\), a committee size \(k\), and a finite set \(T\) of complete client types. A type \(t\) specifies its entire cost vector \(c_t:F\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\); in the approval-election specialization, it is an approval set \(A_t\subseteq F\), with cost \(0\) for approved candidates and \(1\) otherwise. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). The action remains integral: choose \(W\subseteq F\) with \(|W|=k\). Only the population has been continuized: \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\), and the objective is per-capita cost.

My lead is a continuous version of Theorem 21, which is proved in this paper, in Appendix E. Theorem 21 states:

Fix \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) and consider OWA \(k\)-median in which clients care only about the \(\lambda\)-fraction of facilities. For every positive computable function \(\alpha\), there is no polynomial-time \(\alpha\)-approximation algorithm unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof uses \(0/1\) costs already, so the result has a direct approval-voting interpretation. Define the Truncated OWA Population Committee problem as follows. The input is a candidate set \(F\), an integer \(k\), a fixed non-increasing weight sequence \(w^{(k)}=(w_1,\ldots,w_k)\) satisfying \(w_i=0\) whenever \(i>\lambda k\), a finite support \(T\) of approval types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). The task is to choose a committee \(W\) of size \(k\) minimizing

\[ L_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}w_\ell c^\uparrow_\ell(W,t), \]

where \(c^\uparrow_\ell(W,t)\) is the \(\ell\)-th smallest cost that type \(t\) assigns to members of \(W\). For an approval type \(A_t\), this is equivalently

\[ L_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \sum_{\ell=|W\cap A_t|+1}^{k}w_\ell. \]

Thus a voter’s satisfaction saturates once the committee contains enough approved representatives. A solution is an integral committee \(W\); an \(\alpha\)-approximation must return one whose value is at most \(\alpha\) times the optimum.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s OWA-Winner framework: a large electorate choosing a representative body, where many voters share the same approval profile because they belong to recurring demographic, geographic, professional, or issue-based blocs. We can have \(N\gg\tau\), with millions of voters but only dozens or hundreds of distinct approval types. The paper’s original question is unchanged except that a list of voters has become a histogram of voter types.

I expect hardness to transfer, placing this mirror in Class B. Given any discrete instance with voters \(D_1,\ldots,D_N\), create one continuous type for each distinct approval ballot and assign it mass equal to that ballot’s frequency divided by \(N\). The continuous objective is exactly \(1/N\) times the discrete objective, so every approximation algorithm transfers unchanged. The X3C reduction in Theorem 21 puts its combinatorics in the candidate/facility incidence structure and the choice of \(k\) facilities, not in the individuality of the voters. Replicating every voter many times can make the population arbitrarily high-multiplicity without changing the optimization problem.

The natural follow-up questions are whether the hardness survives metric costs, whether bounded support size \(\tau\) yields fixed-parameter algorithms, and whether the truncated-weight condition is the real source of hardness.

A second, positive anchor is Theorem 5, proved in Section 2. It states:

There exists a polynomial-time randomized algorithm for Harmonic \(k\)-median that gives a \(2.3589\)-approximation in expectation.

The theorem applies to general connection costs, without a triangle-inequality assumption. Its continuous mirror is the Harmonic Population \(k\)-Median problem. The input consists of \(F\), \(k\), a finite set of complete cost-vector types \(T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\). Choose \(W\subseteq F\), \(|W|=k\), minimizing

\[ H_\mu(W)= \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell}c^\uparrow_\ell(W,t). \]

The expected solution requirement is

\[ \mathbb{E}[H_\mu(\widehat W)] \le 2.3589\min_{|W|=k}H_\mu(W). \]

The paper’s proof lifts directly: replace each client block in the LP by one type block, multiply its contribution by \(\mu_t\), and apply the same dependent-rounding analysis type by type. The resulting running time is polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(k\), and the encoding length of the masses and costs, rather than in the number \(N\) of individual clients.

For \(0/1\) approval costs, this becomes a continuous PAV problem. If type \(A\) approves \(A\subseteq F\), then

\[ \sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell}c^\uparrow_\ell(W,A) = H_k-H_{|W\cap A|}, \]

so the objective is

\[ \sum_A\mu_A\bigl(H_k-H_{|W\cap A|}\bigr), \]

equivalently maximizing the mass-weighted PAV score \(\sum_A\mu_A H_{|W\cap A|}\). This is exactly the paper’s Corollary 6, also proved here: minimization PAV has a polynomial-time randomized \(2.3589\)-approximation in expectation.

This mirror is especially plausible in large elections, recommendation systems, or representative resource allocation: many agents share the same approval or service-cost profile, while the selected committee or facility set remains indivisible. The expected classification is Class A. Further questions include whether the \(2.3589\) factor can be improved or derandomized, and whether bounded \(\tau\) or special approval-type structure gives exact algorithms.

The weakest point is that the hardness anchor does not show population multiplicity itself causing hardness. Its reduction can be embedded by assigning separate types to the original clients, so it establishes a valid continuous mirror and hardness transfer, but not a continuum-specific obstruction. The harmonic/PAV anchor is stronger on the high-multiplicity motivation: it gives a natural \(N\gg\tau\) regime and an algorithm whose input is genuinely a distribution over types. Together, the two anchors show that this paper supports exactly the programme’s intended trichotomy: some OWA population problems remain hard because the combinatorics survive, while the harmonic/PAV case admits a clean continuous approximation theory.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly sustain the requested universal negative. The strongest objections reduce the novelty or ambition of the mirrors, but they do not defeat them as worthwhile continuous-population questions.

Theorem 21 survives cleanly. Its clients are described entirely by approval sets or cost vectors, so identity is irrelevant: two clients with the same vector are already one complete type. With rational masses \(\mu_t\), the objective is simply the normalized discrete objective,

\[ L_\mu(W)=\sum_t \mu_t\sum_{\ell=1}^k w_\ell c^\uparrow_\ell(W,t). \]

The X3C construction can therefore be encoded as a distribution over types, and duplicating every type arbitrarily many times creates an electorate with genuinely high multiplicity without changing the problem. The fact that the hardness lives in candidate incidence and committee choice does not count against the mirror; it is precisely a valid Class B outcome under the programme. Nor does the zero-gap nature of the approximation hardness make the question ill-posed.

The best criticism is that this theorem does not show population multiplicity causing the hardness. One can represent the reduction with one type per original client, so the continuous formulation does not yet expose a new \(\tau\)-dependent phenomenon. But that limits what the mirror teaches; it does not invalidate the mirror. The natural follow-up—how complexity changes when the number of approval types is small—is itself a legitimate continuous-population question.

Theorem 5 is even harder to defeat. Replacing clients by types gives the LP

\[ \min \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \sum_{\ell=1}^k\sum_{i=1}^m \frac{1}{\ell}x^\ell_{it}c_{it}, \]

with the same feasibility constraints, now indexed by types and weighted by \(\mu_t\). Every step of the dependent-rounding proof is typewise, so the \(2.3589\)-approximation carries over with running time polynomial in \(m\), \(k\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length. This is a genuine high-multiplicity compression from an explicit client list to a distribution over complete cost types.

Here the strongest negative point is that the extension is technically straightforward, and the paper already discusses client multiplicities in Section 3. Thus the continuous result may be less conceptually dramatic than a new pricing theorem or a continuum-specific hardness result. But “the proof is a weighted repetition of the discrete proof” is not a valid objection under the programme: simple LP consequences are explicitly within scope, and existing high-multiplicity treatment supports rather than defeats the modelling choice.

The same applies to Corollary 6. For approval types,

\[ H_\mu(W) = \sum_A \mu_A\bigl(H_k-H_{|W\cap A|}\bigr), \]

equivalently maximizing the mass-weighted PAV score. Approval blocs, recurring demographic groups, and issue coalitions are entirely plausible high-multiplicity populations. The committee remains integral, but that is allowed: continuity concerns the society, not necessarily the selected bundle. Nothing collapses, and no individual identity is needed.

So the negative case can fairly say that these mirrors are mostly Class B or routine Class A instances, rather than the paper’s continuous version revealing a deep new boundary. It can also ask whether the programme’s time is better spent on problems where population multiplicity changes the complexity mechanism itself. But those are prioritization arguments, not grounds for claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. On the stated standard, both anchors survive.
