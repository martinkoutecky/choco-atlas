What the mirror covers

The proposal invokes Theorem 1's exact support result for independent bargaining types; it does not extend the paper's implementation analysis, and Proposition 1 is only mentioned as an analogous result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind the brief requires. Its closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for the bargaining game \((U,0)\), the modified Rubinstein game \(\widetilde G(U,0)\) has infinitely many subgame perfect equilibria, all with the same equilibrium path and payoff \(z^*=N(U,0)\). Proposition 1 gives the analogous support result using weakly subgame perfect equilibrium. These are equilibrium-existence and support results; neither asserts a complexity bound or studies an algorithm. So neither is a qualifying computational anchor.

The strongest positive case I can make is a batch version of Theorem 1. Consider many independent local bargaining pairs—for example, standardized labor or procurement negotiations. Treat a *pair* as the unit of the population. A type \(t\) completely specifies that pair’s bargaining environment and game; \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of all pairs. In the high-multiplicity regime, the number of pairs is large while the support size \(\tau\) is small.

A precise candidate problem is Mass-aggregated exact Nash support. An instance is a finite list of pair types \(t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and for each type a finite description of a bargaining game \((U_t,0)\) covered by the paper’s construction. Ask for a type-specific proposal \(z_t\in U_t\) and a strategy profile for \(\widetilde G(U_t,0)\), represented by the paper’s proposal and rejection rules. A solution must make that profile subgame perfect for each type and give \(z_t=N(U_t,0)\). The aggregate payoff is then \(\bar z=\sum_t\mu_t z_t\). There is no welfare objective: the task is to produce equilibrium support and its mass-weighted outcome. Theorem 1 is the anchor for this candidate, though not a qualifying computational anchor.

The expected direction is tractability if the games have finite encodings: solve the Nash bargaining problem for each supported type, construct its equilibrium rule, then aggregate over \(\tau\) types. Further questions include whether these equilibrium certificates can be produced efficiently for a useful encoded class of feasible sets, and whether support survives when different bargaining pairs are coupled by shared resources.

This is at most a weak positive mirror. The paper’s central result concerns two players within one negotiation; the proposed continuum is over many negotiation pairs. Applying the theorem independently to each pair may be a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but it does not make either bargaining player population-continuous, and it adds no complexity result to the paper. The outcome—the divisible pie—is already continuous, while the population in the paper remains two people. The mirror therefore depends on ChoCo accepting negotiation pairs as the population units; the authors might recognize it as a batch application of their theorem, but could reasonably reject it as a continuous form of their own problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 establish equilibrium-support facts: the modified Rubinstein game has weakly subgame perfect, or subgame perfect, equilibria with the Nash solution as their payoff. They give no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem. The paper’s feasible pie division is already continuous; its society is still exactly two players. Neither theorem makes that population continuous.

The proposed “Mass-aggregated exact Nash support” changes the unit being counted from people to bargaining pairs. That can describe a real batch of standardized negotiations, but each pair remains the paper’s two-player game. If pairs are independent, the mass-weighted outcome is just \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t\) after finding each type’s Nash solution. The theorem supplies the support separately for each type; the aggregation adds no strategic or computational interaction. Calling pairs the population units makes this a high-multiplicity batch application, not a continuous-population version of the paper’s result.

A stronger version could couple the pairs through shared resources or require their proposals to satisfy a common constraint. That might produce a meaningful continuous optimization problem. But coupling the outcomes is not enough to inherit the paper’s equilibrium-support theorem: coupling the bargaining stages changes the game, while imposing only an aggregate feasibility constraint leaves open how the players’ strategic choices respond to it. Either route needs a new model and new results. It may be worthwhile research, but it is not a stronger mirror of Theorem 1 or Proposition 1.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: the paper offers a mathematical equilibrium result, not a computational anchor, and its natural batch extension is separable. I cannot rule out a useful continuous bargaining model for a particular application; the weak point in a universal rejection is that a coupled market of recurring negotiations could be valuable in its own right. But that would be a new population problem inspired by this paper, rather than the computational mirror ChoCo is seeking.
