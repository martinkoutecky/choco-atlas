The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), and \(\tau\) complete ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), run PFR on the type masses, with each type consuming at a rate proportional to its mass, candidate capacities \(1\), and stopping when total consumed mass is \(k\). Let \(p_c\) be the consumed amount of candidate \(c\). Can an exact sampler output a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) with \(\Pr[c\in W]=p_c\) for every candidate and with every sampled committee satisfying continuous PSC: for every set \(S\subseteq C\) and integer \(\ell\in[k]\), if the mass of types ranking all of \(S\) above \(C\setminus S\) is at least \(\ell/k\), then \(|W\cap S|\ge\min(|S|,\ell)\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over complete ranking types, represented by rational masses. PFR produces candidate marginals \(p_c\); the output is a lottery, or exact sampler, over integral size-\(k\) committees with those marginals and continuous PSC in its support.

The objection that survived

Theorem 5 does not bound the cost of building its graph or sampling from it, and its construction ranges over solid coalitions, so the paper does not establish that the proposed sampler is efficient.

fatal: False

Open questions for a prover

  • Can PFR marginals be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • Can the dependent-rounding construction be built and sampled from within the same bound using only the type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s PFR lottery construction. There is an important limitation: the paper contains no numbered theorem stating a complexity result such as polynomial-time solvability or hardness. So, under the strict anchor criterion, it has no qualifying anchor. The closest is Theorem 5, proved here: Phragmén’s fractional rule (PFR) is candidate monotone, satisfies ex-ante PSC, and can be decomposed into a distribution over committees satisfying PSC. That is a result about a rule’s guarantees and the existence of a decomposition, not a runtime bound.

A concrete computational mirror of that construction would be Continuous PFR Sampling. Its input is a candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a list of \(\tau\) complete rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Treat each unit of mass as nonatomic voters of that ranking. Run PFR, scaling the eating rate so candidate capacities remain \(1\) and the process stops after total mass \(k\) has been eaten; let \(p_c\) be the resulting marginal for candidate \(c\). The task is to produce an exact sampler for size-\(k\) committees \(W\) such that \(\Pr[c\in W]=p_c\), and every sampled committee satisfies continuous PSC: for every candidate set \(S\) and integer \(\ell\in[k]\), if the mass of voters ranking all of \(S\) above \(C\setminus S\) is at least \(\ell/k\), then \(|W\cap S|\ge\min(|S|,\ell)\). A solution is a sampler with those exact marginals and that support guarantee.

The regime is plausible: a large electorate may consist of many people sharing a relatively small number of complete rankings, with \(\tau\ll n\). Ranking is the type because it fully specifies the preference information this rule uses; mass is the population share. This preserves the paper’s PFR rule and its committee outcomes. The task asks whether its lottery can be computed efficiently from the type masses, rather than from a long list of individual voters. The authors would likely recognize it: they construct the lottery from PFR and dependent rounding, and report that they know of no computationally efficient way to extract the probabilities from the resulting graph.

I would expect Class A to be plausible, but not established: aggregate the eating process by type and seek an efficient dependent-rounding sampler. Theorem 5 supplies the guarantee to target, not the runtime result. Further questions include whether exact PFR marginals can be computed in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), and whether the rounding construction admits a sampler with that bound.

The soft spot is that this is a computational problem *about implementing* Theorem 5, not a complexity result proved by the paper. Moreover, rational masses can be represented by duplicating voters, so the continuous formulation does not change the theorem’s mathematical guarantee; its promise is a potentially much faster high-multiplicity computation. This case covers Theorem 5’s PFR lottery construction alone.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 3 proves an axiomatic monotonicity property, and Theorem 5 proves guarantees for a lottery; neither classifies complexity or gives a runtime bound. The paper’s central open question—whether a resolute rule can satisfy PSC and candidate monotonicity—is also axiomatic. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that leaves no direct complexity anchor.

The best proposed replacement is exact sampling from PFR on a society given by \(\tau\) ranking types and rational masses. That is a coherent high-multiplicity regime: large elections can have many voters but relatively few ballot types. But it is an implementation question around Theorem 5’s constructive proof, not a computational result of the paper. The proof already gives the pipeline: compute PFR marginals, build a bipartite graph, and apply dependent rounding. The paper’s remark about not efficiently extracting probabilities from that graph concerns the probabilities of committees; the proposed task only asks to draw a committee, which is a different requirement. Coalescing identical rankings into types also appears to leave only the supported ranking prefixes to process, rather than a new population-dependent structure. So the case for a substantial complexity investigation is thin: the mirror may amount to a runtime analysis of an existing construction.

A more ambitious mirror could ask for a mass-perturbation version of candidate monotonicity, since shifting a single voter has zero mass in an atomless society. But that changes the paper’s statement into a new axiomatic question. It does not recover a computational anchor, and the paper gives no reason to expect that this variant would expose a complexity landscape.

That is the strongest negative case, and it is limited. The high-multiplicity setting is sensible, and exact sampling could still be a useful algorithmic result—especially if the construction resists compression. If ChoCo counts such implementation questions as worthwhile mirrors, the case against does not rule this one out.
