The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid ChoCo case from this paper under the anchor requirement. It contains no numbered result asserting a complexity classification or algorithm for a computational problem.

The closest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper. Theorem 1 says that if a choice rule is implementable by a procedurally fair mechanism, it must be permutation monotonic. Theorem 2 says that, for \(n\ge 3\), permutation monotonicity together with no-veto power is sufficient for such implementation. These are characterization results about whether a mechanism exists; they do not analyze computational complexity. The unnumbered corollary comparing ordinary and procedurally fair implementation is also a characterization, not a complexity result.

There is a plausible neighboring question: represent a large population by masses of agents with finitely many preference types, and ask whether a choice rule over those population distributions has a procedurally fair Nash-implementing mechanism. But that is not a faithful, ready-to-hand-to-a-prover mirror of either theorem. With a nonatomic population, an individual’s unilateral deviation has zero mass, so Nash implementation changes unless the equilibrium concept or the strategic units change. Either change would need new modeling choices the paper does not make. And even after making them, the question would be an implementation-existence characterization, not the complexity question ChoCo studies.

So the strongest honest positive-side observation is that the paper’s finite-agent characterization could motivate a new study of implementation in finite-type populations. It supplies no qualifying computational anchor, however, and I would not present that new study as a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 gives a necessary condition for procedurally fair Nash implementation; Theorem 2 gives a sufficient condition. Both characterize when an implementation exists. Neither studies an algorithm, running time, or complexity class. Their continuous counterparts could be worthwhile economic theory, but they would not chart the computational landscape ChoCo is meant to study.

The best candidate for a population model is the no-envy example: take a distribution over finitely many preference types and assign bundles per type, or use a richer allocation rule when people of the same type receive different bundles. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But the theorems concern incentives under individual deviations and fairness under permutations of individual agents. If the model records only type masses and aggregate outcomes, a single agent’s deviation leaves the population distribution unchanged, so the Nash condition loses the individual incentive check. If the model keeps pointwise identities and allocations so that the check survives, the type distribution alone no longer describes the relevant object. Replacing individual deviations with deviations by positive-mass groups could resolve that tension, but it changes the equilibrium concept and asks a new implementation question.

One could add a computational task—for example, decide whether a finitely encoded choice rule is permutation monotonic, or find a mechanism for a rational type distribution. But the paper supplies no finite encoding of the state space, choice rule, or mechanism from which to define that task. Its complexity would depend on those new representation choices; the continuous population would not turn either theorem into a computational result.

So the affirmative case’s “neighboring question” is a possible new research direction, not a mirror of a result in this paper. A measure-theoretic extension of the characterizations may have value on its own, especially for no-envy allocation, but I see no continuous mirror here that fits ChoCo’s computational remit.
