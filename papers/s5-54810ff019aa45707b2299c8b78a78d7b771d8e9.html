Why no mirror

The paper’s main theorem characterizes a choice rule through axioms, and its top-cycle result is structural. Applying the rule after deriving a tournament from ranking masses is a coherent representation, but it is not a continuous analogue of a computational result in the paper.

What the mirror covers

No named result is covered; the characterization theorem and the structural results on flexibility, monotonicity, and Pareto suboptimality remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a natural population-continuous representation of the paper’s choice problem. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking of the alternatives, and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of a large policy electorate. For each pair \(a,b\), the sign of
\[ \sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t-\sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\mu_t \]
gives the majority comparison, assuming there are no ties. This defines a tournament \(T_\mu\). Given the paper’s priority ordering \(P\) and agenda \(A\), the rule first forms \(\max(T_\mu\cap P;A)\), then chooses the majority-maximal alternative in that shortlist. A plausible regime is a large consultation on a small slate of policies, where many participants share rankings and the priority—for example, distance from the status quo—is set in advance. Here mass is a share of participants, and the decision is still one alternative.

One possible computational question would be: given the ranking types and their rational masses, \(P\), and \(A\), output the alternative selected by the two-stage rule. Its solution is the selected alternative; tallying the pairwise comparisons and applying the rule takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). This is a faithful way to feed a continuous society into the paper’s model. But it is a thin computational mirror: the authors deliberately take the tournament as input, so the distribution adds no information needed for their choice, and evaluating the rule is straightforward.

More importantly, the paper has no eligible named computational anchor. Its main result is printed as “Theorem,” without a number, and characterizes the choice rules through Expansion Consistency, Weak WARP, Choice IIA, and Reversal Improvement. It is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about computational complexity. The top-cycle flexibility result in Section 4.2 is also not a numbered complexity result. Neither can anchor a ChoCo question under the stated criteria. So while a continuous-population representation is plausible, I cannot honestly present the evaluation problem—or a new priority-design or robustness problem—as a computational mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its unnumbered Theorem is an axiomatic characterization of the choice rules. The top cycle result and the monotonicity properties are structural claims, not complexity results. The paper establishes no complexity bound for bribery, control, campaigning, winner robustness, or even rule evaluation. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is decisive.

The proponent’s electorate model is coherent: from ranking masses \(\mu\), compute each pairwise margin
\[ M_{ab}(\mu)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t-\sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\mu_t, \]
then use its signs to form \(T_\mu\). But the rule factors through that tournament: \(f_P(\mu,A)=f_P(T_\mu;A)\). Once the pairwise signs are fixed, the masses no longer affect the choice. The distribution space is partitioned by the hyperplanes \(M_{ab}(\mu)=0\); within each region, the tournament and the rule’s choice are fixed. Computing the result from masses is therefore an implementation of the paper’s tournament input, not a computational question the paper studies.

A stronger proposal would be to ask how much mass must be reallocated, under a specified cost such as Kendall distance, to change the chosen alternative. That is a plausible population-level robustness problem. But it adds an intervention model, a cost, and a robustness objective that the paper does not contain; it asks a new question about distances between tournament regions. It could be worthwhile as a separate project, but it does not rescue this article as a continuous mirror. Optimizing the priority would likewise change the paper’s exogenous priority into a design problem.

I would not claim that no future computational work on these rules could be valuable. The negative case is weaker against such adjacent projects than against the proposed mirror. Its firm conclusion is narrower: this paper offers no named computational result for continuization, and lifting its choice rule to a continuous electorate only repackages the tournament input.
