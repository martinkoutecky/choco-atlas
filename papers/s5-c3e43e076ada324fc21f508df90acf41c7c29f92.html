The continuous mirror question

Given a finite deployment-query support \(Z\) with rational probabilities \(q_z\) and feature differences \(\alpha_z\in\mathbb{Q}^k\), and a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), preference vectors \(\theta_t\), and welfare constraints \(\gamma_t^\top\psi\geq c_t\), find an optimal impact \(\psi\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\theta_t^\top\psi\). The feasible impacts are \(\psi=\sum_{z\in Z}q_zs_z\alpha_z\), where \(-\tfrac12\leq s_z\leq\tfrac12\); assume the constraints are feasible. Equivalently, solve the resulting rational LP.

The model it lives in

Use a type as a complete preference-and-protection profile, with mass \(\mu_t\). The decision variables \(s_z\) encode the impact \(\psi=\sum_{z\in Z}q_zs_z\alpha_z\); maximize mass-weighted welfare subject to one linear welfare constraint per type and the bounds \(-\tfrac12\leq s_z\leq\tfrac12\).

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Type-Continuous Constrained Alignment. The population, rather than the impact space, is what I continuize: many people are represented by a distribution over preference types. The paper’s impact vectors are already continuous, but that alone would not make this a population mirror.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It considers maximizing utilitarian welfare over the feasible impact set subject to linear welfare constraints. If the constraints are feasible, an optimum exists; there are nonnegative multipliers for which that optimum also maximizes a tilted linear objective over the impact set, and each multiplier satisfies complementary slackness. Only constraints with positive multipliers tilt the objective. The theorem also says the optimal value is weakly decreasing and concave as the constraint thresholds rise. This is the closest computational anchor: the proof represents the problem as a finite LP, and the paper computes constrained alignments in its experiments. The theorem does not state a polynomial-time bound, and the paper gives no named hardness result.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. An instance has a finite, explicitly listed deployment-query support \(Z\), rational query probabilities \(q_z\), and rational feature-difference vectors \(\alpha_z\in\mathbb{Q}^k\). It also has a finite set \(T\) of preference types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational preference vector \(\theta_t\in\mathbb{Q}^k\) for each type. Each type may also have a linear welfare requirement \(\gamma_t^\top\psi\geq c_t\); the harm-floor version sets \(\gamma_t=\theta_t\). A type is complete for this problem: agents sharing it have the same preferences and the same relevant constraint parameters.

The question is to choose an optimal feasible impact \(\psi\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\theta_t^\top\psi\), subject to every type’s requirement. The feasible impact set is \(\bar\Psi=\{\sum_{z\in Z}q_z s_z\alpha_z:s_z\in[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]\}\), so the question is a finite LP: use one variable \(s_z\) per listed query, box constraints on those variables, and one welfare constraint per type. A solution is an optimal impact (equivalently, optimal LP variables). As in the paper’s use of the closure \(\bar\Psi\), an optimal boundary impact need not come from a finite model parameter; it is an impact-space answer, and exact finite-parameter implementation at such a boundary is a further question.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a widely deployed public or civic AI system: many affected people, but a moderate number \(\tau=|T|\) of stable preference-and-protection profiles. For example, several large constituencies might have shared feature-based evaluations of a standardized system and shared welfare floors. The premise is about that deployment regime, not about all alignment settings. The paper itself already aggregates individual utilities and imposes welfare constraints, so replacing repeated identical agents by their masses preserves the problem’s objects. I expect the authors to recognize this as their constrained alignment problem over a high-multiplicity population, rather than a change to outcome-space continuity.

For explicitly represented rational inputs, this mirror is Class A: the LP has \(|Z|\) variables and \(O(|Z|+\tau)\) constraints, with running time polynomial in \(k\), \(|Z|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The input describes masses directly, so the running time need not depend on the potentially enormous number of people behind their denominators. This covers Theorem 4’s constrained-welfare result, including harm floors; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s strategyproofness, finite-temperature RLHF, or participation-externality results.

The resulting questions are whether succinct or exponentially large query supports admit efficient pricing or separation; whether welfare floors over a genuinely continuous type distribution can be represented or separated efficiently; and when an optimal impact on the boundary can be replaced by a finite model parameter while preserving the constraints.

My weakest point is that the paper is not a complexity paper: it establishes the LP structure and computes examples, but does not analyze input bit complexity or claim a complexity class. The tractability claim belongs to this precise, explicit-support mirror, and rests on encoding the paper’s impact-space problem as a finite rational LP.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4 gives an existence and multiplier characterization, not a complexity result. Its proof reduces the finite problem to an LP; the proposed mirror then adds a direct high multiplicity encoding with one query variable per \(z\) and one constraint per type \(t\). So its polynomial time claim comes from that explicit LP formulation, rather than from a complexity question the paper itself studies. The theorem’s multiplier and value-function statements also carry over by replacing finite sums with mass-weighted sums; they do not expose a new computational role for the population limit.

There is a second, weaker concern about the proposed regime. The paper’s preference vectors are cardinal vectors in \(\mathbb R^k\), and its empirical agents are fitted separately. Exact preference-and-protection types could therefore be nearly as numerous as people. Coarsening similar vectors would change welfare objectives and harm constraints. The constituency scenario with shared types is possible, but it is an additional stylized assumption rather than a regime demonstrated by the paper’s data.

Neither point defeats the best version of the mirror. A large population with a moderate number of genuinely shared preference and protection types is a sensible high multiplicity setting, and Theorem 4’s constraints do not depend on individual identity once those types are specified. The paper’s finite LP and zonotope make the proposed problem precise. I can argue that this is a modest extension of the paper’s optimization framework, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives.
