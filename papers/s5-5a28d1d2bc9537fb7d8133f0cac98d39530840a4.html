What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-on would concern ex ante \(\chi\)-payouts in a role-based market; it does not computationally mirror Theorem 2.2, Theorems 3.5 and 4.8, or Theorem 5.5, which are axiomatic characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s allocation problem. Imagine a large recurring exchange market with many participants but few relevant roles—such as left- and right-glove holders, plus any compatibility or bargaining traits that affect coalition worth. A type records every feature the game uses; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the population. A mass-balanced configuration groups those types into coalitions, and a separate probability law represents uncertainty over configurations. The natural computational target would be the ex ante \(\chi\)-payout per type, retaining \(\chi\)’s sensitivity to outside options. This fits the paper’s motivation of forecasting payoffs before coalitions form, and the market scenario could have many agents but few types.

That is a promising direction, but it does not supply an eligible anchor. The paper has no named computational result: it proves no complexity classification or algorithm for a computational problem, and it does not compute with an LP, MILP, or other formulation. Its named results are axiomatic characterizations. For example, Theorem 2.2, cited from Casajus (2009), characterizes the deterministic \(\chi\)-value; Theorems 3.5 and 4.8, proved here, characterize the probabilistic \(\chi\)-value; and Theorem 5.5, proved here, characterizes the probabilistic Aumann–Drèze value. Their claims are about uniqueness under specified axioms, not computational tractability or hardness. So there are zero anchors under the stated rules, and no anchor-indexed continuous problem or expected A/B/C classification can honestly be given.

The weak point in the positive case is substantial: the paper allows an arbitrary worth function on named players and a distribution over their partitions. Those inputs need not respect types. A population mirror would need worth and coalition formation to depend only on type composition, while preserving the global outside-option effect that distinguishes \(\chi\) from the component-restricted Aumann–Drèze value. It would also need a precise definition of the continuous \(\chi\)-payout—perhaps as a limit of finite high-multiplicity instances—and a proof that the limit is well-defined. That could be a sensible follow-on computational problem, but the paper does not define or analyze it. The paper supports the motivation for such a mirror; it does not provide a computational anchor for one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest reason against a mirror is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2.2 is an imported axiomatic characterization; Theorems 3.5 and 4.8 give two characterizations of the same probabilistic \(\chi\)-value; Theorem 5.5 characterizes the probabilistic Aumann–Drèze value. None classifies the complexity of a problem or presents an algorithm. Recasting those characterizations over a continuous population would still be an axiomatic question, outside the programme’s computational scope.

The proponent’s role-based exchange market is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario: many participants can share a small number of game-relevant types. But computing their ex ante payouts would be a new problem, not a continuous version of one the paper studies. The paper defines those payouts as probability-weighted sums of deterministic values. With a worth table and a distribution over partitions listed explicitly, those sums can be computed from the listed data; if the inputs instead use compact encodings, the resulting complexity depends on the encoding chosen for the worth function and the partition law. The paper supplies no canonical compact representation or computational task that would make this an anchored mirror.

One could develop that follow-on project carefully. It would need a type-composition game, a law over mass-level coalition structures, and a definition of outside-option-sensitive \(\chi\)-payouts for those structures. In particular, it would have to choose how worth scales as the population grows and how the finite-player Shapley component becomes a payoff density. Those choices may be defensible, but they are new modelling and mathematical work; they do not turn the paper’s axiomatic results into computational anchors. Adding bribery, control, or another intervention to obtain an algorithmic task would move still further beyond the paper.

So I would reject the proposed mirror for this programme, while not claiming that no worthwhile continuous coalition-value project could exist. The negative case is strong on the anchor requirement and weaker on the universal claim: the role-based market makes a plausible research setting. Its weakness is that this setting supports a new project, not a computational mirror of Belau’s results.
