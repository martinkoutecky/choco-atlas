The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuum-population version of the paper’s uniform public-lottery pricing problem. But there is a hard limit: the paper has no numbered computational result establishing that a problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifying its complexity. So there is no qualifying anchor for a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The nearest numbered results are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in this paper. Proposition 1 says that, given \(Q^*\) and \(F(v)\), uniform lotteries have equal expected revenues if and only if they attract the same number of entrants. Proposition 2 says that, given \(Q^*\) and \(F(v)\), lotteries attracting the same number of entrants have equal conditional mean and variance of consumer surplus. These are useful structural equivalences, but neither is a computational complexity result. The optimization and inverse-elasticity characterization in equations (3)–(7) are not a numbered complexity theorem either.

A credible mirror scenario is a large public-resource programme—such as permits or admissions—with capacity scaled to the size of the eligible population. A type is an applicant’s private value, along with the paper’s common assumptions of unit demand, risk neutrality, and eligibility. The mass \(\mu_i\) of type \(v_i\) is that type’s fraction of applicants; \(q\) is the number of units supplied per applicant. This is plausible when many applicants fall into a modest number of valuation classes. Scaling capacity per capita is a co-modification needed to keep the lottery meaningful as the population grows.

One precise candidate is Continuous Public-Lottery Pricing. Given rational support values \(v_1<\cdots<v_\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to one, and rational supply \(q\), choose a uniform user price \(p\) and an entry fraction for applicants at the cutoff, if needed. All entrants face the same lottery odds; feasibility requires entrant mass at least \(q\). The objective is to maximize expected aggregate consumer surplus per capita: \(q\) times the entrants’ mean value minus \(p\). Return an optimal price, cutoff participation, and the resulting surplus. This asks the paper’s underlying pricing question with the society itself represented by masses. It is not a problem about continuously divisible goods or about lotteries as a new form of uncertainty.

I would expect this finite-support version to be tractable: sort the valuation classes and check the possible cutoffs, with partial entry at a cutoff when needed to meet capacity. That is a coherent candidate mirror of the paper’s Section II pricing problem, and the fee-equivalence propositions support representing the uniform pricing choice through a user fee. Further questions include whether the finite-\(N\) model converges to this atomless model with a useful error bound, and how the hybrid mechanism changes when a fraction of per-capita supply is sold competitively.

The weakness is substantial. This candidate has no named computational anchor, and the paper already reasons extensively through the value distribution \(F\), so the authors could regard the atomless formulation as a close reformulation of their existing analysis. I would offer it as a plausible continuous-population problem, but not claim that this paper supplies the required complexity result to anchor a ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 are economic equivalences: they relate revenues, and the mean and variance of consumer surplus, across lotteries with the same number of entrants. Neither defines an algorithmic problem or makes a complexity claim. Equations (3)–(7) characterize an optimal price, and equations (13)–(15) extend that analysis to a hybrid mechanism. Turning any of these into a complexity question would add a new computational problem; it would not mirror a computational result in the paper.

The proposed scenario is plausible. In a large public-resource programme, many applicants could share a valuation class, and capacity could sensibly scale with population. The classes’ values and masses define complete types, so the high-multiplicity objection that individuals have different private values does not work. Per-capita supply is also a reasonable co-modification. I would not dismiss the mirror on either ground.

The harder question is whether that plausible model is a worthwhile *computational* mirror. The proposal takes the paper’s distributional pricing analysis and adds a finite-support, rational-input specification, then asks for an optimal price and cutoff participation. That could be a legitimate follow-on problem, but it is an externally supplied computational wrapper: the paper neither poses that input-output problem nor identifies a computational bottleneck or complexity result around it. Proposition 1, Proposition 2, the base lottery pricing analysis, and the hybrid extension all fail to provide a named computational anchor. The fact that a new formulation might be easy to solve is not my objection; the missing anchor is.

I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A deterministic population with repeated valuation classes is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s pricing objective gives a natural question in that regime. My negative case is narrower: the mirror may be worth studying on its own, but this paper does not supply the computational result needed to make it a ChoCo complexity mirror. If the reader accepts unnumbered analytic optimization as an anchor, the finite-support pricing proposal survives and my case against the broader claim is weak.
