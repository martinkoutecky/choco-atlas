What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors cover Theorems 4, 5, and 6; none yields a continuous-population mirror, and the paper's other protocol observations and properties are left without one.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under your anchor rule. The paper has named protocol results, but no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest constructive anchors are Theorem 4, which says the two-party protocol in Fig. 3 is meta-envy-free, envy-free, and strategy-proof, and Theorem 5, which says the three-party protocol in Fig. 4 has those same properties. Theorem 6 says a meta-envy-free pie-cutting protocol exists for any number of parties if such a cake-cutting protocol exists. These establish protocol properties or conditional existence, not complexity results.

The paper’s object is already a continuous *cake*, while its population remains a fixed two or three parties with arbitrary utility measures. Treating a large population as mass over finitely many utility types would change the central question: the original theorems concern who takes which protocol role, and what each party values, not allocating cake among a high-multiplicity society. A model with per-capita cake supply and type-level role assignments could be worth studying, but it would be a new problem, not a defensible mirror of these theorems.

So there is no honest anchor and no continuous problem to state *per anchor* here. The most relevant scope distinction is that the paper continuizes the outcome space, not the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a direct ChoCo mirror is that none of the proposed anchors states a computational problem. Theorem 4 proves fairness and strategyproofness for a particular two-party protocol; Theorem 5 does so for a three-party protocol; Theorem 6 gives a conditional construction for pie cutting from cake cutting. They are results about protocol properties and existence, not complexity, algorithms, or optimization. The other named results are also axiomatic or structural. That leaves no computational result here for a continuous-population mirror to classify.

The best rescue for Theorem 4 is to treat two large, homogeneous groups as the two parties. But if every member of a group has the same valuation, the group is represented exactly by one party: its size adds no meaningful population dimension. If members have different valuations, a group’s aggregate valuation need not preserve what its individual members envy. Giving a whole group one protocol role also leaves its members outside the paper’s role-assignment comparison. Splitting a role across a group would require a new model of role assignment, not a population version of this theorem.

Theorem 5 has the same problem with three roles. A society with more than three voter-like or preference types has no direct place in the protocol: the result assigns three parties to three positions. Comparing type averages after reallocating fractions of each type among positions could be a coherent fair-division question, but it changes what a role swap means and what the protocol allocates. It would be a new mechanism-design problem, with no computational task or input representation specified by the theorem.

Theorem 6 is the closest candidate for a growing population because it quantifies over any number of parties. But it is a conditional lift from cake to pie: it randomizes a cut direction and then invokes a meta-envy-free cake protocol. It does not give an algorithmic classification for those parties, and the random angle is continuity in the outcome geometry, not in the population. A high-multiplicity version might be worth developing if someone first supplies a suitable general cake protocol and a meaningful mass-level notion of role assignment. That would be an adjacent research direction, not a computational mirror of this result.

So I would reject all three as ChoCo anchors. This is a strong scope objection to mirroring this paper’s results, not proof that no bulk cake-cutting project could ever be worthwhile. The paper’s continuous cake is not itself a collision, and I am not treating existing high-multiplicity work as evidence against the idea; the issue is that these particular theorems have no computational question to continuize.
