The continuous mirror question

Given an ordered candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a society supported on \(\tau\) approval-interval types \(t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) and approval set \(A_t\), find an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\), such that for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and \(c\notin W\), \(\sum_{t:\,c\in A_t,\ |A_t\cap W|<\ell}\mu_t<\ell/k\), and no size-\(k\) committee \(D\) satisfies \(|A_t\cap D|\ge |A_t\cap W|\) for every supported type and a strict inequality for at least one supported type.

The model it lives in

A large electorate choosing \(k\) sites from candidates ordered along a corridor, with each type approving an interval of sites. The input is the distribution of mass over those types; the decision variable is an integral committee, and the task is feasibility under mass EJR+ and mass Pareto optimality, with no separate optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 3.10’s joint EJR+ and Pareto-optimality guarantee on Candidate Interval instances. It leaves the unrestricted-domain results, reconfiguration results, and Conjecture 3.15’s counting question outside this anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass EJR+ and Pareto-optimality guarantee extend to approval societies outside SDO domains?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Corollary 3.10, proved in this paper from Theorem 3.3 and Theorem 3.9. It says that on every Candidate Interval approval instance, Algorithm 1 returns a size-\(k\) committee satisfying both EJR+ and Pareto optimality. The paper presents this as a polynomial-time algorithm; its EJR+ construction adapts an algorithm of Brill and Peters, while the joint guarantee is established here. The paper cites coNP-completeness for checking Pareto optimality in the unrestricted domain, but that is prior work, not a numbered hardness result proved here, so I cannot use it as an anchor.

My mirror is Mass-CI EJR+–Pareto Committee. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a fixed order on \(C\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) distinct approval types \(t\), each with approval interval \(A_t\subseteq C\) and positive rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The question is to find a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) satisfying both of these conditions:

  • Mass EJR+: for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every unelected candidate \(c\notin W\),

\[ \sum_{\substack{t:\ c\in A_t\\ |A_t\cap W|<\ell}}\mu_t < \frac{\ell}{k}. \]
In words, fewer than an \(\ell/k\) share of society can both approve \(c\) and have fewer than \(\ell\) approved committee members.

  • Mass Pareto optimality: there is no other size-\(k\) committee \(D\) for which \(|A_t\cap D|\ge |A_t\cap W|\) for every supported type \(t\), with strict inequality for at least one type.

This is a feasibility problem: the solution is the committee \(W\), and there is no separate objective to optimize. The type is a complete approval set; mass is the fraction of the population with that set of approvals. A plausible regime is a large electorate choosing \(k\) sites from options ordered along a corridor, where each resident approves a contiguous range of sites. There may be millions of residents but only a modest number of distinct approval intervals—at most \(m(m+1)/2\) nonempty intervals on \(m\) candidates.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. Replacing each rational mass by an integer number of identical voters gives a finite Candidate Interval instance: the EJR+ quota becomes \(\ell/k\) of the population, and mass Pareto domination agrees exactly with domination in the replicated profile. More directly, Algorithm 1 can compute approval scores and eligibility masses by summing over the \(\tau\) types. It adds at most \(m\) candidates, so this yields an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. The authors should recognize this as their underlying joint EJR+ and Pareto-optimal committee problem with the electorate represented by type masses.

The mirror covers Corollary 3.10’s Candidate Interval result. It does not claim anything about unrestricted-domain checking hardness, the paper’s open questions on general committee monotonicity or reconfiguration, or Conjecture 3.15 on counting Pareto-optimal committees. It also suggests asking whether the counting question has an exact high-multiplicity algorithm with runtime polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and whether the joint EJR+ and Pareto-optimality guarantee extends beyond SDO domains.

My weakest point is that this is a direct weighted high-multiplicity version of an existing algorithmic result. The committee remains integral, and the rational masses can be scaled to a finite electorate; this case does not show discrete hardness dissolving in the continuum. Its positive case is narrower: it gives a credible, author-recognizable continuous population model for the paper’s named joint-committee result, with an exact compressed algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a convincing case against Corollary 3.10 as a continuous-mirror anchor. It gives an algorithmic guarantee, and its natural mirror fits the programme closely: each type is an approval interval, the rational masses describe the electorate, and the output remains an integral size-\(k\) committee. Mass EJR+ compares the dissatisfied share to \(\ell/k\); mass Pareto optimality compares satisfaction across every supported type, with a strict gain for at least one. The problem does not depend on voter identities, and a large electorate with relatively few approval intervals is a credible regime.

The stronger version is to extend Theorem 3.9 to rational masses on any SDO instance, rather than restrict the mirror to Candidate Interval. Its greedy steps use approval scores and eligibility counts only through sums over voters. Replacing those counts with exact sums over the \(\tau\) type masses preserves the proof’s comparisons. The algorithm makes at most \(m\) additions, and rational sums and threshold comparisons have polynomial bit complexity in the input. Scaling masses to integer voter counts alone would not establish that bound, since the resulting electorate could be exponentially large in its binary encoding; the direct weighted algorithm does.

There is no fundamental degeneration: population shares remain meaningful, and the committee stays discrete. The paper itself studies finite electorates, so this is not a continuous-population collision. The skeptical point is that the mirror appears to inherit a tractable greedy construction almost immediately. But that is a limitation on how surprising the result may be, not a sound objection under the programme’s criteria. The honest negative case against this anchor is weak; it survives.
