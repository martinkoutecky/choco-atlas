The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I can’t make an honest case for a continuous mirror tied to one of its named computational results.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here: for arbitrary alternatives \(X\), transformations \(M\), and revealed order-pair data, the data are rationalizable by an \(M\)-invariant preference exactly when they are *strongly acyclic*—that is, repeated application of the paper’s collapse operation never derives the empty restriction pair. This is a general characterization, not a complexity result. The paper gives no finite input model or running-time bound; \(X\) and \(M\) may be arbitrary, and the collapse process need not have a stated computational stopping bound. Theorem 4 similarly characterizes forced out-of-sample comparisons, rather than the complexity of computing them. The resolution result in Theorem 5 is cited from Robinson (1965), not established as a complexity result for the paper’s problem.

A continuum of agents with masses assigned to preference types might motivate a new aggregate rationalizability problem. But that would change the object from one chooser’s preference extension to a population-level question, and the paper’s theorems do not anchor its computational analysis. Presenting it as a continuous mirror of a named computational result would overstate the connection.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no qualifying computational result. Theorem 2 is the closest candidate: it characterizes invariant rationalizability through strong acyclicity. But the paper gives no finite input model, encoding, or complexity bound for that test. Its \(X\) and \(M\) are arbitrary, and the transformations may be infinite. Theorem 4 characterizes which comparisons all rationalizations force; it likewise gives no computational result to mirror. Calling either one a computational anchor would overstate what the paper establishes.

The strongest population version I can construct takes a type to be a complete revealed-preference dataset, together with its alternatives and invariance transformations. Many consumers could plausibly share the same dataset, so high multiplicity itself is sensible here. Yet Theorem 2 then applies independently to each supported type. Requiring every consumer’s data to be rationalizable asks whether every positive-mass type passes the test; asking for the rationalizable share computes \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[\text{type }t\text{ is rationalizable}]\). The masses add bookkeeping, while the characterization’s substance remains type-by-type. Theorem 4 behaves similarly: forced comparisons are determined per type, and averaging them does not yield a common social preference without an added aggregation rule.

A common preference across consumers could couple their data, but that assumes a shared ranking over \(X\). With genuinely different preferences or datasets, combining observations can create contradictions that belong to no individual. Alternatively, one could add a costly intervention that moves mass between behavioral types, or an objective that rewards a target population share. Those could produce computational problems, but the intervention, costs, and collective objective would supply the central problem; they are absent from this paper’s rationalization and prediction questions.

The paper’s lotteries, streams, and vector spaces may already be continuous as alternative spaces. That does not make the society continuous. The best negative case is therefore that neither named result contains a population-sensitive computational question, and the natural distribution-over-consumers formulation is separable. I would not claim that no worthwhile adjacent project can be invented; the weaker point is that making one nontrivial appears to require adding a new collective problem the authors’ results do not anchor.
