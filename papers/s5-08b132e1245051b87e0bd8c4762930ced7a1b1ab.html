The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A,B,C\), rational masses \(\mu^A,\mu^B,\mu^C\) summing to one on each side, and complete strict cyclic rankings over counterpart types, with all \(C\)-types sharing one ranking of \(A\)-types, does there exist a strongly stable mass matching? A matching is a nonnegative tensor \(x_{abc}\) with the prescribed marginal masses; stability means there is no triple \((a,b,c)\) for which some \(\varepsilon>0\) mass from each type can leave current assignments it weakly prefers, with at least two types strictly preferring the proposed partner.

The model it lives in

Three populations of interchangeable infinitesimal units carry masses \(\mu^A,\mu^B,\mu^C\). Each unit receives a whole triple, while the aggregate assignment \(x_{abc}\) may be fractional; type-level rankings are strict, and \(C\)-types share a master ranking of \(A\)-types. A blocking triple requires positive eligible mass from each type, with at least two types strictly preferring the proposed partner.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 1’s one-master-list existence guarantee. It leaves aside the paper’s general constraint-programming models, fairness objectives, and experimental comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational mass profile in this one-master-list regime admit a strongly stable mass matching?
  • Can one decide existence and construct such a matching in time polynomial in the support sizes and mass bit length \(L\)?
  • How does the mass problem relate to finite replicated instances with the same type proportions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Lemma 1, proved in this paper. It says: *every 3dsm-cyc instance with complete lists and one master list has at least one strongly stable matching.* The authors prove it by a serial-dictatorship construction. This is a constructive existence result, though the paper does not state it as a complexity classification. I would not use the NP-completeness result mentioned in the introduction as an anchor: the paper attributes it to Lam and Plaxton rather than proving it here as a numbered result.

A plausible regime is a large three-sided service market: for example, many user sessions, data-source units, and server slots matched into triples. A type records the unit’s full preference-relevant profile, so units of the same type are interchangeable; the input gives each type’s rational share of its side. A platform may have millions of sessions but only hundreds of recurring profiles. Shared service priorities could give server types a common ranking of user types, matching the paper’s one-master-list condition. Each side can be normalized to total mass one, with capacity slots represented as units of the corresponding side.

My lead mirror is One-Master-List 3DSM-Cyc\(_\infty\). An instance has finite type sets \(A,B,C\), rational masses \(\mu^A,\mu^B,\mu^C\), each summing to one, and complete strict cyclic preferences: each \(a\in A\) ranks \(B\), each \(b\in B\) ranks \(C\), and each \(c\in C\) ranks \(A\). Require all \(c\)-types to share the same ranking of \(A\)-types. The question is whether there is a strongly stable mass matching, and, if so, to return one.

A solution is a nonnegative rational tensor \(x_{abc}\): the mass assigned to triple \((a,b,c)\). It must use every type’s mass exactly:
\[ \sum_{b,c}x_{abc}=\mu^A_a,\qquad \sum_{a,c}x_{abc}=\mu^B_b,\qquad \sum_{a,b}x_{abc}=\mu^C_c. \]
Each unit of mass receives a whole triple; only the aggregate assignment is fractional. For stability, a positive-mass blocking triple must be available: there must be some \(\varepsilon>0\) of each of types \(a,b,c\) currently assigned to partners they weakly prefer to leave for the proposed partners, with at least two of the three groups strictly preferring the proposed partner. The matching is strongly stable if no such triple exists. This translates the paper’s “two strictly improve, the third does not mind” blocking condition into a test over positive mass.

I expect this restricted problem to be tractable, probably by adapting the serial-dictatorship construction to finite type supports and divisible capacities. The concrete computational target would be a polynomial-time existence test and construction in the support sizes \(\tau_A,\tau_B,\tau_C\) and mass bit length \(L\). Lemma 1 is a good reason to ask whether the one-master-list structure survives aggregation; it is not evidence that the continuous claim is already proved. The mirror covers only that restricted existence result, not the paper’s general CP models, fairness objectives, or experimental comparisons.

Two follow-up questions are whether every such rational mass profile admits a strongly stable matching, and whether scaling its masses to many identical copies gives a precise discrete-to-continuous correspondence. One could also ask how to optimize egalitarian, minimum-regret, or sex-equal costs over stable mass matchings, but those would be extensions rather than additional anchors from this paper.

The weakest point is that aggregation creates indifference between individuals of the same type, while the paper’s lists are strict over named agents. The positive-mass blocking rule above makes that change explicit, and the continuous question remains recognizable as their matching problem. But Lemma 1 does not itself establish that its serial-dictatorship proof survives divisible flows or the induced indifferences.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proponent’s anchor is not a computational result of the kind this programme is meant to continuize. The paper’s main contribution is CP encodings and finite-instance runtime comparisons. Lemma 1 proves an existence guarantee for complete-list instances with one master list; it does not classify the complexity of a decision or optimization problem. A continuous existence-and-construction problem based on that lemma could be interesting, but it would be a new extension of a structural theorem, not a mirror of the paper’s computational findings.

There is also a real modelling gap. The paper’s agents rank named counterpart agents. If those strict rankings matter, compressing many agents into a fixed set of types can lose distinctions: counterpart identities may occupy different positions in others’ lists, so preserving those distinctions can make the support grow with the population. A fixed-support model instead ranks counterpart types, making members of a type interchangeable and effectively introducing indifference between them. That is a substantive change to the paper’s strict-preference setting.

This does not defeat the best version of the proposal. In a service market where agents genuinely rank categories of interchangeable units, type-level preferences and fractional triple assignments are coherent, and the resulting problem remains recognizably three-sided stable matching. The paper even discusses preference ties as a related direction. So the modelling gap weakens the claim that this is a direct mirror, but it does not show that authors would reject the type-level question.

My negative case is therefore limited: the paper offers no named complexity result to anchor the programme’s usual analysis, and the proposed model requires a meaningful shift from named agents to interchangeable types. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The proponent’s type-level market is plausible; whether it is worth pursuing depends on how strongly the programme requires a mirror of a computational result actually established in the paper.
