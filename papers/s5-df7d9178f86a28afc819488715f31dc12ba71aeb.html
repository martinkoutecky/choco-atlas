The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives, a subset size \(r\) with \(2 \le r < m\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rankings, split each ranking type's mass equally among its \(\binom{m}{r}\) subsets. Each mass block ranks its assigned subset by restriction of the complete ranking and gives an alternative in position \(q\) score \(r-q\). Which alternatives maximize the resulting weighted score?

The model it lives in

The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types. Each type's mass is divided uniformly among its \(r\)-element subsets, and each type-subset block contributes its restricted-ranking Borda scores. For every alternative \(c\), the score is \(S_\mu^r(c)=\frac{r(r-1)}{m(m-1)}B_\mu(c)\), so winner determination reduces to computing Borda scores over the supported rankings.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's asymptotic ranking guarantee and Lemma 1's expected score identity. It leaves the finite-sample convergence thresholds, simulations, and web experiment outside the continuous question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: Random-Subset Borda has a natural continuous-population limit, but the paper contains no named result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise. Its closest named anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Under complete, transitive preferences satisfying individual IIA, and for \(2\le r<m\), it states that for any pair of alternatives \(a_{j_1},a_{j_2}\), \(a_{j_1}\) has a strictly higher Borda score than \(a_{j_2}\) exactly when, with probability \(1\), its Random-Subset Borda average score is higher in the limit as the electorate grows. So this is an asymptotic voting result, not a computational-complexity result of the kind the programme normally seeks.

A plausible mirror is Continuous Random-Subset Borda Winner Determination. An instance is a set \(C\) of \(m\) alternatives, a subset size \(r\) with \(2\le r<m\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings of \(C\). Each ranking is a voter type: its mass is the fraction of the society with that ranking, and every member of a type ranks any subset by restricting that same complete ranking. For each type, split its mass equally among the \(r\)-element subsets of \(C\). Each resulting mass group ranks its assigned subset and gives an alternative in position \(q\) the score \(r-q\); alternatives outside the subset receive zero. The question is to return the resulting weak ranking, or equivalently the winner set of alternatives with maximal score.

The paper’s Lemma 1, also proved there, supplies the key calculation. For each alternative \(c\), its continuous Random-Subset Borda score is
\[ S_\mu^r(c)=\frac{r(r-1)}{m(m-1)}B_\mu(c), \]
where \(B_\mu(c)\) is its ordinary Borda score under \(\mu\). Since the factor is positive, the continuous procedure preserves the full Borda ranking, including ties. This gives a polynomial-time solution in \(m,\tau,L\): compute each supported ranking’s Borda scores, take the mass-weighted sums, and sort. That is a Class A outcome, derived here from the paper’s formula; it is not a polynomial-time result stated by the authors.

The regime is the paper’s own large-scale project-voting example: many residents rank a large set of proposed projects, with relatively few distinct complete preference types compared with the population. The continuous model replaces the finite random assignment of subsets with exact mass shares across subsets. That is a credible population-continuum interpretation of the paper’s large-electorate limit, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their question in the infinite-population regime. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s convergence claim and the expectation identity supporting it. It does not cover the finite-electorate convergence thresholds, the simulations, or the experiment.

The main weakness is that this mirror makes the paper’s asymptotic identity exact by averaging over the continuum, and the resulting computation is elementary. It offers a clear continuous analogue, but little of a computational-complexity landscape. The natural next questions would be whether the identity survives subset-dependent preferences that violate IIA, and how the finite-population probability of recovering the Borda ranking depends on score margins. The latter remains a finite-sampling question rather than part of this continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its Theorem 1 is an asymptotic convergence statement; Lemma 1 gives the expected score formula. Neither asks about the complexity of bribery, control, campaigning, or robustness. The paper’s simulations and experiment investigate finite-sample convergence and people’s responses, rather than an algorithmic problem over a society.

The proposed continuous Random-Subset Borda model is coherent: put mass \(\mu_\pi\) on each complete ranking \(\pi\), then split that mass equally among the subsets. But this replaces each voter’s random subset draw with exact mass shares. The aggregate randomness that drives the paper’s convergence thresholds disappears. What remains is the limiting score identity already underlying Lemma 1: the continuous subset scores are a common positive multiple of ordinary Borda scores. That is a valid population-limit result, but it does not give the paper a computational question. Keeping the finite-sample fluctuations would keep the finite electorate; adding a shared random draw that affects a positive mass would change the paper’s independent assignment mechanism.

A stronger behavioral mirror could make a type specify how its members rank every subset, rather than deriving subset rankings from one complete ranking. That better reflects the paper’s concern about choice overload, but the paper does not formulate a computational task around those response types. Aggregating their scores would still be a new winner-determination problem, not a mirror of a named result. Turning it into a harder question would require adding something such as optimizing the subset-assignment policy or inferring behavioral types—substantial new problems that the paper does not study.

So the case against is that the continuous model is a sensible reformulation of the paper’s limit, but not a worthwhile computational mirror for ChoCo: the paper’s substantive unresolved questions concern finite sampling and human behavior. This negative case has a real limit. The paper proves a discrete-to-limit identity, which the programme explicitly treats as a potentially valuable result, and its large-scale project-voting scenario makes a continuous population plausible. I cannot honestly claim that no continuous mirror survives in any scenario; I can only argue that this paper offers little computational landscape to chart.
