The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. It has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithms 1–3 specify ADRF, and the paper reports measured gas costs that grow roughly linearly with the number of resource types and show no user-count effect in its tests. Those are relevant observations, but they are not a named complexity result. The PDRF result is cited from elsewhere. So there is no honest way to meet the requested anchor requirement for this paper.

The strongest positive opening is an unanchored continuous version of its DRF allocation problem. Take a large cloud or edge workload population with many users but relatively few complete resource-demand types. A type \(t\) specifies a unit-task demand vector \(d_t\) and, if used, its resource weights; \(\mu_t\) is its population fraction. Let \(b\) be the resource supply per capita. Assign each individual an integral number \(q\) of tasks, but allow the continuum mass of each type to be split across different \(q\)'s. Choose a distribution \(\pi_t\) over those integers, with mean \(y_t\), subject to \(\sum_t \mu_t y_t d_t \le b\). Maximize the minimum type-average dominant load, \(\min_t y_t \max_j(d_{tj}/b_j)\), using the corresponding weighted expression when weights apply. A solution is the distributions \(\pi_t\) and the achieved optimum. Because mass can be split between adjacent integer task counts, any real \(y_t\) can be realized while every individual still receives an integral bundle; the resulting optimization is an LP over the supported types.

That is a plausible high-multiplicity question, and the paper’s focus on heterogeneous demand vectors and scalability to many users gives it a recognizable connection to ADRF. Its likely tractability would come from fractionalizing task counts across population mass. The weak point is substantial: this is a new population-level fairness question, not a result the paper names or proves. It measures type-average allocation, so individuals of one type may receive different task counts. I would offer it as a promising mirror direction, not as a case anchored in this paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no paper result here that meets the requested anchor standard: no theorem, lemma, or corollary establishes a computational complexity result. The paper’s main contribution is ADRF, a smart contract implementation of PDRF, supported by measured gas costs. Its reported costs grow with the number of resource types; the paper reports no effect from user count in its tests. So a continuous mirror of a complexity theorem cannot be made from this paper.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror—a distribution over repeated demand types, with per-capita resource supplies and integral task bundles distributed across the mass—could be a sensible *new* DRF question. The brief explicitly permits fractional type averages, so I would not reject it on that ground. But it changes the fairness quantity to type-average allocation and shifts the question away from ADRF’s contribution. ADRF’s demand and claim operations still happen through individual users’ transactions and balances. Representing those operations by type masses does not capture the gas cost of processing them; batching by type would be a different contract model.

The relevant allocation rule also comes from earlier work. The original DRF paper formulates dominant-share allocation for finite users, describes a fractional optimization view, and gives a scheduling algorithm. A type-mass formulation is therefore a plausible high-multiplicity reformulation of DRF, but that does not make it a mirror of ADRF’s new result. Existing continuous-outcome or high-multiplicity work is not, by itself, a reason to reject a mirror; the point is that this paper contributes the blockchain implementation and its gas measurements, not a new allocation-complexity result to continuize. ([Ghodsi et al., “Dominant Resource Fairness”](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~alig/papers/drf.pdf))

That leaves a narrow negative case: this paper is a poor anchor for ChoCo. I cannot honestly extend that into proof that no worthwhile continuous-population DRF question exists. The proponent’s proposal may be worth studying as a project about DRF itself; its connection to this paper is the weak part.
