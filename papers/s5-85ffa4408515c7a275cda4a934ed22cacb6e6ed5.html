What the mirror covers

No continuous computational mirror covers Theorems 1–2 or Corollary 1; they establish deficit bounds, while the large-market limit is economic analysis rather than an algorithmic result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is about regime plausibility, not a qualifying result to mirror. A sensible high-multiplicity regime would have many buyers and sellers, with each type a complete marginal-value or marginal-cost schedule. Mass would be the market share of each schedule type. If a market has \(N\) traders but only \(\tau\) distinct schedules, with \(N\gg\tau\), the population-as-mass description is credible. The paper’s large-market discussion, especially its use of aggregate excess supply, makes that setting recognizable.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, establishes that the Vickrey double auction never has positive revenue and, when trade occurs and the Walrasian price gap is positive, runs a deficit on each traded unit at least as large as that gap. Theorem 2, also proved here, establishes under Assumption 1 that the limiting expected aggregate deficit is bounded below by \(q_W^\infty/z(p_W^\infty)\). Corollary 1 extends that asymptotic bound to each state under conditional independence. These are economic and asymptotic results; none classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard.

So I can’t honestly provide a continuous problem per anchor. Turning either theorem into a task such as computing a deficit from type masses would introduce a new computational question, rather than mirror a named computational result of this paper. The paper offers a plausible many-trader setting, but no anchor for the requested complexity case. That absence is the weakest point—and, under the stated criteria, it prevents a positive anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 bounds the Vickrey double auction’s deficit per trade by the Walrasian price gap; Theorem 2 bounds the expected aggregate deficit in a large market; Corollary 1 extends that asymptotic statement state by state. None defines a computational problem or studies its complexity. Asking an algorithm to calculate one of these quantities would add a problem to the paper, rather than mirror a named result.

Theorem 1 is the best candidate for a closer look. A market with many buyers and sellers but few distinct marginal-value and marginal-cost schedules is a credible high-multiplicity setting. But the theorem’s VCG prices depend on what changes when one particular trader is removed: its proof uses leave-one-out order statistics. In an atomless population, removing one trader removes zero mass, so it has no effect on the aggregate allocation. The VCG externality that defines the payment disappears. Keeping it requires specifying a positive trader size, such as \(1/N\), and tracking a sequence of finite markets. That can support a high-multiplicity or asymptotic analysis, but the sequence and its atom size are doing essential work; a distribution over masses alone does not determine the theorem’s leave-one-out prices. Treating an entire type mass as one trader would instead change the mechanism.

Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 fare better as continuous *economic* statements: they already use a limiting distribution and aggregate excess supply. A proposed mirror that derives the bound from a continuum market would therefore be analytical, not computational. Turning the bound into an algorithmic task requires choices the paper does not supply: how the distribution is encoded, what output is sought, and at what precision. With an explicit finite representation, evaluating the equilibrium and local excess-supply derivative may be a routine calculation; with an unrestricted distribution, computational difficulty depends on the chosen representation. Neither gives a computational mirror anchored in the paper’s results. Conditioning on a state changes the formula’s inputs, not that gap.

One could define a worthwhile new project: compute VCG deficits for markets specified by repeated schedule types, or approximate expected deficits under a chosen distributional input model. But that would need its own computational question and motivation. If the question is simply to compute the finite auction, the paper’s primitives reduce to ordering marginal values and costs; that task is distinct from the deficit bounds it proves.

The case against is not airtight in the broader sense. The proponent is right that repeated trader types in a large market are plausible, and the paper’s large-market analysis makes the population axis unusually natural. That supports a continuous market-design project. It does not, by itself, supply a computational result from this paper to mirror; on that narrower test, the paper is a poor fit.
