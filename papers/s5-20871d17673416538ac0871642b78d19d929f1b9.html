The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result, so it has no qualifying anchors for a continuous computational mirror. Theorem 1, proved here, establishes that sufficiently large finite economies have an allocation and price where everyone is on budget, all but a bounded number of traders are within \(\varepsilon\) of demand, and the exceptions are within \(\varepsilon\) of demand. Theorem 2, also proved here, establishes that sufficiently large economies with complete preferences have a nonempty \(\varepsilon\)-core. Both are existence results; neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational formulation the paper solves.

The closest plausible *new* computational question would take a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete trader types \(\theta=(\succ_\theta,w_\theta)\), where each type specifies preferences and endowment. Its mass is the fraction of consumers of that type. The task would be to find a price \(p\) and per-type bundles \(x_\theta\) such that \(x_\theta\in f(\theta,p)\) and \(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta(x_\theta-w_\theta)=0\), or to find an approximate equilibrium under specified tolerances. A plausible regime is a large consumer population divided among relatively few preference-and-endowment categories. Theorem 1 is conceptually close: it uses a continuum representation to establish approximate equilibrium for large finite economies. But that connection does not establish anything about the computational complexity of the distribution-input search problem, and the paper does not provide an effective finite encoding of its compact preference space.

So the positive opening is that a high-multiplicity computational question can be formulated near the paper’s model. The honest limit is that it is not anchored by a named computational result of this paper. The paper’s contributions are analytic existence theorems, and I cannot supply the requested per-anchor complexity verdict without treating those theorems as something they are not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are existence theorems: they establish an approximate equilibrium and a nonempty approximate core under topological assumptions. They define no algorithmic input and output, search problem, or complexity bound. The paper may be a natural source for analytic questions about large economies, but its results do not anchor ChoCo’s complexity programme.

The most plausible computational extension is to give a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over trader types \(\theta=(\succ_\theta,w_\theta)\) and ask for an approximate equilibrium—or, under the extra completeness assumption, an approximate-core allocation. The high-multiplicity scenario is credible, and the paper itself motivates it. The obstacle is that its type space permits arbitrary preference relations in a compact topological set. It gives no finite encoding, preference or demand oracle, or convention for measuring an algorithm’s input length. Compactness supplies uniform existence; it does not specify how an algorithm receives a preference relation. The core version has the same gap, with an additional condition quantified over coalitions.

One could supply those missing choices by restricting preferences to a finitely described class and defining how demand and blocking are represented. That could produce a worthwhile computational market-equilibrium project. Its computational question would depend on the added representation and would not be a complexity question posed or answered by this paper. So I would argue against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror. The negative case is strongest as a judgment about what the paper contributes; it cannot establish that every new computational question inspired by its model would be unworthy.
