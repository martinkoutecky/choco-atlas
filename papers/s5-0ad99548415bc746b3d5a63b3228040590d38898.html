The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its results concern allocation rules and bargaining equilibria. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are cited characterizations of the ordinary Myerson value. Theorem 3, proved here, characterizes the weighted Myerson value through Component Balance and Weighted Bargaining Power; Theorem 4, also proved here, gives a second axiomatic characterization. Theorem 5 establishes a unique weighted player potential. Theorems 7 and 8 assert equilibrium implementation results for the first mechanism, and Theorem 10 gives a unique equilibrium result for the second. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or presents an algorithm or hardness result for one.

A population mirror is conceivable in a later computational study, but it would need substantial new modeling choices. The paper’s value function is defined on every subnetwork of a finite, identity-specific network, and the allocation depends on those links and subnetwork values. Replacing players by a distribution over types would require specifying how network structure and worth extend to a continuum. The paper’s examples, involving roughly 156 or 195 countries, also do not by themselves establish a high-multiplicity regime. Without a named computational anchor, any continuous problem I proposed would be speculative rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 characterize an allocation rule axiomatically; Theorem 5 characterizes a potential; and Theorems 7, 8, and 10 concern equilibrium implementation. The recursive formulas in Theorems 6 and 9 may help calculate payoffs, but they make no complexity claim. Theorems 1 and 2 are prior characterizations. So none of these results gives the programme’s target: an algorithmic or complexity question with a result to mirror. A continuum version of the axioms would be an axiomatic question; a continuum version of the bargaining mechanisms would be an equilibrium question. Neither is the computational complexity programme described here.

A more ambitious rescue would take a large network with repeated, fully specified agent types and ask for aggregate weighted Myerson allocations or the complexity of finding equilibrium offers. But the paper allows arbitrary worths for every subnetwork, and a player’s network position affects those worths. A distribution over weights or broad social roles alone would lose that interaction structure. A graphon or another succinct, type-level network model might preserve it, but choosing that model and its computational task would introduce assumptions and results absent from this paper. In the second bargaining mechanism, individual players exit one at a time; in a continuum, one person has zero mass, so a meaningful limit would also need a new mass-level exit rule.

The proponent’s concern that the paper’s examples do not establish a high-multiplicity regime is not decisive: a different, plausible repeated-type network scenario could. Nor is identity by itself a decisive objection, since a type can include an agent’s weight and all other relevant attributes. The firmer point is that even the best such scenario would motivate a new computational study of network values or bargaining, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. That supports a strong negative verdict under ChoCo’s stated scope, though it cannot establish that no related project would be worthwhile.
