The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and item types \(G\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), where each item type has positive value for exactly two endpoint types and those values are in \(\{1,d\}\), does there exist an envy-free assignment of integral bundles to individuals? Formally, find masses \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) over bundles \(B\in\mathbb{N}^{G}\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_g=q_g\), such that every positive-mass bundle pair \((t,B),(s,B')\) satisfies \(U_t(B)\ge U_t(B')\), where \(U_t(B)=\sum_g v_{t,g}B_g\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents distributed over finitely many complete types, with type masses \(\mu_t\) and a graph on types describing which item categories they value. Each category has supply \(q_g\); every person receives an integral bundle, while mass may be split across bundles. The decision question is EF existence; the related EFX mirror uses the same model with item removal and asks whether an EFX assignment attains \(W^\star=\sum_g q_g\max_t v_{t,g}\).

The objection that survived

The paper ties each item to a particular pair of agents, while the proposed item categories are valued by every member of two endpoint types; this prevents an exact repeated-graph dictionary and leaves recognition of type-level sparse incidence as an extension for the authors to endorse.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s EF existence hardness and Theorem 8’s UM+EFX existence hardness. It leaves the paper’s other algorithmic results and its price-of-fairness results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a type-pure rounding theorem show when splitting a type’s mass across integral bundles does or does not change EF existence?
  • Does the type-graph version of the Theorem 3 question retain hardness when the type masses and supplies are rational?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuum EF-Existence. It keeps the paper’s central decision question—whether envy-free allocation exists—and makes the population the continuous object while keeping each person’s bundle integral.

An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a finite set \(G\) of item types, each with rational supply \(q_g\) per capita. Each \(g\) has positive value for exactly two endpoint types and value \(0\) for all others. For Theorem 3’s restricted case, the endpoints have the same value, in \(\{1,d\}\). Different item types may connect the same pair of agent types, as repeated cohorts can generate many copies of the same relational role.

A solution assigns every person an integer bundle of item copies. Write \(x_{t,B}\) for the mass of type-\(t\) people receiving bundle \(B\in\mathbb{N}^{G}\). The constraints are \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_g=q_g\) for each \(g\); copies may be assigned to any type, including one that values them at zero. With \(U_t(B)=\sum_g v_{t,g}B_g\), the allocation is EF exactly when, for every two bundles \(B,B'\) receiving positive mass in types \(t,s\), respectively, \(U_t(B)\ge U_t(B')\). The question is whether such a distribution over integral bundles exists. There is no welfare objective in this anchor.

This mirrors Theorem 3, proved in the paper: deciding whether an EF allocation exists is NP-hard even for symmetric \(\{0,1,d\}\)-graphical valuations. Its reduction is from Multi-Colored Independent Set: given a regular graph partitioned into \(k\) color classes, choose one vertex from each class so the chosen vertices form an independent set. The construction uses graph edges valued symmetrically at \(1\), and additional edges valued symmetrically at the graph’s degree \(d\). The continuous question keeps the same EF existence problem and the same two-level graphical valuation structure, while asking what happens when mass can be divided among people who share a type.

The motivating regime could be a large employer or city allocating discrete slots, permits, or equipment to a very large recurring cohort. Participants fall into a relatively small catalog of complete role or location profiles, and each item category is relevant to two such profiles. There might be hundreds of thousands of people but only tens or hundreds of types, with item supplies scaling with the cohort. Individuals still receive whole items; only the mass of people assigned each bundle is fractional. That makes fractional type-level assignment an honest population description rather than a relaxation of an individual’s bundle.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 8, also proved in the paper: for general graphical valuations, deciding whether there exists an allocation that is both utilitarian-welfare-maximizing and EFX is NP-hard. Its continuous mirror uses the same type masses, item supplies, and integral bundles. Define EFX by requiring \(U_t(B)\ge U_t(B'-e_g)\) for every positive-mass pair of bundles \(B,B'\) and every item \(g\) in \(B'\). Let \(W=\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}U_t(B)\), and let \(W^\star=\sum_g q_g\max_t v_{t,g}\) be the unconstrained utilitarian optimum. Continuum UM+EFX asks whether an EFX assignment with \(W=W^\star\) exists. This retains the paper’s item-removal test at the individual level, so EFX does not collapse into ordinary EF as it might if items themselves were smoothed into divisible bundles.

For both problems, my initial expectation is hardness transfer when the type interaction graph is part of the input: the reduction’s combinatorics concern which types are connected and which item roles they share, rather than just the number of people. But that is a hypothesis, not a consequence of the paper’s theorems. The crucial bridge question is whether splitting a type’s mass among different integral bundles can create EF or EFX solutions that no discrete allocation has. A type-pure rounding theorem would support transfer; a tractable configuration formulation for small \(|T|\) would identify a Class A regime. Further questions include complexity parameterized by the type graph’s vertex-cover number, and whether welfare-maximizing EFX allocations can be found or approximated efficiently in that regime.

The main weakness is the type-level version of “graphical.” In the paper, an item is valued by exactly two named agents; here, an item category has two endpoint types and its copies are relevant to members of those types. That is a natural scalable model for repeated cohorts, but it broadens the literal graph condition. Also, the paper’s hardness constructions have a large, identity-rich graph, so they do not themselves show hardness when the number of types is small. I would present these as concrete high-multiplicity versions of the authors’ fairness questions, while treating acceptance of the type-level graphical closure and the discrete-to-continuous dictionary as the points the authors would need to endorse. This case covers Theorems 3 and 8; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s price-of-fairness results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does have computational results to mirror: Theorems 3 and 8 are named hardness results. My strongest case against a mirror is about whether the proposed continuous input retains the paper’s graphical model.

In the paper, an item is an edge valued by exactly two particular agents. In the proposed model, an item category with two endpoint types appears to make every copy valuable to every member of both types. That changes the incidence structure: one item can now matter to many agents. If each copy instead belongs to a particular pair of people, the type masses and item supplies do not say which people are paired. That missing pairing is part of the graph input, and it is where Theorem 3’s independent-set construction puts its combinatorics. Preserving it requires carrying a graph or another relational structure alongside the population distribution.

The strongest repair is to model paired cohorts explicitly, or to take a limit of repeated graphs. That can be sensible, but it leaves open whether the result is a continuous mirror of Theorem 3: if the graph is retained as an input, the population masses alone have not captured the instance; if only fixed graph templates repeat, the construction does not cover the paper’s varying graph structure. A dense continuum graph model has its own problem. Individual edge items become negligible as the population grows, so the integral-bundle structure and the item-level comparisons change.

The same fork applies to Theorem 8. Its EFX condition tests removal of each item from an envied bundle. Keeping that test meaningful requires retaining item atoms and their particular endpoints. Aggregating items by type changes which agents share an item; making them diffuse can make the effect of removing one item vanish. The proponent’s integral bundles preserve the test for each bundle, but the per-type supply constraints alone do not preserve the graph’s pairings.

This is a real obstacle to the proposed mirrors, not a claim that their continuous answers would be uninteresting or unchanged. It also does not prove that no paired-cohort or graph-limit model would interest these authors. That is where the negative case is weak: such a model may be worth studying if its additional relational input is made explicit and the authors accept it as their question. The proposed type masses and item supplies do not yet establish that bridge for either anchor.
