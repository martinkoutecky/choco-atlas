The case FOR (proponent)

I find no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 3.2, proved here, characterizes welfare functions on the single-peaked domain that satisfy weak Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives: they are exactly the fixed-strictly-quasi-concave welfare functions determined by a monotone collection of calibration relations and a tie-breaking rule. Theorem 3.3, also proved here, says that for an odd electorate with at least three voters, the domain of strictly quasi-concave preferences is the unique maximal domain containing spatial preferences on which simple majority voting is transitive. Both are axiomatic results. Neither asserts a complexity bound or gives an algorithmic problem of the kind the requested anchors require.

The nearest plausible population mirror would take each complete single-peaked preference as a type, let \(\mu_t\) be its share in a large policy electorate, and ask whether the resulting mass-weighted majority relation is transitive. That is a recognizable population continuization, but it recasts the paper’s axiomatic question as a new one; it is not anchored in a named computational result. The paper’s continuum is the policy space \(A=\mathbb R\), while its electorate \(N\) remains finite. Since continuizing outcomes or axiomatic questions while keeping society finite is explicitly outside ChoCo’s scope, this does not make a defensible positive computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest mirror is not simply “is mass-weighted majority transitive?” That question risks restating an axiom. The better anchor is Theorem 3.3’s boundary for transitivity: it identifies strict quasi-concavity as the largest domain containing spatial preferences on which majority remains transitive. A population version could ask how much campaign effort it takes to cross that boundary and create a robust majority cycle.

Concretely, represent a large policy electorate by rational masses over finitely many preference types, with each type described by a finitely encoded preference over the policy line. Give conversions between types per-unit costs. Then find the minimum cost of transferring mass so that some \(a<b<c\) form a cycle, with each pairwise majority margin at least a fixed \(\delta>0\). This is a mass-campaigning or winner-robustness problem: it measures how much of a population must change before majority choice loses transitivity. The continuum is the society, not the policy space; type-level costs and masses fit the programme’s model. The theorem makes cycling, and the preference-domain boundary behind it, the paper’s own central object.

This also improves on the proposed raw transitivity question. With only single-peaked types, majority transitivity is already the expected result, so that version may be trivial. Allowing campaign transfers into non-strictly-quasi-concave types uses the domain-extension question Theorem 3.3 explicitly studies. A continuous model can ask for a mass threshold, rather than whether one exceptional voter can be added to a finite domain.

The case has a real weakness: the paper proves an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity result, and it gives neither conversion costs nor a campaign problem. So this is a computational question built from the paper’s named result, not a direct continuous counterpart to one of its algorithms. If “mirror” requires an existing complexity theorem, the case fails that screen. If it permits a new computational problem on the paper’s exact subject—majority transitivity and the domain boundary where it breaks—then Theorem 3.3 gives a credible, population-level question worth investigating.
