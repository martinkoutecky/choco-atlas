What the mirror covers

The proposal covers Proposition 1 and its nearby observation about order-theoretic properties; it leaves the revision, incremental-evaluation, restriction, and weak-order-extension results untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: Proposition 1, proved in the paper, says that satisfiability of a quantifier-free ERO preference formula is in NP. ERO formulas use equalities and disequalities over uninterpreted constants and equality or order comparisons over rationals. The proof reduces satisfiability to choosing a disjunct and checking a conjunction; that conjunction check is cited from earlier work. The paper’s following prose derives a coNP upper bound for order-theoretic properties reducible to ERO validity. I found no named hardness result to anchor on, so I would use Proposition 1 alone. [Chomicki, *Database Querying under Changing Preferences*, Proposition 1](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~chomicki/papers-amai07.pdf)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large product-search service. Millions of users compare the same kinds of catalog entries, but share only a modest number \(\tau\) of qualitative preference rules. For instance, some prefer newer cars within a make while leaving different makes incomparable—the paper’s own kind of partial preference. A type is one complete formula-defined comparison rule; its mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of users with that rule. This makes the population continuous while keeping its support finite.

My lead mirror is Continuous Majority-Preference Satisfiability. An instance gives a tuple schema, \(\tau\) quantifier-free ERO formulas \(C_i(x,y)\), and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\). Define
\[ x\succ_\mu y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i:C_i(x,y)}\mu_i>\frac12. \]
The question is whether there exist tuples \(x,y\) with \(x\succ_\mu y\). A yes solution supplies such a pair; a no solution establishes that no pair has strict-majority support. When \(\tau=1\), this is exactly the satisfiability question in Proposition 1. With several types, mass affects which comparisons survive.

I expect this problem to be NP-complete, hence a Class B mirror: hardness transfers from the discrete version. Membership in NP follows by guessing a tuple pair, evaluating each formula, and adding the matching masses; ERO has compact witnesses for satisfiable comparisons. For hardness, reduce 3-SAT to the one-type case. Represent each Boolean variable by a rational \(z_j\ne0\), with positive literals encoded by \(z_j>0\) and negative literals by \(z_j<0\). Let an edge exist only from a tuple tagged “top” to one tagged “bottom,” and include the 3-SAT formula in the edge condition. This relation is always a strict partial order—there can be no two-edge path—and it is nonempty exactly when the formula is satisfiable. This lower bound is my argument about the proposed mirror, not a hardness theorem from Chomicki. A finite electorate with type counts \(n_i\) maps to masses \(\mu_i=n_i/N\); the hard one-type instance already has \(N=1\). Thus the hardness comes from the formula and choice-domain description, not population multiplicity.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as an extension of their problem: it constructs a preference relation over the same tuple space and asks whether it contains any comparison, using formula-defined types and a composition of relations. The paper already studies composed preferences and their properties. The rational tuple domain itself is not my continuization; the new continuous object is the population distribution over preference types. This case covers Proposition 1 and its nearby order-property observation, not the paper’s revision and incremental-evaluation results.

The weak point is that Chomicki studies an individual preference relation and specific revision operators, while strict-majority aggregation is new. The authors might accept this as a preference-query problem but reject it as their continuous form. Follow-up questions include whether the majority relation is an SPO or weak order, and how to compute its winnow over a finite catalog as the type masses change.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is a named computational result, so the case against cannot rest on there being nothing computational to mirror. But it asks whether one formula defining one preference relation has a satisfying tuple pair. Its input contains no users, electorate, or multiplicity: the relation ranges over possible database tuples, and the proposition does not analyze winnow on a database instance.

The proposed majority-satisfiability problem is coherent, but its key step is adding a social aggregation rule. In the paper, union, prioritized composition, and Pareto composition combine preference information to revise one user’s relation. They do not aggregate different users’ preferences. A weighted majority relation can also be cyclic even when each user’s relation is a strict total order. That makes it a new collective preference model, not a continuous version of the preference relation in Proposition 1. The fact that the one-type case recovers the proposition shows the problem contains the old question; it does not establish that the new multitype question is one the paper studies.

The most faithful population scenario would give many users the same catalog and query, group them by their complete preference and revision context, and measure how many users’ answers change after a revision. That is sensible, but each type’s query and order properties can be evaluated separately; the masses then give a weighted sum of those results. This is distributional batch query evaluation, rather than the paper’s computational problem. Its propositions about satisfiability and order properties have the same mismatch: adding masses after the per-type checks does not make those properties population-level questions.

A more ambitious mirror could choose one shared revised query for a population with conflicting preferences. That would give the population a substantive role, but it requires a collective objective or aggregation rule—such as whose query answer should be preserved—that the paper neither defines nor needs. The paper’s revision is an individual user refining their own preferences. A shared compromise is a new social-choice problem, not a continuization of that revision process.

The paper’s infinite tuple domains and rational attributes do not supply the missing population axis: they describe possible choices, not agents. Still, this negative case is not airtight. A large community of users sharing a catalog is plausible, and weighted query-answer statistics could be useful. The case against is that those are adjacent new models; the paper’s named complexity result itself has no population whose continuization could change the computational question.
