Why no mirror

A continuous STV no-show feasibility question over ballot-type masses is coherent and recognizable, but it is not a computational result of this paper, and the proposed minimum-mass objective is newly introduced. Hence bit (a) fails and the mirror is red.

What the mirror covers

It covers the paper's no-show anomaly definition and empirical no-show-frequency discussion; it leaves upward, downward, and committee-size anomalies, cross-anomaly correlations, closeness analysis, and heuristic-search completeness without computational results.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case begins with a limitation: this manuscript contains no qualifying anchor. It has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or any other computational result. The numbered items are Definitions 1–4, examples, empirical tables, and discussion. Thus I cannot honestly attribute a complexity theorem to the paper.

The closest substantive anchor is the empirical result in Section 6.4: the authors report no-show anomalies in \(39\) of \(1{,}079\) elections, including \(38\) multiwinner elections; \(29\) harmed a political party, and \(18\) displayed no other anomaly found by their procedures. This is not a qualifying theorem, but it identifies the paper’s clearest continuous-population candidate.

My lead mirror would be Continuous Minimum-Mass No-Show\(_\infty\). Let \(C\) be the candidates, \(S\) the number of seats, and \(T\) the finite set of allowed ballot types: complete or partial rankings interpreted under the paper’s weak-order convention. A society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). Define \(W_\infty(\mu,S)\) by the same Scottish STV transfer process, with quota \(1/(S+1)\) per unit mass, proportional surplus transfers, exhausted truncated ballots, and a fixed tie-breaking rule.

Given \(X\notin W_\infty(\mu,S)\), \(Y\in W_\infty(\mu,S)\), and a mass-removal vector \(z\), require

\[ 0\le z_t\le \mu_t, \]

with \(z_t=0\) unless type \(t\) ranks \(X\) above \(Y\). Let \(M_z=1-\sum_t z_t\) and normalize the remaining society as

\[ \mu^z_t=\frac{\mu_t-z_t}{M_z}. \]

The problem is to minimize \(\sum_t z_t\) subject to

\[ W_\infty(\mu^z,S) = \bigl(W_\infty(\mu,S)\setminus\{Y\}\bigr)\cup\{X\}. \]

A solution is therefore the smallest percentage of voters who prefer \(X\) to \(Y\) whose abstention makes \(X\) replace \(Y\), exactly matching the paper’s no-show definition while replacing named voters and integer ballot counts by mass.

The plausible regime is a local-government electorate consisting of many repeated preference blocs: party-loyal groups, neighbourhood or issue constituencies, and recurring truncated-ballot patterns. The paper reports \(661\)–\(14{,}207\) voters per election, with only \(3\)–\(14\) candidates and \(2\)–\(5\) seats. It does not report the number of distinct ballot types, so I would not claim that every observed election is genuinely low-\(\tau\); the mirror relies on the credible scenario \(N\gg\tau\), not on the research area alone.

This should be recognisable to the authors. Their input is already a preference profile recording multiplicities of ballot types, and their no-show search removes batches of ballots. Replacing counts \(n_t\) by proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and batch removals by fractional masses, is a direct high-multiplicity relaxation. It continuizes the population, not the outcomes or the axioms. The floor-and-\(+1\) quota disappears as \(N\) grows, while the STV dynamics and the winner-set replacement condition remain intact.

I would provisionally treat the general problem as a candidate for continuum-specific difficulty: STV’s sequential elimination, quota events, and proportional surplus ratios make \(W_\infty\) piecewise rational and potentially difficult to optimize over. A fixed event order may admit tractable subproblems, but any hardness would arise from the STV dynamics, not from population multiplicity. The mirror therefore generates further questions about minimum-mass upward and downward anomalies, robustness margins, and whether committee-size anomalies persist under the fluid quota.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper does not solve an optimization or complexity problem. Its anomaly searches are empirical heuristics and explicitly do not guarantee that all anomalies were found. Consequently this is a strong mirror of the paper’s underlying no-show question, but not a mirror anchored by a named computational theorem. The quota limit and fractional abstention also require care near exact thresholds. Those are genuine qualifications, but they do not make the proposed continuous problem a different social-choice question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong because the paper has no qualifying computational anchor at all. It contains no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity of deciding, optimizing, or approximating any problem. Its contribution is empirical: it reports anomaly frequencies in \(1{,}079\) Scottish elections using heuristic searches that explicitly do not guarantee detection of every anomaly. Thus the proponent’s Section 6.4 result is not a computational result to continuize.

The proposed Continuous Minimum-Mass No-Show\(_\infty\) is mathematically coherent, but it is an invented optimization problem, not a mirror of a named result in the paper. The paper asks whether a given finite profile contains some abstaining group that produces a specified winner replacement, and then estimates how often that occurs in observed elections. It neither minimizes the number of abstainers nor studies computational complexity. Replacing the existential question by

\[ \min \sum_t z_t \]

therefore changes the contribution rather than continuizing it.

The more faithful mirror would ask only whether there exists a nonzero mass vector \(z\) with \(z_t>0\) on types ranking \(X\) above \(Y\) such that the continuous STV outcome changes from \(Y\) to \(X\). But that still supplies no result from the paper to anchor. Moreover, the paper’s central statistic \(39/1{,}079\) cannot be recovered from a single normalized society: profiles with the same proportions but radically different electorate sizes become identical. To reproduce the empirical question one would need an additional probability distribution over societies, ballot-type supports, truncation patterns, and election sizes. Nothing in the paper specifies such a population-of-elections model, so that version is a new stochastic programme, not a continuous mirror.

The proposed minimum mass also moves away from the paper’s already delicate welfare interpretation. The paper’s no-show criterion uses only \(X\succ Y\), while acknowledging that this does not establish that every affected voter prefers the resulting winner set. A fractional mixture of ballot types makes that weakness more explicit: the optimizer may remove mass from voters with different rankings and different attitudes toward the other \(S-1\) winners. Imposing the stronger and more defensible requirement—one common complete type, or every removed type placing \(X\) in the top \(S\)—would make the model socially clearer, but would further detach it from the reported \(39\)-election statistic, most of whose examples fail that stronger test.

There is a real mathematical relaxation available here, especially if one studies fractional abstention or robustness margins. That is the weak point in the negative case: the continuous question is not nonsensical. But it is a new computational social-choice project prompted by an empirical observation, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s results. On the programme’s stated anchoring standard, no anchor survives.
