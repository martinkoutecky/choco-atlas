The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I see is a narrow high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s minimum-budget payment computation, in a large workforce with a small number of shared valuation schedules. The paper’s named computational anchor is Theorem 5, proved here. It states that COMPUTEPAYMENTS runs in

\[ O\!\left(\sum_{i\in N}\left(|V_i|T_{\mathrm{opt}}+|V_i|^2T_{\mathrm{eval}}+\mathrm{SP}(|V_i|,|V_i|^2)\right)\right), \]

where \(T_{\mathrm{opt}}\) is the time for the option rule, \(T_{\mathrm{eval}}\) the time to evaluate an agent’s value for an option, and \(\mathrm{SP}(n,m)\) the time to find shortest paths on a graph with \(n\) vertices and \(m\) edges. Theorem 5 also gives a modified bound replacing \(|V_i|\) in the shortest-path and evaluation terms with

\[ n_i=\max_{v_{-i}\in V_{-i}}\left|\{\phi^*(v'_i,v_{-i}):v'_i\in V_i\}\right|, \]

the largest number of options agent \(i\) can induce by changing their report while others’ reports are fixed. The paper notes that Bellman–Ford gives \(\mathrm{SP}(n,m)=O(nm)\). The bound is polynomial in the finite type-domain sizes when option selection and value evaluation are efficient; it is not an unconditional polynomial-time guarantee for an arbitrarily represented option space.

The output matters as an optimization problem because the paper’s Theorem 4, also proved here, establishes its pointwise guarantee: for any justified mechanism using the same option rule \(\phi^*\), the proposed mechanism’s payment satisfies \(\tau_i^*(v)\leq\tau_i(v)\) for every agent and report profile, and its total budget is no larger. I would treat Theorem 5 as the computational anchor and Theorem 4 as the guarantee that defines what its computed payments mean.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: High-Multiplicity Minimum-Budget Payments. The input is a finite set of options \(X\), a finite common report domain \(T\), rational utility functions \(v_t:X\to\mathbb{Q}\) for \(t\in T\), a fixed deterministic tie-breaking order on \(X\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). Its support size is \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\), which may be much smaller than \(|T|\). A type \(t\) describes a worker’s complete utility schedule over the plans and their common report menu. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the workforce with that schedule.

The question is to compute the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s minimum-budget mechanism at \(\mu\). For every integer \(q\) making \(q\mu_t\) integral, form a workforce with \(q\mu_t\) workers of each type. The broker chooses a plan maximizing reported total utility and uses the fixed tie-break. For each present type \(t\), compute the payment schedule faced by one such worker when they report each \(s\in T\), holding the other workers’ truthful reports fixed. The requested solution is the eventual limiting payment table \(P_{t,s}(\mu)\), the truthful plan, and the minimum per-capita budget

\[ \beta(\mu)=\sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_tP_{t,t}(\mu). \]

A solution must give the exact table and show it is the limit of the discrete minimum-budget payments, with the corresponding limiting incentive and individual-rationality inequalities.

There is a concrete shortest-path construction for this mirror. Let \(F_\mu\) be the options maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t(x)\), and let \(x_0\) be the tie-broken maximizer. For a worker of true type \(t\) who reports \(s\), the finite workforce scores an option by the aggregate welfare plus the effect of replacing one \(t\)-worker with an \(s\)-reporter. As \(q\) grows, the selected option is eventually

\[ x_{t,s}=\operatorname{lexargmax}_{x\in F_\mu}\bigl(v_s(x)-v_t(x)\bigr). \]

Build the paper’s payment graph on \(\{*\}\cup T\): give \(*\to s\) weight \(v_s(x_{t,s})\), and \(r\to s\) weight \(v_s(x_{t,s})-v_s(x_{t,r})\). If \(d_t(s)\) is the shortest-path distance from \(*\) to \(s\), then \(P_{t,s}(\mu)=-d_t(s)\). Finiteness of \(X\), a fixed tie-break, and a finite type set make the selected options and payment graphs stabilize along the replication sequence.

For explicitly listed options and utilities, this is expected to be tractable: scanning options to find the welfare-maximizing face and directional choices, then solving one shortest-path problem per supported type, takes polynomial time in \(|X|\), \(|T|\), \(\tau\), and the rational input length \(L\). For a compact or infinite option space, the claim becomes conditional on efficient option-selection and value-evaluation routines, just as Theorem 5 is. Further questions include extending the result to implicitly represented options and studying whether the tie-breaking rule itself can be chosen to reduce budget.

The regime is a large workforce coordinated over a finite menu of staffing or workload plans. Workers with the same utility schedule and report domain are interchangeable; millions of workers can have only a modest number of distinct schedules. This fits the paper’s own workforce-management motivation, while making the society a distribution over agent types. The outcome set and utility domain stay finite: the continuization is of the population. The paper’s future-work note about continuous or unknown *type domains* does not rule this out; that is a different axis of continuization.

My weakest point is the incentive interpretation. In a literal atomless society, an individual has zero effect on the aggregate, while the paper’s DSIC constraints compare outcomes after a unilateral report change. The mirror above handles that by defining the continuous object as the high-multiplicity limit and retaining the first-order effect of one worker’s report, especially at welfare ties. That is a defensible computational mirror of the paper’s problem, but a referee could require a standalone continuum-game semantics and reject a limit-defined one. I would make that qualification explicit. The paper contains no named hardness result, so this is a positive case for a tractable mirror, not a claim that its discrete hardness dissolves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The workforce is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and grouping workers by complete utility schedule is sound. The difficulty is strategic: the paper’s payments enforce incentives for a person whose report can change the chosen option. In an atomless society, one person has zero mass, so their report leaves the distribution unchanged. The proposed construction preserves that person’s finite effect by taking a first-order limit. That limit is coherent, but it is not the incentive problem of a society represented by \(\mu\) alone.

A small example shows the difference. Let \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), with two options and utilities \(v_A(x_1)=0,\ v_A(x_2)=10,\ v_B(x_1)=10,\ v_B(x_2)=0\). Aggregate welfare ties, and suppose the fixed tie-break selects \(x_1\). In a replication with even \(q\), a type \(B\) worker who reports \(A\) changes the chosen option to \(x_2\); a type \(A\) worker’s report does not change it. The paper’s payment graphs give truthful payments \(0\) for type \(A\) and \(-10\) for type \(B\), so the limiting per-capita budget is \(-5\).

In the atomless mechanism, either person’s report leaves \(\mu\) and the outcome \(x_1\) unchanged. DSIC then requires the transfers for reports \(A\) and \(B\) to be equal: type \(A\) prefers its truthful report whenever its transfer is higher, and type \(B\) does the reverse. IR requires the common transfer to be at least \(0\), since type \(A\) values \(x_1\) at zero. The minimum budget is therefore \(0\), not \(-5\). The proposed table gets its nontrivial result from retaining the vanishing individual’s influence exactly at the welfare tie.

That distinction defeats both cited results as continuous-population anchors. Theorem 4 carries over to the marked-agent limit because it is inherited from the finite mechanisms. Under ordinary atomless DSIC, its payment comparison instead reduces to choosing a common transfer subject to IR; the shortest-path payment envelope disappears. Theorem 5 does yield a finite computation for the proposed limit: with explicit \(X\) and \(T\), scan for the welfare-maximizing face and each directional maximizer, then run the payment graphs’ shortest paths. That is a coherent calculation, but its algorithmic content is the existing finite payment construction applied to first-order deviations. With compactly represented options, the hard work remains the same option-selection oracle the paper already identifies.

So the strongest version of this mirror is a tangent limit of finite mechanisms, indexed by the infinitesimal agent’s true and reported types. It could be a legitimate niche study if the authors want that object. The case against it is that the nontrivial incentive semantics and budget gains come from the marked agent, while the continuous society itself supplies only the aggregate state. Treating the tangent limit as a population continuization therefore needs an explicit methodological commitment; it does not follow from replacing the electorate by \(\mu\).

This is a strong objection to the proposed mirror, not a proof that no worthwhile one exists. If the authors accept first-order, marked-agent limits as continuous objects, the narrow mirror survives.
