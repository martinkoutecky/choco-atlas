What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror tracks Theorem 2.2’s nine-seat nonemptiness; it leaves the certified proof lemmas and the cited eight-seat Theorem 3.1 without separate computational mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The manuscript has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its closest result is Theorem 2.2, proved here: for every finite approval election with at least nine candidates, there is a committee \(W\) of size nine such that \(9q(W,T)<n|T|\) for every nonempty target \(T\) of size at most nine. This is a deterministic core existence theorem, not a claim that a problem is in P or hard. Theorem 3.1 says that every finite approval election has an eight-seat global PAV maximizer that is Hare-core; it is cited from Peters, and it also makes no complexity claim. The named lemmas and the exact certificate checks support the existence proof, but do not classify a computational problem. So there is no qualifying anchor to offer.

The strongest nearby mirror is a continuous version of Theorem 2.2. Call it Continuous Nine-Seat Approval-Core Search. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), \(|C|\ge9\), and \(\tau\) approval-set types \(A_t\subseteq C\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The task is to return a nine-candidate committee \(W\) such that, for every nonempty \(T\subseteq C\) with \(|T|\le9\), \(9q_\mu(W,T)<|T|\), where \(q_\mu(W,T)=\sum_{t:\,|A_t\cap T|>|A_t\cap W|}\mu_t\). This is exactly the continuous Hare-core condition: the mass of all strict gainers can afford \(T\) precisely when \(9q_\mu(W,T)\ge|T|\). The decision variable is the integral committee \(W\); there is no separate objective.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization electing a nine-seat committee, where many members cast the same approval ballot. The type is the complete approval set used by the paper’s utility model, and mass records each ballot type’s share. Here the number of members can greatly exceed the number of observed types. This preserves the paper’s core question and its quota, rather than making outcomes or voters fractional. The authors would have a good reason to recognize it as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime.

For rational masses, the existence answer follows by choosing a common denominator and replicating each type accordingly, then applying Theorem 2.2. Finding a committee is also polynomial for fixed nine seats: enumerate nine-candidate committees and targets of size at most nine, and check each against the \(\tau\) types using exact rational arithmetic. That tractability claim is my inference from fixed committee size and the input representation; the paper does not claim it. This proposed mirror covers only the nine-seat existence result. A natural further question is what happens when the committee size is part of the input.

My lead, if a structural existence theorem is accepted as a provisional anchor, is therefore Theorem 2.2 and this single mirror. The weak point is substantial: it is not an eligible computational anchor under your rule, and fixed-size enumeration gives the mirror little of the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart. Its positive case is that it is a faithful population mirror with a clear high-multiplicity regime, and the known existence transfer does not make the paper’s finite-population model itself a continuous-population collision.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 2.2 proves existence; Theorem 3.1 is a cited existence result; and the lemmas and exact verifiers support the proof rather than classify an algorithmic problem. The authors explicitly make no claim about the complexity of finding a core committee. Turning Theorem 2.2 into “find a nine-seat core” is a reasonable new search question, but it is not a computational result the paper established.

That objection defeats the proponent’s anchor under the requested standard, but it does not defeat their model. A distribution over approval types is a plausible high-multiplicity society, and the quota condition translates exactly: for rational masses, choose a common denominator \(n\), set \(n_t=n\mu_t\), and divide the paper’s inequality \(9q(W,T)<n|T|\) by \(n\). The identity of the two formulations is not itself a reason to reject the mirror; the programme explicitly treats such discrete-to-continuous identities as possible results.

A variable-seat search or complexity study could be worthwhile, but it would be a newly posed problem rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper. So the negative case is strong on anchor eligibility and weak on the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. I cannot honestly argue that the proposed continuous society is an incoherent or uninteresting object; I can only say the paper does not provide the kind of result this exercise asks us to mirror.
