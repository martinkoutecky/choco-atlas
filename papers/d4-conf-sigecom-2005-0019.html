The continuous mirror question

Given types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), machine classes \(j\) with per-population supplies \(q_j\), weights \(w_{tj}\), and integer parallelism caps \(k_t\), find the maximum welfare and an optimal assignment of mass to integral bundles. For bundle \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{J}\), let \(y_{t,a}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving it; solve \(\max_{y\ge 0}\sum_{t,a} y_{t,a}\sum_j a_jw_{tj}\), subject to \(\sum_a y_{t,a}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), \(\sum_{t,a}a_jy_{t,a}\le q_j\) for each \(j\), and \(y_{t,a}=0\) whenever \(\sum_j a_j>k_t\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over workload types. Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle of machine units, respecting its type’s parallelism cap; machine supply scales with population. The objective is total welfare, and the decision variables are the masses assigned to each type-and-bundle pair.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 1’s finite-parallelism social-optimum benchmark. It leaves Theorems 1–3 on equilibrium existence and performance, the best-response analysis, and the convergence, efficiency, and fairness results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Establish an exact bit-complexity bound for solving the configuration LP in the number of supported types, machine classes, input bit length, and encoded parallelism caps.
  • Characterize when a finite matching instance recovers the scaled LP optimum exactly and when the correspondence holds only asymptotically.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow one: the finite-parallelism social optimum has a natural high-multiplicity mirror. It does not extend the paper’s central analysis of strategic equilibrium.

The anchor is Lemma 1, proved here. It says that in the finite-parallelism model the social optimum equals a maximum-weight matching: make one left-side vertex \(u_{i\ell}\) for each user \(i\) and each of their \(k_i\) machine slots, one right-side vertex for each machine \(j\), and give edge \((u_{i\ell},v_j)\) weight \(w_{ij}\). A maximum-weight matching gives the optimal allocation. The paper then computes this benchmark using a polynomial-time matching algorithm, specifically the Hungarian algorithm. This is the paper’s clearest named computational anchor; it offers no comparable named hardness result. Theorems 1–3 concern equilibrium existence and performance bounds, rather than computational complexity.

A continuous version would have many users but few workload types. A type \(t\) specifies the complete information this welfare problem uses: its preference weights \(w_{tj}\) for machine classes and its parallelism cap \(k_t\). Let \(\mu_t\) be its population mass, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime might be a large cloud or cluster with many repeated jobs from a small set of application classes, where jobs in a class have the same machine preferences and parallelism. To keep the allocation meaningful as the population grows, specify \(q_j\), the supply of machine units of class \(j\) per population member. This scaling of cluster capacity with workload is part of the proposed mirror.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover: given rational \(\mu_t,q_j,w_{tj}\) and integer \(k_t\), find an optimal distribution of each type’s mass over integral machine bundles. Let \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^n\) denote a bundle, with \(\sum_j a_j\le k_t\), and let \(y_{t,a}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving that bundle. Solve

\(\displaystyle \max_{y\ge0}\ \sum_{t,a} y_{t,a}\sum_j a_jw_{tj} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_a y_{t,a}=\mu_t\ \ (\forall t),\qquad \sum_{t,a}a_jy_{t,a}\le q_j\ \ (\forall j).\)

The solution is the optimal welfare and a sparse list of type-and-bundle masses attaining it. Each agent’s bundle remains integral and respects their parallelism cap; only the mass assigned to bundles is fractional. Budgets do not appear because Lemma 1’s social optimum does not use them. For a finite cluster with \(N\) users and \(M_j\) copies of class \(j\), the correspondence is \(\mu_t=N_t/N\) and \(q_j=M_j/N\); the finite matching benchmark is recovered after scaling by \(N\).

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. Its configuration LP has potentially many bundle variables, but the pricing question is simple: for a type, choose up to \(k_t\) machine units with the best positive reduced values. That can be done by sorting the available values, so the usual LP separation machinery gives a plausible exact algorithm polynomial in the support size, machine-class count, and input bit length. This is the same computational shape as the paper’s anchor: make the allocation problem explicit, then control the matching or pricing step.

I would expect the authors to recognize this as a continuous analogue of Lemma 1’s welfare benchmark: it preserves their preferences, per-agent parallelism, and aggregate welfare objective, while replacing repeated named users with masses of indistinguishable users. It does not claim that their fixed-budget price-anticipating game has thereby become a tractable continuum game. Further questions include whether the discrete matching optima converge to this LP under high multiplicity, and what equilibrium, efficiency, and fairness questions remain after introducing a suitable continuum notion of strategic behavior.

The weak point is the scope: the paper’s main contribution is about Nash equilibria of a price-anticipating mechanism, while Lemma 1 supplies only the finite-parallelism social-optimum comparator. This mirror covers that comparator, not the equilibrium theorems, best-response analysis, or fairness results. The case survives as a precise mirror of a real, named optimization result, but it is not a continuous version of the paper as a whole.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror drops the paper’s distinctive problem. The paper studies whether strategic users with fixed budgets reach a stable allocation, and what efficiency and fairness that equilibrium has. The proposed LP removes bids, budgets and strategic behavior; it computes the central planner’s welfare benchmark. A continuous model of the price-anticipating game would need to say how agents’ influence on prices behaves as their mass vanishes. That is a genuine modeling choice, and the resulting market may not preserve the strategic question the paper asks.

But that does not defeat the anchor the other analyst chose. Lemma 1 is a named computational result: it reduces the finite-parallelism social optimum to maximum-weight matching. Repeated workload classes with shared preferences and parallelism limits are plausible types, and scaling machine supply per person is a coherent co-modification. The mass distribution over integral bundles preserves the matching interpretation. A simple algorithmic answer would not make the question unworthy, and a narrow mirror of one result is allowed.

So I can argue that the mirror is modest: it continuizes the welfare benchmark, not the paper’s equilibrium analysis. I cannot honestly argue that it is not a worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario. The anchor survives the strongest objection I can support.
