Why no mirror

The proposed type-mass procurement question is coherent, but the paper's only numbered theorem establishes a preference property rather than a computational result. The paper describes a negotiation process, yet gives no numbered result with an algorithmic guarantee or complexity claim to anchor the mirror, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns stable procurement and contract selection over type masses; it leaves the feeder simulations and their results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-mass market admit stable outcomes when a type's mass can split across feasible integral bundles?
  • Can exact stable allocations and supporting prices be computed with polynomial-size representations and an efficient algorithm in the support size and time horizon?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its strongest named computationally relevant anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the Appendix: “The prosumer, generator and aggregator preferences described by (4), (5), (6) satisfy full substitutability.” In the paper’s model, this means same-side contracts are substitutes and upstream and downstream contracts are complementary. The theorem establishes a structural condition; using results cited from elsewhere, the paper then connects that condition to stable outcomes and its price-negotiation process. Theorem 1 itself gives no complexity bound, so this is a structural anchor, not a P result.

My lead mirror is Type-Mass Stable Flexibility Procurement. Picture a distribution area with thousands of households whose batteries or electric vehicles participate through a small number of aggregators. A type is a complete description relevant to the paper’s utilities: retail schedule and prices, flexible-resource limits and energy state, degradation cost or charging preference, and aggregator access. A type’s mass \(\mu_t\) is its share of participating households. Standard tariffs and recurring device and travel patterns could make the number of types \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of households, while still allowing meaningful differences between battery and vehicle cohorts.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite horizon \(T\), one DSO, a finite set of aggregators and their trading links, and a support of \(\tau\) types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. It includes each type’s rational data from the paper’s prosumer utility and constraints, plus the DSO’s per-capita demand limits and baseline demand. Keep the paper’s individual contract bundles: each household takes an integral number of fixed-unit contracts over time, and its bundle must satisfy its power and storage constraints. Let \(\mathcal B_t\) be the feasible bundles for type \(t\). The allocation variable \(\lambda_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(b\), with \(\sum_{b\in\mathcal B_t}\lambda_{t,b}=\mu_t\). Thus every household may receive an integral bundle even when the type-level average is fractional.

Given this instance, find a stable flexibility-market outcome, or report that none exists. A solution gives the masses \(\lambda_{t,b}\), the resulting contract flows through the permitted aggregator links, and supporting prices for the type–intermediary–time contract classes. The flows must respect the DSO’s demand limits, each aggregator’s per-time upstream/downstream balance, and every allocated household bundle’s feasibility. At those prices, each allocated bundle must maximize its type’s utility as specified by equation (4); the DSO and aggregators must choose utility-maximizing feasible bundles under equations (5) and (6). Stability additionally means there is no feasible deviation by a positive mass of households together with the intermediaries involved that all those participants would choose over the current outcome. The complexity question is to give an exact algorithm or hardness classification in \(\tau\), \(T\), the number of aggregators and the input bit length.

I expect this problem to be Class A, though that is a conjecture to test, not something Theorem 1 proves. The mass variables suggest a configuration LP over type-and-bundle pairs, potentially with exponentially many columns. Its pricing subproblem is a household’s best feasible schedule at given marginal contract prices. The paper’s time-linked storage limits, power bounds, piecewise-linear retail terms and separable quadratic degradation costs look promising for a time-expanded convex-cost-flow formulation. If that pricing problem and the supporting-price construction are efficient, the continuous market could be computed without enumerating households. A first question for a prover is whether the exact integral household bundles preserve that tractability.

This is a credible mirror of the paper’s underlying task: obtaining specified amounts of demand shifting across time through a DSO–aggregator–prosumer network while respecting the participants’ resource constraints and incentives. It also has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many households share complete profiles, while the market’s decision is about shares of cohorts and aggregate flexibility. The mirror covers the paper’s stability and contract-selection results, not its electrical-network simulations. The market’s simple aggregate demand constraints carry over directly; adding feeder locations, phases or detailed power-flow limits would be a further model extension.

Further questions include whether Theorem 1’s stability guarantee extends to this atomless population with allocations split across bundles; whether the resulting prices and allocations have polynomial-size exact representations; and how runtime depends on the horizon and type support. One could also ask when type proliferation erases the benefit: if location, resource state, travel patterns or tariffs make nearly every household a distinct type, \(\tau\) may approach the population size.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 1 is not a named complexity result, and the paper provides no worst-case running-time bound for its price-negotiation or contract-selection procedures. Also, its simulations use 55 individually modeled prosumers. My case depends on the plausible large-cohort regime being accepted as the authors’ problem, and on an efficient type-level pricing oracle actually existing. Those gaps keep this from being a claim of tractability; they do not erase the well-specified continuous question the paper’s market structure motivates.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational result. It proves that each modeled agent’s preferences satisfy full substitutability; the existence and negotiation consequences come from results cited elsewhere. For a population divided into types, the theorem simply applies to each type’s individual preferences. Replacing counts by masses does not create a complexity question about that theorem. Computing a stable type-mass market would be a new problem motivated by the paper, not a continuous version of a complexity result the paper establishes.

That distinction also weakens the proposed Type-Mass Stable Flexibility Procurement mirror. Its allocation and price formulation is coherent, but it changes the market into one where a type’s mass can be split across integral bundles. The paper’s finite-agent theorem does not establish that this atomless market has the proposed stable outcomes or that its prices can be computed efficiently. Those are legitimate questions, but they extend the paper’s result rather than continuize it.

There is also a practical obstacle to getting a useful support size. A complete type here must include the time-indexed demand and resource constraints, retail schedule and prices, storage state, preferences, and trading links that determine an agent’s choices. In the simulations, battery users have different load and PV profiles; vehicle users also differ in arrival time, departure time, and energy need. Under the paper’s own model, those differences create distinct types, so \(\tau\) can grow with the population. Grouping households by device or tariff alone would omit information their utilities use.

A stronger version of the mirror could posit a large, standardized cohort with identical schedules, constraints, preferences, and aggregator access. That is a sensible scenario, and I cannot rule out that its mass-market computation would be useful. But it is a narrower regime than the heterogeneous prosumer market the paper develops. So the negative case is limited: the paper offers no named complexity result to mirror, and its realistic data may leave little support compression. Those points do not justify the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The standardized-cohort version remains a credible mirror of the market mechanism, even though its algorithmic value is unproved.
