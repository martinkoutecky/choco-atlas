What the mirror covers

No named computational result is covered; the paper’s reported findings concern human behavior in glove-game trials, while the ABMSCORE algorithmic work is cited from other papers.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. This paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its findings that participants’ final coalition was in the core in 42% of trials, and that game theory experience showed no significant association with core membership (\(p=0.86\)), are empirical results, not complexity results. The ABMSCORE results cited from other papers cannot serve as anchors here. So there are no qualifying anchors to mirror.

A plausible *unanchored* continuous question comes from the paper’s core-stability benchmark. Call it Continuum Glove-Core Verification. Let \(T\subseteq\mathbb{N}^2\) be a finite set of glove endowment types \(t=(L_t,R_t)\), and let \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) be each type’s population mass, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A coalition with type-mass vector \(x\ne0\) gives each member payoff

\[ u(x)=\frac{\min\bigl(\sum_t L_tx_t,\sum_t R_tx_t\bigr)}{\sum_t x_t}. \]

The instance also gives a proposed partition as a finite list of coalition templates \(x^j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{T}\setminus\{0\}\), repeated with mass \(a_j\ge0\), satisfying \(\sum_j a_jx_t^j=\mu_t\). The question is whether this partition is core-stable. It is blocked exactly when some nonzero coalition composition \(b\) can be formed from members whose current payoff is lower than \(u(b)\); equivalently,

\[ b_t\le \sum_{j:\,u(x^j)<u(b)} a_jx_t^j \quad\text{for every }t. \]

The answer is yes if no such \(b\) exists; otherwise return a blocking composition. This uses the paper’s glove payoff and its core notion, with mass replacing headcount.

A plausible regime is a large market of small workshops or trading organizations that pool complementary left- and right-hand stocks. Their complete types are their endowment pairs; the population may be large while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct endowments stays modest. Mass is the fraction of organizations of each type. As in the paper’s payoff formula, type-level payoffs can be fractional even though each organization holds integral stock.

For this finite-template verification question, I would expect Class A. Only the finitely many current coalition payoffs need to be considered as thresholds. At each threshold, the available endowment types are known; maximizing their possible coalition payoff is a linear program over mixtures of those types. This is a proposed route for the mirror, not a result established by the paper. It also suggests follow-up questions: whether a stable continuum partition exists, whether a specified coalition can occur in one, and how either answer relates to the finite game when repeated types can be split across coalitions.

The match is strongest for the paper’s core benchmark, not its headline behavioral findings. The authors could recognize the same glove game and stability criterion, but this verification problem does not explain why humans reached a core coalition in only 42% of trials or why game theory experience appeared not to matter. The weakest point is therefore substantial: the paper studies human behavior in games of at most seven players, and the proposed mirror replaces that behavioral question with a computational question about the normative core. A single human also has vanishing mass in the limit; treating a cohort as the focal player would be an additional modeling choice. This is a defensible continuous research question motivated by the paper, but it is not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to anchor a mirror to. Its findings are empirical: a human ended in a core coalition in 42% of trials, and the study found no significant association between game-theory experience and core membership. The paper states no complexity result for deciding, finding, or approximating anything. ABMSCORE’s algorithmic results belong to other papers. That leaves the proponent’s Continuum Glove-Core Verification as a new question motivated by the paper’s benchmark, not a mirror of one of its computational results.

The proposed question is mathematically coherent, but its core test loses the population proportions that are supposed to make it continuous. If a coalition composition \(b\) can block using some positive mass of each type, it can be scaled down until it fits any positive supply of those types whose current payoff is below \(u(b)\). Thus, for this blocking test, the masses matter only through whether they are zero or positive. The precise population shares do not affect whether a block exists.

Requiring a blocker to contain at least some fixed fraction of society would restore a role for the masses, but it would add a large-coalition threshold with no counterpart in the paper’s core definition. Preserving the paper’s finite coalition sizes creates the opposite problem: a finite group has measure zero in an atomless society, so ordinary continuum blocking no longer captures those deviations. Either repair changes what stability means.

The behavioral findings are even harder to carry over. The paper asks whether one human, playing among up to six computerized agents, reaches a core coalition. That human’s share tends to zero as the population grows. Replacing the human with a positive-mass cohort would change the experiment by introducing many humans into the same coalition-forming process. Treating participants across separate trials as a continuous distribution would make the distribution of observations continuous, not the society—an angle outside ChoCo’s scope.

A large market of organizations pooling glove inventories could support a worthwhile continuous core question, and the authors might recognize the game and its stability benchmark in it. But that would be an independent application, not a continuization of this paper’s research question. The honest negative case is strongest on that distinction: this paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and its best proposed substitute either sheds the population shares or changes the core notion. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous model could be invented from the glove game is harder to defend; what the paper does not support is a worthwhile *mirror of its contribution*.
