The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest case is that the paper has a plausible continuous-population analogue of its mechanism, but no qualifying computational anchor. It contains no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. The closest is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: MBM is strategyproof, weakly group strategyproof, budget balanced, individually rational, and proportionally preserving ex-post efficient. Corollary 1 and Lemmas 1–5 establish mechanism properties too; none gives a computational complexity result. The discussion’s unnumbered claim that the mechanism has “low computational time complexity” supplies neither a complexity theorem nor a specified computational problem.

A natural population regime would be a large employee shareholder base with many holders in repeated grant cohorts. Let a shareholder’s type be \((v,a)\), where \(v\) is their valuation and \(a=n s_i^{A_0}\) is their scaled initial stake. A type distribution \(\mu\) then has \(\int a\,d\mu=1\). For a target owner fraction \(\alpha\), a direct limit of the MBM would retain the top \(\alpha\) fraction by bids, price shares at the \(\alpha\)-quantile, and scale the retained holders’ stakes proportionally to their \(a\) values. This is a recognizable population analogue of the paper’s threshold buyout.

The corresponding question would be whether that limit mechanism retains Theorem 1’s properties, with dominance evaluated for individual infinitesimal holders and group strategyproofness for coalitions of positive mass. But this is a mechanism-design question, not the computational question the ChoCo programme requires. And the hardest step is real: as \(n\) grows with \(\bar m/n=\alpha\), the distinction between \(\bar m\) and \(\bar m-1\) vanishes. The randomization that gives the finite mechanism’s threshold shareholder zero expected utility disappears in the limit, so inheritance of the theorem’s properties cannot be assumed.

So there is a credible positive population mirror here, but no admissible named result to anchor a ChoCo computational mirror on. I would not assign it a tractability or hardness class from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only candidate is Theorem 1, but it establishes mechanism properties, not the complexity of a computational problem. The same is true of the lemmas and corollary. The paper’s remark that the mechanism has “low computational time complexity” is not a numbered result or a complexity classification. So no anchor in the case for meets ChoCo’s computational remit.

That does not mean there is no sensible population model. In the employee-cohort scenario, one could use types \((v,a)\), where \(a=n s_i^{A_0}\) is a holder’s scaled initial stake, and represent the population by a distribution over those types. Retaining the highest-valued fraction \(\alpha\), pricing at the cutoff, and scaling retained stakes proportionally is recognizably a continuous version of the mechanism. The finite distinction between retaining \(\bar m\) and \(\bar m-1\) holders then disappears at population scale: the two outcomes differ at only one holder, whose population mass vanishes. The lottery that gives the pivotal holder zero expected utility no longer has a pivotal positive-mass participant. A limit study could ask which mechanism properties survive, but that is a mechanism-design question, not a computational result from this paper.

The strongest computational extension I can derive is to take a finite-support distribution over \((v,a)\), compute the cutoff and aggregate transfers, or choose \(\alpha\) to meet an ownership or welfare target. Those are coherent problems, but the paper neither formulates nor classifies them. Turning them into harder optimization problems—for example by adding constraints on final stakes—would introduce a new problem rather than mirror a named result here. A positive-mass coalition-manipulation question would likewise require defining a new continuum notion of coalition and incentive compatibility.

So the negative case is strongest on scope, not on the claim that no continuous model is possible. The proposed cohort model is plausible, and I cannot honestly rule out worthwhile continuous mechanism-design work on it. But this paper supplies no computational-complexity anchor for that work, and its theorem’s continuous analogue does not fill the programme’s stated gap.
