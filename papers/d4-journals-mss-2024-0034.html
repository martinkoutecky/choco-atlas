The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: this paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorem 1, proved in the paper, characterizes when the policy chosen by issue-by-issue majority is the unique pure-strategy Nash equilibrium outcome for every candidate preference profile consistent with the candidates’ tops. It says this holds if and only if the majority policy is a Condorcet winner and either it is one candidate’s top or the candidates’ tops differ on every issue. Propositions 1 and 2 give necessity and sufficiency pieces of that characterization. These are game-theoretic results, not complexity results; the paper establishes no algorithmic running time or hardness classification. So none qualifies as an anchor, and I can’t present a compliant anchored case.

A plausible *future* computational mirror would take a large electorate on \(q\) binary issues. A type would be a voter’s complete strict separable preference relation over \(\{-1,1\}^q\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the electorate with type \(t\). The instance would list the \(\tau\) supported types with rational masses, plus the two candidates’ tops. Assume no issue has a tied majority, so the coordinatewise majority policy \(x_{\mathrm{maj}}\) is defined. The decision problem would ask whether \(x_{\mathrm{maj}}\) is a Condorcet winner—equivalently, whether every other policy \(z\) is preferred to it by mass at most \(1/2\)—and whether it equals a candidate’s top or the candidates’ tops are opposite on every issue. By Theorem 1, a yes answer is exactly the condition for \(x_{\mathrm{maj}}\) to be the unique pure-strategy equilibrium outcome for every admissible candidate preference profile. A no answer could be witnessed by a defeating policy or by failure of the candidate-top condition.

That scenario is plausible for a large electorate with many repeated preference types, and it mirrors only Theorem 1’s equivalence question. Since rational masses can be scaled to integer voter counts, I would expect any hardness of this decision problem to transfer from a discrete version (Class B), if such hardness is established. The paper does not establish it, though, and the proposed task adds a computational question the authors did not study. That is the weak point—and why this is a promising extension, not a qualifying positive case under the stated anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem for an anchored case is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1, Proposition 2, and Theorem 1 characterize equilibrium outcomes; none gives an algorithm, running time, or hardness result. So the proponent’s candidate—deciding whether the theorem’s conditions hold—is a new computational question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

That candidate is also less settled than its formulation suggests. Testing the top conditions is easy, but checking whether \(x_{\mathrm{maj}}\) is a Condorcet winner ranges over \(2^q\) policies. Its complexity depends on how a separable preference type is encoded; the paper supplies no computational input model. A robustness question, such as the least mass transfer that breaks outcome equivalence, would be a stronger proposed extension, but it too would add an optimization problem the paper never studies.

This is a strong reason to reject the proposed mirror as an anchor for this programme. It is not, honestly, a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Voters can plausibly be grouped by complete preference type, and a continuous robustness question around the theorem’s conditions could be meaningful. The negative case is therefore strong against treating this paper as an anchored computational contribution, but weak against the broader claim that a population-level computational question could be worth pursuing.
