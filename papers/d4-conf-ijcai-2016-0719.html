The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest affirmative point is that MOOCs make a credible high-multiplicity setting: a course can have tens of thousands of students, while a standardized rubric and shared task environment may leave relatively few complete evaluator types. The paper reports 325,523 peer assessments across 17 courses. A type for this purpose would include an evaluator’s signal-generating model, task context, effort costs, and available reporting strategies; mass would be the fraction of evaluators of each type. Within each type, \(x_{\theta,k}\) could track the fraction currently using strategy \(k\). The paper’s own model is simpler: it assumes a common signal model and tracks a continuum of agents by strategy, \(x_k\).

That makes the regime plausible, but it also exposes the central problem for a new mirror: the paper already models a continuum population and studies its replicator dynamics. Its basin-of-attraction calculations are therefore not a discrete result awaiting continuization.

There is no qualifying anchor in the paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its mechanism comparisons are presented through plots, simulations, and empirical estimates. The cited Theorem 12.6 of Easley and Kleinberg concerns fixed points of replicator dynamics; the cited propositions of Hofbauer et al. concern learning dynamics and no regret. They are prior results, not this paper’s computational anchors.

A plausible *follow-up*, clearly not an anchor from this paper, would be: given a rational world model, a peer-prediction mechanism, and a finite strategy set, approximate within \(\varepsilon\) the normalized volume of initial strategy distributions whose replicator trajectories converge to truthfulness. The solution would be an estimate of that basin volume. But the paper already estimates this quantity by sampling initial profiles, so presenting it as a fresh continuous mirror would risk restating its existing continuum analysis.

Thus the paper is good evidence that high-multiplicity populations can be sensible in peer prediction. It does not, however, provide a named computational result on which to base the anchor-led continuous mirror requested here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no named result from the paper as an anchor, and that is the strongest objection to a continuous mirror: the paper gives no theorem or other named result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Its findings are numerical comparisons of replicator dynamics and estimated basin sizes. Those are computational in the ordinary sense, but they do not supply the kind of result this programme would continuize.

The best proposed follow-up—approximating the volume of starting strategy distributions that converge to truthfulness—does not fix that gap. The paper already models a continuum population as a distribution \(x\) over strategies, studies its replicator dynamics, and estimates that basin by sampling initial distributions. A rigorous complexity analysis of the basin-volume problem might be new work, but it would formalize computation on the paper’s existing continuous-population model; it would not create a continuous mirror by continuizing a discrete society. Grouping evaluators into types or adding heterogeneity would be a further model extension, not a mirror of a named result here.

MOOCs are a credible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper itself is evidence for that; multiplicity is not a sound objection. The negative case is narrower: I would reject this paper as an anchor-led continuization candidate. I would not claim that no worthwhile ChoCo research could grow out of peer prediction—only that the plausible follow-up identified here is already continuous in its population model and does not mirror a qualifying computational result.
