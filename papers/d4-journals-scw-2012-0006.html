The continuous mirror question

Given a finite outcome set \(Z\), a finite set \(T\) of complete preference types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a quota \(q>1/2\), does a non-empty-valued CSWP exist? A coalition is a mass vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\ge q\); it weakly or strictly prefers an outcome comparison when every type with \(x_t>0\) has that comparison. Any such coalition can move from any outcome to any other, and paths and the CSWP conditions are as in the paper.

The model it lives in

A weighted quota voting game over a population distributed across preference types, with rational type masses and the paper’s farsighted CSWP notion. The object sought is a non-empty path-selection correspondence σ; there is no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

The paper gives no finite or succinct encoding for a CSWP, whose selected path sets may be unbounded, so an algorithm that outputs a witness needs further representation work.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers CSWP existence in voting games, anchored on Theorem 5’s constructive positive case; it leaves the CSSB results, comparisons with the largest consistent set, and other social environments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding CSWP existence for rational type masses and a quota?
  • Can CSWP witnesses be represented finitely or succinctly for algorithmic use?

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind the anchor requirement asks for: no theorem says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or gives an algorithm or complexity bound. Its Theorems 1–5 are results about stability and existence. So I cannot honestly provide a qualifying computational anchor.

The closest substantive result is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says: (i) for a proper voting game with finitely many outcomes, if every winning coalition strictly prefers one of any two distinct outcomes to the other, a non-empty-valued CSWP exists; (ii) with infinitely many outcomes, a non-empty-valued CSWP may fail to exist; and (iii) even with finitely many outcomes, one may fail to exist when myopic dominance is not total. The paper illustrates (ii) with majority voting over the positive integers and (iii) with a three-player, three-outcome majority game. This is a useful anchor for a mathematical population analogue, but it is not a computational-complexity anchor.

The strongest plausible continuous question I see is Quota-Continuum CSWP Existence, as a new computational question built around Theorem 5’s stability concept. Consider a finite outcome set \(Z\), a finite support \(T\) of complete preference types over \(Z\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a quota \(q>1/2\). A coalition is a mass vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\ge q\); it can move the society from any outcome to any other. For outcomes \(a,b\), the coalition weakly prefers \(b\) to \(a\) if every type with \(x_t>0\) weakly prefers \(b\) to \(a\), and strictly prefers \(b\) if every such type strictly prefers it.

A path is a singleton outcome or a finite sequence of outcomes with a feasible quota coalition attached to each move. The question is: does there exist a map \(\sigma\) assigning each \(a\in Z\) a nonempty set of paths starting at \(a\), such that \(\sigma\) satisfies the paper’s conservative internal and external weak-predictor conditions, with coalitions interpreted as these mass vectors? A solution is the map \(\sigma\), represented by the paths it selects. Since coalition preferences depend on which types have positive mass in a coalition, a coalition can be represented by its support \(H\subseteq T\); that support is feasible exactly when \(\sum_{t\in H}\mu_t\ge q\). This gives a finite representation of the coalition choices, while making fractional mass within a type available as the continuum requires.

The scenario is a large electorate choosing among a fixed set of policy or constitutional outcomes. Many people share the same ranking, so the population may be enormous while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of observed preference types is moderate. Here mass means population share, and the decision variable is the collection of paths the predictor endorses. This is recognizably the paper’s quota-voting and farsighted-coalition question: it preserves the outcomes, coalition power, preferences, and CSWP criterion. It does not make fractional outcomes or probabilities the continuous object.

I would not classify this proposed decision problem as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard on the evidence in the paper. Theorem 5 gives existence conditions and counterexamples, not a complexity result; fractional coalitions also change which deviations are available. Its complexity would need to be established independently. My weakest point is precisely that this is a computational extension of an existence theory, not a mirror of a computational result the authors actually prove. A referee could accept it as a natural high-multiplicity question about their model, but the paper itself cannot anchor the promised complexity comparison.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that there is no computational result in the paper to mirror. Theorems 1–4 concern the properties of CSSB; Theorem 5 concerns when a non-empty-valued CSWP exists. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a computational problem. So the proposed “Quota-Continuum CSWP Existence” is a new algorithmic research question built around the paper’s solution concept, not a continuous mirror of a result the paper establishes.

The stronger version of that question is coherent: take a finite outcome set \(Z\), preference types \(T\) with rational masses, and quota \(q\); ask whether a non-empty-valued CSWP exists, perhaps also asking for a witness. This is a sensible high-multiplicity voting model. A coalition’s behavior depends on which types it contains, and a set \(H\subseteq T\) can form a quota coalition exactly when \(\sum_{t\in H}\mu_t\ge q\). Thus the mass society yields a weighted game over types. In this model, however, the paper supplies no computational target or method to carry over. The work would have to define a finite encoding for the selected paths and then establish the complexity of the new decision or search problem.

That distinction matters here because CSWP existence is the paper’s mathematical subject, not an incidental computational result waiting to be continuized. Recasting existence as a yes/no algorithmic problem does not by itself make the paper a computational study of a continuous population. It could become a worthwhile extension if its complexity turned out to be substantial, but that value would come from the new computational investigation, not from a computational contribution in this paper.

I would not argue that the population model is nonsensical: large electorates with repeated preference types are a credible regime, and the mirror keeps the paper’s outcomes, quota coalitions, and stability concept. That makes this negative case limited. I can defeat the claim that the paper provides a computational anchor, but I cannot honestly rule out that a carefully formulated CSWP complexity problem would be worth pursuing on its own.
