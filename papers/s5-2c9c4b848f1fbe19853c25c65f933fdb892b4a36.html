The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper gives a natural *subject* for a continuization, but no qualifying computational result to anchor one. The paper contains no named computational result: Theorem 1 characterizes the Harsanyi power solution by axioms; Theorem 2 characterizes the Myerson, Harsanyi–Shapley–Shubik, and Harsanyi–Banzhaf solutions; Theorems 3 and 4 extend those characterizations. None establishes a complexity or algorithmic claim. So I cannot name a paper result and state a continuous problem “per anchor” as requested.

A plausible follow-on model would treat agents with the same economic and voting roles as a type, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the population of type \(t\). A coalition would become a vector of participating masses; its economic worth and whether it can control the committee would depend on that vector. The allocation would be a payoff density for each type, distributing economic surplus according to its voting power. In a setting such as a large group of polluters and victims with a small number of shared role types, the high-multiplicity regime is credible.

But this is only a research direction, not a mirror of a result in this paper. Making it faithful would require defining continuous counterparts to winning coalitions, Harsanyi dividends, and the Shapley–Shubik or Banzhaf power measures. In an atomless population, an individual may have zero pivotal power, so those measures cannot simply be carried over unchanged. The paper neither poses nor solves the computational question that such a model would raise. The gap is not that its subject is inherently impossible to continuize; it is that its contribution is axiomatic characterization, rather than computational social choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 characterize allocation rules by axioms; none gives a complexity bound, algorithm, or hardness result. That is a serious mismatch with ChoCo’s purpose. It does not prove that no useful continuous question can be asked, but it means a proposed mirror would need to find its computational problem somewhere beyond the paper’s stated contributions.

The strongest version of the proposed model is plausible: in a large population with a few economic and political roles, describe each coalition by a vector of participating masses and seek a payoff density for each type. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But it requires the worth function and voting rule to be invariant among agents of the same type. The paper allows arbitrary characteristic functions and arbitrary voting games; neither structure is given as a function of type masses. Choosing symmetric games, a representation for worth as a function of masses, and a continuum version of voting power supplies the main model, rather than simply continuing one already in the paper.

One could then study computation for a chosen succinct class, such as weighted voting games with a specified production function. That might be worthwhile work, but its difficulty would depend on those new input representations and assumptions. In the paper’s unrestricted formulation, the coalition worths and winning coalitions can be given explicitly over all subsets; the solution is a finite sum of Harsanyi dividends weighted by power indices. There is no stated complexity problem for a continuum to resolve, and hardness introduced by a succinct encoding would belong to that encoding’s model.

The voting indices also need a substantive limit choice. Shapley–Shubik power uses random orderings of a finite committee, while Banzhaf power uses a distribution over coalitions. For an atomless population, an individual has zero mass, so individual pivotality vanishes; a type-level density or normalized limit may still be defined, but the choice of replication and normalization becomes part of the new question. That could lead to interesting asymptotic power theory, yet it is not a computational result established or posed here.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope claim, not as a proof that no sensible continuum model exists. Large groups of polluters and victims could fit the programme’s population premise. What does not carry over is a computational contribution from this paper: its contribution is axiomatic, and turning its setting into a computational continuum problem requires adding the symmetry, representation, and limit choices that would define a different research question.
