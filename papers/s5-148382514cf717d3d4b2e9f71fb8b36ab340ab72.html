The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets your anchor requirement: the paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification.

Its six numbered theorems are about topological and behavioral properties of binary relations: when connectedness forces completeness or transitivity, how those implications characterize connectedness, and how path-connected sections or the Phragmén–Brouwer property constrain transitivity. They do not study a computational problem or give an algorithm or complexity reduction. The propositions are likewise structural.

A continuum of consumers appears in the applications, but each consumer’s preference relation is still the object of analysis. Making a population distribution over consumer types the central object would pose a new computational question, not mirror one of the paper’s named results. Treating the continuous choice set \(X\) as the mirror would continuize the object space rather than the population.

So there is no honest anchor, and therefore no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. The paper’s continuous mathematics is not continuization of society in ChoCo’s sense.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s central observation is the strongest case against a mirror: this paper states no named computational result. Its six theorems and supplementary propositions characterize when topological conditions on a choice set and a binary relation imply completeness, transitivity, or related properties. None poses a computational problem or gives an algorithm, complexity bound, or reduction. There are no anchors to defeat; a more ingenious choice among the paper’s results cannot supply the missing computational question.

The paper’s continuum is also on the wrong axis for ChoCo. In the theorems, \(X\) is a set of alternatives and \(R\) is a preference relation over those alternatives. Even the application to economies with a continuum of traders applies assumptions to each consumer’s preferences; it does not study a distribution of consumer types as computational input. Repeating a preference type across many agents merely repeats the theorem. Aggregating their relations could define a population-level problem, but would require a social aggregation rule and a computational task that this paper does not provide.

A plausible new project could ask how a continuum population of preference types affects social completeness or transitivity. The paper’s discussion of collective choice points toward that territory, but it supplies neither a formal aggregation model nor a computational result to mirror. That could be worthwhile as new work; it would not be a continuous mirror of this paper under the programme’s criteria. The negative case is therefore strong against a mirror, though it cannot rule out adjacent research inspired by the paper.
