The continuous mirror question

For a fixed query \(x\) and finite reply set \(\mathcal{Y}\), given \(q\) advertiser types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), reward functions \(r_\theta\), reference distribution \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), proposal distribution \(\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}\), and per-capita weight \(\lambda>0\), approximate in total variation the policy maximizing \(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\sum_\theta\mu_\theta r_\theta(x,y)]-\lambda D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\). Using \(M\) proposals and weights proportional to \(\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_j\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}(y_j\mid x)}\exp(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta r_\theta(x,y_j)/\lambda)\), determine the sample count and running time needed for error at most \(\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), in terms of \(q\), input size, and proposal-overlap parameters. For a finite population of \(n\) advertisers, this recovers the per-capita objective with \(\lambda=\tau/n\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete advertiser reward and context profiles, each carrying mass \(\mu_\theta\); the decision variable is a reply distribution \(\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\), and the objective is mean advertiser reward minus \(\lambda D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its strongest computational anchor is Corollary 4.1, proved here via Theorem A.1: for any reported aggregate reward \(r\) and any candidate generator \(\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}\) whose support covers \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), MOSAIC’s policy based on \(M\) candidate replies converges as \(M\to\infty\) to the distribution maximizing expected advertiser reward minus \(\tau D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\). That optimum is \(\pi_r^*(y\mid x)\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\tau)\). This is an algorithmic convergence result, not a complexity-classification result; I use it as my sole anchor.

A plausible mirror is Continuum Advertiser Aggregation. Fix a query \(x\) and a maximum reply length, so the admissible reply set \(\mathcal Y\) is finite. A type \(\theta\) specifies an advertiser’s complete reward function over those replies and any context used by the allocation rule. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over types, given by its \(q\) positive-mass types and their rational masses. Its per-capita reward for reply \(y\) is \(\bar r_\mu(x,y)=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta r_\theta(x,y)\). With a per-capita reference weight \(\lambda\), the allocation problem is to maximize \(\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[\bar r_\mu(x,y)]-\lambda D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\). The solution is the corresponding tilted distribution \(\pi_\mu^*(y\mid x)\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(\bar r_\mu(x,y)/\lambda)\).

The computational question is: given \(x\), the rational type masses, reward and probability evaluation access, \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), and a generator \(\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}\) with \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}\), how many candidate replies suffice to output a policy within total variation distance \(\varepsilon\) of \(\pi_\mu^*\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and what is the running time in \(q\), the input bit length, \(\varepsilon\), and \(\delta\)? The candidate solution is MOSAIC’s importance-weighted sampler: draw \(y_1,\ldots,y_M\sim\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}\), assign weights \(w_j=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_j\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}(y_j\mid x)}\exp(\bar r_\mu(x,y_j)/\lambda)\), then return a candidate with probability proportional to its weight. Corollary 4.1 gives convergence for this mirror. Aggregating rewards costs \(q\) evaluations per candidate, so the allocation stage takes \(O(Mq)\) reward evaluations, in addition to candidate generation and probability evaluations.

The regime I find most credible is a large market of small advertisers using standardized campaigns: for example, many franchisees or resellers with the same reward model, approved claims, and terms. Their complete types can coincide, leaving millions of advertisers represented by a much smaller \(q\). A finite electorate with counts \(n_\theta\) maps to \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/n\); dividing the original objective by \(n\) gives the per-capita version with \(\lambda=\tau/n\). Thus the mass representation is the high-multiplicity form of the paper’s allocation problem. The paper’s objective already depends on advertisers through their aggregate reward, so I am not claiming a new objective; the mirror’s point is to encode a large population by its type support and study the resulting computational costs.

I expect Class A behavior for approximation in a well-conditioned regime: with bounded importance weights and efficient reward oracles, finite-sample bounds should yield an efficient approximation algorithm whose cost depends on \(q\), not the total advertiser count. Corollary 4.1 itself supplies only asymptotic convergence, however; it does not establish a polynomial bound in \(\varepsilon^{-1}\) and \(\log(1/\delta)\) for all inputs. The further questions are to prove such bounds under explicit overlap conditions, determine worst-case sample complexity when rewards concentrate on rare replies, and relate the rational-mass version to discrete high-multiplicity instances.

This case covers Section 4’s allocation and convergence result. Theorem 5.1, which proves dominant-strategy truthfulness for finite advertisers and a fixed candidate set, is not a complexity anchor here, and this mirror does not extend its payment guarantee to a continuum of advertisers. My weakest point is that the full MOSAIC mechanism treats truthfulness as central, while this mirror covers its allocation side. Also, when brands and reward functions are genuinely unique, \(q\) may be close to the advertiser count; the high-multiplicity regime then offers little compression. The absence of a named hardness result also means this paper provides no discrete hardness whose dissolution in the continuum could be tested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Corollary 4.1, but it establishes asymptotic convergence, not a complexity bound for a population problem. The proposed continuous version is mathematically natural; the difficulty for the negative case is explaining why it would be worth a separate continuization study.

For allocation, the mechanism sees advertisers only through their aggregate reward. With type masses \(\mu_\theta\), that aggregate is \(R_\mu(y)=\sum_\theta \mu_\theta r_\theta(y)\). Substituting \(R_\mu\) for the finite sum leaves the optimal distribution and MOSAIC’s candidate weights unchanged. The proof of Corollary 4.1 applies to that aggregate directly; with bounded importance weights, Appendix A.3 already supplies a finite-sample total-variation bound. Computing the aggregate for each candidate takes \(q\) reward evaluations. Thus the strongest proposed mirror has no new population-dependent allocation problem: repeated types can be collapsed to weighted rewards, and the candidate reply space and its sampling difficulty remain the same.

That leaves two regimes. If many advertisers really share complete types, including rewards and context, the representation compresses, but the allocation computation is the same weighted sum the paper already uses. If their complete types are distinct, then \(q\) may be as large as the advertiser count, so there is little high-multiplicity advantage. In either regime, any serious sample-complexity question is driven by overlap between the generator and target distributions, and by the cost of generating and scoring text. Absolute continuity alone gives no uniform finite-sample guarantee; the paper’s bounded-weight result depends on the reply-space size, which grows rapidly with maximum reply length. Those are real computational issues, but they do not appear to be issues created by making the advertiser population continuous.

Theorem 5.1 offers no stronger population mirror. In a non-atomic society, one advertiser’s report has zero mass and cannot affect the allocation, making individual truthfulness vacuous. Giving advertisers positive mass restores meaningful individual incentives but brings back an atomic population. One could study strategic type blocks, but that changes the incentive question rather than continuing the theorem’s individual-advertiser guarantee.

So my case against is that the only plausible mirror either reduces exactly to the paper’s existing aggregate-reward computation or moves to a different, likely vacuous incentive problem. I would not claim this proves that no valuable model can be devised. The negative case is weakest on the standardized-advertiser regime: a study of finite-resource approximation bounds or lower bounds there could still be useful. But Corollary 4.1, as anchored here, does not by itself expose a distinct continuous-population complexity landscape.
