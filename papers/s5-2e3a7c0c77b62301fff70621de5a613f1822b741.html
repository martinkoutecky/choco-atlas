What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered: the main Theorem and supporting fragmentation Lemma are structural, while the examples and closing question do not supply a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is that the paper’s finite electorate can be represented by masses on preference types: \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society with complete ranking \(t\). One could then ask whether a Pareto social welfare function with uniformly bounded dependence on alternatives must have a mass-based analogue of sub-dictatorship. That is a recognizable population lift, but it is an axiomatic question, not a computational one—and the paper does not define what “mass-based sub-dictatorship” should mean.

There is no eligible computational anchor. The main result, printed as an unnumbered “Theorem:” and proved here, says that for infinite \(X\), a social welfare function on \(W(X)^N\) or \(L(X)^N\) satisfying Pareto and uniformly bounded dependence is sub-dictatorial: for every alternative-set size at least three, there are infinitely many sets of that size on which, in specified restricted domains, one individual’s strict preferences determine strict social preferences. This is a structural theorem about aggregation, not a result about complexity, algorithms, or running time. The paper’s graph lemma and its references to Arrow and Ramsey do not supply computational anchors either.

The obstacle to a stronger positive case is built into that theorem: it singles out a named individual, and the paper does not assume anonymity. Grouping people by ranking and replacing the electorate with masses can therefore lose information the rule uses. Including identity in each type preserves the model but can leave as many types as individuals, defeating the high-multiplicity regime. Requiring an aggregate-only rule would change the assumptions and create a new axiomatic problem.

So there is no per-anchor continuous computational problem to state, and no Class A/B/C prediction to make from this paper. Its subject is the information needed to order alternatives in a finite society; turning the population into a continuum would be a conceivable new axiomatic study, but it falls outside the programme’s computational scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to continuize. The main theorem says that Pareto and uniformly bounded dependence force sub-dictatorship on certain restricted domains. That is an axiomatic structural result. The graph lemma and its Ramsey-theoretic proof are mathematical machinery for that result; neither states a computational problem or its complexity. The paper gives no finite encoding of a social welfare function over an infinite alternative set, no computational input or output, and no running-time question. So the proponent has no named computational anchor. There is no basis here for a ChoCo-style complexity mirror.

The strongest population version would represent complete rankings as types with masses \(\mu_t\), then ask whether Pareto and bounded dependence imply a mass-level form of sub-dictatorship. That is a plausible axiomatic question for a large electorate. But the theorem singles out an individual whose preferences determine the social ordering. In a continuous population, a singleton has zero mass. Recasting the conclusion around a positive-mass group means changing that group’s preference together; it is a different intervention and needs a new theorem. Preserving the paper’s named individuals in the type description can preserve identity, but may leave one type per person and remove the high-multiplicity regime. Requiring the rule to depend only on the distribution instead changes the class of social welfare functions being studied.

One might add a computational task, such as evaluating an encoded rule or finding its sufficient sets. The paper specifies no rule encoding or such task, and its domain consists of profiles over infinitely many alternatives. Choosing a representation and a finite input model could produce worthwhile research, but it would supply the computational subject that the paper itself does not have. The paper’s own closing question about connected alternative spaces concerns the alternatives and preference domain, not a continuous population.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated computational programme: the mass-based idea may lead to new axiomatic work, but none of this paper’s results anchors a continuous computational mirror. The limit of the claim is its universality. One could invent a computational problem around a new encoding of these rules, but that would be a new project, not a mirror of a result in this paper.
