The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, the paper has no eligible anchor under your rule: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. It does contain named algorithms, but no NP-hardness result and no formal complexity bound for them. The strongest positive case I can make therefore uses Algorithm 2 as a surrogate anchor, not as a qualifying theorem. Algorithm 2 is presented in this report; the underlying deferred-acceptance idea is credited to Gale and Shapley [7].

Algorithm 2 takes candidates’ preference lists, program capacities and merit lists, and optionally minimum cutoffs and prior-round state. Candidates apply in preference order. A program retains the highest-priority eligible applicants; those rejected advance to their next choice. It protects candidates meeting its minimum cutoff, and handles equal-rank applicants as a group: when rejecting the whole lowest-ranked tie group would leave fewer than capacity, the algorithm keeps that group, even if this creates supernumerary seats. It outputs candidate allocations, updated preference-list positions and program waitlists. The report presents this as its full deferred-acceptance algorithm for multiple rounds and tied ranks, but does not state or prove a numbered complexity theorem for it.

Lead mirror — Mass Deferred Acceptance for Ranked Admissions. An instance has a finite set \(P\) of actual or virtual programs and an explicit support \(T\) of applicant types. Each type \(t\) specifies a complete preference list, eligibility for each program, its merit tier at each program, and—when modeling a later round—its relevant prior-round status. The input gives each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and each program a rational per-capita capacity \(q_p\). Virtual programs can encode the paper’s category, disability, home-state and gender pools; type data must include every characteristic those rules use.

The question is to compute the allocation \(x_{t,p}\) of each type’s mass to each program, plus any unassigned mass, by running the mass version of Algorithm 2: type mass applies in preference order, programs retain merit-priority mass, rejected mass advances, and minimum cutoffs and equal-rank group treatment follow the stated rules. The output is the candidate-optimal fair allocation and the resulting waitlists, with any over-capacity admissions arising from the specified tie or guarantee rules. The objective is mechanism-specific: compute the candidate-optimal stable allocation, rather than optimize a separate social-welfare score.

A plausible regime is a large centralized intake—perhaps hundreds of thousands of applicants to colleges or standardized training programs—where many applicants share the same preference list, eligibility profile and merit tiers. For example, if priority is based on a small set of score bands and category rules, the number of applicants \(N\) can greatly exceed the number of complete types \(\tau\). This is the same candidate-program allocation problem the authors study, with population counts replaced by masses and seat counts by per-capita supplies. I expect this mirror to be tractable: each type’s mass advances through at most its listed programs, so a direct mass-DA implementation should run in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|P|\), the preference-list length and the rational input bit length \(L\). When masses and capacities are multiples of \(1/N\), scaling by \(N\) recovers the corresponding finite counts.

The mirror covers Algorithm 2’s allocation core in a round, including the minimum-cutoff and tie rules; the full multi-round mechanism can be represented by adding the previous-round state to each type and repeating the computation. It does not establish that every detail of the report’s implementation—such as all dereservation runs and reporting-center rules—has the same compressed bound. The question it offers the authors is whether their fairness, candidate-optimality and truthfulness claims persist for this mass-based high-multiplicity input.

The weakest point is that the report’s real merit lists rank individual candidates, and it says ties are negligible. If those individualized ranks must be part of a type, then \(\tau\) may be close to \(N\), removing the compression advantage. The mirror is strongest for a credible admissions regime with repeated preference and priority profiles, such as one using standardized merit bands; it is not a claim that the 2018 JEE instance itself had few types. That leaves a sensible mirror of the paper’s central DA computation, but its status as a computational-complexity contribution is limited: the report supplies no qualifying numbered result, and the discrete DA procedure is already an algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational complexity result. The proponent acknowledges this and uses Algorithm 2 as a surrogate anchor. But Algorithm 2 is the paper’s implementation of deferred acceptance, whose central algorithmic idea the report credits to Gale and Shapley. A mass version would create a new problem to study; it would not mirror a computational result this paper establishes.

Even granting Algorithm 2 as an anchor, the proposed mass deferred acceptance has an awkward choice. Preserve the paper’s merit lists and individual preferences exactly, and applicants are generally distinguishable by their ranks, choices, and eligibility. The complete types may then be nearly as numerous as the applicants, so the mass representation offers little compression. Coarsen applicants into repeated preference and merit tiers to obtain a small support, and the model changes the priority structure that drives the paper’s fairness guarantees. That coarsening could be a sensible new admissions model, but it is not the paper’s central computational problem.

For that coarsened model, the allocation itself appears to reduce to applying deferred acceptance to type masses and per-capita capacities. Each mass follows a preference list, programs retain mass by priority tier, and rejected mass moves on. This is a natural mass variant, but the discrete algorithm is already polynomial; the continuous version offers no apparent complexity landscape for the programme to chart. It may merit a short formalization, but its likely role is an elementary extension of a known algorithm, not a substantial ChoCo mirror.

The proposed mirror also cannot carry over truthfulness in the usual individual sense. In an atomless society, changing one person’s report changes zero mass and cannot affect the allocation, so individual truthfulness becomes vacuous. Giving a whole positive-mass type the ability to misreport instead asks about group manipulation, a different problem. Adding round status, revised credentials, and reporting decisions to the type definition is legitimate, but heterogeneous behavior splits types and can erase the compression that motivated the model.

I would not claim that a continuum matching model is inherently meaningless: with repeated preference and priority profiles, mass allocation is coherent. That is the weak point in a universal negative. The stronger conclusion is narrower: this report offers no computational theorem to mirror, and its best algorithmic surrogate is standard deferred acceptance, for which continuization seems to add little computational substance. If pursued, the mass model would be a separate matching-market study rather than a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
