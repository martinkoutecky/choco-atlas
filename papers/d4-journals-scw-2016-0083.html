What the mirror covers

The proposed model uses Proposition 2’s equilibrium characterization as its anchor. It leaves Theorem 1’s comparative statics, the welfare comparisons, and the acceptability results without a surviving computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: a continuous-population version of the paper’s equilibrium contribution problem. Take a large investment partnership in which many partners share one of a small number \(\tau\) of payoff-relevant profiles. A type \(t\) is a partner’s CARA risk-aversion coefficient \(a_t\); all other payoff-relevant features—cost of contribution, success probability, returns, and agreement—are shared. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of partners of type \(t\), and let \(s_t\) be their common contribution rate. The population mean contribution is \(\bar{s}=\sum_t\mu_t s_t\).

Under \(PE[\gamma,\alpha]\), the type-\(t\) partner’s net payoff is \(\gamma r s_t+(1-\gamma)r\bar{s}\) on success and \(-\alpha(1-\beta)s_t-(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\bar{s}\) on failure. This is the per-partner limit of the paper’s proportional/equal allocation rules. Given those payoffs, the partner chooses \(s_t\geq0\) to maximize expected CARA utility. A continuous equilibrium is a vector \((s_t)_{t\in T}\) in which each type chooses a best response to the population mean and \(\bar{s}=\sum_t\mu_t s_t\).

Call the task Typed Continuum Partnership Equilibrium. Its input is rational type masses \(\mu_t\), risk coefficients \(a_t\), shared parameters \(p,r,\beta\), and a fixed agreement \((\gamma,\alpha)\). Ask for the equilibrium contribution rate of each type and the mean contribution; an approximation version can ask for rates with at most \(\varepsilon\) utility regret per type and an aggregate consistent to tolerance \(\varepsilon\). For a regime with many partners but few shared types, this is a genuine population continuization: mass replaces head counts, while the agreement and strategic contribution choice remain the paper’s. The authors could recognize the model as their partnership game with a large, typed population.

The closest named result is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says that if \(PE[\gamma,\alpha]\) is acceptable and \(\max\{\alpha,\gamma\}>0\), the game has a unique Nash equilibrium, with each partner’s contribution given by
\[ s_i^*= \frac{ n(r+1-\beta)\frac{1}{a_i} -\bigl((1-\gamma)r+(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\bigr)\sum_{j\in N}\frac{1}{a_j} }{ n(r+1-\beta)\bigl(\gamma r+\alpha(1-\beta)\bigr) } \ln\!\left( \frac{pr(n\gamma-\gamma+1)} {(1-p)(1-\beta)(n\alpha-\alpha+1)} \right). \]
If \(PE[0,0]\) is acceptable, it instead has a continuum of equilibria: every nonnegative contribution profile whose total is
\[ \frac{n}{a_n(1-\beta+r)} \ln\!\left(\frac{pr}{(1-p)(1-\beta)}\right) \]
is an equilibrium. This explicit characterization is the best foothold for a continuous mirror. In the positive-contribution regime with \(\gamma,\alpha>0\), its high-multiplicity limit gives
\[ \bar{s}= \frac{\sum_t\mu_t/a_t}{r+1-\beta} \ln\!\left(\frac{pr\gamma}{(1-p)(1-\beta)\alpha}\right), \]
when the expression yields an acceptable interior equilibrium. That suggests a tractable answer for this restricted mirror.

The limitation is material: Proposition 2 is an equilibrium characterization, not a stated complexity result. The paper has no named result asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. Theorem 1, also proved here, says equilibrium total contributions increase with \(\gamma\), decrease with \(\alpha\), and that both effects grow with the number of partners; it is a comparative-statics result, not a complexity anchor. Under your strict anchor rule, there is therefore no eligible computational anchor, and this proposed equilibrium-computation problem is an extension of the paper rather than a mirror of a computational result it proves.

The boundary also marks where this positive case weakens. Productive efficiency in Theorem 1 favors \(\gamma=1,\alpha=0\) when that agreement is acceptable. In the atomless limit, however, an individual’s contribution no longer affects their equal share of failure losses when \(\alpha=0\); the finite-population formula’s contribution scale grows with \(n\) at that boundary. So the clean tractability claim above covers the interior equilibrium problem, not the paper’s productive-efficiency optimum or its full agreement family. That is my weakest point: a plausible population mirror exists, but the paper itself supplies no qualifying computational result to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its propositions and theorems characterize Nash equilibria, establish comparative statics, and compare welfare across agreements. None classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Turning one of those results into “compute the answer from rational inputs” would add a computational question to the paper; it would not continuize a computational question the paper already studies. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is decisive.

Proposition 2 is the best foothold for a continuous model. A large partnership with finitely many risk types and rational type masses is a sensible population regime, and its mean-field equilibrium is a recognizable extension of the partnership game. The objection is not that the limit is ill-posed or that the individual contribution becomes meaningless. The proposed “Typed Continuum Partnership Equilibrium” is still a new equilibrium-analysis problem, though, not a complexity result in the paper. Asking for algorithms or regret guarantees would place a computational wrapper around the model without connecting to a computational problem proved here.

Theorem 1 fares no better as an anchor. One can normalize total contributions per partner and ask whether its comparative statics persist for a type distribution; that is a reasonable continuous question. But it remains a comparative-statics question. A stronger alternative would ask for the welfare-maximizing agreement given rational type masses. That is also a possible research question, but it extends the paper’s agreement-design analysis; it does not uncover a computational result in it.

I would not claim that no plausible high-multiplicity partnership regime exists. Large professional partnerships are conceivable, and the paper itself studies how results change with the number of partners. The negative case is therefore weak if “no worthwhile continuous mirror” means no worthwhile continuous economic model in any scenario. It is much stronger, and decisive by ChoCo’s criteria, if the claim is about a mirror worth the computational-complexity programme’s time: the paper offers no computational result for that mirror to chart.
