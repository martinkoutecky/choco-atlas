What the mirror covers

The proposed target is Theorem 2's existence and uniqueness characterization; it does not cover Theorem 1, Proposition 1, or the appeals discussion.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow and rests on Theorem 2. Kojima proves here that, for a finite market, the student-proposing stable mechanism \(\phi^S\) is robustly stable if and only if the priority structure is acyclic. Since \(\phi^S\) is the unique stable and strategy-proof mechanism, a robustly stable mechanism exists exactly in the acyclic markets, and then it is \(\phi^S\). Theorem 1’s impossibility is the cyclic side of this picture, so I would not add it as a separate anchor.

This is a structural existence characterization, not a theorem stated as a complexity classification: the paper does not analyze the encoding or running time of deciding acyclicity. If the anchor requirement means an explicit result such as “in P” or “NP-hard,” then the paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 2 is the nearest foothold for a positive case because it turns a mechanism-existence question into a condition on the market’s priorities.

Lead mirror: Tier-Robust-DA. Consider a large school district with many applicants but relatively few combinations of reported ranking and legally defined priority category. A complete type \(t\) consists of a ranking \(P_t\) and the applicant’s priority category at each school. The input gives rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_c\). Thus a discrete market with \(n_t\) applicants of type \(t\) and quota \(q_c\) corresponds to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(\kappa_c=q_c/n\), while the number \(N\) of applicants can be much larger than the support size \(\tau\).

The decision rule is mass-level student-proposing deferred acceptance. Its allocation variables are \(x_{t,c}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_c x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,c}\leq\kappa_c\). If a school’s capacity cuts through a priority category, the corresponding cohort is split in mass; each applicant still receives one school. The question is: is this DA rule immune, for every preference distribution consistent with the given priority-category masses, to a positive-mass cohort that first changes its reported ranking and then rematches to a preferred school by taking a vacancy or displacing lower-priority mass? A “no” answer has a report deviation and a feasible rematching flow as a witness; a “yes” answer needs a structural characterization or algorithm certifying robustness.

The scenario is plausible in large school systems where applicant numbers dwarf the number of ranking and priority-category combinations. The paper’s focus remains recognizable: stable assignment, truthful reporting, and the same combined manipulation. The mirror’s expected direction is Class A, as a first conjecture: with finite support and rational capacities, a mass version of the priority-cycle obstruction may reduce to finitely many flow or capacity tests. Theorem 2 does not establish that; finding the right continuous analogue of acyclicity is the research question. It also prompts questions about finite-to-continuum approximation, whether priority rules can be repaired by moving a small amount of mass between categories, and how the result changes when schools’ priorities are interdependent.

The mirror covers the existence and uniqueness characterization in Theorem 2, not the paper’s specific appeal-process discussion or Proposition 1’s guarantee for a single student’s deviation.

The soft spot is substantial. The paper assumes strict priorities over individual students; under those priorities, exact types that include priority position are not repeated, so the paper’s own market does not supply a regime with \(N\gg\tau\). The mirror therefore uses priority categories with mass splitting, and it replaces an individual manipulation by a positive-mass cohort deviation. Those changes are defensible co-modifications for a high-multiplicity market, but they mean the paper motivates the question rather than proves that this precise continuum model is its direct limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to continue. Theorem 2 characterizes when a mechanism exists: the student-optimal stable mechanism is robustly stable exactly when priorities are acyclic. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 are also existence and incentive results. None poses a complexity question or gives an algorithm whose behaviour over a continuous population could be studied. Deciding acyclicity or finding a priority repair could be computational questions, but the first is a new wrapper around the paper’s condition and the second is a new design problem. Neither is a computational result of this paper being continuized.

The proposed Tier-Robust-DA also has a fork in its meaning. In the paper’s model, priorities are strict over named students. A student’s exact priority position distinguishes them from every other student, so preserving the model’s full type description gives support size equal to the population. To obtain repeated types, the mirror groups students into priority categories and lets capacity split a category. That may be a sensible school-assignment model, but it changes strict priorities into tiers. Then an individual has zero mass: their report cannot affect the aggregate deferred-acceptance outcome, so the paper’s individual misreport-and-block condition disappears in the limit.

The strongest repair is to ask whether a positive-mass cohort can misreport and then rematch. That is coherent, but it changes the strategic unit from one student to a coordinated group. It also needs a rule for blocking at a school whose capacity cuts through a priority tier: a fractional allocation to the tier does not say which students can displace whom after assignment. Lottery-based tie-breaking could supply that missing detail, but it adds another part of the model. The result would be a new group-robustness problem for a continuum assignment market, not a continuous characterization of Theorem 2. The paper itself signals that group robust stability is a neighboring question.

This is a case against treating the paper as a strong ChoCo mirror, not proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large district with repeated ranking and priority categories is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the repaired cohort question may be worth studying. The negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational anchor and weakest against that repaired scenario: once its additional choices are made, I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile project.
