The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete XOS buyer types \(\Theta\), masses \(\mu\), per-capita item supplies \(q\), the paper’s oracle access, and a benchmark routine \(A_\infty\) returning a feasible mass allocation \(x^A\) with XOS witnesses, compute anonymous prices \(p\) such that the denominator-compatible high-multiplicity market with \(N\mu_\theta\) buyers of each type and \(Nq_j\) perfect-substitute copies of item \(j\), with each buyer receiving an integral bundle \(B\subseteq M\), has limiting per-capita welfare at least \(\frac{1}{2}W(x^A)-\varepsilon\) for every arrival order and demand tie-breaking.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity posted-price combinatorial auction with finite complete types \(\Theta\), mass \(\mu\), per-capita supplies \(q\), integral bundles \(B\subseteq M\), mass variables \(x_{\theta,B}\ge0\), feasibility constraints \(\sum_Bx_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,B\ni j}x_{\theta,B}\le q_j\), objective \(W(x)=\sum_{\theta,B}x_{\theta,B}v_\theta(B)\), and anonymous item prices inducing sequential demand.

The objection that survived

The opponent correctly notes that a type-frequency distribution \(\mu\) is not automatically the paper’s profile distribution \(F\), and that the per-capita-supply model plus benchmark routine \(A_\infty\) require an explicit finite-to-continuum construction.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 3.1 and 4.1, including the XOS, submodular, gross-substitutes, and MPH-\(k\) restrictions. It leaves aside the revenue literature, simultaneous Walrasian comparisons, and Appendix C’s impossibility example.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite replicated markets with \(N\mu_\theta\) buyers and \(Nq_j\) copies be shown to converge to the stated mass process with the same welfare guarantee?
  • Can the configuration LP for \(x_{\theta,B}\) be solved with demand-query separation in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\lvert\Theta\rvert\), representation length, and \(1/\varepsilon\)?
  • For MPH-\(k\) types, what compressed clone representation preserves the \(m^k\) dependence and the paper’s price guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is an algorithmic Class A mirror. This paper has no numbered NP-hardness or completeness theorem: the hardness remarks in the introduction and the welfare-loss example in Appendix C are not qualifying computational anchors. Its best anchors are therefore the two constructive pricing theorems proved in the paper, with Theorem 3.1 as the lead.

The natural regime is a large resource market with finitely many recurring buyer types. Items are resource categories, such as cloud instances, spectrum channels, or service slots. A type \(\theta\) is a complete valuation description \(v_\theta:2^M\to[0,1]\), including its XOS or MPH representation and demand behaviour. The population is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite type set \(\Theta\), with \(|\Theta|=\tau\ll N\), where \(N\) is the number of microscopic buyers. Goods must scale with the population: if the original market has \(n_0\) buyer positions and one copy of each item, repeat the market \(N\) times, giving \(Nn_0\) buyers and \(N\) copies of each item. After normalizing population mass to \(1\), the per-capita supply is \(q_j=1/n_0\). This preserves the original supply-to-population ratio while making the high-multiplicity limit nondegenerate.

Individual buyers still receive integral bundles \(B\subseteq M\). Only the mass assigned to each bundle is fractional. An allocation is

\[ x_{\theta,B}\ge 0, \]

subject to

\[ \sum_{B\subseteq M}x_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{\theta}\sum_{B\ni j}x_{\theta,B}\le q_j \quad\text{for every }j\in M. \]

Its per-capita welfare is

\[ W(x)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{B\subseteq M}x_{\theta,B}v_\theta(B). \]

For rational data, clearing denominators turns \(x\) into a finite allocation of integral bundles to many repeated agents. Thus this is genuinely population continuization, not fractionalizing an individual’s outcome.

The lead problem is XOS Continuum Posted Pricing\(_\infty\). Its instance consists of \(M,\Theta,\mu,q\), XOS valuation descriptions or the same value/XOS/demand-oracle access assumed by the paper, an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon\), and a welfare routine \(A_\infty\) returning a feasible mass allocation \(x^A\). Given prices \(p\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^M\), buyers arrive in an arbitrary order, and each infinitesimal buyer of type \(\theta\) chooses a utility-maximizing available integral bundle,

\[ B\in\arg\max_{B'\text{ available}} \left(v_\theta(B')-\sum_{j\in B'}p_j\right). \]

The question is to compute anonymous item prices \(p\) such that every admissible arrival order and demand tie-breaking produce welfare at least

\[ \frac12 W(x^A)-\varepsilon. \]

When \(A_\infty\) is optimal, this becomes a \(1/2\)-approximation to the continuous optimum. The solution is the price vector \(p\), together with the induced posted-price allocation.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that, for a distribution \(F\) over XOS valuation profiles given by sample access, if one has black-box access to a welfare-maximization algorithm \(A\) and an XOS query oracle, then for every \(\varepsilon>0\) one can compute item prices in time \(\operatorname{POLY}(m,n,1/\varepsilon)\) such that, for every buyer arrival order, the expected posted-price welfare is at least

\[ \frac12\mathbb E_{v\sim F}[\operatorname{SW}(A(v))]-\varepsilon. \]

The continuous problem is the high-multiplicity version of exactly this question: the random finite profile is replaced by its finite-support population distribution \(\mu\), and the expectation becomes integration over population mass. The paper’s large-market motivation makes this especially plausible: it explicitly argues that posted prices are useful when aggregate demand can be predicted, and its discussion asks about markets with identical copies of items.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable. The mass-welfare problem is a configuration LP with exponentially many bundle variables but only \(\tau\) type constraints and \(m\) supply constraints. Its pricing/separation step asks, for each type,

\[ \max_{B\subseteq M} \left(v_\theta(B)-\sum_{j\in B}p_j\right), \]

which is precisely a demand query. Thus the continuous allocation can plausibly be computed by column generation, and the paper’s welfare-contribution pricing argument can be performed at type level. The expected complexity is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the representation length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), under the same oracle assumptions as Theorem 3.1. The substantive phenomenon is that discrete bundle-assignment hardness can disappear when repeated agents may be split by mass across integral bundles.

The second, broader mirror is MPH-\(k\) Continuum Posted Pricing\(_\infty\). The instance and allocation variables are the same, but every type is MPH-\(k\), equipped with the paper’s MPH query oracle. The question is to compute anonymous prices such that every arrival order obtains at least

\[ \frac{1}{4k}W(x^A)-\varepsilon. \]

Its anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved here, which states that if an MPH valuation instance is given by sample access, together with a black-box welfare algorithm and MPH query oracles, then for every \(\varepsilon>0\) item prices can be computed in time

\[ \operatorname{POLY}(m^k,n,1/\varepsilon) \]

and guarantee expected welfare at least

\[ \frac{1}{4k}\mathbb E_{v\sim F}[\operatorname{SW}(A(v))]-\varepsilon \]

for any arrival order. The continuous version should remain Class A for fixed \(k\), with complexity polynomial in \(m^k,\tau\), and the input length. It preserves complements rather than eliminating them; the \(k\)-dependence is the same meaningful boundary identified by the paper.

This mirror covers the paper’s central XOS and MPH-\(k\) posted-price results, while treating the submodular and gross-substitutes corollaries as restricted valuation classes rather than separate anchors. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s revenue literature, simultaneous Walrasian equilibria, or Appendix C’s linear welfare impossibility.

The weakest point is the supply scaling. With one fixed copy of each item and an unbounded population, the market becomes degenerate; with per-capita supplies, the model is no longer literally the paper’s one-copy auction. That objection is real. The defence is that the scaling is the high-multiplicity analogue of the paper’s own suggested identical-copy markets, and it keeps every individual allocation integral while preserving anonymous prices, sequential demand, welfare maximization, and arbitrary arrival order. A second issue is that the paper proves the finite Bayesian theorem, not this exact continuum limit; finite-population convergence, oracle representation, and rounding deserve separate proofs. Even so, the resulting questions are recognisably the authors’ posted-price problems, and the configuration-LP/separation structure gives a credible continuous computational programme rather than a merely cosmetic replacement of \(n\) by a mass distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror is not, as stated, the paper’s problem. The paper’s \(F=\prod_i F_i\) is a distribution over complete valuation profiles, and \(A\) is run separately on each realized profile. Replacing that by a population distribution \(\mu\) over valuation types does not make \(\mathbb E_{v\sim F}\) “become integration over mass”: \(\mu\) gives type frequencies, not a joint distribution over profiles or an outcome of \(A\).

There is also no canonical limit. If the paper’s single copy of each item is retained while the population grows, each item has vanishing per-capita supply and the normalized welfare problem degenerates. If supply is scaled with population, one has introduced a capacitated multi-unit auction. To obtain it from the paper, every valuation must be lifted from \(2^M\) to bundles of cloned items, and the treatment of clones—perfect substitutes, independent units, or separate markets—is an additional modelling choice. Different choices produce different computational problems.

For Theorem 3.1, the proponent’s \(A_\infty\) quietly changes the computational interface. The paper assumes a black-box algorithm \(A\) on integral valuation profiles. The proposed continuum version assumes an algorithm returning a mass allocation \(x^A\). Once that allocation and the relevant XOS witnesses are supplied, the prices are just an aggregate:

\[ p_j = \frac12 \sum_{\theta,B:j\in B} x^A_{\theta,B}\,a_{\theta,B,j}. \]

That is not the paper’s sampling-based pricing problem; it is a deterministic post-processing step. If \(x^A\) is not supplied, computing it is a new capacitated welfare-optimization problem. Its configuration LP may indeed be solvable by demand-query separation, but that is a new result, not a direct continuous reading of Theorem 3.1.

The same objection applies to Theorem 4.1. With an MPH witness attached to each mass allocation, its price formula aggregates hyperedge contributions,

\[ p_j = \frac12 \sum_{\theta,B}x_{\theta,B} \sum_{\substack{T\ni j\\T\subseteq B}} \frac{w_{\theta,B}(T)}{|T|}. \]

Again, the purported continuum theorem is either immediate bookkeeping given the allocation, or a new capacitated MPH optimization problem. Cloning items can additionally destroy the claimed \(m^k\) representation unless the clone structure is given a special compressed encoding.

These are real weaknesses in the case for the particular formulation: it conflates a type mixture with the paper’s profile distribution, and it has not supplied the finite-to-continuum dictionary needed to show that the posted-price process converges to a well-defined mass process.

But they do not support the universal negative claim. A corrected model with finitely many complete valuation types, rational masses, per-capita supplies, and perfect-substitute item copies is a sensible high-multiplicity combinatorial auction. For rational data it can be expanded into a finite repeated market, and Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 can plausibly be applied to that expansion and then compressed. The paper itself explicitly identifies markets with identical item copies as a natural direction.

So the honest conclusion is that I cannot defeat both anchors. The proposed argument overstates how directly the theorems transfer, but a worthwhile continuous mirror survives—at least for Theorem 3.1, and probably for Theorem 4.1 under a carefully specified clone and MPH representation. The negative case can demand a formal dictionary and reject the claim that the displayed LP alone proves the mirror, but it cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile scenario exists.
