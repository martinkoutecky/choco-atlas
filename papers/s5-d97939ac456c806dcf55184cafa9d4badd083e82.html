The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of weight types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), integer weights \(a_t\), and a rational quota \(\theta\) with \(0<\theta\leq\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t\), let \(D\) be the positive integers \(d\) for which every \(d\mu_t\) and \(d\theta\) is integral. For each \(d\in D\), form the original weighted voting game with \(d\mu_t\) players of type \(t\), weight \(a_t\), and threshold \(d\theta\). Let \(x_t^{(d)}\) be each type’s per-player award in that game’s nucleolus and set \(z_t^{(d)}=d x_t^{(d)}\). Determine whether \(z^{(d)}\) converges as \(d\) grows through \(D\), and, if it does, compute its limit to any requested precision.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete player types given by their weights. For each admissible population scale \(d\), the decision variables are the finite game’s player awards, chosen to lexicographically maximize the sorted coalition excesses; coalition repetitions are retained exactly. The continuous output is the limit of the normalized type awards \(z_t^{(d)}=d x_t^{(d)}\), satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t=1\) when it exists.

The objection that survived

The proposal gives no criterion or effective convergence rate for the normalized nucleoli; without one, an algorithm may not be able to return the limit to requested precision even on convergent instances.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 for weighted voting games. It leaves the general cooperative-game scheme and the extensions to intersections of voting games outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the normalized nucleolus sequence converge for every rational type distribution and quota?
  • Can convergence and its rate be characterized well enough to compute the limit to requested precision?
  • Can the limit be computed efficiently in the support size \(\tau\), input bit length, and requested precision?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is the high-multiplicity limit of the weighted-voting nucleolus. It keeps the paper’s cooperative game and its fairness objective intact at every finite population size, then asks what the per-type allocation approaches as the population grows.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for a weighted voting game with \(n\) players, nonnegative integer weights \(w_i\), and nonnegative integer threshold \(W\), the nucleolus can be computed in time polynomial in \(n\), the numerical values \(w_i\), and \(W\). That is a pseudo-polynomial algorithm: its running time need not be polynomial in the binary input length of the weights and threshold. The introduction also reports that computing the nucleolus is NP-hard, citing Elkind et al. (2009), but the supplied paper gives that claim no numbered result, so Theorem 1 is my only numbered anchor.

Consider a large federation of member organizations in which each organization has a voting weight from a small set of standardized weights. The organizations are the players. A type \(t\) is a weight class, since weight is the complete description relevant to this game. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of organizations in that class. A federation with hundreds of thousands of organizations but only a few weight classes is a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

I would pose the continuous problem as follows. The input is a finite support \(T\) of size \(\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), integer type weights \(a_t\), and a rational quota \(\theta\), with \(0<\theta\leq\sum_t\mu_ta_t\). Let \(D\) be the positive integers \(d\) for which every \(d\mu_t\) and \(d\theta\) is integral. For each \(d\in D\), form the original finite weighted voting game with \(d\mu_t\) players of type \(t\), each of weight \(a_t\), and threshold \(d\theta\). Let \(x_t^{(d)}\) be the per-player allocation to type \(t\) in that game’s nucleolus. It is well defined and equal within each type by symmetry and uniqueness. Define the normalized type allocation by \(z_t^{(d)}=d x_t^{(d)}\).

The named problem is Convergent High-Multiplicity Nucleolus: on inputs for which \(z^{(d)}\) converges as \(d\) grows through \(D\), compute its limit \(z\), to any requested precision. The limit satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t=1\). Each finite game uses the paper’s original objective: choose a nonnegative allocation of the grand coalition’s unit value whose sorted coalition excess vector is lexicographically maximum. The continuous answer is the limiting per-type allocation, not an allocation for a fractionalized version of the finite game.

This is a literal high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 1. It makes the society itself a distribution over complete player types; the quota scales with population so the voting rule has a stable per-capita meaning. I would tentatively expect tractability when \(\tau\) is small: symmetry reduces the output to \(\tau\) values, and coalition compositions become mass vectors. A limiting version of the paper’s successive LP scheme, with pricing over type-level coalition compositions, looks promising. This is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 1: that theorem’s pseudo-polynomial running time in \(d\) does not itself give an efficient algorithm for the limit.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s weighted-voting nucleolus result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s general cooperative-game scheme or its intersection-of-games extensions. Further questions include whether the limit exists for every rational instance, whether its value is independent of the admissible scaling sequence, and whether it can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and the requested precision.

My weakest point is the lexicographic objective. Finite nucleoli count every coalition separately; many coalitions can have the same type composition, with multiplicity \(\prod_t\binom{d\mu_t}{d q_t}\). A limit that treats each mass composition as one coalition could therefore change the answer. The proposed mirror avoids that shortcut by taking limits of the original finite nucleoli, but it leaves open whether the limit exists and whether it has a simpler continuous optimization description. That is a real technical obstacle, while the underlying question remains one the authors could recognize as the high-multiplicity limit of their own problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The serious obstacle is not the choice of scenario. Theorem 1 is a named computational result, and a society with many interchangeable players in a few weight classes is plausible. Since the nucleolus is unique, players of the same weight receive the same allocation. Those facts make this a stronger candidate than a mirror built around individually distinct parties.

The difficulty is that the nucleolus ranks *coalitions*, not just their type-level compositions. For a composition \(k\), the same excess occurs \(\prod_t \binom{d\mu_t}{k_t}\) times in the finite game’s excess vector. Those repetitions can affect later stages of the lexicographic ranking when different compositions attain the same minimum. A continuum of masses \(q_t\) records which compositions are possible, but it does not by itself say how to rank their excesses: there is no canonical counting measure over the continuum of coalitions. Treat each composition once, and the result need not match the finite nucleolus. Use a probability measure over coalitions, and the choice of measure becomes part of the model.

The proponent’s better formulation—take the limit of the original finite nucleoli—preserves those repetitions. But it is a sequence-limit problem, not yet an intrinsic continuum nucleolus. It needs a convergence result and a characterization of the limit; an entropy-based characterization may have to preserve increasingly fine multiplicity effects. The paper’s pseudo-polynomial algorithm does not supply those ingredients. This is a real modeling and mathematical burden, though it does not make the limit question ill-posed.

I cannot honestly turn that burden into a decisive objection. The exact-replica limit is a well-defined question on instances where it converges, and proving a discrete-to-continuous identity is explicitly within the programme’s remit. So the negative case is weak at its strongest point: coalition multiplicity makes the mirror technically nontrivial and its continuum formulation noncanonical, but it does not establish that the high-multiplicity nucleolus is not worth asking about.
