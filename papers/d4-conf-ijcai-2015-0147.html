The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is weak and prospective. Under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named complexity result: it gives no theorem that classifies a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. Its closest hooks are Propositions 5 and 6, both proved here. Proposition 5 says every possibilistic discrimin-optimal outcome has no counteroffer that an agent can certify from their knowledge; Proposition 6 says every other outcome has at least one such counteroffer. Together with the paper’s finiteness argument, they characterize terminal agreements, but give no complexity bound for finding one. I would not present them as hardness or algorithmic-complexity anchors.

A plausible *future extension*, rather than a mirror anchored in those results, is negotiation in a large membership organization. A type would specify a member’s Boolean action template, prioritized goals, and GPL knowledge about other types; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of members of type \(t\). Let \(x_{t,a}\) be the mass of type \(t\) choosing local action \(a\), with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). Feasibility constraints and goal formulas would be evaluated on the aggregate \(x\). The question would be: given rational \(\mu\), the type descriptions, and those constraints, find a feasible aggregate agreement \(x\) for which no type has a GPL-certifiable counteroffer under the paper’s reciprocity rule. The solution is such an \(x\); the goal is an agreement, not a welfare optimum. A recurring organization with many members but relatively few preference, role, and knowledge types gives this a credible high-multiplicity setting.

The weak point is substantial: this extension changes how Boolean actions and goals are represented, and the paper’s results do not establish its complexity. The continuous mirror is therefore a reasonable research direction, but there is no paper-compliant anchor from which to claim that discrete hardness dissolves, transfers, or becomes continuum-specific.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no paper compliant anchor here: the paper states no complexity result. Propositions 5 and 6 characterize which outcomes can end the protocol under incomplete information; the finite convergence argument says the protocol terminates. None gives a running time, a hardness classification, or an algorithmic bound. Turning “find a terminal agreement” into a search problem could be worthwhile, but it would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a result this paper establishes.

The proposed large membership organization is the strongest repair, but its aggregate \(x_{t,a}\) does not generally retain enough information to evaluate this paper’s game. The integrity constraint \(\delta\) can couple named agents’ Boolean variables, each agent’s utility depends on the full outcome, and the knowledge bases \(K_i^j\) are indexed by particular agents. Aggregate action counts can lose the correlations and pairwise knowledge those definitions use. Types that record all relevant roles and relationships may grow with the population, defeating the small support that makes the high multiplicity formulation useful.

Keeping support small would require an anonymous variant: aggregate constraints and goals, type level knowledge, and a rule for evaluating GPL and counteroffers on mass allocations. That is a plausible model, but it changes the language and semantics. It also changes the protocol’s individual turn order—and its first proposer advantage—if turns instead occur by type. The authors might recognize that variant as a worthwhile extension, but it is not a population lift of the negotiation model they analyze.

So the negative case is strongest as a verdict on this paper: it offers no complexity result to continuize, and its best proposed computational extension needs substantial new modeling choices before a continuous question is defined. The broader claim that *no* such anonymous Boolean game could ever be worth studying is weaker; the paper alone cannot establish it.
