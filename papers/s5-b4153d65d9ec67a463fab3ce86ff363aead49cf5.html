The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the paper’s discussion of intertemporal majority voting over a common resource. Imagine a large community managing a fishery or carbon budget. Its members share the same felicity function, technology, and other relevant characteristics, but belong to a modest number of discount-factor types. Type \(\beta\) has mass \(\mu_\beta\), the fraction of the electorate in that type. The collective decision is a sequence of consumption or extraction rates \(e_s\), with resource dynamics \(c_s=e_s f(k_s)\) and \(k_{s+1}=(1-e_s)f(k_s)\).

The nearest result is in §4.2, where the paper reviews Borissov, Pakhnin, and Puppe (2017). It reports that, when agents have identical felicity functions and differ only in discount factors, intertemporal majority voting chooses consumption rates period by period, with perfect foresight about future votes. The instantaneous Condorcet winner is the median-discount-factor agent’s preferred rate, and the resulting path coincides with that agent’s optimal consumption path. This is cited from elsewhere, not proved in this survey.

A plausible follow-on computational problem would be: given a binary-rational distribution \((\mu_\beta)\) over finitely many discount-factor types, an initial resource stock, a specified common technology and utility function, and a requested horizon \(H\) and precision \(\varepsilon\), compute the first \(H\) rates of the perfect-foresight intertemporal-majority path to error at most \(\varepsilon\). A solution is that rate prefix, with a fixed tie-breaking rule if a vote has multiple winners. Its natural regime is \(n\) very large relative to the number \(\tau\) of shared types. The median-type characterization suggests tractability when the median agent’s dynamic optimization problem is efficiently solvable; the complexity of broader technology and utility classes would remain open.

That is a credible population-continuum research direction, but it is not a compliant anchor for this task. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational-complexity result. The median-voter finding is an unnumbered, cited economic result, and the survey reports no algorithm, running-time bound, or hardness classification. The no-Condorcet-winner discussion in §4.1 is another nearby analytical result, not a computational one.

So my positive case stops at a plausible mirror candidate: the population model fits the paper’s many-agent setting, and the authors could recognize the voting question. The weakest point is decisive under your rubric: there is no eligible named computational result to mirror. The candidate above should be treated as a follow-on problem, not as an anchored continuous mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this survey names no computational result to mirror. Its central claims concern axiomatic properties of social welfare functions, existence or nonexistence of voting outcomes, and economic implications. Section 4.2’s median-discount-factor result is a review of Borissov, Pakhnin, and Puppe (2017), not a theorem in this paper about an algorithm or a complexity class. The paper gives no computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, running-time bound, or hardness result.

The proponent’s proposed society—many people grouped by discount-factor type—is sensible, and computing a finite prefix of its intertemporal majority path could be a worthwhile research project. A stronger version could ask for the path across a broad class of technologies and utility functions. But that would formulate a new computational problem around an economic model; it would not mirror a computational result of this paper. The same applies to computational variants of its planner and Condorcet-winner discussions.

So the case against is strong under the requested anchor rule: the one proposed anchor is explicitly a follow-on, and no eligible anchor remains. The broader claim that no version of this research could ever be worthwhile is harder to defend. The population model is plausible; what this paper lacks is a computational result that makes it the source of a continuous mirror.
