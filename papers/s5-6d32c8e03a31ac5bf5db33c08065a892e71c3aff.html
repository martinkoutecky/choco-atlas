The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: this paper supports a credible high-multiplicity extension, but it does not contain a conventional computational-complexity anchor. None of its numbered results proves membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, approximation, or parameterized tractability. The closest candidate is Theorem 2, which gives an explicit optimization characterization and an implementable rule.

Theorem 2 in Section 5.2 is proved in this paper. For a nonatomic manna \((\Omega,\mathcal B)\), \(n\) agents, and a continuous monotone utility \(u\), it considers either a moving-knife rule \(MK_n^\lambda\) or a price-based Bid-and-Choose rule \(B\&C_n^\theta\). If \(\tau^\lambda\) and \(\tau^\theta\) solve the optimization problem in equation (7), then:

  • under \(MK_n^\lambda\), an agent can guarantee \(\Gamma^\lambda(u;n)\) by stopping at the prescribed knife positions \(t_k^\lambda\) when \(k-1\) agents have already been served;
  • under \(B\&C_n^\theta\), an agent can guarantee \(\Gamma^\theta(u;n)\) by stopping at \(t_k^\theta\) and choosing the best available share of \(\theta\)-size \(t_k^\theta-t_{k-1}^\theta\);
  • in either case, the guarantee lies between the paper’s two benchmarks: \(\operatorname{minMax}(u;n)\le \Gamma^\alpha(u;n)\le \operatorname{Maxmin}(u;n)\).

The relevant computation is \(\Gamma^\alpha(u;n)=\max_{\tau\in\Upsilon(n)}\min_k u^\alpha(t_k,t_{k+1})\), where \(\Upsilon(n)\) is the set of increasing sequences \(0=t_0\le\cdots\le t_n=1\). Lemma 4, also proved in the paper, establishes continuity, existence of an optimum, and an optimal sequence in which all consecutive-share utilities are equal.

My lead mirror would be:

Continuum Bid-and-Choose Guarantee, \(\mathrm{CB\&C}_\infty\). An instance consists of \(K\) divisible commodities with per-capita supply \(\omega\in\mathbb Q_+^K\), a positive price vector \(p\) with \(p\cdot\omega=1\), finitely many utility types \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and continuous coordinatewise-monotone utilities \(u_t:[0,\omega]\to\mathbb R\). The interpretation is a large population of \(N\) agents, with approximately \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), and total supply \(N\omega\).

A type-level allocation is a probability kernel \(\pi_t\) over ordinary bundles \(z\in[0,\omega]\). Thus every individual receives a genuine bundle, while \(\pi_t\) records how bundles are distributed among members of type \(t\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_t\mu_t\int z\,d\pi_t(z)\le\omega\).

A \(B\& C\)-policy supplies each type with a bid \(b_t\), interpreted as the per-capita \(p\)-size of an acceptable share, together with a rule for choosing a best available bundle of that size. The mass of type \(t\) consumes aggregate price \(\mu_t b_t\). A type guarantee \(g_t\) is the essential infimum of \(u_t\) over the bundles received by type \(t\), under every admissible ordering, tie-breaking, and behavior of the other type blocks. The decision question is:

Given thresholds \(q_t\), does there exist a feasible type-level \(B\&C\)-policy with \(g_t\ge q_t\) for every \(t\)?

The optimization version returns a Pareto-maximal guarantee vector \((g_t)_{t\in T}\), or, for a chosen welfare weighting, maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t g_t\).

This is recognizably the same problem as Theorem 2: heterogeneous agents divide a nonatomic manna, bids are based on a common size measure, agents choose best available shares, and the object being computed is a guaranteed utility. The population is now the continuous object. The per-capita scaling is essential: without replacing \(\omega\) by \(N\omega\), every individual’s bundle would vanish as \(N\) grows.

The natural regime is a large population of households, employees, students, or service recipients sharing a small number of recurring preference types over divisible resources. For example, a university could allocate per-capita teaching, housing, or service capacity among many students whose preferences fall into a finite family of monotone utility types. The type is a complete utility description, not merely a demographic label. Clearing denominators in \(\mu\) recovers a finite clone population, while finite repeated-type allocations induce the kernels \(\pi_t\).

I would expect the finite-piecewise-linear version to be a Class A candidate. The mass constraints are linear, and the Bid-and-Choose structure reduces the strategic part to price thresholds and best-bundle subproblems, suggesting a configuration LP or column-generation approach. The crucial open question is whether those best-bundle subproblems admit efficient separation for the chosen utility representation. For unrestricted continuous monotone utilities, the problem is representation-dependent and could instead become continuum-specific hard; the paper itself does not settle this.

I would not use Theorem 1 as a second anchor. It is proved here and says that the \(D\&C_n\) rule guarantees \(\operatorname{minMax}\) to every agent, while the first Divider can obtain \(\operatorname{Maxmin}\), but it is another implementation theorem rather than a computational result. Moreover, its named Divider and finite sequential matching step make its atomless-population extension substantially less canonical.

The weakest point is therefore serious: Theorem 2 guarantees an individual agent in a finite population, whereas an atomless individual has measure zero and cannot affect the allocation. My mirror repairs this by treating positive-mass types as the strategic units and requiring an essential guarantee for almost every member of a type. That is a meaningful high-multiplicity co-modification, but it changes the quantifier from individual deviation to type-block behavior. The original authors might accept it as a natural extension, but not as an immediate limit theorem.

Thus the positive case is substantively plausible and mathematically concrete, with an expected Class A boundary around the pricing problem. Procedurally, however, it is weaker than the programme’s ideal examples: if the named-computational-anchor requirement is enforced literally, this paper has no qualifying anchor, and \(\mathrm{CB\&C}_\infty\) should be described as a well-motivated new computational extension rather than a direct mirror of an established complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with a threshold problem: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 2 is an implementation and existence theorem, not a complexity theorem. Equation (7) characterizes a guarantee over arbitrary continuous utility functions, with no finite encoding, input size, decision version, or algorithmic claim. Lemma 4 proves continuity and equalization of an optimum; it does not expose a computational problem. Thus a proposed \(\mathrm{CB\&C}_\infty\) would be a new problem inspired by the paper, not the continuous counterpart of one of its computational results.

More fundamentally, Theorem 2 has no population dependence of the kind ChoCo needs. Its guarantee for agent \(i\) is determined by that agent’s utility \(u_i\), the manna, the benchmark measure, and \(n\). The other agents’ utilities and their frequencies never enter. This is deliberate: the theorem protects one named agent against every possible behaviour and preference profile of the others. If the population is replaced by a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many utility types, preserving the theorem’s meaning gives only

\[ g_t=\Gamma^\theta(u_t;n) \]

for each type \(t\). The masses \(\mu_t\) merely say how many times the same individual guarantee is evaluated. They do not create mass transfers, type-level feasibility constraints, or a pricing problem. Changing \(\mu\) while keeping the type support and \(n\) fixed changes nothing in the theorem’s object.

The proposed Bid-and-Choose mirror imports precisely the structure that the paper does not have. In the paper, a bid is a stopping time determining an agent’s position in a sequential procedure; it is not a per-capita resource demand \(b_t\). The resource received at a later step is the difference between stopping times, and the agent then chooses a best available share of that size. Consequently, the constraint

\[ \sum_t \mu_t b_t\le 1 \]

is not a restatement of the paper’s feasibility condition. It defines a new market-clearing or block-allocation model.

The best possible repair is to take \(N\) clones of each type, scale the manna to \(N\omega\), and pass to a limit in which each clone still receives a nonvanishing per-capita bundle. But this does not solve the mismatch. If clones retain the paper’s individual semantics, each one has its own stopping decision and the theorem applies independently of the type distribution. The limit is just repeated individual protection, not a continuous society problem. If, instead, all members of a type are made into one strategic block, the object changes: the block must coordinate stopping times, tie-breaking, and bundle choices, and must guarantee utility to almost every member. That is a coalition or type-block guarantee, not the individual guarantee proved in Theorem 2.

The proposed kernel \(\pi_t\) does not remove this problem. Requiring an essential-infimum guarantee makes the block problem substantially stronger than the paper’s statement; requiring only an average over \(\pi_t\) abandons the paper’s worst-case individual guarantee. Allowing each member to choose separately returns us to the original finite-agent semantics, where \(\mu\) is computationally inert. These are not three formulations of one mirror, but three different problems.

There is also no canonical atomless version of the sequential rule supplied by the paper. A continuum of agents requires an additional measurable service schedule, a convention for ties among equal-type agents, and a definition of what it means for an infinitesimal agent to stop the clock and receive a positive bundle. Scaling the manna and introducing such a schedule may produce an interesting continuum-time allocation model, but its substantive content comes from those added choices rather than from continuizing Theorem 2.

This does not show that no one could study that new model. Finite piecewise-linear utilities, per-capita supplies, and type-level scheduling might support a worthwhile fair-division project. The honest negative conclusion is narrower but decisive for this programme: the paper offers no computational result whose population high-multiplicity version is being exposed. Its sole plausible anchor is an individual worst-case guarantee whose quantifiers erase the population distribution. Once the distribution is made consequential, the proposed object is no longer the paper’s problem.
