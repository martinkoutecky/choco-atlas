The continuous mirror question

Given \(r\) referenda, a target \(q\in\{0,1\}^r\), and a finite support of ballot-and-price types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), what is the infimum cost of influencing mass from each type so that \(q\) has a strict majority on every referendum, and is that infimum attained? The instance should also admit a feasible transfer within any requested rational \(\epsilon>0\) of the infimum.

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) has ballot \(v_t\in\{0,1\}^r\), price \(p_t\ge 0\), and mass \(\mu_t\). Choose \(y_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) to transfer to ballot \(q\), minimizing \(\sum_t p_t y_t\), subject to \(b_j+\sum_{t:v_{t,j}\ne q_j}y_t>1/2\) for every \(j\), where \(b_j=\sum_{t:v_{t,j}=q_j}\mu_t\). Replacing strict inequalities by weak ones gives an LP for the infimum; the strict-feasibility and attainment questions can then be checked in time polynomial in \(r\), τ, and the rational input bit length.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is in Section 2: the underlying Optimal-Weighted-Lobbying problem. It already asks about referendum outcomes and the cost of changing voters’ ballots, so the continuous version can preserve the problem’s objective and winning conditions.

My anchor is Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper. For \(m\) voters and \(n\) referenda, let \(d_j\) be the number of additional Yes votes needed for a strict majority on referendum \(j\), and let \(D_0=\sum_{j=1}^n d_j\). The theorem says that the paper’s greedy algorithm for Optimal-Weighted-Lobbying—repeatedly choosing the voter with the lowest price per newly corrected ballot entry—has approximation ratio at most
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{D_0}\frac1i \le 1+\ln D_0 \le 1+\ln\!\left(n\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\right\rceil\right). \]
The paper says this also gives the same ratio for the unit-price Optimal-Lobbying problem. This is an algorithmic anchor, rather than a numbered hardness result.

I would call the mirror Fractional Weighted Lobbying. An instance has \(r\) referenda, a target vector \(q\in\{0,1\}^r\), and a finite support \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^r\). Each type \(t\in T\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational price \(p_t\ge 0\) for influencing one unit of that type. A type is the complete ballot vector together with its price. The lobby may influence an amount \(y_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type and change those ballots to \(q\), paying \(p_t y_t\). The problem is to find the infimum cost of making the target win a strict majority on every referendum; a solution reports that infimum, whether it is attained, and, for any rational \(\epsilon>0\), a feasible transfer within \(\epsilon\) of it.

This keeps the paper’s model intact: paying to influence a voter costs the same regardless of the replacement ballot, so converting influenced voters directly to the full target vector is without loss. Put \(b_j=\sum_{t:t_j=q_j}\mu_t\), the mass already matching the target on referendum \(j\). The mirror’s feasible transfers satisfy
\[ b_j+\sum_{t:t_j\ne q_j} y_t>\frac12 \qquad\text{for every }j, \]
and the cost is \(\sum_{t\ne q}p_t y_t\). Replacing \(>\frac12\) by \(\ge\frac12\) gives a linear program whose optimum is the infimum: converting all mass to \(q\) yields strict majorities, and mixing any boundary solution with that full conversion makes it strictly feasible at costs arbitrarily close to the LP optimum. LP methods also let a prover check whether the infimum itself is attained. The expected outcome is Class A: tractable, in time polynomial in \(r\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\).

A plausible regime is a large union or membership organization voting on a package of proposals. Many members may share the same Yes/No ballot pattern and the same standardized outreach price. If there are \(N\) members but only \(\tau\ll N\) ballot-and-price types, the society is naturally described by their mass fractions. Influencing, for example, a fraction of one segment still means that every influenced member receives an ordinary \(0\)-\(1\) ballot; only the amount of mass selected is divisible. The authors should recognize this as their lobbying problem with population granularity relaxed. For a finite society with \(N_t\) members of type \(t\), the discrete instance has masses \(\mu_t=N_t/N\), while its feasible changes are restricted to multiples of \(1/N\).

The paper states in prose that Christian et al. [CFRS06] proved unweighted Optimal-Lobbying W[2]-complete, and that the weighted version inherits W[2]-hardness in its budget-parameterized form. That is useful motivation, but it is cited background, not a numbered hardness anchor proved here. The continuous LP therefore raises a good follow-up question—how much of that difficulty is voter indivisibility, and what rounding guarantee relates the LP to finite high-multiplicity instances? It would be misleading to claim that Theorem 2.2 itself establishes a hardness result that dissolves in the continuum.

This mirror covers Theorem 2.2 and its underlying lobbying optimization problem. It does not cover the paper’s average-case, junta, or Dodgson frequency-of-correctness results: those analyze distributions over encoded instances or election profiles, rather than making the population itself continuous.

The weakest point is that the mirror relaxes the discrete choice to fractional mass, while Theorem 2.2 analyzes a greedy approximation for indivisible voters. That relaxation may be exactly where the tractability comes from. I think the mirror remains credible because it preserves the target, the strict-majority conditions, the per-voter price, and the option to replace an influenced ballot with the target ballot; it changes only whether a large, repeated type can be influenced at one-person granularity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 2.2’s harmonic guarantee concerns choosing indivisible voter rows: one selected voter incurs one price while changing that row can repair several referendum deficits. The proposed mirror replaces those choices with \(\tau\) divisible variables and \(r\) linear majority constraints. It is a standard fractional multicover LP, so its polynomial solvability follows directly from convexifying the voter choices; the paper’s greedy analysis contributes no additional machinery to that result. The theorem’s discrete approximation guarantee also says nothing about the LP’s integrality gap or how to round its solution back to a finite election. That is the best case against the mirror: its immediate result may be too routine to justify much research time. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0703097)

That objection does not defeat the strongest version of the mirror. A large referendum electorate with repeated ballot-and-price types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Treating price as part of a complete type preserves the relevant distinctions, and per-capita cost and strict-majority conditions have natural mass interpretations. The resulting LP remains meaningful even if its answer is immediate. Studying its gap or rounding behavior could also connect it back to finite high-multiplicity elections.

So I cannot honestly support the universal negative. The anchor survives the programme’s test: there is a named computational result, and a sensible continuous-population question follows from it. The negative case rests on a low-priority judgment—“the LP is too obvious”—rather than a fundamental flaw in the mirror.
