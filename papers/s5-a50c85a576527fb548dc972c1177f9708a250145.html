The continuous mirror question

Given a rational bounded-degree planar role graph \(H\) with \(\tau\) roles, at most four goods, and the normalized linear-influence parameters and endowments \(w_t\) of each role, form a continuum of copies of \(H\), with mass \(1/\tau\) on each role. Each role has per-agent endowment \(\tau w_t\); in its utility, replace each neighbor-allocation coefficient \(a_{t,i,s,j}\) by \(a_{t,i,s,j}/\tau\). Find a normalized common price vector \(p\) and one bundle \(y_t\) per role such that each role’s budget violation and optimality regret are at most \(\epsilon=\tau^{-16}\), and mass-weighted market-clearing error is at most \(\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

Use a continuum of copies of the finite role template: agents in role \(t\) have mass \(1/\tau\), interact with the corresponding roles in their own copy, and receive the same bundle \(y_t\). The decision is a common price vector and role bundles satisfying approximate individual optimality and mass-weighted clearing; there is no welfare objective. Scaling endowments by \(\tau\) and influence coefficients by \(1/\tau\) makes a solution yield an approximate solution to the paper’s finite instance.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the PPAD-hard linear-influence result of Theorem 3 and admits the analogous threshold-influence mirror of Theorem 4; it leaves Theorem 5’s hierarchical-network algorithm and the equilibrium-existence result aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does PPAD-hardness persist when the role support is bounded independently of the source instance, or is growing template size essential?
  • Does the threshold-influence mirror remain hard with a fixed number of goods?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a market made from a continuum of repeated social communities. Let a finite template \(H\) have \(\tau\) trader roles, with the paper’s local influence edges, endowments, and utility parameters. Take the society to be \([0,1]\times T\), where \(T\) is the set of roles and role \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t=1/\tau\). Each point \(u\in[0,1]\) represents a copy of the same community. Thus, in a finite approximation with \(N\) people, each role has about \(N/\tau\) indistinguishable members. A type includes its role in the template, so it captures the member’s full endowment, preferences, and local neighborhood.

The common market has \(h\) goods and a common price vector \(p\). In the symmetric type-level question, every member of role \(t\) receives bundle \(x_t\). The population consumes mass \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t\), and its endowment is \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t\). A solution is a price vector and role bundles that approximately clear the market, keep each role’s bundle within budget, and make it approximately optimal given its neighbors’ role bundles. There is no welfare objective: this is an equilibrium-search problem. For example, the linear-influence utilities take the form
\[ u_t(x_t;x_{N(t)})= \sum_i\left(c_{t,i}+ \sum_{s\in N(t)}\sum_j a_{t,i,s,j}x_{s,j}\right)x_{t,i}, \]
with the paper’s nonnegative normalized parameters. The threshold version uses
\[ u_t(x_t;x_{N(t)})= \sum_i\left(c_{t,i}x_{t,i}+ \min\left\{0,\sum_{s\in N(t)}\sum_j a_{t,i,s,j}x_{s,j} -d_{t,i}x_{t,i}\right\}\right). \]
For both questions, impose the type-level versions of Theorem 1’s conditions that guarantee existence. Require a planar bounded-degree template in the linear case, and a planar constant-degree template in the threshold case.

This is plausible for large markets made of many copies of a local social-market structure—for example, repeated communities trading through a common market. It preserves the paper’s goods, budgets, individual optimization, and local social influence. Crucially, the type mass is the fraction of the whole population in that role; it is not a count of named traders.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3 (Main), proved in this paper. It states that Planar-Linear-Market is PPAD-hard: finding an \(m^{-16}\)-approximate equilibrium is PPAD-hard even when the input satisfies Theorem 1’s existence conditions, has at most four goods, uses normalized linear-influence utilities, and has a bounded-degree planar influence graph. The proof reduces from Sparse-Nash, whose PPAD-completeness is cited as Theorem 2 from [4].

The corresponding question is Continuous Template Linear-Market Equilibrium. Its input is a rational, finite role template with \(\tau\) roles, uniform masses \(1/\tau\), at most four goods, per-role endowments and normalized linear-influence parameters, and a bounded-degree planar influence graph. Its output is a common normalized price vector \(p\) and one bundle \(x_t\) per role that form a \(\tau^{-16}\)-approximate type-symmetric market equilibrium. The approximation conditions are per-role budget feasibility and optimality regret at most \(\epsilon\), and mass-weighted market-clearing error at most \(\epsilon\), with \(\epsilon=\tau^{-16}\).

The discrete market in Theorem 3 embeds in this question. Given a paper instance with \(m\) traders, give each trader one role of mass \(1/m\). Scale its per-role endowment and bundle by \(m\), and divide each neighbor-influence coefficient by \(m\). The aggregate resources and clearing equations then match the source instance, while each role’s utility is scaled by \(m\), preserving its preferences. Repeating the template across the continuum supplies arbitrarily many identical copies of every role. Thus I expect hardness to transfer, in Class B: the reduction’s combinatorics are in the goods, game choices, and role network, and replication does not remove them. The paper’s reduction proves the anchor; adapting this scaling argument to the stated type-symmetric search problem is the additional step a prover should formalize.

A second, separate anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here. It states that Planar-Threshold-Market is PPAD-hard for finding an \(m^{-11}\)-approximate equilibrium with normalized threshold-influence utilities and a planar constant-degree influence graph, with no fixed bound on the number of goods. Its proof adapts the cited market reduction in [3].

The associated question is Continuous Template Threshold-Market Equilibrium. Use the same role-template population and approximate-equilibrium conditions, with threshold-influence utilities and any number of goods; set \(\epsilon=\tau^{-11}\). Applying the same mass and utility scaling embeds the paper’s finite role market in the continuous population. I likewise expect hardness to transfer. Further questions include whether that hardness survives with a fixed number of goods, and whether either problem becomes tractable when the role template has extra structure such as the hierarchical structure of the paper’s Theorem 5.

The authors would, I think, recognize these as their equilibrium problems in a repeated-community, high-multiplicity regime. The weakest point is that the construction repeats an entire local network template. In the hardness reductions, the number of roles \(\tau\) still grows with the source instance; the continuum gives many copies per role, but does not compress the reduction into small support. And real social networks need not consist of exact repeated templates. So this is a defensible mirror for a specific large-market regime, not a claim that the paper’s results automatically characterize arbitrary continuum networks.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about what has actually been continuized. Each trader’s utility depends on allocations of particular neighbors. A distribution over role types alone does not specify those links across copies. Preserving the paper’s model requires adding a finite template graph and a way to match each role to its neighbors in the same copy, then asking for role-symmetric allocations. Replacing those links with averages over neighbor types would give a different, mean-field market.

That narrows the proposed mirror, but it does not defeat it. Repeated communities with a common market are plausible, and the template records each role’s full structural position. The type support grows with the paper’s network size, so this does not compress the reduction to a small number of types. But the programme allows \(\tau\) to grow; that limitation alone does not make the question unworthy.

For Theorem 3, the source traders—including the planarization gadgets—can be roles of mass \(1/m\). Set each role’s endowment and bundle to \(m\) times its source values, and divide its linear neighbor-influence coefficients by \(m\). The role utility is then \(m\) times the source utility, and the mass-weighted clearing equations match. Thus the type-symmetric continuous question preserves the named PPAD-hard equilibrium problem. Its reliance on a growing finite template is a scope reservation, not a decisive objection.

Theorem 4 survives the same check. With bundles and endowments scaled by \(m\), retaining the threshold parameters and neighbor coefficients makes each role’s utility \(m\) times the source utility. The role graph carries the source’s planar, constant-degree structure, while the number of goods remains unrestricted. Again, the embedding is into a repeated-template population, but that is a defensible high-multiplicity regime.

Neither anchor lacks a named computational result, and the paper’s analytic equilibrium-existence literature does not already settle their computational questions. Nor does the need to adapt the population model defeat them: here, the repeated-community structure is the natural way to keep local social influence meaningful as the population grows.

So the honest negative case is weak. Its best point is that these are repeated-template, role-symmetric market questions, rather than a general continuum model of arbitrary social networks. I cannot use that to rule out either anchor under the programme’s stated scope.
