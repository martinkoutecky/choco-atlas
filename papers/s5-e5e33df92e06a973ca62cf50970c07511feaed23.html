The case FOR (proponent)

The strict answer is that this paper has no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires: its numbered results give economic characterizations, but none establishes a problem’s complexity or computes with an algorithmic formulation. The nearest anchor for a positive case is Theorem 3 (No-learning frontier), proved in this paper. It gives an exact threshold characterization, but it is not itself a computational-complexity result.

Theorem 3 says: for each trader \(i\), public event \(E\), and inherited announcement \(v\), let \(C_i(s;E)=E\cap\Pi_i(s)\) be the information cell after state \(s\), and let \(\bar\eta_i(E,v)\) be the maximum, over \(s\in E\), of the minimum entropy capacity needed to make \(v\) a conditional expected payoff on that cell. Then \(v\) is rationalizable after every trader’s possible private signal exactly when \(\eta_i\geq\bar\eta_i(E,v)\) for every trader. If these inequalities hold and the true state \(s^\star\in E\) has \(Z(s^\star)\neq v\), every trader uniquely repeats \(v\) whenever called in the myopic market-scoring-rule game; no further information is revealed, and information does not aggregate at \(s^\star\). The threshold is computed from the paper’s \(\eta_i^\star(E',v)=-\log(1-a_i+a_i e^{-I_i(E',v)})\), where \(a_i=p_i(E')\) and \(I_i(E',v)\) is the minimum \(\mathrm{KL}(r\Vert p_i^{E'})\) over conditional beliefs with \(\mathbb E_r[Z]=v\); it is \(+\infty\) when \(v\) lies outside the relevant payoff range.

My strongest proposed mirror is the Continuum No-learning Frontier. A type is a complete trader description \(t=(\Pi_t,p_t,\eta_t)\): the trader’s information partition, full-support reference belief, and entropy capacity. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support of these types, with each positive binary-rational mass representing a fraction of the market. This fits a large recurring prediction market with many participants but relatively few information roles—for example, desks sharing the same data access, forecasting model, and information-processing capacity. Other relevant features, such as the scoring rule, are common across types.

Given the finite state space, payoff \(Z\), public event \(E\), target announcement \(v\), and the type distribution \(\mu\), the problem is to compute each supported type’s no-learning threshold and decide whether \(v\) is an absorbing wrong announcement for the society. A yes solution supplies, for each type and each possible private cell, a conditional belief \(r\) whose expected payoff is \(v\) and whose entropy cost fits the capacity; the thresholds give the corresponding frontier. The masses also report what fraction of the trader population can rationalize \(v\). The robust no-learning condition requires every positive-mass type to meet its threshold.

I would expect an approximate version of this problem to be tractable: it separates into conditional KL projections, each with a one-dimensional dual representation in the paper. A prover could investigate running time in the number of states, support size \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and requested precision. That expectation is a new algorithmic question; Theorem 3 does not prove polynomial time or analyze bit complexity. The mirror covers the paper’s prediction-market no-learning result, rather than its aggregation axioms or other applications.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 3 concerns individually called traders, and the no-learning condition depends on every positive-mass type meeting its threshold. Population masses mainly identify the support and the share that can sustain \(v\); they do not otherwise change the frontier. A referee could reasonably ask whether that is a consequential high-multiplicity computational problem. So there is a plausible population mirror here, but under the requested anchor standard the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest negative feature is that it has no named computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. Theorem 3, the proponent’s anchor, is the closest thing: it characterizes when an inherited price is self-confirming by comparing each trader’s capacity with a threshold. But it gives no computational problem, algorithm, or complexity bound. Its minimization is over beliefs on the finite state space; that is continuity in the probability model, not in the population. Turning the threshold formula into an algorithmic task would be a new question added to the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result it establishes.

The best version of the proposed mirror would give each trader type \(t=(\Pi_t,p_t,\eta_t)\) a mass under a society distribution \(\mu\), then study the distribution of critical capacities \(h_t(E,v)\). For a finite-support society, Theorem 3’s absorbing-price condition becomes: every positive-mass type satisfies \(\eta_t\ge h_t(E,v)\). The masses still do not enter the condition except through whether a type is present. One could compute the share \(\mu\{t:\eta_t\ge h_t(E,v)\}\), but that is a new descriptive statistic; Theorem 3 does not say what happens dynamically when the other traders cannot repeat the price. Deriving a population-level dynamic result would require additional assumptions about who trades when and how departures from the inherited price affect subsequent announcements. The paper supplies no such model.

A recurring market with many desks sharing information access, beliefs, and capacity is plausible, so grouping identical traders is not itself a distortion. The problem is that this population story does not give the paper’s result a population-level computational question: its frontier remains a type-by-type condition. Other possible anchors do not supply one either. Theorem 2 is an axiomatic characterization of utility aggregation and belief-set inclusion. A continuum version would still be an axiomatic aggregation question, outside the programme’s scope. The mechanism-design application could be given a distribution over private types, but that would introduce a designer’s objective over type frequencies; the paper’s implementation constraints are stated type by type, and it provides no computational result about optimizing that objective.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about this paper’s fit for ChoCo: its contributions concern information technology, belief aggregation, and economic consequences, rather than computational social choice over a population. It is not airtight against every possible future model. The recurring-market scenario is credible, but making it a worthwhile mirror would require supplying the missing population dynamics and computational question, rather than continuizing a result the paper already asks.
