Why no mirror

Theorem 5 guarantees Pareto efficiency but does not state that an allocation can be computed efficiently or that computing one is hard. A fluid cohort version of LTRF is plausible, but it cannot supply the required named computational result from this paper.

What the mirror covers

A fluid cohort scheduler would cover LTRF's allocation policy and its stated fairness properties; it would leave the adaptive quantum policy, implementation details, and experimental comparisons unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible continuous-population scenario here. In a large pay-as-you-use cloud, many tenants may share a relatively small set of contracts and recurring workload profiles. A type would include the full profile the scheduler uses: payment weight, demand over the horizon, task characteristics, and LTRF settings. Mass \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of tenants of type \(t\). The decision would be each type’s per-tenant resource allocation over time, with aggregate capacity scaled per unit of population. The scheduler would track the same lending and accumulated-resource accounts as LTRF. That keeps the population continuous while leaving the time horizon as part of the original problem.

A possible prover question is: given a finite support of types with rational masses, weights, and piecewise-constant demand profiles, compute a feasible resource-allocation schedule that follows the fluid version of LTRF’s weighted accumulated-resource rule and maximizes useful allocation subject to capacity and demand. I would initially expect this finite-profile version to be tractable, but that is a conjecture to investigate, not a result of this paper. The mirror would need to specify how its fluid service relates to the paper’s discrete tasks, execution-time estimates, arrivals, and lending accounts.

The anchor requirement is the obstacle. The paper has no named result classifying the complexity of a computational problem—no theorem that establishes hardness, polynomial-time solvability, or a parameterized result. Its Theorems 1–5 prove properties of LTRF, rather than complexity results. The closest candidate is Theorem 5, proved here: it claims LTRF is Pareto efficient, meaning no client can receive more resources without reducing another client’s resources; the proof argues that the discount mechanism encourages preemption of unused resources, so the system is fully utilized when tasks are pending. That is a meaningful guarantee about the scheduler, but it is not a computational-complexity anchor under the stated criterion.

So the positive case is that a high-multiplicity fluid scheduler is a plausible mirror of the paper’s central problem. The strongest weakness is that the paper does not establish the kind of named computational result this exercise requires. There is also a modeling risk: if a type must encode each tenant’s distinctive workload and arrival history, the support may grow with the tenant count and erase the high-multiplicity advantage.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the one the proponent already identifies: this paper has no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorems 1–5 establish properties of LTRF. Theorem 5, their chosen anchor, says the scheduler is Pareto efficient; it gives no complexity claim about computing an allocation. The paper’s algorithmic contribution is LTYARN, described and evaluated as a scheduler. A continuous version of Theorem 5 would ask whether a fluid allocation has that property—a question about an allocation guarantee, not the computational landscape the programme targets.

The proponent’s population scenario is plausible. A large cloud could have many tenants with the same contract and recurring workload profile; a type could include the complete profile, and capacity could scale per capita. I would not reject the mirror on the ground that tenants are inherently unique.

But their proposed prover question does not land on Theorem 5. If it follows LTRF’s weighted accumulated-resource rule, it asks how to execute the paper’s online scheduler on a fluid population. If it instead asks for a schedule maximizing useful allocation subject to capacity and demand, it introduces an optimization objective the paper does not study. Theorem 5’s Pareto-efficiency claim cannot by itself specify that objective or the resulting computational problem. Adding a finite horizon, known demand profiles, and a new optimization target might yield worthwhile work, but that would be a new scheduling problem rather than a continuous mirror of this result.

There is a real modeling strain too: LTRF’s state depends on arrivals, task completions, runtime estimates, and accumulated resource accounts. Encoding every tenant’s workload and evolving state exactly may make the type support grow with the population. Repeated-profile cohorts could avoid that, so this is a qualification rather than my decisive objection.

My negative case is strong about the paper’s anchors and weak as a universal claim that no related continuous research could be worthwhile. The cloud scenario could motivate such research. What I cannot identify is a computational result in this paper for that research to continuize; making one would require choosing a new problem.
