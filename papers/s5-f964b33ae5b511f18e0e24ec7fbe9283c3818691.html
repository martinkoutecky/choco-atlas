The continuous mirror question

Fix \(p\in(1,2)\). Given a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over locations \(z_t\in\mathbb R^2\), with positive binary-rational masses summing to one, define each rotated coordinate median as the lower weighted median: the smallest projected value whose cumulative mass is at least \(1/2\). Let \(m_\theta\) be the facility location formed from the two medians at uniformly random orientation \(\theta\), and let \(C_p(\mu;y)=\left(\sum_t\mu_t\|z_t-y\|_2^p\right)^{1/p}\). What is the supremum over such \(\mu\) with positive optimum of \(\mathbb E_\theta[C_p(\mu;m_\theta)]/\min_{y\in\mathbb R^2}C_p(\mu;y)\)?

The model it lives in

Types are ideal locations \(z_t\in\mathbb R^2\) with masses \(\mu_t\). For each orientation, the mechanism’s decision \(m_\theta\) is determined by the two weighted projection medians; the benchmark minimizes the mass-weighted \(p\)-power Euclidean cost over facility locations \(y\in\mathbb R^2\). The question is the worst-case expected ratio, whose value is \(L(p)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the exact-ratio theorem, including its universal upper bound and matching finite-profile lower-bound family. It leaves aside the separate truthfulness proposition, optimality among all mechanisms, and extensions to other geometries or objectives.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Theorem [Exact approximation ratio]. The supplied text omits its numerical theorem counter, so I won’t guess one. The theorem, proved in this paper, says that for every \(1<p<2\), the supremum of URCM’s expected \(L_p\) social cost divided by the optimum, over all finite profiles, is
\[ L(p)=\frac{2^{2-1/p}}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}(\cos^p\theta+\sin^p\theta)^{1/p}\,\mathrm d\theta. \]
Its upper bound holds for every measurable choice within the coordinate-median intervals, and odd-size profiles approach the bound. The lower-bound construction was previously established by Chan, Lin, and Wang; this paper supplies the matching upper bound and gives a self-contained tightness proof.

I would name the mirror URCM\(_\infty\)-Ratio. Fix \(p\in(1,2)\). An instance is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over locations \(z_t\in\mathbb R^2\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. A type is a resident’s ideal location: people of that type have the same cost \(\|z_t-y\|_2\) for a facility at \(y\). For a uniformly random orientation \(\theta\), the mechanism returns the point whose two coordinates are the lower weighted medians of the projections of \(\mu\) onto the rotated axes. Define
\[ C_p(\mu;y)=\left(\sum_t\mu_t\|z_t-y\|_2^p\right)^{1/p}, \qquad \operatorname{OPT}_p(\mu)=\min_y C_p(\mu;y). \]
The question is to determine
\[ \sup_{\mu:\operatorname{OPT}_p(\mu)>0} \frac{\mathbb E_\Theta[C_p(\mu;m_\Theta)]}{\operatorname{OPT}_p(\mu)}. \]
A solution is a universal upper bound and a family of distributions whose ratios approach it.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is municipal facility planning for large cohorts attached to a few residential districts: many residents share each district’s ideal location, while the number of district types stays small. The paper’s tightness family fits this regime with three types: equal large cohorts at \((-1,0)\) and \((1,0)\), and a small remote cohort at \((0,R)\). Set \(N=R^4\) and give the two large types mass \(N/(2N+1)\) each and the remote type mass \(1/(2N+1)\). The support stays at \(\tau=3\) as the underlying population grows. For almost every orientation, the rare type eventually pushes the weighted medians to the far endpoints of the two large cohorts, while its contribution to normalized social cost vanishes. The resulting expected ratio tends to \(L(p)\), just as in the paper.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem: the facility decision, random rotation, type-level preferences, social objective, and optimum benchmark are unchanged. Only the population is represented by masses. This mirror covers the exact-ratio theorem, not the proposition about universal strategyproofness or the broader question of the best mechanism. That boundary fits the paper’s own emphasis that its approximation ratio is evaluated on true profiles, not on equilibria of strategic reports.

The expected answer is tractable in the programme’s trichotomy: the exact value is \(L(p)\), with no sign of continuum-specific hardness. In fact, for rational masses, replication by a common denominator converts every instance into a finite profile with repeated locations. The cost changes by a common population-normalization factor, which cancels in the ratio. Conversely, the paper’s lower-bound profiles are already empirical measures of this kind. So the bridge is exact and short; the paper’s theorem essentially settles the rational finite-support mirror. Natural follow-ups would be to extend the result to general probability measures with finite \(p\)-moment, and to study the complexity of evaluating or approximating URCM’s expected cost on one encoded distribution.

The weak point is that this is a strong mirror but a modest novelty claim. For binary-rational masses, the continuous formulation is equivalent by replication to the finite-profile model the paper already analyzes; it exposes a high-multiplicity regime more than it creates a new mathematical problem. Also, individual truthfulness becomes vacuous for agents of zero mass in a literal nonatomic population. That does not undermine the welfare-ratio mirror, but it means the case should be presented as a continuization of the theorem’s performance question, not as a continuum version of the paper’s incentive result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is one anchor: the theorem titled Exact approximation ratio. It is a genuine theorem about a mechanism’s welfare guarantee, but it gives no running time, hardness, or algorithm for a computational problem. It evaluates one fixed mechanism over all profiles. That leaves the paper without the kind of complexity result ChoCo is set up to mirror.

The proposed finite-support mirror is an exact re-encoding of that theorem’s instances. If each mass is rational, write \(\mu_t=k_t/M\) and make a finite profile with \(k_t\) copies of location \(z_t\). For every orientation, the weighted lower medians match the ordinary medians of that profile, and
\[ C_p(\mu;y)=M^{-1/p}C_p(X;y),\qquad \operatorname{OPT}_p(\mu)=M^{-1/p}\operatorname{OPT}_p(X). \]
The scale cancels in the ratio. So the proposed three-location municipal example, including its rare remote cohort, is already a finite profile from the theorem’s domain. It makes a plausible population story, but it does not create a distinct computational question.

A stronger mirror would use an arbitrary probability measure over locations with finite \(p\)-moment, allowing a genuinely nonatomic population. That is a sensible mathematical extension, but the paper’s proof appears to carry over directly: replace sums by integrals, and the median count conditions by the corresponding half-mass conditions for projection quantiles. The finite lower-bound family remains available, so it still supplies the lower bound. This extension would broaden the theorem’s measure-theoretic domain; it would not chart a new complexity landscape.

Trying to add a computational task does not repair the anchor. Arbitrary measures have no finite encoding until one specifies how they are represented. With rational atomic inputs, replication returns to the finite profiles above. Complexity for some chosen density or oracle representation might be worth studying, but that would be a new input-specific evaluation problem, not a mirror of this theorem. The paper’s global ratio calculation itself reduces to a one-dimensional angular integral, with no apparent role for the continuous-optimization tools motivating ChoCo.

So the strongest case against is about programme fit: the natural high-multiplicity instance regime is already covered pointwise by the paper, while a truly nonatomic extension is analytic and does not supply a computational result. This is not a proof that no follow-on could be useful; the population interpretation is coherent, and the negative case is weakest if ChoCo counts analytic extensions as worthwhile mirrors. But on the programme’s stated computational criterion, I would not treat this as a surviving mirror.
