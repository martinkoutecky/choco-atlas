The continuous mirror question

Given finite firm and worker type sets \(F,W\), rational type masses \(\alpha_f,\beta_w\), and explicitly encoded rational piecewise-linear path-independent mass-choice maps \(C_i\) on feasible per-capita partner-mass vectors, define matchings as feasible link-mass matrices and each type’s bundle as its incident link vector divided by its mass. Define stability by individual rationality and the absence of a positive link mass that both endpoint types choose when offered, and define Blair comparisons by applying each type’s map to the combined bundles. Given two stable matchings \(x,y\), determine and compute their least stable upper bound and greatest stable lower bound under the corresponding Blair orders, or report if either does not exist.

The model it lives in

A finite-support many-to-many market with rational masses of worker and firm types and explicit path-independent mass-choice maps. A matching is a feasible matrix of link mass; each type’s average bundle is its incident link mass per unit of type mass. Stability and Blair orders are defined through the type-level choice maps, and the task is to compute the stable join and meet of two given stable matchings.

The objection that survived

The mass-choice maps are not determined by the paper’s finite-agent choice functions, so a microfoundation for the selected extension remains open; the proponent acknowledges this without supplying one.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s join and meet construction, using the quasi-stable lattices and operators of Theorems 1 and 2; it leaves their complexity, computation of the full stable set, and direct computation of quasi-stable meets unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the type-level quasi-stable sets form lattices, and do the re-equilibration operators remain isotone with stable matchings as exactly their fixed points?
  • For an explicit rational piecewise-linear representation of the choice maps, can the stable join and meet be computed efficiently?
  • Which mass-choice maps have a microfoundation from repeated finite agents with integral partner bundles, and when does this model agree with the discrete high-multiplicity problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result that classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or parameterized-hard. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: given two stable matchings \(\mu,\mu'\), the firm-side join is the fixed point reached by iterating \(T^F\) from \(\lambda_{\mu,\mu'}\), where each firm chooses from its partners in the two matchings; the firm-side meet is the fixed point reached by iterating \(T^W\) from \(\gamma_{\mu,\mu'}\), where each worker makes that choice. The corollary gives the corresponding worker-side operations by duality. This is an exact construction of the lattice operations, but it gives no running-time bound. So it is an algorithmic anchor in the ordinary sense, not a complexity-class result of the kind the brief prefers.

The strongest mirror I see is computing lattice operations in a high-multiplicity matching market. Consider a large labor platform with many worker units and many project or employer units. A type records everything relevant to choice: preferences, capacity, prices, and other parameters. There may be millions of agents but only \(\tau_W\) worker types and \(\tau_F\) project types, each with a rational population mass \(\beta_w\) or \(\alpha_f\), summing to one on each side.

One concrete continuous version takes an instance to include those type masses and, for every type \(i\), a rational piecewise-linear mass-choice map \(C_i\). Its input is a vector of available partner mass by type; its output is the mass it chooses, coordinatewise no greater than the available mass. Require path independence, \(C_i(q+r)=C_i(C_i(q)+r)\), wherever the offers are defined. A matching is a matrix \(x=(x_{fw})\) of matched pair mass, with \(0\le x_{fw}\le\alpha_f\beta_w\). Type \(f\)’s per-capita matching vector is \(x_{f\cdot}/\alpha_f\), and type \(w\)’s is \(x_{\cdot w}/\beta_w\). Individual rationality means each type’s choice map returns its current vector. A blocking deviation is a positive mass of a currently unmatched type-pair that both choice maps would accept when added to their current vectors. Define the Blair orders by the same choice-from-the-union test as in the paper, applied to these per-capita vectors.

The continuous problem is: given this market and two stable mass matchings, compute their join and meet among stable mass matchings under the Blair orders. A solution is the least stable upper bound and greatest stable lower bound, respectively. Theorem 3 suggests a concrete route: form the mass-valued analogues of its quasi-stable starting points, then re-equilibrate with the corresponding type-level operators. The quantities \(\alpha,\beta\) are population masses, and fractional entries of \(x\) are aggregate matching mass; the underlying agents still have complete, shared type descriptions. This is a population mirror of the paper’s matching and stability question, not an outcome-lottery or time-continuization.

I would expect Class A for an explicit, finite-support version with rational piecewise-linear choice maps: the paper’s monotone re-equilibration structure gives a plausible route to a finite-dimensional algorithm. That is a low-confidence prediction, not a consequence of Theorem 3. The theorem establishes neither finite termination in the continuous state space nor a bound in \(\tau_F,\tau_W\), and the bit length. Further questions are whether the quasi-stable sets remain lattices for this mass-choice model, whether the operators remain isotone with stable matchings as exactly their fixed points, and whether their fixed points can be found efficiently. A useful bridge would also prove when this problem agrees with the high-multiplicity discrete problem and when a continuous solution yields an additive approximation.

My weakest point is the aggregate choice model. The paper’s agents choose sets of named partners; the proposed instance gives types mass-valued choice maps. A referee could reasonably ask for a microfoundation showing that these maps arise from identical agents with integral partner bundles, or say that the aggregate choice rule changes the problem too much. I think the mirror is worth posing because it preserves the paper’s central ingredients—many-to-many matching, path-independent choice, pairwise stability, and Blair lattice operations—while making the population itself the continuous object. But that microfoundation, and any complexity claim, remain to be established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 3 is its closest anchor: it identifies the join and meet by iterating Tarski operators from quasi-stable matchings. That is a constructive result, and a finite market’s iteration terminates because the matching space is finite. But the theorem gives no input representation or running-time bound; in the worst case, the number of iterations is bounded only by the size of that finite space. The paper’s contribution is chiefly an algebraic characterization and an interpretation of re-equilibration, not a result in the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proponent’s labor-platform example is plausible, and the strongest version would need neither mass-valued choice maps on both sides nor a mass of firms. Keep firms finite and let a large workforce consist of repeated worker types, with type recording every relevant preference and parameter. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The difficulty is that the paper’s primitive is a choice function over sets of named partners. Replicating workers does not by itself say how a firm’s choice from a set of people extends to a choice from fractions of a worker type. Different extensions can agree on every finite, integral cohort and still make different choices at fractional mass, changing stability and the lattice operations. A mass-valued choice map can settle the definition, but its representation then supplies the computational problem; it is not determined by the paper’s path-independent choice functions.

A clone-based extension could pin the choices down by imposing anonymous, quota-based or responsive preferences within each type. That yields a coherent model, but it narrows the paper’s “path-independence alone” setting. In particular, the paper says that with the law of aggregate demand the initial choice-based constructions already give stable matchings, removing the re-equilibration step that motivates Theorem 3. The broader extension keeps path-independence but asks a new question about a chosen class of mass-choice maps, whose complexity may depend more on how those maps are encoded than on population size.

So the anchor does not establish a worthwhile *continuous complexity mirror*: it establishes an abstract finite-market construction that can inspire one, but leaves the crucial continuum primitives and computational input model to be supplied. This is not airtight. A well-designed cohort model could make those choices canonical, and studying its lattice operations could be valuable. The negative case is that the paper itself gives little reason to expect such a model to be a high-multiplicity relaxation of its general result, rather than a new continuous matching theory.
