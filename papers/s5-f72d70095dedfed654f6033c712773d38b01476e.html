The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying anchor. The numbered theorems are about mechanism properties: Theorem 5.1 claims conditions under which a bidder has an incentive to bid higher; Theorem 5.2 claims higher rewards under a stated fair-value condition; Theorem 5.4 claims truthfulness; and Theorem 5.6 claims allocative efficiency. In Theorem 5.6, “efficient” means Pareto efficient, not polynomial-time computable. None classifies the complexity of a problem. The paper uses Sandholm’s winner-determination algorithm and gives its own payment algorithm, but supplies no numbered complexity result for either. So there is no anchor-specific continuous problem or complexity prediction to give under your rules.

The best positive lead, if an unanchored proposal is useful, is high-multiplicity winner determination. Imagine a large market for replicated resource bundles with many bidders but relatively few complete bid profiles. A type \(t\) would specify a bidder’s bids and fair values for every package, plus any other attributes used by the mechanism. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of bidders of that type. If resource capacity scales with market size, let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) bidders receiving the whole package \(S\). With \(\sum_S x_{t,S}\leq\mu_t\) and per-capita resource-capacity constraints, maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\Upsilon(t,S)\). For an explicit list of packages, this is an LP, a plausible Class A question about the allocation stage described in Section 4.

That regime could fit markets where many applicants share the same complete bid and value schedule. But this LP is a proposed research question, not a result established by the paper: it scales supply with population and does not establish that the paper’s GVA payments, fairness rules, or incentive claims carry over. Its biggest weakness is therefore the missing anchor. I would not present it as a continuous mirror of a numbered computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 5.1 concerns incentives to bid higher; Theorem 5.2 concerns rewards; and Theorem 5.4 asserts truthfulness. Theorem 5.6’s “efficiency” means Pareto efficiency, not computational efficiency. None classifies the complexity of a problem. Section 4 invokes Sandholm’s winner-determination algorithm, but states no complexity result for it; Algorithm 1 is a payment formula applied after winners are known. A continuous version of the incentive or fairness claims would be an axiomatic or mechanism-design question, not the computational mirror this programme seeks.

The proponent’s scaled-supply allocation LP is the best counterexample to that objection. With replicated bidders, a type containing a complete bid schedule, and supplies that grow with the population, variables \(x_{t,S}\) can represent the mass of type \(t\) receiving package \(S\). That is a coherent allocation model. But it is a mirror of an unnumbered subroutine, not of any theorem in this paper, and it drops the features that distinguish the paper’s proposal: its GVA payments, fairness table, and claims about individual incentives. With explicit bids for packages, the LP is polynomial in the listed input; if bids are represented compactly, the unresolved issue becomes the valuation encoding and its pricing problem, which this paper does not specify. Either way, it is a generic high-multiplicity allocation question rather than a complexity result of the paper.

Keeping the paper’s finite supply does not rescue the mirror. As the bidder population grows, the winners form a vanishing fraction of society, so a distribution over bidder types does not capture which bidders win or the tail of bids that determines the allocation. Scaling supply fixes that allocation problem, but it also removes the scarcity regime of the paper’s auction. And the strategic claims fare worse under an atomless population: one bidder’s report has zero effect on the allocation and on a VCG-style marginal-contribution payment. A deviation by an entire positive-mass type can matter, but that is a coordinated group deviation, not the individual truthfulness or reward claim in these theorems.

The negative case is strongest against the paper as an anchor: its computational content is borrowed or procedural, while its numbered results are not about computational complexity. It is weaker as a universal claim. The scaled-supply LP is a sensible adjacent research question, and this paper’s description of winner determination gives it a connection to the text. The case against must therefore rest on the narrower point that this connection does not supply a worthwhile continuous mirror of a named computational result here.
