The continuous mirror question

Given known renter types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1-\eta\), \(m\) room categories with normalized capacities \(q_r\) summing to \(1\), and one hidden renter of mass \(\eta=1/N\), determine whether there is a rent vector \(p\) with \(p_r\geq 0\) and \(\sum_r q_r p_r=\bar R\) such that, for every category \(r\) the hidden renter might choose, the known mass can be assigned to categories it most prefers at \(p\). Formally, find flows \(x^{[r]}_{t,s}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_s x^{[r]}_{t,s}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x^{[r]}_{t,s}\leq q_s-\eta\mathbf{1}[s=r]\), and \(x^{[r]}_{t,s}>0\) only if \(s\in D_t(p)\), where \(D_t(p)\) is type \(t\)'s set of most-preferred categories.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity rental market has known types \(t\in T\) carrying masses \(\mu_t\), \(m\) room categories with capacities \(q_r\), and one hidden renter who removes mass \(\eta=1/N\) from whichever category they choose. Find a single rent vector and, for each possible choice, a preference-respecting flow of known renter mass into the remaining capacity.

The objection that survived

The proposed type-mass Hall condition is a new theorem: the paper's slack counts distinct roommates and rooms and does not directly establish feasibility for category capacities and fractional type flows.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's secretive-roommate rent-finding guarantee and the construction in Section 5; it leaves the Sperner arguments and Theorems 2–3 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the paper's preference assumptions imply feasibility for all rational type masses and category supplies?
  • For fixed \(m\), can the price regions and robust flows be searched in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\), and what dependence is needed when \(m\) grows?
  • How does this flow problem relate to finite high-multiplicity rental instances when \(\eta=1/N\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Under the stated assumptions on preferences, it says that for an apartment with \(n\) bedrooms, knowing the preferences of only \(n-1\) roommates suffices to find an envy-free rent division. The paper’s construction gives the missing operational detail: at the resulting prices, the secretive roommate may choose any room, and the known roommates can still be matched to rooms they prefer. The existence result had previously been proved nonconstructively by Asada et al. (2017); this paper gives an elementary constructive proof and an algorithm in Section 5, though it states no running-time bound. It offers no comparable hardness result, so this is the algorithmic anchor I would use.

My mirror is Secretive Cohort Rental Harmony. Consider \(N\) room slots grouped into \(m\) room categories, with category \(r\) having normalized supply \(q_r\), where \(\sum_r q_r=1\). There is a large population of renters with known types \(T\), type masses \(\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1-\eta\). Add one secretive renter, represented by a hidden type of mass \(\eta=1/N\). A type is a complete description of renters’ preferences across categories and rents; renters of a given type are interchangeable. Suppose each known type has a rational piecewise-linear utility curve \(u_{t,r}(p_r)\) for category \(r\) at rent \(p_r\), satisfying the paper’s preference assumptions. The hidden renter’s preferences are withheld. Let \(D_t(p)\) be the categories in which known type \(t\) is happiest at prices \(p\).

The input includes the type masses, room supplies, and known preference curves. The question is to find a single rent vector \(p\), with \(p_r\geq0\) and \(\sum_r q_rp_r=\bar R\) for the specified average rent \(\bar R\), and a contingent allocation for every possible room category \(r\) the hidden renter might choose. For each such \(r\), the allocation \(x^{[r]}_{t,s}\) must satisfy
\[ \sum_s x^{[r]}_{t,s}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x^{[r]}_{t,s}\leq q_s-\eta\,\mathbf 1[s=r], \]
and \(x^{[r]}_{t,s}>0\) only when \(s\in D_t(p)\). In words, whichever category the secretive renter takes, all known renters can be placed in categories they prefer at the same rents. The solution is the rent vector and these contingent mass assignments; there is no welfare or cost objective in the paper’s problem.

This is a population continuization: a large dormitory or housing cooperative has many slots, a modest number of recurring renter types, and supply that scales with the population. The room categories and their capacities are part of the mirror because supply must scale with demand. Rent already varies continuously in the paper; that is not the continuization claim. In ChoCo terms, the relevant regime is \(N\gg \tau,m\), where \(\tau=|T|\). The hidden renter is a singleton type of mass \(1/N\), while the rest of the population is grouped into recurring types.

The connection to Theorem 1 is unusually direct. Its proof establishes that every group of \(k\) known roommates collectively prefers at least \(k+1\) rooms locally, then uses Hall’s theorem to match them after any one room is removed. The mirror asks for the mass-weighted counterpart: known type mass must have enough preferred capacity to leave one room’s worth of slack. The authors would recognize the secretive-roommate logic, rent vector, preference-based assignment, and final matching question. The changed objects—type masses and category capacities—are exactly what makes that question meaningful for a large population.

I expect a tractable result for a fixed number \(m\) of room categories. With piecewise-linear preferences, one can divide price space into regions with fixed demand sets, then check the robust assignments using capacitated matching or flow. This suggests a fixed-\(m\) algorithm, with the full dependence on \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\) still to be established. In ChoCo’s terms, that is a plausible Class A direction, not a claimed complexity result for the paper. Further questions include whether the mass-weighted Hall slack always exists under nonuniform supplies, what happens when \(m\) grows, and how the continuous assignments relate to finite high-multiplicity instances.

The weakest point is the singleton hidden mass. At normalized mass \(1/N\), it preserves the “one roommate takes one room” condition, but it vanishes in a strict atomless limit. Replacing it with a hidden cohort of fixed positive mass would avoid that issue, but would demand a stronger theorem: the known population would need enough slack to absorb the whole cohort. The paper’s one-room slack does not establish that extension. The mirror is therefore strongest as a high-multiplicity problem with a finite hidden atom, and weaker as a limit with no atoms at all.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s main theorem is a constructive existence result, not a complexity result. Theorem 1 says that the preferences of \(n-1\) roommates suffice; Section 5 gives a path-following algorithm. But it gives no running-time bound, and the preference assumptions provide no finite input representation. The algorithm surveys preferences on a triangulation fine enough for a one-cent margin, with no bound on the number of queries or on how preferences are accessed. The paper therefore supplies no defined complexity problem for a continuous mirror to inherit. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 concern Sperner labelings, not computational social choice.

The proposed housing-cohort model is coherent, and its changes to supplies and assignments are legitimate co-modifications. Its best high-multiplicity version, though, exposes a mismatch in the theorem’s central feature. In the proposed model, the secretive renter has mass \(1/N\). As \(N\) grows, the capacity removed by that renter tends to zero. In the limit, the guarantee across possible choices of one room loses its force: the remaining question is whether a divisible population can be assigned at common rents. That is a sensible market-allocation question, but it no longer captures the theorem’s distinctive guarantee about one unknown person choosing among finitely many rooms.

Keeping the secretive part substantial by giving it positive mass \(\delta\) avoids that collapse. It also asks for a different theorem. The paper’s proof creates enough preference slack for the known roommates to remain matchable after one room is taken. It establishes no corresponding slack after a cohort takes mass \(\delta\), and the cohort’s preferences, internal allocation, and possible choices now need specification. This could be a worthwhile robust market problem, but its significance would rest on a new model and a new result, rather than on a computational contribution made by this paper.

There is a related scaling difficulty. In the paper, \(n\) counts both roommates and distinct rooms, so increasing the population also increases the dimension of the rent simplex. Grouping identical renters does not compress that rent vector. Grouping identical rooms and assigning a common rent to each category does, but then the model’s central variables are category prices and capacities; the single-room contingency becomes a capacity perturbation that vanishes in the high-multiplicity limit. The authors might recognize this as a related rental-allocation question, but the paper itself offers little reason to expect its secretive-roommate argument to survive the change.

So the case against is strongest as a claim about fit: the sole social-choice anchor has no stated complexity landscape, and the best population model either washes out its one-person feature or turns it into a positive-mass robust-market problem. That is a real reason to rank this paper low for ChoCo. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from it; the positive-mass variant remains plausible, and that is where this negative case is weakest.
