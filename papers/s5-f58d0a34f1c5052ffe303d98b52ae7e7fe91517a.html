The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s College Admission with Common Quotas result, because large admissions systems naturally have many applicants but comparatively few complete preference and priority types. The paper has no numbered theorem asserting NP-hardness, so I use its named algorithmic result as the anchor rather than treating the hardness statements in the introduction as anchors.

Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper, says that if each college belongs to at most \(\ell\) common-quota sets, then one can modify each common quota by at most \(2\ell-1\) and obtain a stable feasible matching, even with ties. Its algorithm runs in polynomial time *after* a stable fractional solution \(x^*\) has been found with Scarf’s algorithm. That qualification matters: the theorem does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for the whole task.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a national admissions process with millions of applicants but a moderate number \(\tau\) of complete applicant types. A type could encode an applicant’s ordered program list, funding status, and priority category at every quota set. For example, applicants might be grouped by application order and standardized score band. These details must be part of the type: applicants who differ in a preference or priority the problem uses are different types. Colleges and quota sets remain explicit; the continuous object is the applicant population.

I would call the mirror Continuous Common-Quota Admission Repair. Its input is a finite college set \(C\), quota sets \(Q_1,\ldots,Q_p\) with each college in at most \(\ell\) sets, and \(\tau\) applicant types \(T\). Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a strict ranking of acceptable colleges, and a priority ranking wherever a quota set uses it. The baseline quota \(b_j\) for each \(Q_j\) is a rational mass of applicants; singleton sets represent individual college quotas.

A solution consists of adjusted quotas \(b'_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_c x_{t,c}\le\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_{t,\,c\in Q_j}x_{t,c}\le b'_j\) for every quota set. The objective is to minimize \(\Delta=\max_j|b'_j-b_j|\).

Stability uses the paper’s blocking logic at the level of positive mass. Type \(t\) has positive mass willing to move to \(c\) if some of its mass is unmatched or assigned to a college it ranks below \(c\). A pair \((t,c)\) blocks if that willing mass is positive and, for every quota set containing \(c\), either the quota has positive slack or it includes positive mass of applicants ranked below \(t\). The allocation is stable if no such pair blocks. Since a type is atomless, an \(x_{t,c}\) value represents a portion of that type’s people each assigned to one college; it is not a lottery giving each applicant a fractional seat.

For the continuous problem, the minimum quota change is \(0\): the paper itself constructs a stable fractional solution for the common-quota model via Scarf’s Lemma before rounding it. The substantive computational question is whether one can find the stable mass allocation in time polynomial in the explicit college and quota descriptions, \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). My tentative expectation is continuum-specific hardness for this search problem, perhaps of PPAD type. The paper notes that Scarf’s algorithm is not generally polynomial and cites PPAD-hardness for computing fractional stable solutions in general settings. That is motivation, not a hardness result for this particular common-quota mirror; a reduction for this restricted model would still be needed.

I think the authors would recognize the question: it keeps their colleges, overlapping quotas, priorities, and blocking notion, while changing the applicants into a population with type masses. It also follows the paper’s own fractional-stability step. The mirror covers Section 4 and Theorem 4.2, not the paper’s hypergraph or flow results. It asks whether the type-supported stable allocation can be computed efficiently, and whether the discrete quota-repair guarantee then supplies a clean finite-to-continuous dictionary: a repair of \(2\ell-1\) seats becomes a mass change of \((2\ell-1)/N\) in a society of \(N\) applicants.

My weakest point is that the paper’s headline output is an integral matching of named students after quota changes, while this mirror permits a type cohort to be split across colleges. That is a real co-modification. It seems defensible here because the cohort members are identical in every respect the model uses, and the mass assignment can be implemented by assigning different applicants in the cohort to different colleges. The paper’s fractional construction makes this bridge especially credible, but it does not settle the support-sensitive complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed admissions setting is credible: a large system can have many applicants but relatively few complete preference and priority types. The type definition also handles the important detail that priority must be included. I would not reject the mirror on high-multiplicity grounds; that regime supports it.

The problem is that Theorem 4.2’s contribution is to round a fractional solution into an integral matching by changing quotas. In the proposed mass model, \(x_{t,c}\) is already the aggregate fractional matching. Section 4 constructs a stable fractional matching under the original quotas using Scarf’s Lemma. An atomless cohort can be partitioned among colleges to realize those assignment masses while each applicant still receives one college. So the natural continuous quota-repair objective has optimum \(0\). If quotas are measured as population shares, the theorem’s \(2\ell-1\) seat adjustment also shrinks to at most \((2\ell-1)/N\). The feature the theorem repairs—integrality at finite scale—has vanished.

The proponent’s more interesting question is whether one can compute the stable mass allocation efficiently. But that shifts the anchor: it asks for the pre-rounding Scarf solution, not for the quota-repair guarantee of Theorem 4.2. A type-compressed version could still be worthwhile, especially if rational masses encoded in \(L\) bits let an algorithm depend on \(\tau\) rather than on an enormous number of applicants. The paper’s general PPAD-hardness discussion does not settle that restricted question. But its value would come from studying compressed computation of fractional stability, not from mirroring this theorem’s near-feasible integral result.

Thus I have a strong objection to calling the stated repair problem a continuous mirror. I cannot honestly turn that into the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the support-sensitive Scarf search remains a plausible research question, and the high-multiplicity admissions regime makes it sensible. My negative case is weakest exactly there.
