The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 proves nonnegative utility for an honest bidder, Theorem 2 proves that misreporting does not increase a bidder’s utility, and Theorems 3–5 prove negligible privacy advantages for the specified adversaries. None asserts that a problem is hard, tractable, or belongs to a complexity class. The computational claims instead appear as unnumbered background statements: exact combinatorial-auction welfare maximization is NP-hard, and the paper cites prior work for an \(O(\sqrt m)\) approximation bound that cannot be improved to \(O(m^{1/2-\varepsilon})\). Those are promising substantive anchors, but they do not meet your numbered-result requirement. Strictly, then, this paper supplies zero eligible anchors.

If those unnumbered claims are admitted as the basis for a positive case, the strongest mirror is continuous single-minded combinatorial-auction winner determination. Consider a large standardized market for advertising inventory, cloud capacity, or spectrum. Each buyer’s type \(t\) consists of a requested bundle \(S_t\subseteq [m]\) and a reported value \(b_t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers with that type. There are \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(q_j\) be the per-buyer supply of good \(j\), rationally encoded. The decision variable \(x_t\) is the mass of type-\(t\) buyers who receive their entire requested bundle. The problem is

\[ \max \sum_t b_t x_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le x_t\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:\,j\in S_t}x_t\le q_j\quad\text{for every }j. \]

The question is to compute an optimal \(x\). Individual buyers still receive whole bundles; the continuum lets the auction allocate a fraction of a type’s mass. For a finite cohort of size \(N\), this corresponds to \(N\mu_t\) buyers of each type and \(Nq_j\) units of each good. The regime is \(N\) large relative to \(\tau\): many buyers share the same bundle and value, while inventory scales with the cohort so that the market remains nontrivial.

This mirrors the paper’s winner-determination problem closely: choose winners with nonconflicting bundles to maximize reported social welfare. The continuous version is an LP with \(O(\tau)\) variables and \(m\) resource constraints, so I expect it to be tractable in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. Its scope is only welfare winner determination, including the NP-hardness the paper discusses in prose. It does not mirror the privacy protocol or the payment and truthfulness theorems.

The weak point is the market regime: the paper’s auction has a fixed set of goods, whereas this mirror scales inventory with the population and permits fractional selection within a type. That co-modification is a natural way to define a nondegenerate large-market version, but authors or a referee might regard it as a large-market extension rather than their original fixed-inventory problem. The next questions would be whether rounding the LP gives useful guarantees for finite cohorts, how the integrality gap behaves, and whether the paper’s truthful payment result has a meaningful mass-market analogue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper gives no numbered computational result to mirror. Theorems 1 and 2 establish individual incentive properties; Theorems 3–5 concern privacy advantages. The NP-hardness of exact winner determination and the \(O(m^{1/2})\) approximation claim appear as background, with the latter attributed to prior work. The paper’s own contribution is a privacy-preserving protocol and payment construction, not a complexity classification.

That distinction matters for the proposed LP. It describes a sensible continuous question for a capacitated market, but it does not mirror a computational result established in this paper. The paper sells a fixed set of heterogeneous goods, each allocated at most once. With a population of size \(N\), keeping that supply fixed makes per-capita supply shrink as \(1/N\). The LP avoids that collapse by giving the market \(Nq_j\) units of each good. That can be natural for repeated inventory, such as ad impressions, but it changes the problem into a market with scalable category capacities. The resulting fractional packing problem does not address the paper’s privacy guarantees, payment verification, or encrypted winner determination.

Those contributions also resist aggregation. The privacy theorems concern information about individual bidders’ bids and bundles, while the protocol uses bidder-specific signatures and reveals individual winners. To preserve those tasks, a “type” would need to retain bidder-specific cryptographic information, leaving little multiplicity to exploit. Aggregating bidders instead would require new privacy and verification questions, rather than continuizing the theorems the authors prove.

The negative case is strongest on eligibility, not on the claim that no related continuum model could be worthwhile. A repeated-inventory combinatorial auction is a plausible research problem, and the LP is a credible starting point for it. But that is an adjacent model motivated by the paper’s background problem, not a continuous mirror of one of its named computational results. The universal claim remains hard to prove; the paper alone does not support a stronger objection to every possible auction scenario.
