What the mirror covers

No results are mirrored. Theorems 1.3–1.6 and the other probabilistic results remain outside the proposed computational mass-matching question.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large labor market with many applicants and positions but relatively few preference types. A type would specify an agent’s complete preferences over counterpart types; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the market of type \(t\). A matching variable \(x_{tu}\) would say how much mass of type \(t\) is matched to type \(u\). One could ask for the range of aggregate rank totals over stable mass matchings. That is a sensible adjacent continuous problem, and it connects to the paper’s study of ranks across stable matchings.

But it is not anchored by a named computational result in this paper, as your criteria require. Theorem 1.3, proved here, gives an asymptotic formula for the expected number of stable matchings. Theorems 1.4 and 1.5 give high-probability concentration of rank totals, and Theorem 1.6 bounds the fractions of participants with multiple stable partners. Theorem 1.1 is an asymptotic result attributed to Ashlagi, Kanoria, and Leshno. These are probabilistic statements, not results classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. Lemma 1.2’s integral formulas are analytical tools, and the paper’s uniform random variables encode random finite preference lists; they do not make the population continuous.

So the strongest honest positive case is only that stable matching and aggregate ranks invite a continuous-population analogue in a plausible market regime. I would not present the rank-range question as a mirror of a named result here: changing from random lists over individual partners to shared rankings over partner types changes the model, while the paper’s main contribution is asymptotic analysis rather than computational complexity. There are no eligible anchors, and thus no per-anchor prover questions under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1.1 is an asymptotic statement about random preferences, attributed to Ashlagi, Kanoria, and Leshno. Theorems 1.3–1.6 concern expected counts, concentration of ranks, and fractions of participants with multiple stable partners, all under a random preference model. Lemma 1.2 gives integral formulas for probabilities. The paper describes Gale–Shapley and cites an earlier running-time bound, but it does not establish a complexity result of its own. Under the programme’s criteria, there is no eligible anchor.

The best candidate for a replacement is a high-multiplicity labor market: let agents share a small set of preference types over counterpart types, represent matches by masses \(x_{ab}\), and ask for the minimum and maximum aggregate rank across stable mass allocations. Per-capita ranks could be defined by the mass of counterpart types preferred to the assigned type. That is coherent and may be worth studying. But it asks for optimization over stable allocations in a specified society; Pittel’s rank theorems instead concern concentration across stable matchings in a random instance of rankings over named individuals. Making the input a rational type distribution creates a new computational problem. Keeping the random experiment makes the target a probabilistic asymptotic question, not a ChoCo complexity question.

The type choice makes that gap concrete. If a type records an agent’s full ranking over named partners, the random model offers little repeated-type compression. If many agents share rankings over partner classes, ranks become class-level or mass-weighted quantities, and the original random-rank observables have changed. Those changes may be sensible, but they do not produce a computational mirror of the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strong for this paper, but weaker as a universal claim: a continuous stable-matching problem could plainly be worthwhile. What the paper does not supply is a named computational result for it to mirror.
