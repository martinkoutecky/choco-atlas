The continuous mirror question

Given candidates C, committee size k, and τ supported approval types A with positive rational masses μ_A summing to 1, is there an algorithm running in poly(m,τ,L) time that returns an integral size-k committee W satisfying all of the following? For every ℓ∈[k] and unelected candidate c, ∑_{A:c∈A, |A∩W|<ℓ} μ_A < ℓ/k (mass EJR+); no fractional committee x∈[0,1]^C with ∑_c x_c=k has ∑_{c∈A}x_c ≥ α★|A∩W| for every supported A, strictly for at least one; no size-k committee strictly improves every supported type's utility; and PAV_μ(W) ≥ γ★ max_{|W'|=k} PAV_μ(W'), where PAV_μ(W)=∑_A μ_A H_{|A∩W|}.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution μ over approval-set types A⊆C, given by its τ positive rational masses. The selected outcome remains an integral size-k committee W; its per-capita objective is PAV_μ(W)=∑_A μ_A H_{|A∩W|}. Fractional committees x with 0≤x_c≤1 and ∑_c x_c=k remain the paper's efficiency comparator, while type utilities are u_A(x)=∑_{c∈A}x_c.

The objection that survived

The extra (1+ε)-PO-for-every-ε guarantee does not follow immediately: a type with arbitrarily small positive mass can witness a strict improvement with tiny normalized score gain, so the thresholded local-search proof may not establish that guarantee in polynomial time in m, τ, and L.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.8's EJR+, α★-fPO, weak-PO, and γ★-PAV approximation guarantees over rational approval-type masses. It leaves the transfer of the additional (1+ε)-PO-for-every-ε guarantee unresolved, along with the paper's other local-PAV bounds and counterexamples.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one compute in poly(m,τ,L) time a committee meeting mass EJR+, α★-fPO, weak PO, and the γ★ approximation to weighted PAV from binary rational type masses?
  • Can the paper's (1+ε)-PO-for-every-ε guarantee also be obtained with running time polynomial in m, τ, and L, or must it depend on the smallest positive type mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is approval based committee selection from a distribution of ballot types. A type is a complete approval set \(A\subseteq C\); its mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of the electorate using that ballot. The input is \(C\), \(k\), and the \(\tau\) supported ballot types with positive rational masses summing to one, encoded in \(L\) bits. A committee remains an ordinary size-\(k\) set of candidates. Its per-capita PAV score is
\[ \mathrm{PAV}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_A \mu_A H_{|A\cap W|}. \]
This changes the population representation while keeping the paper’s decision and objective intact.

A plausible regime is a large association, electorate, or platform community choosing a fixed-size committee from a known slate, where many people submit the same approval sets. There may be millions of voters but comparatively few distinct ballots. Those sharing a type have the same preferences and utility under every committee. The mass quota \( \ell/k \) in EJR+ is then the population-scale counterpart of the paper’s discrete quota \( \ell n/k \).

My lead anchor is the PAV winner-determination hardness cited in the paper’s Related Work and preliminaries. The paper points to Aziz et al. (2015, Corollary 2); in the primary AAMAS proceedings version I checked, the relevant result is Corollary 1, which says PAV winner determination is NP-hard even when each voter approves two candidates. The proceedings version labels Corollary 2 as a different result, so the paper appears to have a citation-number mismatch. The hardness is cited from elsewhere, not proved in this paper. [Aziz et al., *Computational Aspects of Multi-Winner Approval Voting*](https://ifaamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2015/aamas/p107.pdf)

The corresponding problem, Continuous PAV Winner Determination, is: given the distribution input above, find a size-\(k\) committee maximizing \(\mathrm{PAV}_{\mu}(W)\). A solution is any exact maximizer. This is a direct high-multiplicity version of the problem the cited result concerns: from a discrete election, set \(\mu_A\) to the number of voters with ballot \(A\), divided by \(n\). Then \(\mathrm{PAV}_{\mu}(W)\) is the discrete PAV score divided by \(n\), so the maximizers are identical. The reduction is polynomial, and duplicating every voter preserves \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) while making the population arbitrarily large. I therefore expect this problem to be Class B: the hardness transfers. The reduction’s combinatorics lie in the candidate set and the interactions among approved pairs, not in the ability to target individual voters.

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 3.8, proved in this paper. It says Algorithm 1 runs in polynomial time and returns a committee satisfying EJR+ and \(\alpha^\star\)-fPO; the same committee is weakly Pareto optimal, is \((1+\varepsilon)\)-PO for every \(\varepsilon>0\), and achieves a \(\gamma^\star\)-approximation to optimal PAV score. Here \(\alpha^\star\approx1.346\) solves \(h(\alpha^\star)=1\), and \(\gamma^\star=h(1)\approx0.79\).

Its mirror, Continuous EJR+–Efficient PAV Committee, has the same type-mass input. It asks for a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) with all those guarantees, where \(\mathrm{PAV}_{\mu}(W)\ge\gamma^\star\max_{|W'|=k}\mathrm{PAV}_{\mu}(W')\). For EJR+, the exact mass interpretation is: for every \(\ell\in[k]\) and unelected candidate \(c\), the mass of types that approve \(c\) but have utility below \(\ell\) in \(W\) must be less than \(\ell/k\). For \(\alpha^\star\)-fPO, there must be no fractional committee \(x\in[0,1]^C\), \(\sum_c x_c=k\), that improves every supported type’s utility by the factor \(\alpha^\star\), strictly for at least one type. The fractional committee is retained as the paper’s efficiency comparator; the continuous object being introduced is the society \(\mu\).

I expect this second problem to be Class A. Theorem 3.8 is stated for explicit voters, so the compressed-input running time is an extension to establish, not a theorem already proved in the paper. But the algorithm’s sums can be grouped by approval type, its fractional objective has one utility term per supported type, and its committee output remains integral. That makes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\) a credible target.

These mirrors cover exact PAV winner determination and the paper’s combined approximation-and-representation guarantee. They do not claim to continuize every local-search result or every counterexample in the paper. The exact-search question is the stronger anchor: it shows a meaningful continuum mirror whose answer remains hard, while Theorem 3.8 offers a tractable, explicitly approximate companion.

My weakest point is that the exact mirror is very close to weighted PAV under another input encoding; the transferred hardness is immediate, not a new structural theorem. The algorithmic mirror also needs a careful weighted-input analysis before its compressed polynomial bound can be claimed. That limits novelty, but does not make the questions inauthentic: the paper studies finite voter lists, whereas these formulations ask for the complexity in the number of supported types and mass bit length. Natural next questions are whether exact winner determination remains hard under small \(\tau\), how the guarantees vary with \(k\) and \(\tau\), and how to state a two-way discrete/continuous dictionary when rational masses may require a large common denominator to realize as literal voter counts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection concerns the exact-PAV anchor: its NP-hardness is cited background, not a result proved in this paper, and the paper’s citation number appears mismatched. The proposed mass version is still coherent—normalizing ballot counts preserves the maximizing committees—but that supports a mirror of prior work more than a mirror of this paper’s contribution. Its main weakness is low novelty, not invalidity.

The harder anchor is Theorem 3.8, and I cannot honestly defeat it. With ballot types \(A\) and masses \(\mu_A\), its objective becomes \(\sum_A \mu_A h(u_A(x))\). The PAV score and EJR+ quota likewise have direct mass versions. Grouping voters by type makes an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) a credible target. That may be a straightforward extension, but the changed input size is a computational question in the programme’s scope.

There is one real caveat in transferring the theorem wholesale. Its \((1+\varepsilon)\)-PO guarantee relies on local-search improvements measured in unnormalized voter counts. With rational masses, a type may have mass \(2^{-L}\); improving only that type can change normalized PAV score by just \(2^{-L}\). The paper’s fixed improvement threshold then no longer establishes the guarantee, and a threshold scaled to the smallest mass could require exponentially many steps in \(L\). This exposes a genuine issue for the full guarantee. It does not defeat a better mirror asking whether EJR+, \(\alpha^\star\)-fPO, and the \(\gamma^\star\)-PAV approximation extend to mass input; those guarantees appear to have a plausible weighted formulation.

So the negative case can challenge the exact-PAV anchor’s connection to the paper and identify a delicate part of Theorem 3.8’s transfer. It cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 3.8 remains a credible continuous-population question, and the small-mass obstacle may itself make the boundary worth studying.
