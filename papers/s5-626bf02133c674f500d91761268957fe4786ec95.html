The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives, a committee bound \(K\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), choose a committee \(S\) with \(|S|\le K\) and an allocation \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). What is the polynomial-time approximability of minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_t(a))\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over complete ranking types. Choose at most \(K\) representatives and allocate each type's mass among them; minimize total positional dissatisfaction. With CC's nonbinding capacity, an optimal allocation sends each type's mass to its most preferred selected representative.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's total-dissatisfaction inapproximability result for CC. It leaves the Monroe variants, the minmax objectives, the satisfaction approximation results, and the general resource-allocation model aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the complexity change when the support size \(\tau\) is bounded or treated as a parameter: does this problem admit an exact, fixed-parameter, or improved approximation algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is Continuous CC Total-Dissatisfaction Approximation. Take \(m\) alternatives and a committee budget \(K\). The society is a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings over those alternatives, with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Fix a normal increasing positional scoring family \(\alpha\), where \(\alpha_m(i)\) is the dissatisfaction of a voter whose assigned alternative is in position \(i\).

Choose a committee \(S\) with \(|S|\le K\), then allocate each type’s mass among its selected representatives: \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\) for \(a\in S\), with \(\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). Minimize

\[ D_\alpha(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}\,\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_t(a)). \]

The output is the committee and a feasible mass allocation. This is the continuous population version of CC-Assignment-Inc: every member of the society gets one selected representative, while a representative may serve any amount of mass. The paper’s CC capacity is the whole electorate, so in the normalized model the capacity is \(1\) and does not bind.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: for every normal IPSF \(\alpha\) and every constant \(\beta>1\), there is no polynomial-time \(\beta\)-approximation algorithm for \(\alpha\)-CC-Assignment-Inc unless \(P=NP\). In other words, even approximating the minimum *total* positional dissatisfaction within any fixed factor is impossible under that assumption. The paper proves this by reduction from Vertex-Cover.

A natural regime is a large multiwinner election or large membership organization choosing \(K\) representatives from \(m\) candidates. Many constituents share the same complete ranking, so the electorate can be represented by \(\tau\) ranking types with rational population shares, where the total number of people is far larger than \(\tau\). Here a type is complete for the problem: its members have the same ranking and are evaluated by the same scoring rule.

The expected classification is Class B: hardness transfers. For any discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of ranking \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). In CC, representatives have no binding per-capita quota, so for any chosen committee all voters of a type can be assigned to their most preferred selected alternative. Thus grouping the discrete voters into types preserves the optimum dissatisfaction, up to the normalization by \(n\). A continuous approximation algorithm on these empirical distributions would give the same approximation for the discrete problem, contradicting Theorem 3. Fractional type allocations do not evade the reduction: they cannot improve on assigning each type’s mass to its best selected representative.

This mirror covers the paper’s CC total-dissatisfaction inapproximability result. I would not extend the claim to Monroe’s equal-quota variant: there, splitting a type’s mass can change which assignments are feasible, so the discrete hardness does not transfer by the same argument.

The weak point is that the reduction does not establish hardness when the support size \(\tau\) is small, and CC’s nonbinding capacities make the allocation step separable once the committee is chosen. So this is a strong, recognizable continuous formulation of the paper’s problem, but its hardness case does not show that continuous optimization technology fails in the compressed-support regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is that CC’s continuous allocation largely disappears once the committee is fixed. For a committee \(S\), each type sends all its mass to its most preferred member of \(S\), so the optimum is

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\min_{a\in S}\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_t(a)). \]

The remaining choice is a discrete committee. For every finite profile, taking \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives exactly the discrete total dissatisfaction divided by \(n\). Thus fractional assignment adds no flexibility here; the mirror is a support weighted version of the same committee selection problem. The paper’s Vertex-Cover reduction also does not establish tractability when support is small.

But that does not defeat the mirror under the programme’s criteria. A large electorate with many voters sharing rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the objective above is a clear computational question on rational population shares. The exact correspondence with finite profiles is itself a legitimate result; a continuous mirror need not produce a different answer. And the absence of a low support algorithm is a limitation of what Theorem 3 tells us, not a reason the question is ill-posed.

So I can make a case that this is a less distinctive continuization than one with a nontrivial mass allocation or a pricing problem. I cannot honestly make the stronger case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 3’s CC total-dissatisfaction result survives as a well-posed continuous mirror, with the expected Class B hardness transfer.
