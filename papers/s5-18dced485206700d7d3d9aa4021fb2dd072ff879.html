What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive candidate is a continuous version of Theorem \(\ref{thm:optimaldictatorship}\), proved in this paper. In the paper’s finite committee, a member’s type could be their payoff \(u_i\) and information cost function \(c_i\). In a large regulatory committee with \(n\gg\tau\), let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of members with each of \(\tau\) complete types \(t=(u_t,c_t)\). A voting rule could map the masses of each type casting each vote to an enactment probability. The lobbyist chooses a Bayes-plausible distribution over interim beliefs, and members choose private experiments as in the paper. The question would be whether some voting rule’s lobbyist-preferred equilibrium weakly improves, for every competing rule, the probability of enacting a good policy, the probability of blocking a bad one, and each type’s per-member information cost.

That would mirror the theorem’s full claim: with a finite committee and unrestricted public experiments, the dictatorship of the member with the highest upper persuasion threshold is dominant—relative to every voting mechanism, it weakly raises enactment probability in the good state, weakly raises blocking probability in the bad state, and weakly lowers every member’s expected information-acquisition cost. The theorem is proved here. Grouping members by \((u_t,c_t)\) gives a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, rather than objecting that types hide individual prices or costs.

But this is only a speculative extension, not a sound anchor for the requested computational case. The theorem’s key object is a single most-demanding dictator. In an atomless committee, an individual has zero mass and cannot affect a rule based on aggregate vote masses; treating an entire type group as the dictator would change the paper’s mechanism. The authors might recognize large-committee design as a worthwhile extension, but the theorem does not establish that this is its continuous analogue.

Under the anchoring rule, the paper has no named computational-complexity result. The named results—the dictatorship equilibrium characterization in Lemma \(\ref{lem:dictatoreqm}\), the dominance theorem, and Proposition \(\ref{prop:dict_mostinfo}\) about Blackwell-more-informative experiments—are game-theoretic results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or parameterized-tractable. The paper has continuous distributions over beliefs and experiments, but its committee population remains finite; continuity of those objects is outside this programme’s target. So there is no honest anchor-backed prover question here. The large-committee question above is the best positive lead I can offer, and its gap from the paper’s single-dictator result is the case’s weakest—and likely decisive—point.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper offers no computational result to mirror. Theorem \(\ref{thm:optimaldictatorship}\) establishes an equilibrium dominance claim; Lemma \(\ref{lem:dictatoreqm}\) characterizes equilibria; Proposition \(\ref{prop:dict_mostinfo}\) compares experiments by Blackwell informativeness. None states a complexity bound or gives an algorithmic problem. The paper also permits arbitrary convex information-cost functions, measurable voting spaces, decision rules, and experiments. To ask for running time, one would first need finite encodings for those objects and a specified output. That would define a new computational problem.

The proposed large-committee version preserves the theorem’s economic question: whether a voting rule dominates its competitors in equilibrium. It still asks for a dominance theorem, rather than an algorithm or complexity classification. Making the input a rational distribution over types does not supply the missing computational task. Restricting the mechanisms and cost functions enough to make one computable could yield useful work, but its conclusions would concern that newly specified mechanism-design problem.

There is also a structural obstacle to carrying over the theorem’s central object. In the finite model, one member’s vote can dictate the outcome, and the most-demanding member is selected by maximizing an individual threshold. In an atomless population governed by aggregate vote masses, an individual has zero mass and cannot dictate. A positive-mass type group could be made decisive, but that makes the group a strategic bloc; designating one member instead requires identity information that the type distribution omits. Either choice changes the strategic unit at the heart of the theorem.

The paper’s continuous distributions over beliefs and experiments do not create a collision: its committee still has finitely many individually identified members. They also do not provide evidence that the population itself has a useful high-multiplicity regime. The honest negative case is therefore strong within ChoCo’s computational remit: none of the proposed anchors yields a computational mirror without substantial new modeling. It cannot establish that every related continuous committee-design question lacks research value; a carefully specified population game might be worthwhile on its own.
