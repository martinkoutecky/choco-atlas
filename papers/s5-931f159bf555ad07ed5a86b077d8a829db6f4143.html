Open questions for a prover

  • For a specified sequence of finite POSGI markets with per-capita LMSR scaling, do their correlated-equilibrium distributions converge to a nonatomic obedience equilibrium?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that at every trading period of the authors’ POSGI prediction market, at least one correlated equilibrium exists. Its proof gives obedience inequalities over distributions on joint strategy profiles, and the paper uses those constraints in its CE calculation algorithm. This is an eligible anchor under the formulation allowance, but it is an existence result—not a numbered theorem that an equilibrium computation is in P. The paper has no named hardness or complexity-class result; its CECalc running-time claim appears separately in the algorithm discussion.

My lead mirror is POSGI-\(\infty\)-CE for one trading period. Take a binary-outcome market at a specified stage, with a current per-capita securities state and LMSR liquidity parameter \(\beta\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) trader types. A type records everything the stage game uses: the trader’s current belief and information-signal model, holdings, and available actions. In the paper’s small game the actions are buy one unit, hold, or sell one unit; retain that action set here. Types are risk-neutral to match Theorem 1. The instance also specifies the POSGI transition and per-capita reward functions, including the market-maker rule. For \(N\) traders, the natural scaling is to divide holdings and liquidity by \(N\), so the LMSR price remains defined as the population grows.

For each type \(t\), let \(\lambda_t(a)\) be the fraction of that type recommended action \(a\), with \(\sum_a\lambda_t(a)=1\). The aggregate order flow is \(z(\lambda)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_a\lambda_t(a)d_t(a)\), where \(d_t(a)\) is the action’s share change. A mediator draws a distribution \(\nu\) over these mass splits and privately recommends an action to each trader. Write \(U_t(a;z)\) for type \(t\)’s expected stage reward from action \(a\), given the realized aggregate flow \(z\). The question is: find a probability law \(\nu\) over mass splits such that, for every type \(t\) and every pair of actions \(a,a'\),
\[ \int \mu_t\lambda_t(a) \bigl[U_t(a;z(\lambda))-U_t(a';z(\lambda))\bigr]\,d\nu(\lambda)\ge 0. \]
A solution is such a law satisfying every obedience inequality. It can be represented with finite support: if the reward functions are continuous, the obedience integrals give finitely many moments, so a feasible law has a finite-support witness. The task’s objective is feasibility—find any correlated recommendation law. Theorem 1 does not optimize social welfare; selecting a Pareto-favored equilibrium would be an additional question.

The regime is a large internal or public forecasting market for a binary event: many employees or platform users trade, while a relatively small set of exact profiles recurs—people with the same belief, information channel, holdings, and action constraints. Thus \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). This is not a claim that traders are interchangeable despite different private information; that information is part of their type. The market’s binary security remains indivisible for each trader. What becomes continuous is the population and the share of each type taking each action.

I expect the equilibrium-existence question to remain on the tractable side: it is the continuum version of a finite-action obedience problem, and the paper’s theorem already supplies the finite-game existence intuition. That does not establish a polynomial-time exact algorithm for the continuous search problem. The finite-support witness is not itself an algorithm: finding its support points involves continuous aggregate flows and the LMSR reward functions. Useful follow-ups are whether those flows admit an efficient separation or optimization method, how exact computation compares with an \(\varepsilon\)-CE algorithm, and whether finite-market CE outcomes converge to this mirror as \(N\) grows.

This mirror covers only Theorem 1’s per-stage, risk-neutral CE result. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s full multiday dynamics, its risk-averse Proposition 1, or its simulation claims about prices and utilities. The other named result I would not use as an anchor: Proposition 1 makes a separate incentive-compatibility claim, and adding it would broaden the case without improving its foundation.

The weakest point is that the atomless limit removes an individual trader’s ability to move the aggregate price. The paper’s finite-agent CE coordinates strategically consequential actions; this mirror approaches a price-taking population equilibrium. That is a real change in strategic force. The case survives because it retains the paper’s stage, private-information types, action choices, LMSR rewards, and obedience question, while changing the population regime the programme is meant to study. But the authors might reasonably regard convergence of their strategic CE to this price-taking limit as a new question, rather than as a direct replacement for their finite-agent theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 does not establish a computational result. It establishes that a correlated equilibrium exists at each stage of a finite game, a fact that applies to finite normal-form games generally. The paper’s separate claim that CECalc runs in \(N|\Phi_i|^2\) time is not a numbered complexity result, and it does not give a bit-complexity bound in terms of the game’s input: the joint strategy space, utility data, and their encodings are absent from that accounting. So the proposed task “find a law \(\nu\)” adds a computational question to an existence theorem; the paper does not supply the computational result that ChoCo is meant to continuize.

The strongest plausible population regime is a large market with many traders sharing a small set of beliefs, information models, holdings, and risk preferences. That is a coherent high-multiplicity setting. But if LMSR liquidity and market state scale with \(N\) to preserve per-capita prices, one trader’s action changes the aggregate price by \(O(1/N)\). The limiting obedience condition is therefore price-taking: an individual’s deviation no longer has the strategic market impact present in the finite game. If actions retain market-scale impact, the strategic actor has become a positive-mass cohort acting together, which changes the paper’s individual recommendation problem into a group-deviation problem.

There is a related change in the proposed device. The paper treats the market price as a recommendation signal. A law over mass splits with private recommendations gives a mediator direct control over assignments across traders. Restricting the mirror to the paper’s price signal yields a price-taking population response problem; keeping the unrestricted \(\nu\) introduces a different mechanism. Either could motivate new work, but neither follows from Theorem 1’s computational content.

One could study whether finite-market equilibria converge to a population equilibrium, or analyze the complexity of finding a correlated equilibrium in a specified finite-type market model. Those are plausible research questions. The negative case is strongest at ChoCo’s source gate: the paper offers no named computational result for them to mirror. The broader claim that no such population-market question could ever be worthwhile is harder to defend; the concession is that a useful extension may exist, but it would be a new computational project rather than a continuization of a named result in this paper.
