What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 characterizes truncation-proofness and Theorem 2 characterizes lower-tail invariance; neither establishes computational complexity. The opponent's coalition question would add a new strategic problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Theorem 1, proved here, says that under unilateral substitutes and irrelevance of rejected contracts, the cumulative offer process (COP) is the unique stable, truncation-proof mechanism. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that under those conditions it is the unique stable mechanism invariant to changes in doctors’ lower-tail preferences. These are axiomatic characterizations; neither asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithmic result.

A continuum model of doctor and hospital types might be worth studying, but using it to restate these theorems would continuize an axiomatic question, while the programme asks for computational mirrors. I therefore have no qualifying anchor and no continuous problem per anchor to propose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The absence of a complexity theorem rules out a literal computational mirror of the paper’s two theorems. It does not rule out a worthwhile computational question anchored in Theorem 1, which identifies the COP as the unique stable, truncation-proof mechanism under unilateral substitutes and irrelevance of rejected contracts.

A natural high-multiplicity version has rational masses of doctors with finitely many complete preference types, facing hospitals whose capacities scale with the cohort and whose choices over contract-type masses have an explicit representation. The computational question is: what is the minimum mass of doctors who must jointly truncate their preference lists for a positive mass of them to improve under the resulting mass-COP, while no member of the coalition is worse off? The input can split a type’s mass among its possible truncated reports; the output remains a mass allocation, with each unit of mass receiving an integral contract. A single doctor has zero mass in this model, so the relevant strategic quantity is the size of a coordinated deviation. That makes the truncation theme computational and population-level, rather than a vacuous continuum restatement of individual truncation-proofness.

This regime is plausible for large admissions or hiring cohorts with recurring preference and eligibility profiles. It also has a close analytic precedent: Che, Kim, and Kojima extend their continuum matching model to contracts and explicitly leave efficient computation of stable matchings as an open research direction. That supports the model while leaving the computational gap open. Typed stable-matching work likewise treats groups of similar agents as a meaningful algorithmic regime, and complexity results for substitutable choice functions show that the choice-function representation can itself be a substantial computational issue. [Che, Kim & Kojima](https://www.columbia.edu/~yc2271/files/papers/Continuum-submission.pdf), [Meeks & Rastegari](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109), [Deng, Panigrahi & Waggoner](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/10595).

The case has a real limitation: this is a derivative computational problem, not the continuous counterpart of a complexity result stated in the paper. Its strength depends on accepting coordinated truncation control as a problem the authors would recognize. But Theorem 1 supplies a specific mechanism and strategic concern, and the continuous matching literature points to an unresolved computational task. That is enough to reject the claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
