The continuous mirror question

Given bases \(B\), a fleet size \(M\), a horizon \(H\), a relocation limit \(r\), a coverage threshold \(f\), and supported zone types \(t=(A_t,\zeta_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), decide whether there is a sequence of integer placements \(x^s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{B}\), \(s=1,\ldots,H\), with \(\sum_{b\in B}x_b^s\le M\), such that each round covers mass at least \(f\), consecutive placements satisfy \(\sum_{b\in B}|x_b^{s+1}-x_b^s|\le 2r\), and the spread of average type coverage is at most a given \(\delta\). Type \(t\) is covered in round \(s\) when \(\sum_{b\in A_t}x_b^s\ge\zeta_t\); its average coverage is \(y_t=H^{-1}\sum_s\mathbf{1}[\sum_{b\in A_t}x_b^s\ge\zeta_t]\), and the spread is \(\max_t y_t-\min_t y_t\).

The model it lives in

The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete service-zone profiles \(t=(A_t,\zeta_t)\). Ambulance placements remain integral; the schedule must meet a mass-weighted coverage threshold each round, obey the paper’s relocation bound, and minimize the maximum-minus-minimum average coverage over supported types.

The objection that survived

Theorem 22’s graph depends only on configuration copies and placement distances; type masses affect which configurations qualify, but the proponent does not show that this creates a new complexity boundary or algorithmic opportunity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers ambulance fairness scheduling with relocation and Theorem 22’s feasibility characterization; it leaves the general framework theorem and the special-case horizon bounds for simplicial and sparse allocations without mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the \(\delta\)-decision problem in the number of bases, support size, fleet size, horizon, and mass bit length?
  • Does bounded support yield a polynomial-time or fixed-parameter algorithm, or does configuration sequencing remain hard even for a small number of supported types?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is an important limit on the anchors: the paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as being in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest computational anchor is Theorem 22, proved in the paper and used in the ambulance algorithm as a feasibility characterization. I rely on it as a structural computational result, not as a complexity classification.

My lead mirror is a continuous version of the paper’s transition-constrained ambulance fairness problem. Consider a large region partitioned into many equal-sized service zones, served by a common fleet at a fixed set \(B\) of bases. A zone’s complete type is \(t=(A_t,\zeta_t)\), where \(A_t\subseteq B\) is the set of bases from which an ambulance can reach it within the response threshold, and \(\zeta_t\) is its demand threshold. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the supported types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of zones of type \(t\). This regime is plausible when many microzones share a modest number of reachability-and-demand profiles, so the number of zones is much larger than the support size \(\tau\).

Continuous-Type Ambulance Fairness with Relocation. An instance consists of \(B\), a fleet size \(M\), supported types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a horizon \(H\), a minimum covered mass \(f\), and a relocation limit \(r\). In each round \(s\), choose integer ambulance counts \(x^s_b\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{b\in B}x^s_b\le M\). Type \(t\) is covered in that round when
\[ \sum_{b\in A_t}x^s_b\ge \zeta_t. \]
Require at least mass \(f\) to be covered in every round, and require \(\sum_{b\in B}|x^{s+1}_b-x^s_b|\le 2r\) between consecutive rounds. The average benefit of type \(t\) is \(y_t=H^{-1}\sum_s \mathbf{1}[\sum_{b\in A_t}x^s_b\ge\zeta_t]\). The task is to find a feasible schedule minimizing \(\max_{t\in T}y_t-\min_{t\in T}y_t\). A decision version asks whether a schedule with spread at most a supplied \(\delta\) exists.

This preserves the paper’s binary coverage benefit, fairness range, per-round efficiency requirement, and relocation rule. Type masses turn “at least a fraction \(f\) of zones” into the direct condition \(\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[\text{type }t\text{ is covered}]\ge f\). For fairness, the maximum and minimum are over supported types: every zone within one type receives the same benefit history, just as identical stakeholders do in the finite model. Any finite zoning instance maps to this model by setting \(\mu_t\) to the fraction of zones of type \(t\); rational masses also correspond to finite populations after clearing denominators. This is a genuine high-multiplicity extension, not continuity of the outcome or time space.

Theorem 22 is the anchor. In full, it says that for a point \(q\) feasible for the paper’s master problem, form the configuration graph by making one vertex for each copy of each selected configuration, and joining two vertices when their corresponding ambulance allocations differ in \(L_1\) distance by at most \(2r\). If this graph has a Hamiltonian path, the configurations can be ordered into a schedule satisfying the transition bound. Conversely, if a feasible schedule uses only configurations indexed by \(I=\{j:q_j\ge1\}\), the corresponding graph has a Hamiltonian path. The result is proved in the paper. Its statement calls the scheduled object “AWT,” although that label was earlier used for the model without transition constraints; the graph and proof concern the transition condition.

The type-based mirror retains this structural question exactly: each configuration has a type-coverage vector, while the transition graph is still built from the base allocations themselves. Theorem 22 therefore gives the mirror a concrete computational core. It does not establish NP-hardness of the full fair-scheduling problem, and the paper does not prove that classification.

I expect the general mirror’s difficulty to be Class B if the discrete transition problem is hard: a finite zoning instance embeds as a rational distribution, and the sequencing condition remains after aggregating identical types. The visible combinatorics lie in ordering configurations across rounds, not in the number of repeated zones. Theorem 22 makes a Hamiltonian-path source of difficulty credible, though it does not prove that the ambulance configuration graphs are general enough to yield hardness. Whether small support \(\tau\) or a fixed number of bases enables an efficient algorithm is open; so are the complexity of the \(\delta\)-decision version and whether column generation can exploit the compressed type profiles.

The mirror covers the ambulance location-and-relocation result and its transition-feasibility characterization. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s general T-period fairness theory or its special-case horizon bounds. Its weakest point is the scenario: real zones often have distinct geography, and the paper’s fairness objective treats zones equally rather than weighting each zone by resident population. The case holds when service zones are the stakeholders and many share the same complete coverage-and-demand profile; if that profile support is large or the authors reject such zones as interchangeable, the high-multiplicity rationale weakens.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with the anchor: the paper has no named theorem classifying a problem’s complexity. Theorem 22 is its closest computational result, but it characterizes when a fixed multiset of ambulance configurations can be ordered into a schedule satisfying the relocation limit.

That characterization is population-blind. Its graph has one vertex per selected configuration copy, and an edge depends only on the \(L_1\) distance between ambulance placements. It contains no zone count, benefit, or type mass. Replacing zones with types leaves the graph and Theorem 22 unchanged. So the theorem does not itself anchor a question whose computational content comes from continuizing the population. A weighted type model may pose a new fairness-optimization question, but that is a different anchor.

The strongest version of that new model is coherent: group zones with the same reachability set and demand threshold, give each type a rational mass, and require a mass fraction to be covered. Yet the paper’s existing formulation appears to absorb this change directly. Identical type rows in the fairness constraints can be collapsed; the coverage constraint becomes a weighted sum; the ambulance configurations and their relocation graph stay the same. The paper already uses column generation for configurations and constraint programming for feasible schedules. Aggregating repeated profiles may be useful preprocessing, but the paper gives no reason to expect it to create a new complexity boundary or algorithmic opportunity.

That is the limit of the negative case. Repeated microzones with shared profiles could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and a complexity study in support size and rational mass could still be worthwhile. I can argue that Theorem 22 is a weak anchor and that the likely mirror mostly compresses the existing model. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror.
