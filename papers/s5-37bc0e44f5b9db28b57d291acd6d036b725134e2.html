What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror is anchored in the paper's named results: Theorems 2 and 3 give axiomatic characterizations, Theorems 5 and 6 concern equilibrium payoffs, and Theorem 4 gives a recurrence without a computational guarantee.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is a society made of many repeated collaboration cells. Each voter type would be a person’s complete role in a cell: their position in its network, links, exogenous weight, and relevant game data. The mass of a type would be its fraction of people across the cells. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for recurring teams, and it makes population mass the object of interest.

But the paper gives no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: none of its numbered results establishes an algorithmic running time, tractability, or hardness. The closest candidate is Theorem 4, proved here. It gives a recursion for the weighted position value:
\[ Y^{w}_i(g,v)=\frac{1}{|g|}\left[\sum_{l\in g_i}w_l^i\bigl(v(g)-v(g\backslash l)\bigr)+\sum_{l\in g}Y^{w}_i(g\backslash l,v)\right]. \]
That is a computationally shaped identity, but the paper gives it no complexity bound; evaluating the recursion naively can involve exponentially many subnetworks. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3, both proved here, are axiomatic characterizations. Theorems 5 and 6, also proved here, concern existence and payoff outcomes of sub-game perfect equilibria, not the complexity of finding them. Theorem 1 is cited from elsewhere and characterizes the unweighted position value.

An unanchored continuous question could be: given a finite set of network-component templates \(q=(H_q,v_q,w^q)\) with rational masses \(\mu_q\), suppose the population consists of many copies of these components. For each type \(t\), defined by a complete player role within a template, compute its average weighted-position allocation
\[ a_t= \frac{\sum_q \mu_q\sum_{i:\,\operatorname{type}_q(i)=t}Y_i^{w^q}(H_q,v_q)} {\sum_q \mu_q\,|\{i:\,\operatorname{type}_q(i)=t\}|}. \]
The solution is the exact allocation vector \((a_t)_t\). This has a credible regime—many repeated teams and relatively few distinct roles—but it does not provide a valid anchor for this paper. With arbitrary templates, the network’s combinatorics remain intact, so population mass alone gives no reason to expect hardness to disappear. Restricting templates enough to guarantee easy computation risks simplifying away the problem the authors study.

So the repeated-team setting is the best plausible population mirror I see, but it is weak as a case for the programme: it is an unanchored extension, and the paper supplies no named computational result from which to formulate the required prover question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

No anchor survives. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 characterize an allocation rule axiomatically; Theorems 5 and 6 characterize equilibrium payoffs. Theorem 4 gives a recursion, but makes no algorithmic or complexity claim. It specifies neither an input representation nor a running time, and its recurrence ranges over subnetworks. So the paper contains no named computational result from which to pose the requested complexity mirror.

The repeated-collaboration proposal is a coherent new aggregation question, but it is not anchored in a result here. If a type records the complete component and player role, each copy receives the allocation already determined for that component; averaging across copies is a population-level summary added to the paper’s output. If types omit component details that affect allocations, the proposed average merges players who are not interchangeable under the paper’s model. Refining the types restores that information, leaving a mixture of independently specified network games.

A large connected population with a graphon-like limit is a stronger possible direction. It would need a family of finite network games, a scaling rule for the value function on subnetworks, and a definition of limiting weighted-position allocations. The paper supplies none of these, and its results establish no computational claim to carry into that model. That could become a worthwhile continuous network-allocation project; it would be a new project rather than a mirror of this paper.

The negative case is strongest under ChoCo’s paper-mirror standard: this paper offers no computational result to mirror, and the proposed team average does not supply one. I cannot rule out every useful continuum model for network allocation; the evidence here supports excluding this paper as a continuous-mirror candidate.
