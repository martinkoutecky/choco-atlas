The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a cohort version of the paper’s farsighted matching problem. There is a real computational result to build from: Algorithm 2 computes the unique farsightedly stable matching in \(O(n^2)\) time, linear in the size of the preference-list input. Its correctness and running time are argued in the paper’s text.

There is a qualification: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that under Assumptions 1 and 2 every boy can ensure a partner no worse than his Gale–Shapley partner. Theorem 2, also proved there, says that a boy who was the sole proposer to a girl in Gale–Shapley cannot farsightedly get a strictly better partner. Neither is a complexity result. The authors say the results in Section III had appeared in their 2016 preprint and acknowledge Wako’s independent prior discovery of essentially the same results. So Algorithm 2 is my lead computational anchor, but it does not meet your specified theorem-family anchor rule.

The concrete mirror I would investigate is Cohort Farsighted Matching. An instance has finite proposer types \(P\) and acceptor types \(Q\), with rational masses \(\mu_p\) and \(\nu_q\) summing to one on each side. A type records the complete preferences and other problem-relevant attributes; all agents within it are indistinguishable. Each proposer type has a strict ranking over acceptor types, and each acceptor type has a strict ranking over proposer types. A candidate outcome is a matrix \(x_{pq}\geq 0\), where \(x_{pq}\) is the mass of type \(p\) matched to type \(q\), with row sums \(\mu_p\) and column sums \(\nu_q\).

The task is to find an outcome with no allowed farsighted improvement. To define one, let positive masses of proposers make a finite sequence of aggregate proposals. Acceptors respond mechanically, retaining the most-preferred available proposal mass up to their capacity and rejecting the rest. The sequence is admissible only if the proposing mass ends strictly better off than before and every proposer ends matched. An outcome is stable if no such sequence can strictly improve positive proposer mass. This is a feasibility and outcome-selection problem, not a welfare-maximization problem. It keeps the paper’s proposal game and no-self-harm condition while allowing each type’s mass to split among partners.

A plausible regime is a large, recurring placement market: many applicants and positions, but relatively few preference and priority profiles, such as annual cohorts of graduates and job openings. The relevant comparison is millions of agents against a much smaller number \(\tau=|P|+|Q|\) of complete types. I would expect the aggregate problem to be tractable, perhaps by extending the paper’s elimination procedure to rational masses. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Algorithm 2. The main follow-up questions are whether an outcome always exists and is unique, whether it can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length, and when it agrees with the limit of finite markets formed by replicating the types.

This mirror covers the farsightedly stable matching and its comparison with Gale–Shapley. I would leave the paper’s top-trading-cycles example, preference misrepresentation, and threats outside this case.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper ranks named individuals in strict preference lists, while this mirror ranks types and lets identical agents share a type, effectively making members of a type interchangeable. The mass-level proposal dynamics also need careful formalization; splitting a cohort may create deviations that have no exact one-to-one counterpart. Those changes are plausible for a large placement market, but they are the reason I would present this as a candidate mirror of Algorithm 2, not as an established continuous analogue of a numbered complexity theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Algorithm 2’s key operation has no direct type-level counterpart. The algorithm finds a particular proposer who was the sole proposer to a girl, fixes that pair, removes both named agents, and repeats. Theorems 1 and 2 also quantify over individual boys and rely on chains of named partners. In a population distribution, a singleton has zero mass: its guarantee and its status as a “hopeless man” disappear. Replacing that singleton with a positive-mass block changes the claims the results make.

The proposed cohort model is plausible, but it changes what must be represented. Under the paper’s strict rankings, an acceptor’s ordering over named proposers can distinguish people who share the same preferences. Exact types must preserve every distinction that affects acceptance, so support size can grow with the market. Grouping applicants into preference and priority tiers avoids that growth, but it introduces ties within cohorts and capacity-based acceptance. The matrix \(x\) records final mass assignments; it does not by itself encode the individual proposal order that drives the paper’s displacement chains. A mass-level farsighted game could be defined, but its coalition moves and acceptance rules would be new model choices, rather than a straightforward lift of Algorithm 2.

The strongest version of the mirror would use a recurring placement market with a small number of applicant and position tiers, then ask whether a unique farsightedly stable assignment of masses exists and can be computed. That is coherent, and work on type-based matching supports the plausibility of such regimes. [Continuum matching models](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/) also show that large-market assignments can be studied through aggregate quantities. Those are reasons to pursue the model; they do not establish that the paper’s singleton guarantees, uniqueness argument, or elimination algorithm survive the change. The established continuum models concern ordinary matching stability, while the proposed question adds a particular farsighted coalition process.

So the negative case defeats the claimed continuity of the paper’s anchors: Theorem 1’s individual guarantee, Theorem 2’s sole-proposer witness, and Algorithm 2’s one-by-one elimination all depend on named agents. It does not defeat every possible continuous research question inspired by the paper. I can’t honestly sustain the universal claim that no cohort-based farsighted matching problem is worth studying; the more defensible conclusion is that this would be a new computational model whose connection to these results remains to be shown.
