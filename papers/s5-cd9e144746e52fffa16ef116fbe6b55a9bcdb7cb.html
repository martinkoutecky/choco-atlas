The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a finite-support contextual Fisher equilibrium. The regime could be a large course-allocation market: millions of students, but only \(\tau\) exact preference-and-budget profiles, with \(m\) courses. A type includes everything that affects its utility or budget. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students with that type; course supplies are expressed per student. The decision is a bundle \(x_t\) for each type and a price \(p_j\) for each course. Type-level bundles may be fractional even if individual students receive whole seats, as the programme allows.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, cited from Gao and Kroer [30], not proved in this paper. It says that for concave, continuous, non-negative, degree-one homogeneous utilities with \(u_i(\mathbf{1})>0\), the Eisenberg–Gale program—maximizing \(\sum_i B_i\log u_i(x_i)\) subject to \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le Y_j\) and \(x_i\ge0\)—has an optimum. Any optimum, together with optimal Lagrange multipliers \(p\ge0\), gives a market equilibrium, up to arbitrary assignment of zero-price goods; each buyer spends their budget, so \(\langle p,x_i\rangle=B_i\). The paper computes with this formulation to approximate equilibria. It states no named hardness or polynomial-time classification, so this is a formulation anchor, not a complexity theorem.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Finite-Support Contextual Fisher Equilibrium, \(\varepsilon\)-approximation. An instance has \(m\) goods with positive rational per-capita supplies \(y_j\), and \(\tau\) buyer types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), budgets \(B_t\), and rational linear utilities \(u_t(z)=\sum_j v_{tj}z_j\), with \(v_{tj}>0\). Given rational \(\varepsilon>0\), output rational bundles \(x_{tj}\ge0\) and prices \(p_j\ge0\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=y_j\), \(\sum_j p_jy_j=\sum_t\mu_tB_t\), and the Nash Gap is at most \(\varepsilon\). Here the Nash Gap is the mass-weighted log welfare buyers can attain at prices \(p\), minus the mass-weighted log welfare delivered by \(x\). The paper’s Theorem 2, proved here, establishes that under market clearance and the price constraint this gap is non-negative and is zero exactly at equilibrium. That makes it a useful certificate for the approximation task, though I do not count it as a second complexity anchor.

I expect this problem to be Class A for approximation: the type masses reduce the population to \(\tau\) bundle variables, and the EG objective gives a convex-optimization route whose scale depends on \(m,\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\varepsilon\), rather than the number of students. This case covers the paper’s equilibrium-computation target for finite-support populations and CCNH utilities, with linear utilities as a crisp input class. Further questions include whether the approximation guarantee is polynomial under standard conditioning assumptions, how the result changes for richer utility descriptions, and how sampling error in the type masses affects the returned equilibrium.

The paper gives no hardness result to anchor a claim that population continuization dissolves discrete hardness. Its strongest support is that this high-multiplicity regime is plausible and that the equilibrium computation compresses naturally by type. The authors would plainly recognize the model: their Section 4.1 rewrites the objective and market-clearance constraints using expectations over buyers, says the buyer count disappears, and explicitly allows buyers to be infinite or distributed according to \(F\in\Delta(B)\).

That recognition is also the case’s weakest point under ChoCo’s novelty test. The paper already formulates a continuous-population market, so this proposed mirror may be an extraction of a complexity question from their existing model rather than a new continuization. The honest positive claim is that their work supplies an unusually direct, author-recognizable continuous problem whose finite-support complexity could be charted. The claim that continuization itself newly introduces the population as a continuous object would not hold.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a mirror is strongest on novelty, not on whether the proposed market makes sense. The course-allocation example is a credible high-multiplicity regime: buyer types can include all relevant preferences and budgets, and per-capita supplies give a coherent mass model. I would not object that bundles or outcomes are fractional.

Theorem 1 is a poor anchor for a ChoCo mirror. It is an equilibrium characterization cited from Gao and Kroer, not a computational result established by this paper: it gives existence and connects optima of the Eisenberg–Gale program to equilibrium, but says nothing about worst-case complexity or an algorithm’s running time. The paper’s own Theorem 2 certifies the Nash Gap; it also does not classify a computational problem. And the paper’s actual computational contribution already takes buyers to be sampled from a distribution, allows the infinite-buyer case, and proposes and evaluates an approximation method there. Thus the central population-continuous computation is already the paper’s subject.

The finite-support, rational-input \(\varepsilon\)-problem is a sensible formalization, but that makes it a complexity follow-up to the paper’s continuous model, not a mirror that newly continuizes one of its results. A worst-case guarantee could be valuable; it would sharpen what the paper’s empirical method leaves open. But the paper supplies no discrete computational result whose fate under continuization that guarantee would chart. Calling that follow-up a mirror stretches the programme’s target.

The cited infinite-dimensional Fisher-market work does not independently establish that buyer-population continuity is already solved: its core model has finitely many buyers and a continuum of items, so it continuizes the other axis. [Gao and Kroer, *Infinite-Dimensional Fisher Markets and Tractable Fair Division*](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2022.2344). Using it as a collision would confuse outcome-space continuity with population continuity.

So I cannot honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile continuous question exists in any scenario. The proposed model is sound, and formal complexity guarantees could matter. The narrower negative is that this paper offers no discrete computational result to mirror, while its own main computational problem is already framed for a continuous population. The proponent’s anchor is therefore a plausible research direction, but a weak fit for the programme’s continuization brief.
