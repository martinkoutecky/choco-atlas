The continuous mirror question

Given finite states and signals, rational masses (\mu_t)_{t\in T}, and rational type data specifying priors, signal likelihoods, self-reliance weights, and interactions \(a_{tu}\), decide whether the role-based continuum update makes the population learn the true state. Each type \(t\) has a belief distribution \(B_{t,k}\) after \(k\) rounds; for every (\varepsilon>0), does (\sum_t \mu_t B_{t,k}(\{b:b(\theta^*)\ge 1-\varepsilon\})\to 1 as \(k\to\infty\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic sensor population with finitely many complete roles \(t\in T\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\). The belief law \(B_{t,k}\) evolves by private Bayesian updates followed by mixing with type-level mean beliefs \(\bar b_{u,k}\), using the role’s fixed weights; the target is asymptotic concentration on \(\theta^*\).

The objection that survived

The mirror covers the learning guarantee in Corollary 7 and the posterior-elimination result in Theorem 6 for role-structured populations; it leaves arbitrary local-network topologies and the cited one-step forecast result aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper suggests a plausible high-multiplicity social-learning problem. Its sensor-network motivation gives a credible regime: many sensors but relatively few roles, with a role specifying the sensor’s signal likelihood, initial prior, self-reliance, and pattern of influence on other roles. The population is described by masses \(\mu_t\) over those roles, with the number of sensors much larger than the number of types.

One precise continuous question would be Continuum Asymptotic Learning. Each type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\) and a distribution \(B_{t,k}\) of individual beliefs after \(k\) rounds. A type-\(t\) agent with belief \(b\) receives a private signal \(s\), forms the Bayesian posterior
\[ b^s(\theta)=\frac{b(\theta)\ell_t(s\mid\theta)} {\sum_{\theta'}b(\theta')\ell_t(s\mid\theta')}, \]
then mixes it with type-level average beliefs:
\[ b'(\theta)=\lambda_t b^s(\theta)+\sum_u a_{tu}\bar b_{u,k}(\theta), \qquad \lambda_t+\sum_u a_{tu}=1. \]
Here \(\bar b_{u,k}\) is the mean belief among type \(u\), and individual agents retain their own signal draws. Given rational masses and parameters, finite signal and state sets, and a true state \(\theta^*\), ask whether, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[ \sum_t\mu_t\,B_{t,k}\bigl(\{b:b(\theta^*)\ge 1-\varepsilon\}\bigr)\longrightarrow 1. \]
That is a recognizable population-continuum analogue of the paper’s learning objective. The authors might accept it as a natural extension, especially given their sensor-network application. A computational study could ask how to decide or certify this property from a finite description of the types and their interactions. The paper itself does not settle that computational question.

That gap matters for the requested anchors: the paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification. The closest result is Theorem 6, proved here: under Assumption 1 (strong connectivity, positive self-reliances, and positive prior probability on the true state for some agent), every agent’s belief on each state outside that agent’s observational-equivalence set converges to zero almost surely. Corollary 7, also proved here, adds distinguishability and concludes that every agent’s belief in the true state converges to one almost surely. Theorem 1 is a cited result from Jadbabaie, Sandroni, and Tahbaz-Salehi establishing convergence of one-step forecasts under Assumption 1. These are convergence theorems, not complexity results. The paper’s description of a “computationally tractable algorithm” does not supply a numbered complexity theorem.

So this is a defensible proposal for a continuous-population research question, but not an anchor-valid case under your criteria. Its weakest point is central: the proposed question turns a finite-network convergence result into a measure-valued learning model, while the paper gives no computational claim to mirror. It risks returning to the analytic social-learning tradition that ChoCo’s computational programme is meant to distinguish from.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Theorem 6 and Corollary 7 prove almost-sure convergence under stated assumptions; neither analyzes the complexity of a decision or optimization problem. Theorem 1 is also an analytic convergence result, and it is cited from earlier work. The paper’s description of a “computationally tractable algorithm” refers to agents carrying out local Bayesian updates and averaging. It supplies no input/output problem or complexity classification.

The proposed Continuum Asymptotic Learning question is coherent, but it creates that missing computational problem rather than continuizing one the paper studies. A stronger version could ask whether a finite description of a population model guarantees learning, or how to choose its parameters to ensure learning. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they add a verification or design objective absent from the paper’s results.

There is also a modeling cost. In the paper, an agent’s update depends on the beliefs of its particular neighbors. Grouping sensors by their signal likelihoods and self-reliance does not preserve that information: two otherwise identical sensors can have different neighborhoods. To make the proposed type-average update exact, one must impose a block-structured interaction network in which agents interact through role-level averages. That is a plausible sensor-network model, but it changes the paper’s local-network model. Preserving more general topology would require types to encode network position or an interaction kernel, which undermines the finite-role premise.

So the named anchors fail the paper’s computational-result test, and the proposed continuum model does not repair that gap without adding a new problem and, in its clean finite-type form, changing the network structure. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile project on block-structured continuum social learning; that remains the negative case’s weak point. But such a project would be an extension prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of its computational results.
