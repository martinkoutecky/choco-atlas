Theorem 1

PDF text-layer excerpt — equations and symbol order may be approximateLet there be an odd number $n=2m+1$ of binary independent classifiers, each classifier being correct with probability $\Pr(\hat{y}=y)=p>1/2$, where $y, \hat{y} \in\{-1,1\}$ are the true and predicted labels, respectively. Moreover, assume that the prior labels are equiprobable $\Pr(y=1)=\Pr(y=0)$. Let $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\hat{y}_n}$ be the sum of these $n$ classifier predictions, and let $\hat{y}_{\text{majority}}^n=\text{sign}(S_n)$ be a majority decision rule over the $n$ classifiers. Then, the latter majority rule is a Bayes classifier which minimizes the classification error.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(p\in\mathbb{Q}\) with \(p>1/2\) and a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over prediction types \(C=\{+,-\}\), choose \(y\in\{+,-\}\) maximizing \(\ell_\mu(y)=\sum_{v\in C}\mu_v\log\Pr(v\mid y)\), where \(\Pr(v\mid y)=p\) when \(v=y\) and \(1-p\) otherwise; return both labels in case of a tie.

The model it lives in

A population of classifiers with types given by their observed binary predictions and masses \(\mu_+\) and \(\mu_-\). Each classifier has the same reliability \(p>1/2\), and the decision variable is the predicted label \(y\). Choose the label maximizing per-capita log-likelihood under equal prior labels; equivalently, choose the sign of \(\mu_+-\mu_-\).

The objection that survived

A continuum of observed predictions has no literal finite-sample joint probability, so the mirror relies on interpreting normalized log-likelihood as a per-capita rate.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s Bayes-optimality result under equal prior labels and a shared error rate \(p\). It leaves Theorems 2 and 3’s finite-sample accuracy monotonicity and convergence claims aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: the paper has no named complexity classification—no result says a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. Its best algorithmic foothold is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for an odd number \(n=2m+1\) of independent binary classifiers, each correct with probability \(p>1/2\), with equally likely true labels, the majority prediction is a Bayes classifier that minimizes misclassification probability. The paper derives this by showing that the log-likelihood ratio has the same sign as the sum of the predictions.

A possible lead mirror is *Continuous Bayes Aggregation*. The alternatives are \(C=\{+,-\}\). A type is a classifier’s observed prediction \(v\in C\); the society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those two types. The regime is a large ensemble of weak classifiers with a shared symmetric error rate \(p\), where many classifiers produce only two possible predictions, so the number of classifiers is much larger than the support size \(\tau\le 2\). Given \(p\in\mathbb Q\), \(p>1/2\), and \(\mu\), choose the Bayes label maximizing the per-capita log-likelihood
\[ \ell_\mu(y)=\sum_{v\in C}\mu_v\log\Pr(v\mid y), \]
where \(\Pr(v\mid y)=p\) if \(v=y\), and \(1-p\) otherwise. The solution is a label in \(\arg\max_y\ell_\mu(y)\). Since
\[ \ell_\mu(+)-\ell_\mu(-)=(\mu_+-\mu_-)\log\frac{p}{1-p}, \]
the solution is the majority label; ties return both maximizers. Comparing the rational masses gives an exact algorithm polynomial in their bit length. I expect this mirror to be tractable.

This is recognizably Theorem 1’s likelihood-ratio aggregation with empirical counts replaced by population fractions. The per-capita objective is the natural high-multiplicity version of the finite-sample log-likelihood. A continuous society of millions of classifiers over two prediction types is plausible, though this is a particularly simple regime.

The scope is only Theorem 1’s Bayes-optimality result. Theorem 2’s monotonic improvement in finite-\(n\) accuracy and Theorem 3’s convergence of that accuracy to \(1\) are probability statements, not complexity results; in a deterministic continuum model, their uncertainty largely disappears. That is the weakest point: this mirror gives a small, tractable aggregation problem, not a substantive complexity question of the kind the programme targets. Theorem 1 supports a modest positive case for a continuous population analogue, but the paper offers no hardness anchor and no named complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper proves no computational result. Theorem 1 is a Bayes-optimality characterization; Theorem 2 proves a monotonicity property of a probability; Theorem 3 proves its limit. None defines a computational problem or studies its complexity. That is a categorical gap under ChoCo’s stated scope: there is no algorithmic result here whose complexity landscape the continuous model could chart.

The proposed *Continuous Bayes Aggregation* is mathematically coherent, and it is the best candidate. But it adds a task—given rational prediction masses, compute a Bayes label—that the paper does not pose as a computational problem. In the paper, the predictions are random observations from independent classifiers. Their empirical masses are a realized profile, not a distribution over stable classifier types. If type means a classifier’s behavior and reliability, those masses do not record the realized predictions needed for the likelihood calculation. If type means the observed prediction, the mirror is coherent, but it turns the theorem into a new argmax problem. The paper’s proof supplies the identity between that choice and majority voting; it does not supply a complexity result about computing it.

The other two theorems do not provide a stronger anchor. Their object is the accuracy probability over a finite sample of independent classifiers. Keep that sampling uncertainty and \(n\) remains a finite-sample parameter; take an exact population limit and the random fraction of correct classifiers is replaced by its expectation. The monotonicity and finite-\(n\) probability behavior then disappear into the limit. Studying the remaining sampling fluctuations would continuize the noise or the sample-size analysis, rather than make the society itself the computational object.

One could generalize the setup to many labels, heterogeneous classifier types, or an implicitly represented outcome space. That might yield worthwhile computational questions. But the extra structure would be doing the work: the paper’s binary, shared-\(p\) majority theorem neither poses nor motivates those harder problems. This negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational result, not on the claim that a continuous analogue would be easy or uninteresting. Its weakness is that ChoCo could deliberately admit a simple Bayes-choice problem as a new mirror; if so, the proposed two-type model is a plausible example, though a small one.
