The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), nonnegative score rates \(a_{ut}\), quota groups \(g:T\to G\), and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_g\), compute a quota-feasible selected mass vector \(x\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t r_t\), where \(r_t=\sum_{u\in T}\mu_u a_{ut}\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_t\le\kappa_g\). Can an anonymous randomized mechanism realize this optimum while each individual's inclusion probability remains unchanged when that individual's outgoing score row changes?

The model it lives in

An atomless population partitioned into finitely many types \(T\), with type masses \(\mu_t\), score rate \(a_{ut}\) from source type \(u\) to candidate type \(t\), and quota capacities \(\kappa_g\). The decision is selected mass \(x_t\) per type; maximize \(\sum_t x_t\sum_u\mu_u a_{ut}\) subject to the type supplies and quota constraints, and realize the chosen masses through anonymous selection. A single individual's row change leaves aggregate rates unchanged.

The objection that survived

An individual's row has measure zero, so impartiality no longer constrains the allocation; the proponent acknowledges this effect but does not show that the resulting problem retains meaningful strategic content.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a high-multiplicity limit of Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that, for any finite independence system and nonnegative weighted scores, the Partition mechanism is impartial and \(1/4\)-optimal: it randomly divides agents into \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), then selects a highest-scoring feasible subset of \(P_2\), scoring candidates only by nominations from \(P_1\). Its expected total score is at least one quarter of the best feasible set’s score. This is an approximation result, not a named hardness or complexity-class result; the paper has no such anchor.

A plausible regime is recurring peer review or grant selection with millions of applicants but a fixed, much smaller set of cohorts—for example, discipline and eligibility classes. Capacity scales with the applicant pool: each track can accept a fixed fraction of its applicants. People in a cohort share their eligibility and their score pattern across candidate cohorts. That is a restrictive but plausible model for reviews or endorsements organized around stable classes.

Here is a precise mirror. Let \(T\) be a finite set of types with masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one. Represent the population by an atomless space \(X\), partitioned into type classes \(X_t\) of mass \(\mu_t\). A source of type \(u\) gives each candidate of type \(t\) score intensity \(a_{ut}\ge 0\). Thus \(K(x,y)=a_{ut}\) when \(x\in X_u\) and \(y\in X_t\). Each type belongs to a quota group \(g(t)\), with capacity \(\kappa_g\). A selected set \(S\) must satisfy
\[ x_t=\mu(S\cap X_t)\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_t\le\kappa_g. \]
The selected units are still chosen integrally; atomlessness lets the mechanism select any required mass from a cohort. The population-normalized score is
\[ V(S)=\int_X\int_S K(x,y)\,d\mu(y)\,d\mu(x) =\sum_{t\in T}x_t r_t,\qquad r_t=\sum_{u\in T}\mu_u a_{ut}. \]

Call this Cohort-Quota Impartial Selection\(_\infty\): given \((T,\mu,A,g,\kappa)\), find the best universal approximation ratio \(\alpha\) attainable by a randomized selection mechanism that always returns a feasible set, guarantees \(\mathbb{E}[V(S)]\ge\alpha\max_{S'\text{ feasible}}V(S')\), and preserves each individual’s selection probability when that individual’s outgoing score row changes. The type support \(\tau=|T|\) is fixed or small compared with the population.

I expect this problem to be tractable with \(\alpha=1\). The type-level optimization is the linear program maximizing \(\sum_t r_t x_t\) subject to the supply and quota constraints; an optimal mass vector can be realized as a measurable set of selected people. A mechanism that samples the required mass within each type attains the optimum. Changing one person’s outgoing row changes the score kernel only on a measure-zero slice, leaving the aggregate score rates and that person’s selection probability unchanged. The paper’s Partition mechanism also has a direct continuum version with its \(1/4\) guarantee, but the exact optimizer improves on it here. The LP is polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of quota groups, and the input bit length.

The authors could recognize this as a population limit of their weighted impartial-selection problem: it keeps the nomination score objective, feasible selection, and individual-row impartiality, while scaling quotas with the population. The mirror covers Theorem 2’s selection guarantee in the partition-quota setting. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s upper bounds or its knapsack, matroid, and graphic-matroid mechanisms.

The weakest point is also central: in an atomless society, one person’s row has zero aggregate mass, so individual impartiality stops constraining the mechanism. The exact result shows how the strategic obstacle can disappear at the population limit; it does not show that the finite-agent difficulty disappears at any particular population size. A natural follow-up is to study the finite-size rate: with \(\tau\) fixed and quotas proportional to population, how quickly can the best exactly impartial finite mechanism approach the continuum optimum?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only anchor in the proponent’s case. It gives an impartial \(1/4\)-approximation for arbitrary independence systems, but it does not analyze the running time or complexity of computing that mechanism. The proposed mirror narrows the constraints to cohort quotas, where the main difficulty in Theorem 2 disappears.

The cohort model is plausible, and its limit is clear: an individual’s outgoing score row contributes zero mass, so changing that row cannot change the aggregate score rates or the optimal quota allocation. Impartiality therefore imposes no effective restriction. The mechanism can choose the optimal masses by solving the stated linear program, then realize them as subsets of the atomless cohorts. This is a valid limit statement, but its central finding is that individual impartiality loses force in an atomless population. It does not chart the computational consequences of impartial selection.

One might retain more of the paper’s structure by keeping the pairwise nomination data and a general independence system. That creates a representation problem. If nominations depend on named candidates, agents with different nomination patterns may need different types, making the support grow with the population. If nominations are replaced by type-to-type rates, as in the proposed kernel, the model compresses the pairwise data and again makes each individual’s report negligible. General feasibility constraints pose a similar issue: a small type-level description may not capture identity-dependent constraints such as graphic matroid cycles; a representation that does capture them may carry the original combinatorial optimization into the continuous problem.

The best repair is to make impartiality apply to a positive-mass cohort: changing a cohort’s nomination rates must not change that cohort’s selection mass. This restores strategic influence, but it changes the axiom from individual impartiality to group immunity. That could be an interesting problem in its own right; the paper’s results do not establish that it is the high-multiplicity version of their problem. Giving each agent positive mass would also preserve influence, while keeping the strategic problem finite; as those masses shrink, the individual effect again vanishes.

So the negative case is strongest against this particular computational mirror: the simple quota model turns Theorem 2 into ordinary cohort optimization, while richer models either lose compact type support or need a new incentive concept. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror. The peer-review setting makes repeated cohorts credible, and a study of how finite impartial mechanisms approach a nonatomic limit could be valuable. That finite-size question would need its own analysis; the atomless LP alone does not supply it.
