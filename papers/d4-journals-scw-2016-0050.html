Why no mirror

The proposed type-aggregate Banzhaf limit is a plausible high-multiplicity extension of the paper's inverse-design problem. However, none of the three named anchors is a qualifying computational result: Corollary 4.22 and Theorems 4.30 and 4.34 are structural or analytical impossibility results. Section 5's ILP discussion is not a numbered qualifying result, so bit (a) fails.

fails bit a — no named computational result to mirror

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers a weighted, type-aggregate Banzhaf inverse-design problem over a high-multiplicity society; it leaves the paper's other power indices, general simple and complete games, and ILP implementation without a qualifying continuous computational anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(\tau\) and a nondegenerate quota, does the aggregate limit \(\Pi_t\) exist and admit a closed form in terms of \(\mu\) and \(w\)?
  • Can the weighted continuous inverse-design problem be solved with a finite-dimensional exact or additive-approximation algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here, but it has an important qualification: the paper contains no named \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its exact ILP method is described in Section 5, and the conclusion informally says that the problem is “generally NP complete,” but that is not a numbered result proved in this paper. The strongest compliant interpretation is therefore to use its named approximation theorems as computational anchors.

The best mirror is a high-multiplicity inverse power-design problem. Let \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) be a fixed set of member types, where a type includes everything relevant to the voting rule: voting weight, eligibility, constitutional role, and desired power category. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of committee members of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is a large federation with \(M\) interchangeable delegates but only, say, \(10\)–\(30\) standardized mandate classes, so \(M\gg\tau\).

The decision variable is still the object studied by Kurz: a monotone binary voting rule. For a type-anonymous rule \(V\), write

\[ V:[0,1]^\tau\to\{0,1\}, \]

where \(x_t\) is the fraction of type \(t\) voting yes. This does not make individual votes divisible: each member still votes yes or no; only the aggregate of a repeated type is represented by a fraction. For a weighted rule, for example,

\[ V_{w,q}(x)=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t w_t x_t\ge q. \]

To define power faithfully, take a finite blow-up with \(q\mu_t\) members of type \(t\), apply the induced finite voting game \(v_q\), compute the paper’s normalized Banzhaf vector, and aggregate it by type:

\[ \Pi_t^{\mathrm{Bz}}(V,\mu) = \lim_{q\to\infty} \sum_{i\text{ of type }t} \widehat{\mathrm{Bz}}_i(v_q), \]

whenever the limit exists. Thus \(\Pi_t^{\mathrm{Bz}}\) is the total power assigned to the whole type, and \(\sum_t\Pi_t^{\mathrm{Bz}}=1\).

This gives the precise problem I would put to a prover:

\[ \textsc{Continuous Inverse Banzhaf Design}_\infty: \]

given rational \(\mu\), a target type-power vector \(\sigma\in\Delta_\tau\), and \(\delta\ge0\), decide whether there is an admissible type-anonymous voting rule \(V\) such that

\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau} \left| \Pi_t^{\mathrm{Bz}}(V,\mu)-\sigma_t \right| \le \delta. \]

The optimization version minimizes this distance and outputs the rule \(V\), or weights and quota in the weighted case. This is the population-continuized inverse power problem: the agents become mass, but the voting rule, binary decision, power index, and inverse-design objective remain intact.

My lead anchor is Corollary 4.22, proved in this paper. It states that for \(1<k<n\), a target vector

\[ \sigma=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k,0,\ldots,0) \]

and \(P\in\{\mathrm{Bz},\mathrm{ColPrev},\mathrm{ColIni},\mathrm{PGI},\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{Shift},\mathrm{SDP}\}\), over any of the classes \(V\in\{S,C,T\}\) of simple, complete, or weighted games,

\[ \|\widehat P(v)-\sigma\|_1 \ge \min\left\{ \frac{2}{f_2^P(k)}, \frac{2\Lambda}{f_2^P(k)+k f_1^P(k)+3} \right\} \]

for every \(v\in V_n\), where

\[ \Lambda= \min_{v'\in V_k} \|\sigma' - \widehat P(v')\|_1, \qquad \sigma'=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k). \]

For Banzhaf, the paper proves \(f_1(k)=1\) and \(f_2(k)=k+1\). In particular, for

\[ \sigma=\left(\frac34,\frac14,0,\ldots,0\right), \]

taking \(k=2\) gives \(\Lambda=\frac12\) and hence

\[ \|\widehat{\mathrm{Bz}}(v)-\sigma\|_1\ge\frac18 \]

for every simple game with \(n\ge3\).

The continuous question above mirrors this result by replacing named players with repeated member types and replacing the coordinate-wise power vector with aggregate power by type. The target \(\sigma\) can represent, for example, a constitutional requirement that two large mandate classes receive \(75\%\) and \(25\%\) of total influence. It is not an arbitrary relaxation: for every rational \(\mu\), finite committees with repeated types map exactly into the continuous instance by normalization, and the objective is precisely the finite \(\ell_1\)-objective after grouping identical members.

I expect the weighted, fixed-\(\tau\), additive-approximation version to be Class A. In a diffuse high-multiplicity regime, choosing per-member weights roughly as

\[ w_t=\frac{\sigma_t}{\mu_t} \]

makes the total constitutional weight of type \(t\) proportional to \(\sigma_t\). The limit results discussed in Section 6 suggest that the aggregate Banzhaf power should then approach the desired type distribution, with errors controlled by the number of types, the largest type weight, and the population size. This should lead to a finite-dimensional continuous optimization or approximation algorithm. The exact zero-error boundary, especially at tied quotas or concentrated “island” targets, remains an interesting further question.

The paper’s other named negative results reinforce the research value of this boundary. Theorem 4.30, proved here, says that for every \(p\neq\frac12\), no Alon–Edelman-type shortening theorem exists for \(p\)-binomial semivalues and ordinary \(k\)-rounding. Theorem 4.34 similarly says that no such result exists for the Johnston index under any shortening function. These are not standard complexity classifications, so I would not use them as separate anchors, but they suggest that different power indices may behave genuinely differently after continuization.

The weakest point is formal rather than substantive. If ChoCo’s anchor rule requires a numbered theorem explicitly asserting \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or a comparable complexity classification, this paper has no qualifying anchor and should receive no fully positive verdict. Even substantively, the continuous Banzhaf functional above is a necessary type-level co-modification: raw individual power vanishes in an atomless population, so the meaningful observable is aggregate power of a positive-mass type. The authors would likely recognize this as the high-multiplicity version of their inverse problem, but Corollary 4.22 does not itself prove that its lower bound or its exact ILP behavior survives the limit.

So the strongest honest case is: this paper supplies a very natural continuous-population inverse-power problem, with a plausible Class A weighted regime and a meaningful boundary for concentrated targets and alternative indices. It is a strong substantive mirror, but only a qualified formal ChoCo case because its named results are approximation theorems, not explicit complexity classifications.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s construction is a respectable new question, but it does not rescue this paper as a ChoCo mirror. The decisive problem is that the paper contains no qualifying computational theorem. Section 5 gives an ILP formulation; the statement that the problem is “generally NP complete” is informal and unproved here. The numbered results are approximation inequalities and impossibility results about one shortening argument, not complexity classifications, algorithms, or reductions.

Corollary 4.22 also does not survive the proposed high-multiplicity interpretation. Its target
\[ \left(\frac34,\frac14,0,\ldots,0\right) \]
concentrates power on two named players while all remaining players are intended to be null. In a \(q\)-fold population blow-up with two positive-mass types, there are \(\Theta(q)\) players in each of those types. A type-level target \((3/4,1/4)\) therefore distributes the first target coordinate over \(\Theta(q)\) individuals; it is not the target used by Corollary 4.22. The paper’s \(k\)-pure reduction removes individual players, whereas removing a type removes a positive-mass block of \(\Theta(q)\) players. The parameter \(\Lambda=\frac12\) from the two-player problem consequently has no reason to persist.

This distinction is not cosmetic. The finite lower bound is precisely about power concentrated on finitely many individuals. Under high multiplicity, those individuals either become positive-mass classes, in which case the concentration phenomenon disappears, or remain singleton types of vanishing mass, in which case the target is a singular zero-mass demand. Giving such singleton types unbounded voting weights can preserve their influence, but then the model is driven by distinguished constitutional actors, not by a continuous population. Dividing by type mass and studying “power density” would repair the vanishing, but that is a new normalization rather than the paper’s normalized power vector.

The proposed weighted construction exposes the problem rather than solving it. Taking \(w_t\) approximately proportional to \(\sigma_t/\mu_t\) is a natural high-multiplicity limit heuristic, already of the Penrose-limit kind discussed in Section 6. It may make aggregate type power approach the desired vector. But then the inverse problem has become a finite-dimensional design problem over a restricted weighted family, not the paper’s inverse problem over simple, complete, or weighted games. If all monotone type-anonymous rules are retained, the decision variable is an arbitrary function \(V:[0,1]^\tau\to\{0,1\}\), with no finite representation and no defined computational input model. If one restricts to weighted rules to regain finite encoding, one has introduced a new tractable model whose connection to Corollary 4.22 is only motivational.

There is also no canonical population limit for the paper’s general games. A finite simple game \(v:2^N\to\{0,1\}\) does not determine how coalitions of fractions of repeated types should behave. One must choose an extension \(V\) on every grid of population fractions. Different monotone extensions can agree on the original finite game and have different pivotality limits. Thus
\[ \lim_{q\to\infty}\sum_{i\text{ of type }t}\widehat{\mathrm{Bz}}_i(v_q) \]
is not an invariant of the paper’s input; it is an additional modelling choice. Retaining a fixed finite \(q\) avoids that issue, but then one has merely encoded a finite committee by rational multiplicities, not obtained a continuous computational object.

Theorems 4.30 and 4.34 do not provide independent anchors. Theorem 4.30 says that ordinary \(k\)-rounding fails for \(p\)-binomial semivalues when \(p\ne\frac12\); Theorem 4.34 says that an Alon–Edelman-style approximation theorem fails for the Johnston index under shortening functions. Neither result asserts hardness of inverse design, nonexistence of an algorithm, or even a lower bound for a continuous objective. Their natural population translations are questions about continuity of a chosen power functional under deleting low-mass types. Those are limit or sensitivity questions, not computational problems from the paper. Recasting them as inverse design would again create a new problem with no theorem in the paper to mirror.

The negative case is strongest at the anchor and object levels. A large federation with repeated delegate classes could certainly motivate a new study of aggregate voting power. I cannot honestly claim that such a study would be mathematically worthless. But it would be a new high-multiplicity theory of constitutional design, relying on a newly chosen extension and normalization, while the paper itself contributes no qualifying computational result to continuize. That is not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper under the programme’s standard.
