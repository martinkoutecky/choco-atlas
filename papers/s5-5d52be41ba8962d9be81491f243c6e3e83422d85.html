Why no mirror

Theorem 1 is a named computational result, but its \(\mu\) is a sampling law for a finite stream, not the mass distribution of a realized continuous population. Replacing indivisible arrivals with nonatomic mass changes the online coloring problem: mass can be split between the two signs, giving zero discrepancy. The proposed finite-size rate therefore preserves the original atomic problem rather than providing its continuous-population mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 1; it leaves the interval, stripe, and envy results and the lower bounds unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: I would mirror the tree-balancing result, not claim that the paper’s interval, stripe, and envy results all become continuous-population problems.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for Online Tree Balancing with \(n\) arrivals at leaves of a complete \(m\)-ary tree, if \(h\leq \log\log n/C\) for a sufficiently large constant \(C\) and \(m\geq100\), there is an efficient online algorithm whose maximum subtree imbalance is \(O(\log^2 n)\) with high probability. This is an algorithmic performance result, rather than a hardness classification; the paper supplies no comparable named computational-hardness result to anchor instead.

A plausible mirror is High-Multiplicity Stochastic Online Tree Balancing. Its instance is a complete \(m\)-ary tree of height \(h\), a horizon \(N\), and the uniform distribution \(\mu\) over its \(\tau=m^h\) leaves. Each of \(N\) arrivals independently draws a leaf from \(\mu\). Treat each arrival as mass \(1/N\), so the total population mass is one and the type distribution is \(\mu\). A type is a leaf: two arrivals of that type belong to exactly the same subtrees. After seeing an arrival’s leaf, an online policy irrevocably assigns it sign \(\sigma_i\in\{-1,+1\}\). For a node \(v\) and prefix \(k\), define its mass imbalance by
\[ D_v(k)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\leq k:\,\ell_i\text{ is below }v}\sigma_i. \]
The underlying problem is to find an online policy minimizing
\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{k\leq N,\ v\in T}|D_v(k)|\right]. \]
A solution is a causal policy; an algorithmic result may instead give such a policy with a specified high-probability bound.

The regime is many repeated arrivals from relatively few types. For example, with \(m=100\) and \(h=\Theta(\log\log N)\), the number of leaf types is polylogarithmic in \(N\), while \(N/\tau\) grows much faster. One credible setting is assigning a large, stochastic stream of requests across two channels while balancing every region in a hierarchical partition. Requests in the same leaf are identical for the balancing objective, but each request still receives an integral assignment. This preserves the paper’s defining online decision and gives type-level mass a clear meaning.

Theorem 1 supplies a Class A result for this uniform-distribution case: its policy yields maximum subtree mass imbalance \(O(\log^2 N/N)\) with high probability. The mirror question naturally extends to nonuniform rational \(\mu\), asking how the achievable bound and runtime depend on \(N\), support size \(\tau\), and the bit length of \(\mu\). The paper’s theorem does not settle that extension. The interval, stripe, and envy results are supporting context here, not additional anchors for this case.

The weakest point is that the theorem’s challenge depends on a finite sequence of random, indivisible arrivals. In a purely nonatomic, static society, type mass could be split evenly between the two signs and the discrepancy would vanish. Normalizing the theorem’s bound by population size also makes it tend to zero. So this is most convincing as a high-multiplicity online family, where the question is the finite-size discrepancy rate; it is less convincing if a continuous mirror must be a static optimization problem over mass transfers alone. The authors could recognize it as their tree-balancing problem in a repeated-type population regime, but the continuous-limit interpretation is doing less work than the retained online structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and the proposed normalization points to the main problem: \(D_v(k)\) is the integer imbalance of \(N\) arrivals divided by \(N\). The theorem’s \(O(\log^2 N/N)\) bound is therefore a finite-stream discrepancy bound expressed per capita.

In a genuinely nonatomic arrival stream, the policy can split each arriving parcel equally between \(+1\) and \(-1\). Every subtree then has zero signed mass at every prefix, without knowing anything about future arrivals. The same split makes interval and stripe discrepancy zero; for the envy application, splitting each arriving valuation type equally between the players gives zero envy. Online causality does not prevent any of these splits.

Keeping each arrival indivisible preserves the interesting problem, but also preserves \(N\) atomic agents. Then \(\mu\) specifies the law for sampling a finite stream; it does not describe a realized continuous population. The nonzero discrepancy comes from finite granularity and sampling, and the continuous limit has optimum zero. One could study the rate of convergence to that limit or extend the finite-stream result to nonuniform arrival laws. Those may be worthwhile questions about stochastic online algorithms, but they do not create a nontrivial continuous-population computational problem of the kind ChoCo is charting.

I grant that repeated-type online balancing is a sensible high-multiplicity problem; that fact supports its study in its own lane. The negative case is strongest against its being a useful continuous mirror for this programme, and weaker as a claim that the online problem itself lacks value.
