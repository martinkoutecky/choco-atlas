The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous-population model here, but the paper supplies no qualifying anchor: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered conclusions and formulas describe an allocation method and a case study; they are not named complexity results. So I cannot honestly claim that a particular result in this paper becomes easier or harder under continuization.

The strongest positive case is that the underlying allocation problem could sensibly have a high-multiplicity regime. Imagine many independent local water authorities, such as irrigation districts, with only a few distinct claim levels. A type records an authority’s claim \(c_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of authorities with that claim. The ten city-level jurisdictions in the paper are not themselves a high-multiplicity instance, but this larger regime preserves the paper’s resource-sharing problem and its bankruptcy-rule allocations.

If mirroring the paper’s computed procedure were allowed in place of a named anchor, I would propose Continuous Fair-and-Stable Bankruptcy Selection. An instance consists of rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational claims \(c_t\), and a per-authority water supply \(e<\sum_t\mu_t c_t\). Apply the paper’s seven bankruptcy rules to obtain each type’s per-authority award \(q_t^\phi\). For each alternative \(\phi\), set \(p_t^\phi=q_t^\phi/e\), compute its population Gini value
\[ G^\phi=\frac{\int\!\int |p_t^\phi-p_u^\phi|\,d\mu(t)d\mu(u)} {2\int p_t^\phi\,d\mu(t)}, \]
and compute stability from the population coefficient of variation of the normalized losses \(d_t^\phi=\max_\psi p_t^\psi-p_t^\phi\). Then apply the paper’s fairness–stability weighting procedure and return the rule with minimum combined index, together with its type-wise awards. This keeps the paper’s decision question—choosing among bankruptcy allocations by balancing fairness and stability—while making the population the continuous object. I would expect this fixed-rule selection problem to be tractable by evaluating the seven rules and aggregating over the support.

The weakness is substantial: that would be a new formulation of the paper’s procedure, not a mirror of a named computational result. The paper studies ten jurisdictions, does not analyze complexity, and gives no hardness result whose dissolution could be tested. Moreover, with per-authority claims and supply scaled to population size, APRO’s individual residual guarantee vanishes under persistent scarcity, so APRO can collapse to PRO in the limit. The model is a credible high-multiplicity allocation question, but the paper does not provide the required anchor for a strong continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the lack of an anchor. The paper gives explicit formulas for seven bankruptcy rules, defines fairness and stability indices, and applies a selection procedure to ten jurisdictions. It proves no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent acknowledges this, then offers the paper’s procedure as a substitute. That may motivate a new study, but it cannot be a mirror of a computational result in this paper. None of its named outputs—the rule allocations, the fairness–stability ranking, or the basin recommendations—survives as a qualifying anchor.

The proposed continuous version is not nonsense. A distribution over many similar water authorities, with supply scaled to population, could be coherent. But making that model precise requires more than grouping authorities by claim: types may also need to encode their place in the river network, legal rights, and outside options. Even after that repair, applying the seven fixed rules and aggregating their outputs is a new formalization of the paper’s decision procedure, not a complexity question the paper asks or answers.

There is also a gap in what the proposed stability measure would mean. Equation (4), despite its Shapley–Shubik label, operationally normalizes each jurisdiction’s shortfall from its best allocation among the seven alternatives. The paper specifies no coalition game or exit payoffs. Integrating that quantity over types can preserve a distribution of shortfalls, but does not by itself preserve the interpretation as strategic power or individual willingness to cooperate. Adding a model that did support that interpretation would add new substance, rather than mirror a result here.

So the negative case is strong about this paper’s fit for ChoCo, but weak as a universal claim that no continuous water-allocation study could be worthwhile. Many similar authorities may form a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The narrower conclusion is that this paper offers no computational result for that regime to mirror; the proponent’s proposal is a plausible adjacent research lead, not an anchored continuous mirror of the paper.
