The case FOR (proponent)

The honest answer is that this paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its Theorem 1, proved here, says that the strong core may be empty; the weak core is nonempty and always contains an allocation satisfying equal treatment of equals; and some economies have no weak-core allocation satisfying equal-endowment no envy. Those are existence and fairness claims, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper explicitly leaves open whether an algorithm using only ordinal preferences can find a weak-core allocation satisfying equal treatment of equals. I would not present Theorem 1 as an algorithmic result.

There is still a plausible continuous follow-up. Consider a large housing cooperative or resource exchange with a fixed catalogue of \(K\) housing categories and a small number \(\tau\) of member types. A type records both a strict ranking of the categories and a complete per-member endowment vector \(e_t\); \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the membership. The population is large, while \(\tau\) and \(K\) remain moderate. The aggregate supply of category \(g\) is \(\sum_t \mu_t e_{t,g}\). An allocation gives each member a unit-sum bundle, respects those supplies, and is individually rational under first-order stochastic dominance.

One precise follow-up is Atomless Weak-Core-ETE Search: given rational type masses, rankings, and endowments, find a type-uniform allocation \(x_t\) that is individually rational and satisfies equal treatment of equals, with no positive-mass coalition able to reallocate its members’ endowments so that every member strictly improves by stochastic dominance. A coalition may split a type and assign different bundles to its members; its aggregate assignment must equal its aggregate endowment. This keeps the paper’s blocking question while making population mass, rather than named individuals, the scale of the model. Continuous optimization and finite type support offer a plausible route, but the paper establishes neither tractability nor hardness for this search problem.

That is a credible population mirror of the paper’s subject, and its authors could plausibly recognize it as such. It is not, however, a continuous problem *anchored in a named computational result of this paper*. Theorem 1 is the closest anchor in substance: its weak-core/ETE existence claim is the natural statement to revisit, while its strong-core and EENE examples would need separate continuous proofs. The weakest point is decisive for the requested case: the paper’s main result does not classify an algorithmic problem, so there is no supported claim here that discrete computational hardness dissolves—or survives—in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 supplies no computational result to continuize. It proves existence and fairness properties; it gives no complexity classification or algorithm. The paper’s final open question—whether ordinal information alone suffices to find a weak-core allocation satisfying ETE—is the only computational hook. The proposed “Atomless Weak-Core-ETE Search” turns that open question into a new computational problem; it does not continue a computational result established by the paper. On a strict reading of the requested result-anchored exercise, the anchor fails.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A large housing cooperative with a fixed catalogue of resource types, and types recording both preferences and complete per-member endowments, is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Scaling supplies with population is a natural co-modification. The resulting ordinal-only search for a type-uniform weak-core allocation is coherent, and the paper’s open algorithm question gives it a direct connection to the authors’ agenda. ETE being built into type-uniform output does not make the search meaningless.

So the negative case is strong on the missing computational-result anchor and weak on the universal claim. I can reject the proponent’s mirror under the stated “named computational result” standard, but I cannot honestly show that no worthwhile continuous follow-up exists in any scenario. If a continuous version of the paper’s open algorithm question counts as a sufficient anchor, the proponent’s case survives.
