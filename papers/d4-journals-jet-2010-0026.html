The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a qualifying anchor under your rules: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Its numbered results concern the possibility and characterization of consistent aggregation. The strongest nearby result is Theorem 1 (proved here): for a simple implication agenda, a quota rule is consistent if and only if \(m_b\le m_a+m_{a\to b}-n\) for every \(a\to b\). That is a useful characterization, but the theorem does not assert that a computational problem is in P. A polynomial-time check for explicit agendas follows from the displayed inequalities; that is an inference, not a complexity result stated by the paper.

There is a credible population-continuous extension of that result. In a large membership organization deliberating over a fixed agenda, let a type \(t\) be a member’s complete, consistent judgment set, including judgments about the agenda’s connection rules. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of members with that type. A regime with millions of members and relatively few recurring judgment profiles makes \(N\gg\tau\) plausible; the same story would be much less persuasive for a small committee.

The closest new computational question would be Robust Quota Consistency\(_\infty\). Given a simple implication agenda \(X\) and rational thresholds \(q_p\in(0,1]\) for \(p\in X^+\), decide whether the following rule always produces a consistent collective judgment: accept \(p\) when the mass of types accepting \(p\) is at least \(q_p\), and otherwise accept \(\neg p\). A solution is a yes/no answer for all rational finite-support societies over the complete, consistent judgment types of \(X\). In this continuous setting, Theorem 1 suggests the condition \(q_b\le q_a+q_{a\to b}-1\) for every \(a\to b\); checking those inequalities is tractable. This is a plausible mirror of the paper’s quota-rule consistency question, but it is a proposed extension—not a named computational result in the paper.

The scope would be the consistency of propositionwise quota aggregation, especially Theorem 1’s simple implication agendas. It would not cover the paper’s broader logical characterizations as computational results. The main weakness is that the source paper studies which aggregation rules preserve consistency, not the complexity of finding or using them. So the continuous question is recognizable to the authors, but it cannot meet the requested anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem for this anchor is that the paper proves no computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 characterizes when quota rules produce consistent judgments. It is a named result, but it does not ask for an algorithm or classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s contribution is about which aggregation rules satisfy an axiom.

I would not challenge the proposed large-membership setting: complete judgment sets make sensible types, and many members could share one. Nor is the continuous extension incoherent. Normalizing the thresholds gives \(q_b \le q_a + q_{a\to b} - 1\). The weakness is that Robust Quota Consistency\(_\infty\) quantifies over every society, so the society distribution disappears from the input. It recasts the paper’s universal consistency question as a condition on thresholds; it does not mirror a computational result about societies represented by masses.

A stronger rescue could give a particular society and ask whether its quota outcome is consistent. That changes the paper’s question: it asks about an outcome on one profile rather than a rule’s consistency across the universal domain. Or it could optimize threshold choice, but that adds an objective absent from the paper. Theorem 3 might also motivate computation over encoded logics or agendas, but then the computational subject is the logic or agenda; the continuous population is incidental.

So this defeats the proposed anchor as a computational mirror, while leaving a coherent continuous extension of the paper’s axiomatic result. That distinction is also where the negative case is weakest: the authors might recognize that extension as worth studying. What the paper itself does not provide is a computational result to anchor it to.
