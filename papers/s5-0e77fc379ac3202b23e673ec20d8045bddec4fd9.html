The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of provider types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational piecewise-linear utilities \(u_t\) over vectors of accessible type mass, satisfying mass analogues of the common-ranking and limited-complementarity conditions, does there exist a measurable symmetric sharing relation \(W:\Omega^2\to\{0,1\}\) with no blocking measurable coalition of positive mass? If so, can one construct a finite description of such a relation, and with what complexity in \(|T|\), the input bit length, and the utility description size?

The model it lives in

An atomless population \(\Omega=\bigcup_{t\in T}\Omega_t\) has \(\lambda(\Omega_t)=\mu_t\). A feasible network is a measurable symmetric binary relation \(W\); an agent \(a\in\Omega_t\) receives utility \(u_t(z(a))\), where \(z_s(a)=\lambda(\{b\in\Omega_s:W(a,b)=1\})\). The task is to find a stable network, with stability tested against measurable coalitions of positive mass that can change their internal links and drop links to outsiders; there is no welfare objective.

The objection that survived

The type-mass utility assumes providers of the same type are interchangeable, while Proposition 3.1 allows identity-specific neighbor preferences; its hypotheses do not establish compression to \(\tau\) types.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3.1’s stable-outcome existence and construction problem. It leaves the competitive-price results, the mixed-VCG mechanisms, and the differential-privacy extensions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every finite type instance admit a stable measurable network under the mass analogues of the paper’s preference assumptions?
  • When a stable network exists, can one always give it a finite description and construct that description efficiently?
  • How does atomless stability against positive-mass coalitions relate to stability in finite populations of interchangeable clones, where even a small finite coalition can block?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper’s best anchor is Proposition 3.1, proved in the paper. It says that in the bilateral data-sharing network game, if agents have a common total ordering over other agents (the top-agent property) and satisfy limited complementarity, then a stable sharing graph exists and the ordered-match algorithm finds one in \(O(N^2)\) time. Here, stability means no coalition can rearrange its sharing links so every member is at least as well off and at least one is strictly better off. The paper gives no comparable named hardness result, so this is an algorithmic lead rather than a claim that the continuous model dissolves a known hardness result.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large population of hospitals or banks that share data in bilateral exchanges. Let a type specify the data batch and every preference-relevant feature of an institution: its value from accessing other data types, its sharing costs, and its eligibility constraints. The masses \(\mu_t\) are the fractions of institutions of each type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is credible when there are many institutions but relatively few such complete types, and when institutions share a common ranking of potential partners—for example, because data-source classes have a common ordering of value.

Continuous Top-Agent Sharing Stability is the mirror I would put to a prover. The input is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational piecewise-linear utilities \(u_t\). An agent of type \(t\) evaluates a vector \(z\in\prod_{s\in T}[0,\mu_s]\), where \(z_s\) is the mass of type-\(s\) agents with whom they share data. Assume the utilities obey continuous versions of the paper’s two conditions: all types rank partner types in the same order, and if adding mass of one type is harmful, adding a bundle consisting of that type and lower-ranked types is harmful as well.

The population is an atomless space \(\Omega=\bigcup_t\Omega_t\), with \(\lambda(\Omega_t)=\mu_t\). A feasible outcome is a measurable symmetric relation \(W:\Omega^2\to\{0,1\}\), where \(W(a,b)=1\) means agents \(a\) and \(b\) share data. Agent \(a\)’s access vector is \(z_s(a)=\lambda(\{b\in\Omega_s:W(a,b)=1\})\). A solution is a \(W\) with no blocking measurable coalition \(A\) of positive mass: members may alter links within \(A\) and drop existing links to outsiders, but may not create links to outsiders who do not join; almost every member must weakly prefer the result, and a positive-mass subset must strictly prefer it. The question is whether such a stable network exists and can be constructed from the finite type description. There is no welfare objective in this anchor: the task is to find stability, as in the paper.

I expect the existence question to be tractable in the structural sense: a finite common ordering and limited complementarity give the ordered-match proof a natural type-level analogue. Whether the result admits an efficient algorithm in \(\tau\), input bit length, and utility-description size is itself an important further question. So are whether stable outcomes can always be represented succinctly, how finite clone populations converge to them, and whether relaxing either condition yields continuum-specific hardness or nonexistence.

The weakest point is the jump from a finite ordered list of named agents to an atomless population. Proposition 3.1’s proof processes agents one by one; a continuum has no individual top agent, and stability against positive-mass coalitions may behave differently from stability against finite coalitions. That makes the algorithmic extension genuinely open. Still, the mirror retains the paper’s central object—bilateral sharing networks and coalition stability—and makes mass the unit of the question. It covers Proposition 3.1 alone; I would not stretch it to the paper’s pricing or VCG results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3.1 is the paper’s only clear computational anchor: it gives an \(O(N^2)\) algorithm for finding a strongly stable network under the top-agent and limited-complementarity assumptions. The strongest version of the proposed mirror would use finitely many provider types, rational masses, utilities over the mass of each type they can access, and stability against blocking coalitions of positive mass. That is a coherent continuum network problem. The difficulty is that it adds the anonymity assumption that makes it coherent: an agent must care about how much data comes from each type, rather than which providers supply it.

That assumption is not supplied by Proposition 3.1. Its agents have preferences over sets of named counterparties; the two structural conditions do not imply that identities can be replaced by type masses. A hospital may value access to one particular dataset because of its coverage, compatibility, or legal status, even if another provider has the same nominal type. To preserve those distinctions, a type must encode the agent’s relevant relationships with potential partners. In a realistic data-sharing instance, those descriptions can all differ, so \(\tau\) grows with \(N\). This is not the mistaken objection that costs should be properties of named individuals: it is that the *links and preferences themselves* can be counterpart-specific.

The proponent’s hospital and bank scenario does not fix that. Those institutions are few and have distinctive datasets and sharing constraints. A much larger cohort of nominally similar providers is imaginable, but treating them as interchangeable sources of data changes the paper’s network game into an anonymous game over quantities of partner types. That might be a useful model, but the paper’s result does not establish the assumptions needed to get there. And a finite table of type-to-type link densities would not, by itself, describe every measurable network or determine which coalitions can improve; a compact continuum algorithm would need an additional argument that a suitably regular, type-level network suffices.

There is also a gap in the high-multiplicity bridge. In a finite network, even one agent can belong to a blocking coalition. In an atomless population, a finite set has measure zero, so the proposed positive-mass blocking notion ignores deviations that matter in every finite clone population. Requiring stability against coalitions of at least some mass \(\varepsilon\) could restore a connection, but then \(\varepsilon\) is part of the problem and the pure continuum notion is not a direct high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s stability concept.

These points make the anchor substantially weaker than it first appears: its faithful continuum version either keeps identity-level network detail and loses finite type compression, or gains compression by imposing an anonymity model absent from the theorem. They do not prove that no worthwhile mirror exists. A data-sharing setting with genuinely interchangeable providers and a well-justified mass-based utility could still support one; the negative case is strongest against treating Proposition 3.1 itself as already yielding that mirror.
