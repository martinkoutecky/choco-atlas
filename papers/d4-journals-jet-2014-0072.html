What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered. Theorem 1, Proposition 1, Theorem 2, and Corollary 1 give incentive and implementation characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result that can serve as an honest anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says a social choice rule is securely implementable with partially honest agents if and only if it is strategy-proof. Proposition 1, also proved here, characterizes strategy-proof rules on a maximal single-crossing domain as augmented representative voter schemes. Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 give the corresponding secure-implementation characterizations, including the anonymous case. These are results about incentives and implementation, not about computational complexity or algorithms.

The nearest plausible population regime is a large electorate with many voters but relatively few preference rankings. One could represent rankings by masses and consider a limit of the representative-voter rules. But the paper’s central claim concerns individual deviations and Nash equilibria: in an atomless population, a single report does not change the distribution or outcome, so those incentive conditions need a new definition. That would be a possible new research question, not a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes secure implementation by strategy-proofness; Proposition 1 characterizes strategy-proof rules on a single-crossing domain; Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 restate that characterization for partial honesty and anonymous rules. None asks for an algorithm, a complexity bound, or an optimization. A large electorate with a distribution over the finite set of rankings is entirely sensible, and high-multiplicity election work supports that regime. But the paper gives no representation of a rule as an algorithmic input, so turning its characterizations into a complexity problem would require adding a separate task.

The best candidate is a continuous version of Theorem 1. For an atomless society, one voter’s report does not change the distribution \(\mu\), and therefore cannot change an outcome \(F(\mu)\). Individual strategy-proofness then loses its bite: every rule is immune to a single voter changing the aggregate. Partial honesty does not recover the finite result. If everyone is partially honest, truthful reporting can be individually enforced because an individual deviation leaves the outcome unchanged; that makes implementation impose no strategy-proofness condition on \(F\). If only one voter is honest, that voter has zero mass and cannot constrain the reports of the rest of the population. To retain the theorem’s substantive force, one would need to make a positive-mass group able to deviate together, changing the question to coalition or group implementation. Giving a type block that power would likewise treat the block as a strategic player, rather than preserve the paper’s model of individually reporting agents.

There is a stronger limit of Proposition 1 and the anonymous characterization worth considering. A representative-voter scheme has \(n\) voter peaks and \(n-1\) fixed ballots, so in a large electorate the fixed ballots need not disappear: their empirical distribution can converge to a phantom-ballot distribution, and the rule can have a meaningful median-style limit over voter mass and phantom mass. That is a plausible continuous *axiomatic* characterization. It still does not mirror a computational result in this paper. The nonanonymous schemes are harder to carry over naturally: their fixed ballots are indexed by named coalitions, so a continuum version needs extra structure on identity or position. Either way, a computational question would need to be supplied separately.

So the negative case is strong against a paper-anchored computational mirror, but weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous follow-on could exist. A mass-robustness or coalition-implementation problem might be worthwhile; it would add a computational objective or a new equilibrium concept that this paper does not study.
