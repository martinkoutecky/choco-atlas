The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: a continuous population model of how citizens evaluate participatory budgeting under Greedy and the Method of Equal Shares. A plausible regime is a large municipality where many residents share a ballot and a similar response profile, so there are \(n\) residents but only \(\tau\) recurring types. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), its ballot over projects, its stance toward the eventual outcome, and its input, throughput and output ratings. Given project costs and a budget, apply each voting method to the mass-weighted ballots, then report the population-weighted legitimacy ratings and their differences—for example, whether fairness ratings and acceptance differ between residents who favour and oppose the outcome. This corresponds closely to the paper’s RQ1 and RQ2. Its Table 12 comparison—58.03% represented under Equal Shares versus 41.37% under Greedy—also shows that the voting-method comparison has a concrete population-level quantity to carry over.

But this is not an eligible anchor-backed case under your rules. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary or Proposition asserting a computational result. RQ1 and RQ2 are research questions, and the factor analyses, representation figures and regressions are empirical findings in tables, not named complexity results. I therefore cannot honestly restate an anchor or provide one continuous problem per anchor.

The candidate evaluation problem would mostly compute weighted statistics once the type response profiles are supplied; the paper gives no discrete hardness result whose fate in a continuum could be studied. Nor does it choose a single objective for selecting a voting method: collapsing input, fairness and outcome satisfaction into one score would add a normative choice the authors did not make. The weakest point is thus substantive, not just formal: the proposed model is a reasonable high-multiplicity extension of their empirical questions, but it does not yet look like a computational problem with the algorithmic or complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart. The authors might recognize the population-level estimand; it is much less clear they would recognize it as a continuous computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its results are empirical findings about legitimacy: factor analyses, comparisons between survey respondents, and regressions relating satisfaction to fairness, trustworthiness, and voting outcomes. It states no complexity theorem or algorithmic result about a computational problem. The Greedy method and Method of Equal Shares are mechanisms the authors compare; their presence does not turn the paper’s findings about perceptions into computational results.

A continuous population model of those findings is easy to imagine, and I would not reject it because no plausible high-multiplicity setting exists. In a large municipality, types could include a ballot, participation and outcome status, and a response profile for each survey item. With masses \(\mu_t\), one could calculate population-weighted ratings, compare winners and losers, or fit weighted versions of the paper’s statistical models. But those are population estimands and statistical analyses, not a computational complexity question. The factor analyses use a fixed, small set of survey items; the other analyses calculate standard comparisons and regressions. Once type responses are supplied, aggregation is routine. Keeping the dimensions separate avoids imposing a new normative score, but it does not create an algorithmic landscape.

A stronger proposed mirror might make the voting method’s outcome depend on the mass-weighted ballots, then ask how the population’s legitimacy ratings change under Greedy or Equal Shares. That is a coherent extension, but the paper does not provide a general model mapping ballots, outcomes, or fairness properties to citizens’ ratings. Supplying ratings for every relevant situation would be new behavioral input; learning those ratings would be a statistical research problem. And if the question instead is how to compute Greedy or Equal Shares on a continuous electorate, that is a question about the aggregation rules’ algorithms, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s empirical findings.

One could go further and choose a voting method or project set to maximize legitimacy, perhaps using a vector of input, throughput, and output measures. But that requires specifying how legitimacy responds to choices and outcomes across a population—structure this paper does not establish. With fixed ratings, optimizing a weighted score is straightforward; with an inferred response model, the main problem becomes estimation and validation. Either route adds a different research question rather than exposing a computational result already present here.

So the negative case is not that a large-population survey model would be nonsensical. It is that the most faithful continuous version remains an empirical or statistical reformulation, while the more algorithmic versions require replacing the paper’s question. That is a strong reason not to prioritize a ChoCo mirror of this paper, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile future project connecting participatory budgeting algorithms to population-level legitimacy models.
