The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational complexity result in this paper: it classifies no problem as NP-hard, tractable, FPT, or similar. The strongest possible seed for a positive mirror is Proposition 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that a group-based qualification system has a pair of teams forced to collude under a family \(\mathcal R\) of match-result profiles if, in every profile in \(\mathcal R\), at least two of \(B(\bar R)\cap T_1(\bar R)\), \(B(\bar R)\cap T_2(\bar R)\), and \(B(\bar R)\cap T_3(\bar R)\) are nonempty, and for some group \(G_i\), \(b_i\ge1\) and \(a_i+b_i<c_i<n_i\). Here forced collusion means that scoring more goals cannot improve either team’s qualification position, and that each team has some profile in which doing so makes it worse off. The proposition establishes a structural existence claim through a construction; it does not give a computational classification.

The most plausible positive extension is a mass-prevalence question for forced collusion in large qualification systems. Consider a federation running many parallel groups under a common repechage rule. A team’s type records its complete qualification-relevant state and role in a group template: its current standings and tie-break statistics, its match options, its qualification preferences, and the group context that determines which other teams it faces. The input gives rational masses for these types, together with the group co-occurrence information needed to assemble teams into groups. Each group remains finite, and each match outcome remains an integral scoreline; mass records what fraction of the large federation’s teams occupy each type. The action is a team’s choice of match performance, represented by the feasible result profiles in \(\mathcal R\).

The question is: what fraction of the team population belongs to a pair of teams forced to collude? For each supported group context, identify the teams that participate in at least one forced pair under its specified family of result profiles, then sum their type masses. A solution is the exact rational fraction, with a witness group context, pair, and result profiles when that fraction is positive. This is an analogue of Proposition 3.1’s forced-collusion phenomenon, rather than an anchor the paper itself supplies. With bounded group size and explicitly represented result profiles, I would expect this extension to be tractable by checking each supported context and aggregating its mass. That expectation is about the proposed extension, not a result of the paper.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many standardized regional or youth qualification groups, with a relatively small number of recurring standings and match-state templates. It preserves the paper’s core issue—how a team’s goals affect its own qualification position and its opponent’s—while making prevalence across many teams meaningful. The paper itself notes that the number of groups can be increased arbitrarily in the construction behind Proposition 3.1.

The soft spot is substantial: the proposition concerns the incentives of a particular pair inside a finite group. A type distribution by itself does not say which teams meet or which groups they share; adding enough group context to answer those questions can make the number of distinct types grow with the number of teams. The proposed mirror also asks for the prevalence of a phenomenon, while the paper proves only that it can occur. So Proposition 3.1 is a promising structural foothold, but this paper has no qualifying computational anchor for the ChoCo complexity programme. The proposed mirror covers only forced collusion under the general repechage conditions, not the paper’s entire analysis or its policy recommendations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3.1 is the paper’s only strong anchor, and it proves an existence claim by constructing finite qualification instances. It does not state a decision, optimization, or counting problem whose complexity could be continuized. That is a serious gap, though it does not by itself rule out the prevalence question the other analyst proposes.

The strongest version of that question would give rational masses to feasible group templates, with each team’s type containing its role and all qualification-relevant information, and ask what fraction of teams belong to a forced-collusion pair. That is coherent: it preserves finite groups and integral scorelines while taking the number of recurring groups large. For a fixed set of templates, though, the answer is just a weighted sum, \(\sum_g \lambda_g q(g)\), where \(q(g)\) is determined by checking the finite group. The mass records how often the paper’s phenomenon occurs; it does not enter the incentive mechanism or create a new population-level computational problem. If the templates are given succinctly, the hard part is generating or checking the local finite cases, not handling their masses.

One could make the mass matter by optimizing over group assignments—for example, finding an assignment that minimizes the share of teams exposed to forced collusion. But that changes the question: the paper studies incentives under a given qualification system, while this version gives an organizer a new group-design problem. A distribution over team types alone would not determine the answer, since group co-occurrence matters; adding a feasible-template measure repairs that, but makes the group templates the operative objects.

So the case against this paper as a ChoCo priority is that its continuous layer is prevalence accounting, while a richer continuous optimization requires adding a different decision problem. This is not an airtight claim that no worthwhile model can be built. Under the recurring-group scenario, the proposed model is sensible and could be useful for sports policy. The negative case is strongest on the narrower point that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and its plausible extension does not yet make population mass central to the computation.
