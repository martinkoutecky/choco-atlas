The continuous mirror question

Given τ complete buyer types \(t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita supply \(\sigma\), integral bundle sets \(B_t\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_+^m\), valuations \(v_t\), and payment functions \(q_{tj}\), compute a price vector \(p^\star\) and masses \(\lambda_{t,x}\geq 0\) such that \(\lambda_{t,x}>0\) only if \(x\in\arg\max_{y\in B_t}\{v_t(y)-\sum_j q_{tj}(p^\star_j)y_j\}\), \(\sum_{x\in B_t}\lambda_{t,x}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,x}\lambda_{t,x}x=\sigma\), with \(p^\star\) componentwise minimum among equilibrium prices.

The model it lives in

A continuum of buyers grouped into τ complete types, each type having mass \(\mu_t\), common preferences and payment functions, and integral individual bundles. The goods retain per-capita supply \(\sigma\); decision variables assign type mass across demanded bundles and choose a common price vector, minimizing that vector componentwise over equilibria.

The objection that survived

Clearing denominators makes the equilibrium set equivalent to that of a finite replicated market, so the unresolved issue is whether minimum prices can be computed from the compressed rational masses without expanding the population.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My positive case is for the market’s population mirror: a continuum of buyers grouped into a small number of complete types. The paper has no numbered theorem asserting NP-hardness or membership in P. Its strongest anchors are constructive algorithmic results, so I would not present them as a complexity classification.

Consider a market with many local branches of manufacturers purchasing interchangeable grades of components. A type records everything the paper’s model uses: the branch’s bundle valuation, bundle limits, and payment functions under its tax or platform-fee contract. Standardized operations and contracts can produce many branches but relatively few types: \(N\) may be very large while the support size \(\tau\) stays small. Branches of one type have identical preferences and payment rules; idiosyncratic branches become separate types. The individuals in this scenario are genuine market participants, not a mathematical fiction imposed on the whole research area.

Normalize the population to mass \(1\). An instance gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and per-capita supply \(\sigma\). Each individual still receives an integral bundle from a bounded integer set \([0,b]_{\mathbb Z}\), and has the paper’s M♮-concave valuation \(v_t\) and piecewise-linear payment functions \(q_{tj}\). The continuous variable is the mass of type-\(t\) buyers assigned each demanded bundle, not the goods or an individual’s bundle. Supply scales with population, as it should in this regime.

My lead is Continuous Minimum-Equilibrium Computation. Its input is those type masses, per-capita supply, valuation and payment data, and demand/exchange oracles. It asks for a price vector \(p^\star\) and masses \(\lambda_{t,x}\) such that \(\lambda_{t,x}>0\) only when \(x\) maximizes \(v_t(x)-\sum_j q_{tj}(p^\star_j)x_j\), the masses satisfy \(\sum_{x\in D_t(p^\star)}\lambda_{t,x}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,x}\lambda_{t,x}x=\sigma\). Among equilibrium prices, \(p^\star\) must be the componentwise minimum. Thus every individual still receives an integral bundle; a fractional type-level average means that different parts of the type’s mass receive different demanded bundles.

The anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in the paper: “The output \(p^\star\) of Algorithm 1 coincides with the minimum equilibrium price vector.” Algorithm 1 starts at zero and raises prices along a direction chosen to preserve the minimal maximally over-demanded set. Theorem 3.1, also proved there, supplies the supporting finite-termination claim. This is a close mirror of the authors’ actual question: compute a buyer-optimal Walrasian equilibrium under strong substitutes and payment frictions. The paper’s oracle and polymatroid machinery gives a plausible route to a weighted, type-level version. I expect this problem to be Class A, but that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 3.2. The paper’s iteration bound depends on discrete market quantities, so it does not establish polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\) for the continuous version.

A distinct, more technical question is Continuous Stable-Direction Computation. Given the same market data and a current price \(p\), define weighted over-demand by \(O_\mu(X;p)=\sum_t\mu_t\min_{x\in D_t(p)}x(X)-\sigma(X)\). Let \(X^\star\) be its inclusion-minimal maximizer. The problem is to find \(d\geq0\) with \(\operatorname{supp}^+(d)=X^\star\) such that, for all sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\geq0\), \(X^\star\) remains the inclusion-minimal maximizer at \(p+\varepsilon d\). This asks for the direction used by the auction, with over-demand computed from population mass rather than a sum over named buyers.

Its anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in the paper: “The direction \(d^\star\) returned by Algorithm 2 satisfies (LSC).” The paper also states that Algorithm 2 computes such a direction in strongly polynomial time, using weighted polymatroid intersection. I expect the weighted type version to be tractable as well: masses change the coefficients of the aggregate problem, while each type retains the same demand and exchange structure. But the theorem does not prove that extension or its bit complexity.

This is within scope because the society is what becomes continuous. The model retains discrete goods, integral individual bundles, type-specific valuations, and the paper’s payment frictions. The fractional object is the distribution of demanded bundles across a type’s mass. Conventional continuum-agent equilibrium work is relevant machinery and context; it does not answer the computational question of finding the minimum equilibrium or a valid price-update direction. Nor is this merely outcome-space continuity: the population distribution is the object being modeled.

The weakest point is that the paper’s algorithm is built for finitely many buyers and integer supply. A weighted continuum version may have new termination and bit-complexity issues, and the finite algorithm’s guarantees do not transfer just by replacing counts with masses. That makes this a genuine research problem rather than an already-proved consequence. It does not undermine the mirror itself: the type definition preserves every feature the authors use, and the many-branches scenario gives the population regime a concrete interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the two anchors are really one, and their continuous versions may amount to a weighted reformulation of the same finite market problem.

For rational type masses \(\mu_t\) and rational per-capita supply \(\sigma\), clear denominators with some \(D\). The resulting finite market has \(D\mu_t\) copies of each type and supply \(D\sigma\). Its equilibrium price vectors, including the minimum one, coincide with those of the mass formulation. That makes Theorem 3.2 a close match at the level of the equilibrium concept, but it also narrows the claimed novelty: the question is whether the algorithm can work on the compressed weighted input, with running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and bit length \(L\), instead of expanding those copies. The paper does not establish that bound even for its own algorithm: Theorem 3.1 proves finite termination, while Proposition 5.3 gives finiteness for some updates rather than a polynomial bound. So the positive analyst’s Class A expectation is open, not supported by a direct transfer of the theorem.

Theorem 4.4 is a weaker independent anchor. Stable-direction computation is a subroutine of this particular auction, rather than a separate market outcome. Its weighted version would extend the same demand-polymatroid and convex-flow machinery to rational type weights. That could be technically interesting, but the paper gives no reason to treat it as a second, distinct continuous problem; its value largely depends on whether it unlocks the minimum-equilibrium computation above.

These objections reduce the force of the case for a mirror, but they do not defeat it. Clearing denominators can make the finite market exponentially larger in \(L\), so the compressed algorithmic question is real. And standardized buyers with the same valuations and payment rules form a plausible high-multiplicity regime; the paper’s own model does not make buyers individually distinctive in a way that rules out such groups. Fractional mass assigned across integral demanded bundles is also coherent here.

So I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the prompt asks for. The anchors are dependent, and the paper’s stated results do not settle the continuous input’s complexity. But the continuous equilibrium question remains well-posed, and the proposed market scenario survives the strongest objections available in the text.
