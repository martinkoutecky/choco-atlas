The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is narrow: estimating the consensus ranking of a large population. The paper has no numbered theorem of its own classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or similar. Its best named computational anchor is Theorem 2.2, page 29 of Marden (1995), which the paper cites and uses in §2.2; it is not proved in this paper. As the authors state it, the theorem gives a closed-form solution to the consensus-ranking optimization: order the items by their average rank. That makes it an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result.

A continuous version is *MMS Population Consensus*. An instance has \(m\) items and a finite support of \(\tau\) complete rankings \(r^{(t)}\), each with a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Given a fixed concentration \(\theta>0\), find a consensus ranking \(\rho\) maximizing the population’s average log-likelihood under the Spearman Mallows model:
\[ Q_\mu(\rho;\theta) =\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\log P(r^{(t)}\mid \rho,\theta). \]
A solution is a permutation \(\rho\). Since the normalizing constant does not depend on \(\rho\), maximizing this objective is equivalent to maximizing \(\rho^\top\bar r_\mu\), where \(\bar r_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t r^{(t)}\). Theorem 2.2’s result gives the solution: assign ranks in the order of the items’ coordinates in \(\bar r_\mu\), with arbitrary tie-breaking among equal coordinates. This takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length: compute the weighted averages, then sort them.

The corresponding regime could be a large electorate ranking a fixed slate of candidates, or a large panel ranking a fixed set of options. Each voter’s complete ranking is their type because the problem uses no other individual information; mass is the fraction of the population with that ranking. With many respondents and a moderate slate, it is plausible to have \(N\gg\tau\), even though the full type space has \(m!\) possible rankings. This is a sensible high-multiplicity instance regime, and the paper’s own estimation problem already treats repeated rankings through their frequencies. The authors could recognize the mirror as the population form of their consensus-estimation subproblem.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable. It is an exact continuous-population analogue of the paper’s consensus MLE update, but it does not show that discrete hardness dissolves: this particular consensus optimization already has a closed-form solution for a finite sample. The mirror covers only the estimation of \(\rho\). It does not cover the paper’s mixture-model fitting, selection of the number of components, estimation of \(\theta\), its partition-function approximation, or inference from partial rankings. Those suggest further continuous questions—for example, maximizing the population mixture likelihood over component weights, consensuses and concentrations—but the paper provides no comparable numbered complexity anchor for them.

The weak point is that the continuous step here is largely replacing empirical frequencies with rational population masses. That is a natural high-multiplicity formulation, and it preserves the authors’ actual consensus-estimation objective, but it creates little new complexity theory. There is also a strict anchoring caveat: the named theorem is a result the paper cites and relies on, not one it establishes. If “results of this paper” excludes cited results, then this paper has no qualifying named anchor, and the positive case is correspondingly limited.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed anchor is not a result of this paper. The closed-form consensus estimate is Theorem 2.2 of Marden (1995), which the authors cite and use. This paper does not prove it, and it states no numbered theorem or corollary classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Its own contributions are an EM inference procedure, an approximation to the partition function, and empirical evaluations. Under the paper-result standard in the brief, there is no named computational result here to mirror.

That point does not make the proposed population question ill-posed. Given ranking types with masses \(\mu_t\), maximizing the population log-likelihood at fixed \(\theta\) is a sensible problem, and the authors could recognize it as their consensus-estimation step applied to population masses. A large electorate with repeated rankings is a plausible setting. I cannot reject the mirror on the grounds that its algorithm is simple, that it changes the answer, or that the paper’s examples are small.

A stronger proposed mirror would give the ranking types as a distribution and ask for a best-fitting Mallows mixture, perhaps including partial rankings and censoring patterns. That too is a coherent population-level inference problem. But it extends the paper’s statistical method; it does not mirror a complexity result established by the paper. The Mallows model’s probability distribution over rankings is also not, by itself, an instance supplied as a continuous society: the paper estimates its parameters from a finite sample.

So the negative case is strongest on the assignment’s anchor requirement and weak on its broader universal claim. If a cited result counts as an anchor, the consensus mirror survives; I do not see an honest modelling objection that defeats it.
