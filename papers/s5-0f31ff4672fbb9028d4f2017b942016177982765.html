The continuous mirror question

Given finite seller and buyer type sets with rational masses, linear utility parameters, and integer price intervals for each type pair, compute a stable mass allocation in time polynomial in the type support and binary input length. Each unit of mass receives at most one match at an integer price, and for every possible type-pair contract and allowed price, at least one side has zero mass that strictly prefers that contract to its current assignment.

The model it lives in

Use seller and buyer types as complete utility profiles, with rational masses and an integer price interval for each type pair. Let \(x_{sbp}\ge 0\) be mass matched at integer price \(p\), with unmatched mass for each type; require each type’s mass to be fully allocated and every proposed contract to have zero strictly preferring mass on at least one side.

The objection that survived

Greinecker already studies a closely related continuous-population stability model and proves existence through limits, so the model and existence component are not new; that work does not settle the compact-input computation bound.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the constructive stable-outcome procedure in Theorems 3.1 and 3.2. It leaves open polynomial or bit-complexity bounds and does not cover the paper’s stated many-to-many extension.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a stable allocation be computed in time polynomial in the type support and binary input length when price intervals are given compactly, without expanding the population or enumerating every integer price?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s constructive search for a pairwise stable outcome, with the population made continuous while money remains integral. The paper’s named computational anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved here: *the algorithm terminates after a finite number of iterations*. Its proof uses the fact that prices move monotonically within bounded integer intervals and, when they do not decrease, the set of available seller–buyer pairs shrinks. The companion Theorem 3.1, also proved here, says that when the algorithm terminates, its outcome \((X;p,q,r)\) satisfies both stability conditions: every matched trader gets nonnegative utility, and no seller–buyer pair can block at any allowed integer price. These results establish a finite constructive existence procedure; they do not establish polynomial running time.

Call the mirror Continuous Pairwise-Stable Outcome Construction. An instance has finite seller-type and buyer-type sets \(S\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\alpha_s,\beta_b\) summing to one across both sides, and, for every pair \((s,b)\), an integer price interval \([\ell_{sb},u_{sb}]\). A type is a complete trading profile: it includes an agent’s utility parameters and their terms of trade with every opposite-side type. For example, let a seller of type \(s\) get \(\sigma_{sb}(p)=a_{sb}p+d_{sb}\) at price \(p\), and a buyer of type \(b\) get \(\rho_{bs}(p)=-c_{bs}p+e_{bs}\), with \(a_{sb},c_{bs}>0\). The input asks for a stable outcome, not for an optimal one.

A solution is a nonnegative flow \(x_{sbp}\) assigning mass of seller type \(s\) to buyer type \(b\) at the integer price \(p\), plus unmatched masses \(x_{s0},x_{0b}\). It must use each type’s mass exactly once:
\[ x_{s0}+\sum_{b,p}x_{sbp}=\alpha_s,\qquad x_{0b}+\sum_{s,p}x_{sbp}=\beta_b. \]
Every positive matched flow must give both sides nonnegative utility. For each possible contract \((s,b,p)\), count the mass of sellers of type \(s\) whose current utility is strictly below \(\sigma_{sb}(p)\), and the mass of buyers of type \(b\) whose current utility is strictly below \(\rho_{bs}(p)\); unmatched mass is counted when the proposed contract beats utility zero. Stability requires that at least one of those two masses be zero for every \((s,b,p)\). Thus no positive mass on both sides wants to leave its current assignments for that contract. Each trader still makes at most one match and pays or receives an integer amount; the outcome can split a type’s mass across contracts, so its type-level averages may be fractional.

A plausible regime is a large recurring market for standardized goods: many sellers hold one unit of a grade, many buyers seek at most one, and traders fall into a modest number of complete profiles of valuations, price bounds, and preferences across counterpart types. For instance, a market with hundreds of thousands of traders but a few dozen such profiles has \(N\) much larger than \(|S|+|B|\). This is the high-multiplicity regime the mirror needs. It is a property of this market regime, not of matching markets in general.

I expect a type-compressed stable-outcome algorithm to be a Class A candidate, especially if the allowed price levels are explicit. But I would not claim a bit-polynomial algorithm: the paper’s finite-termination theorem gives no such bound, and its conclusion explicitly notes that complexity may depend on price-interval sizes. The main computational question is whether one can construct the flow in time polynomial in the support size and binary input length, without expanding the population or all integer prices. For rational masses, a common-denominator replication turns the instance into a finite market covered by the paper’s existence theorem; aggregating a stable matching back to type masses gives a stable flow. That supports existence, but replication may take time proportional to a denominator that is exponential in its bit length.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it retains two-sided one-to-one trade, bounded integer transfers, individual rationality, and pairwise blocking. Continuity changes the population representation and lets identical traders be assigned in aggregate. Their cited earlier work by Farooq uses continuous money, but the traders remain finite; that is not already a continuous-population model. I am not claiming that the paper proves a new complexity classification, or that continuous population makes its matching question easier.

The soft spot is that the anchor is finite termination, not a complexity bound. The proposed mirror therefore has a clear stable-outcome search problem and a credible high-multiplicity regime, but its Class A status is a research conjecture. The paper contains no named hardness result to anchor a claim that hardness dissolves or persists.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the case for slides between two different targets. The paper’s Theorem 3.1 verifies that a terminal outcome is stable; Theorem 3.2 proves that its procedure eventually terminates. Neither gives a polynomial running time, and the paper explicitly says the iteration count may depend on the price intervals. So the paper supplies a constructive existence result, but no complexity classification for a continuous mirror to extend.

There is also already a close continuous model. Greinecker formulates two-sided matching directly over population distributions, allows contracts between matched types, and defines stability through positive-mass blocking. The paper proves existence and characterizes these outcomes as limits of stable finite markets. With finitely many types and integer prices as contracts, that covers the proponent’s *continuous stability model* remarkably closely. It does not, however, settle the algorithmic question: it offers existence and limit results, not a bit-complexity bound for finding a stable flow when price intervals are given compactly. [Greinecker, *Pairwise Stable Matching in Large Economies*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA16228)

The best version of the mirror survives that objection. Give each type a rational mass, retain indivisible goods and integer-price contracts for each individual unit, and ask for a stable mass allocation in time polynomial in the type support and binary input length. A common-denominator expansion gives a finite market and an existence argument, but the denominator can be exponential in its encoding length; it does not settle the compressed computation problem. Nor does the market story fail: recurring standardized-goods markets can plausibly have many traders but few complete valuation and price-bound types. Aggregating those traders preserves the question of whether a positive mass on both sides can profitably form a contract.

So the negative case can reject the claim that the continuous *model and existence question* are new. It can also question whether that model alone merits priority, given the paper’s narrow algorithmic anchor. But it cannot honestly rule out the stronger high-multiplicity complexity question. The prior continuous theory does not answer it, and the paper’s own termination proof leaves its bit-complexity open. On the programme’s stated standard, that is a real surviving mirror; the case against is weak.
