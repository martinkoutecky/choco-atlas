The case FOR (proponent)

I see a plausible continuous problem in the paper’s policy representation and coalition analysis, but I cannot give it the kind of anchor you asked for. There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result in this paper. Table 4 reports representativeness indices for different election methods, and Tables 5–7 compare coalitions; those are computed findings, not complexity theorems. The paper also formulates and computes question-selection optimizations in (1)–(3), but those equations are not named results of the required kind, and the question-selection objective is about distances between parties, not the population. So there is no qualifying anchor to restate.

My strongest positive case is a new computational question built directly on the paper’s Third Vote coalition model: Third-Vote Coalition Robustness under Mass Reweighting. An instance has \(q\) policy questions, \(k\) parties with fixed answers \(p_{j\ell}\in\{-1,0,+1\}\), a target coalition \(K\), and a rational society \(\mu\) over voter types \(t\in\{-1,0,+1\}^q\). A type is a complete answer vector, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with that vector. For the mass-transfer action, type \(t\) may be converted into type \(t'\); the unit cost is their Hamming distance, the number of answers changed.

For each question \(\ell\), the society’s positive and negative masses determine the represented share of either side; neutral responses are treated as in the paper, with their passive positions distributed in the positive-to-negative ratio. From those issue-level shares, calculate each party’s popularity \(P_j\) and universality \(U_j\) using the paper’s definitions, then its Third Vote index \(a_j=(P_j+U_j)/2\) and seat share \(s_j=a_j/\sum_h a_h\). The problem is to find a transfer \(x_{t,t'}\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t,t'} d_H(t,t')x_{t,t'} \]
such that the target coalition’s seat share is at least a specified rational \(\eta>1/2\). The output is the minimum-cost transfer and the resulting society, or a certificate that the target is infeasible. This keeps the paper’s underlying question—how its representativeness rule distributes parliamentary power—while asking how much of a large electorate’s policy profile must change to make a coalition securely eligible.

A natural regime is a large student-body, citywide, or national electorate answering the same moderate-length questionnaire. There, many people can share the same complete response type, so \(n\) can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported types. Costs must also be part of the type: if people with the same answers face different campaign costs, group them separately. The paper’s KIT data motivate the model, but do not establish that its particular sample has \(\tau\ll n\); that regime needs to be stated as the scenario, not assumed from the experiment.

I expect tractability when \(q\) and the number of parties are fixed: only issue-level masses enter the representativeness indices, while the least-cost way to reach those margins can be posed as a mass-transfer optimization. For unrestricted \(q\), I would tentatively expect continuum-specific hardness: the agenda can create many majority patterns and nonlinear index comparisons even after voter-level granularity disappears. That is a conjecture, not a claim established by this paper. It prompts exact and approximate algorithms, parameterizations by \(q\), \(\tau\), and \(k\), and a discrete-to-continuous comparison of the minimum campaign cost. A related extension would test Third Vote Plus and the paper’s blended election rules.

The mirror covers the policy-representation and coalition results in Sections 3 and 4. The weakest point is also decisive for the requested format: the paper does not state this robustness problem or prove a named computational result about it, and it already presents electorate opinion through aggregate percentages. This is therefore a credible continuous-population extension of their model, but not an anchor-based continuization of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Its reported findings in Sections 3–4 are calculations of representativeness and coalition indices. The optimization models in (1)–(3) select questions using party positions; the electorate’s distribution does not enter their objective. Those are the paper’s most computational parts, and continuizing the population would not change them.

The closest population mirror is already implicit in the Third Vote calculations. For a distribution \(\mu\) over answer vectors, each issue’s positive and negative shares are just marginals such as \(\sum_{t:t_\ell=+1}\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:t_\ell=-1}\mu_t\). The party indices, seat shares, and coalition statistics then depend on those marginals and the parties’ answers. Replacing ballot counts with rational masses gives the same calculations; the KIT experiment’s voluntary sample does not establish a large-population, small-support regime, but even in a national electorate the rule uses aggregate issue shares rather than voter identities or the full joint type distribution.

The proposed mass-reweighting problem is the strongest alternative. It could be a worthwhile new robustness or campaigning project, but it adds the intervention, its cost metric, and a target coalition threshold—none appears in the paper. Hamming distance is one possible assumption; costs grounded in actual persuasion would require still more modeling. That makes the problem an extension inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a result it proves. The conjectures about tractability for fixed \(q\) or hardness for unrestricted \(q\) do not supply the missing connection.

So the negative case is strong against a paper-anchored mirror: the computational results do not concern the population, while the policy calculations already operate on aggregate shares. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no high-multiplicity persuasion or robustness problem here could be worth ChoCo’s time. I can only say that such a problem would need its own motivation and model; this paper does not furnish the computational anchor.
