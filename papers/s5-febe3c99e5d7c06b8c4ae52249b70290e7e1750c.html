The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population-level extension I can see is a replicated exchange economy: many consumers fall into finitely many complete types \(t\), where a type fixes endowment, feasible strategies, and preferences. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of consumers of type \(t\); an allocation specifies each type’s per-capita bundle. A coalition is described by how much mass it draws from each type, and a candidate allocation is stable if no such coalition can \(\alpha\)-block it under the permitted outsider responses. The output would be an allocation in the \(\alpha\)-core. That is a recognizable extension of the paper’s central economic question.

But I cannot honestly make it an anchor-based computational mirror. The paper proves existence results, not complexity or algorithmic results. Its in-paper Main Theorem (proved here, but unnumbered) says that an \(\alpha\)-continuous generalized game has a nonempty \(\alpha\)-core. Theorem 5.1 proves nonemptiness for a class of games with voting preferences, and Theorem 5.2 does so for a class with Knightian preferences. Neither theorem gives an algorithm or complexity classification. The numbered Proposition 6.1 is also an existence result. There is no qualifying named computational result to anchor a continuous problem to.

There is a further modeling gap: the paper’s agents remain a finite set \(I\). Its infinite-dimensional strategy spaces and state-contingent choices concern what agents can choose, while continuization here concerns the population. In the proposed extension, coalition choices and outsider responses would need new definitions for masses of agents; the paper’s existence theorem does not establish that extension’s computational properties.

So the positive case is a plausible research direction around high-multiplicity \(\alpha\)-core computation, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The weakest point is decisive for this task: the paper supplies no computational anchor, and its core existence results alone say nothing about whether finding a core allocation is tractable or hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper gives the continuization programme no computational result to mirror. Its unnumbered Main Theorem, Theorems 5.1 and 5.2, and Proposition 6.1 all assert non-emptiness of a core. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a finite input model. The general game is described through strategy spaces, preference correspondences, and topologies that need not have any finite encoding. Turning “the core is nonempty” into “compute a core allocation” would require choosing an encoding and asking a new algorithmic question; it would not translate a computational result from the paper.

The proposed replicated exchange economy is the strongest candidate for that new question. Repeated consumers with the same endowments, feasible strategies, and preferences form sensible types, and coalitions can be described by the mass they draw from each type. The paper’s exchange-economy setting and balancedness condition support that modelling choice. But its theorem only establishes existence under abstract continuity assumptions. It does not say how to find an allocation, or how computation depends on the number of types, bit length, or any other input parameter. A finite-type, rationally encoded version could be worth studying; its computational landscape would be a fresh contribution.

The two application theorems do not provide better anchors. Theorem 5.1 has finitely many players choosing mixed strategies over possibly infinitely many actions; the represented individuals shape each player’s preferences, rather than forming a population whose members receive allocations and join blocking coalitions. Theorem 5.2 has finitely many players choosing state-contingent strategies; its countable states describe uncertainty, not agents. One could replicate players of either kind into complete types and define mass-based blocking, but that again creates a new population model and a new computational problem. The original theorems establish existence for finite-player games.

So the negative case is strong against calling this paper a computational mirror: the natural population models are plausible, yet no paper result classifies computation for them. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project exists. A high-multiplicity \(\alpha\)-core computation problem in a suitably encoded exchange economy may be worthwhile; the paper simply supplies no computational anchor for it.
