The continuous mirror question

Given a finite binary attribute set \(V\) and finite-support rational distributions \(\mu,\nu\) over irreducible CP-theory preference sets on \(V\), compute the exact value \(D_d(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{A,B}\mu_A\nu_B d(A,B)\).

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete irreducible CP-theory preference set over the same finite \(V\). The two societies are rational mass distributions \(\mu,\nu\) over finite supports; the query is their expected pairwise distance under the paper’s metric \(d\), computed using Theorem 4’s context-uniform formula.

The objection that survived

The population layer adds only a \(\tau_\mu\tau_\nu\) weighted aggregation over pairwise distances, and the template-library regime limits the mirror’s reach.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

It covers exact evaluation of the population average of \(d\) via Theorem 4; it leaves the reference metric \(d_D\), the stand-alone metric claims, approximation quality, and runtime comparisons outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the bit complexity of evaluating \(D_d\) from compact CP-theory descriptions, in terms of \(|V|\), support sizes, and input bit length; can the context-uniform formula avoid exponential normalization?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I can defend is a cross-population disagreement query. It treats a stakeholder’s complete preference set as their type, and asks how far apart two large stakeholder communities are on average.

Fix a finite set \(V\) of binary attributes. A type is an irreducible CP-theory preference set \(A\) over \(V\): two people share a type only if they have the same conditional preferences and the same relative-importance statements. The two communities are rational distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) over finite supports of such types, with masses summing to one. Mass means the fraction of a community with that complete preference type.

The problem, Continuous CP-Preference Disagreement, is: given \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), return the exact rational value

\[ D_d(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{A\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)} \sum_{B\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)} \mu_A\nu_B\,d(A,B), \]

where \(d\) is the paper’s proposed metric. This is the expected distance between a person drawn from each community. There is no intervention variable; the query is to compute this population-level statistic. The outcomes remain discrete. It is the stakeholder population that is represented continuously.

My one anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that for any CP-theory preference sets \(A,B\), the paper’s distance \(d(A,B)\) equals the context-uniform ACP computation \(d_{\mathrm{cu}}(A,B)\). In that formula, each normalized statement \(p\) contributes \(2^{|V|-1-|\operatorname{Var}(\varrho_p)|}\); the corresponding contributions for \(A\) and \(B\) are summed, then twice the overlap contribution is subtracted. That overlap sums \(2^{|V|-1-|\operatorname{Var}(\varrho_p\wedge\varrho_q)|}\) over compatible normalized statements \(p,q\) with the same preference structure and consistent conditions. The paper computes with this formula in its experiments. Theorem 3, also proved here, establishes that \(d\) is a metric on the preference sets.

This is a computationally useful anchor, but it is not a numbered theorem showing that the problem is in P or establishing another complexity class. The paper claims polynomial computation in the size of the normalized ACP representation; that representation can be exponentially larger than the compact CP-theory input. So I would expect the stated population query to be tractable when types are supplied in the normalized representation: compute the pairwise distances using Theorem 4, then take the rational weighted sum. I would not claim polynomial time in the compact CP-theory input length.

The regime I have in mind is a large membership or stakeholder community using a shared, finite library of elicited preference templates over the same attributes. A type is the full template, including its conditions and importance relations; it is not a partial demographic label. The population may be large while the number of distinct templates, \(\tau\), stays moderate. This fits the paper’s motivation of comparing stakeholder preferences and its interest in later preference aggregation. It also covers only the distance-computation contribution, not the paper’s broader claims about preference languages or all of its experimental comparisons.

The weakness is that complete CP-theory preference sets are rich: outside a template-based setting, many people may have unique types, so \(\tau\) could approach the population size. Also, this mirror averages the paper’s proposed \(d\), which is an overestimate of the dominance-relation distance \(d_D\) in general; it does not claim to compute the exact average \(d_D\). Those limits matter, but they leave a clear continuous-population question grounded in the paper’s actual distance computation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4 is not a complexity result. It proves that two representations of the paper’s proposed distance give the same value. Theorem 3 establishes a metric property. The paper’s complexity discussion is in prose: computation is polynomial in the normalized ACP representation, which may be exponentially larger than the compact input. So the paper offers useful computational methods and experiments, but no named complexity classification for a population problem.

For the proposed mirror, once the pairwise distances are available, the population query is the bilinear sum
\[ D_d(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{A,B}\mu_A\nu_Bd(A,B). \]
Its population-specific work is combining \(\tau_\mu\tau_\nu\) weighted values. The main computational task remains the same one the paper already studies: computing \(d(A,B)\) for a pair of preference sets. That makes the mirror a plausible population statistic, but a weak fit for a programme about how continuization changes the algorithmic landscape. This limitation does not make the query ill-posed; it limits the computational contribution the population model appears to add.

The type regime is possible: a large survey or membership community could choose among a shared library of complete CP-theory templates. But exact types must match in conditional preferences and relative-importance statements, so the library is a substantive modeling assumption. The paper’s motivating stakeholders are holistic preference holders; a population of template-selecting individuals is a different application. This is a concern about how often the regime is useful, not an objection to high multiplicity itself.

A more principled community comparison would be a Wasserstein distance over the type space, minimizing expected \(d(A,B)\) over couplings of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). That avoids treating two identical communities as far apart simply because two independent draws can differ. Yet the resulting transport problem is generic once the pairwise distance matrix is known; its CP-theory-specific computational challenge is still the paper’s pairwise calculation. A population consensus problem, such as choosing a type \(Q\) to minimize \(\sum_A\mu_A d(A,Q)\), could be more substantial, but it needs a choice of eligible consensus types and an objective the paper does not establish.

So the negative case is limited: the proposed mirror may be useful for template-based communities, and the paper’s distance computation makes it computable. The case against rests on the narrowness of that regime and the small amount of new population-level computation, not on a fundamental impossibility. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror here.
