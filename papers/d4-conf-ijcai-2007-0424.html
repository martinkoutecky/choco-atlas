The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard. Theorem 1, attributed to Maheswaran and Başar (2003), gives existence and uniqueness of a market-clearing bid total under assumptions on agents’ demand functions. Theorem 2 characterizes when agents’ optimal bids form a unique Nash equilibrium. Theorems 3 and 4, proved here, establish individual rationality and nondecreasing social welfare for the deal optimization mechanism. These are equilibrium and mechanism properties; none establishes a problem’s complexity or an algorithmic classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness.

There is a plausible population setting: many sensor-network or bandwidth users could fall into a small number of complete types, each sharing the same valuation function, beliefs, and other relevant parameters. With capacity scaled per user, the type masses could describe the population. But a continuum of individual agents makes each person’s bid negligible, changing the strategic incentives in the paper’s finite-agent auction. Treating each type as a single bidding bloc avoids that issue only by changing who the strategic players are. So this suggests a possible analytic population model, but not a faithful computational mirror anchored in a result of the paper.

The divisible resource is continuous, but the paper’s society remains a finite list of named agents. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper supplies no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 1 proves existence and uniqueness of a market-clearing bid total under assumptions on demand functions. Theorem 2 characterizes when optimal bids form a Nash equilibrium. Theorems 3 and 4 establish individual rationality and nondecreasing social welfare. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The \(O(N)\) allocation calculation is a simple implementation observation, and the experiments are empirical comparisons.

A plausible population of users grouped by complete type is easy to describe, especially for sensor or bandwidth markets. But the best computational mirror still needs a well-defined input and task. In an atomless population, one person’s bid has no effect on the aggregate price, so the paper’s individual strategic deviations become price-taking behavior. Computing market clearing for finitely many types could be made into an algorithmic problem, but the paper gives no computational representation for its general demand functions. Making each type a strategic bloc restores strategic influence only by specifying a new game and its objectives.

The other theorems do not supply a stronger anchor. Theorem 2’s beliefs concern distributions over other agents’ total bids; the paper gives no computational encoding for those distributions. The deal-optimization theorems prove welfare properties of a mechanism, rather than the complexity of computing its outcomes. A continuous version could preserve those properties, but that would still be an analytic mechanism result.

So the proponent raised no computational anchor that survives the paper’s own subject matter: none was raised in the first place.
