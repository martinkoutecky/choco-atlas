What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. For every \(\rho\in(-\infty,1)\), the augmented trading post mechanism \(\mathrm{ATP}(\rho)\), where each agent’s bids satisfy \(\sum_j b_{ij}^{1-\rho}\le 1\), has a pure Nash equilibrium and every equilibrium allocation maximizes CES welfare with parameter \(\rho\). The proof also says an equilibrium can be computed in polynomial time, using the price-curve equilibrium results it cites. The theorem’s statement is about implementation rather than a named complexity class, so this is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result. I do not see a numbered result in the paper establishing NP-hardness or another hardness classification.

A plausible regime is a large population of network flows that repeatedly use the same link sets: for example, many simultaneous sessions with only a modest number of distinct routes. A type \(t\) is the full set \(R_t\) of links a flow requires; agents of that type have identical bandwidth utility and budget. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of flows of type \(t\), with \(\tau\ll n\). Link capacities are expressed per unit of population, so the model describes a growing cohort with correspondingly provisioned capacity. That normalization keeps per-flow rates meaningful as the cohort grows.

My proposed mirror is the Continuous CES Bandwidth Equilibrium problem. Its input is a finite link set \(M\), rational per-capita capacities \(s_j\), a support of types \(R_t\subseteq M\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and a fixed \(\rho\in(-\infty,1)\). A type receives a per-flow allocation \(y_{tj}\ge 0\), with utility \(U_t=\min_{j\in R_t}y_{tj}\); feasibility requires \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{tj}\le s_j\) for each link. The objective is to maximize \(\left(\sum_t\mu_t U_t^\rho\right)^{1/\rho}\) when \(\rho\ne0\), and \(\prod_t U_t^{\mu_t}\) when \(\rho=0\).

A solution is an allocation \(y\) and nonnegative price coefficients \(q_j\) such that each type’s allocation maximizes its bandwidth utility subject to \(\sum_j q_j y_{tj}^{1-\rho}\le1\), and the market clears: \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{tj}\le s_j\), with equality whenever \(q_j>0\). Equivalently, this asks for a price-curve equilibrium with \(g_j(z)=q_jz^{1-\rho}\). For a finite-precision algorithmic version, the output can be an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to such an equilibrium. This gives a concrete problem over the support, with allocation and price variables indexed by \(t,j\), rather than by individual flows or all possible routes.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. Its finite-support allocation problem is a convex optimization problem with \(O(\tau m)\) allocation variables, and the paper’s cited price-curve machinery supplies the equilibrium interpretation. The natural follow-up questions are the approximation and bit complexity in \(m,\tau,L,\varepsilon\), and how quickly equilibria of finite high-multiplicity trading-post games approach this continuum equilibrium.

The scope is Theorem 4.1’s CES welfare implementation for \(\rho<1\). It does not cover the paper’s dominant-strategy impossibility result, Theorem 5.1, or its maxmin results, Theorems 5.2 and 5.3; those depend on strategic notions that this mirror does not preserve. It also leaves out utilitarian welfare at \(\rho=1\).

The weak point is that the continuum changes the strategic question. In an atomless population, one flow’s deviation does not affect aggregate bids, so the equilibrium becomes price-taking behavior. That is closely related to the price-curve equilibria used in the paper, but it drops the strategic interaction that makes Theorem 4.1 distinctive. I think the authors would recognize this as a continuous-population version of their bandwidth allocation and welfare problem; they might reasonably say it mirrors the allocation result more faithfully than the theorem’s strategic implementation result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor is Theorem 4.1. The strongest objection is that its central claim is strategic: finite agents choose bids, and every pure Nash equilibrium implements a CES optimum. In an atomless population, one agent’s deviation has zero effect on aggregate bids. The limiting equilibrium is therefore price-taking, which removes the feature that distinguishes Theorem 4.1 from the price-curve results it builds on. Treating each whole type as one strategic player would preserve an effect on aggregate bids, but would make a type’s entire mass act as a single agent; that is a different game.

That objection does not defeat the best mirror, though. With many sessions sharing route sets, type masses and per-capita link capacities make sense. Maximizing weighted CES welfare over those types is well-posed, and its price-curve equilibrium formulation is a natural question about a continuous society. The paper’s cited price-curve work is not itself a novelty collision: it treats a finite set of agents. Nor does the weighted problem’s likely convex-program solution settle whether the question is worth asking; the programme explicitly counts tractable outcomes.

So the negative case is weak. The mirror changes the strategic interpretation, and its algorithmic core may be a direct support-weighted version of the paper’s existing welfare optimization. But neither point rules out a worthwhile continuous-population question, and I see no identity dependence or degeneration that would do so. Under the programme’s stated standard, Theorem 4.1 survives: the case against it is a caution about what the mirror means, not a reason to reject it.
