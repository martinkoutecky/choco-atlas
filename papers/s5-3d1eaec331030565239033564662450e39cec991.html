The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed finite seller-buyer network motif and a finite set of buyer types \(t=(v,R)\), with society described by a mass distribution \(\mu\) over type profiles on the motif and \(k\) items supplied per motif, construct a computable direct mechanism that assigns integral bundles and payments within each motif, is strategy-proof and individually rational, and guarantees normalized aggregate surplus at least \(\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor/k\) of optimum.

The model it lives in

A continuum of copies of a fixed finite network motif, with \(\mu\) giving the mass of each valuation and diffusion-role profile; supply scales per motif, local allocations remain integral, and welfare and payments aggregate by mass.

The objection that survived

Buyer-type masses alone do not specify reachability or the APG ordering; the mirror needs a fixed network motif or equivalent relational structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's strategy-proofness and welfare guarantee. It leaves Theorem 2's mechanism-design impossibility, Proposition 1, Theorem 1, and the GIDM counterexample outside this computational anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the GAPG rule extend to this mass-distribution model while preserving strategy-proofness and its \(\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor/k\) welfare guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby continuous angle is a large social-network auction with many buyers drawn from a small set of complete types: each type specifies its marginal values and diffusion role, and its mass is the fraction of buyers of that type. The mechanism would assign buyers integral bundles and payments, and the question would be how much worst-case welfare a strategy-proof mechanism can guarantee. That is a plausible population continuization of the paper’s economic problem.

But it is not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. Theorem 2, proved here, says that no strategy-proof mechanism is \((\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor/k+\epsilon)\)-efficient for any \(\epsilon>0\). Theorem 3, also proved here, says the GAPG mechanism is strategy-proof and \(\lfloor\sqrt{k}\rfloor/k\)-efficient. These are bounds on mechanism-design performance, not results about computational complexity or algorithms. The paper gives no named result asserting hardness, tractability, or another computational property of a problem.

So I can’t give a continuous computational problem per eligible anchor without stretching the programme’s scope. The proposed angle continues the population and the welfare-guarantee question, but it leads to mechanism-design analysis, not the computational questions ChoCo targets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–3 establish mechanism-design properties: impossibility under a bundle of axioms, welfare-guarantee bounds, and strategy-proofness of proposed mechanisms. The GIDM example is a counterexample to strategy-proofness. None asks how hard it is to compute an outcome, mechanism, or optimum, or gives an algorithm or complexity bound. In ChoCo’s terms, these are not computational anchors.

The proposed mirror—buyers drawn from a distribution over value and diffusion types, with a question about the welfare guarantee of strategy-proof mechanisms—keeps the paper’s subject, but it remains a mechanism-design question. Turning it into “compute an allocation” or “find the best strategy-proof mechanism” would introduce a new computational problem, not continue one of this paper’s results. That problem might be worthwhile on its own; it would not make this paper a computational mirror.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many repeated communities with the same buyer roles, values, and network motif. Scaling the supply with population could avoid the trivial limit where finitely many items go to a measure-zero set of buyers. But a distribution over roles alone does not specify who can reach whom, and reachability and the APG ordering depend on the network. A continuum network model would need to preserve individual deviations that can affect diffusion; in an atomless graphon, changing one buyer’s links is a measure-zero change, while keeping repeated finite communities retains the local strategic structure by construction. Either route could support a new model, but neither supplies a computational claim from this paper to mirror.

This leaves the negative case strong on ChoCo’s scope, but not as a literal claim that no useful continuous project could ever arise from the paper. A repeated-community model could be sensible research. The point is narrower: the paper’s named results are about what truthful mechanisms can guarantee, not the computational landscape of a problem, so there is no worthwhile computational mirror of these results for the programme to pursue.
