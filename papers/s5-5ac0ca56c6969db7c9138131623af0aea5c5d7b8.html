The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\) with \(|C|=m\) and a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), find a candidate \(c\in C\) and masses \(x_{t,d}\ge0\) such that \(\sum_{d\in C}x_{t,d}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,d}=1/m\) for every \(d\), and \(x_{t,d}=0\) unless type \(t\) ranks \(c\) at least as high as \(d\).

The model it lives in

A bipartite flow model with ranking types as supply nodes of mass \(\mu_t\), candidates as demand nodes of mass \(1/m\), and edges from \(t\) to \(d\) exactly when \(t\) ranks the selected candidate \(c\) at least as high as \(d\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's candidate-selection and matching-witness result. It leaves the metric-distortion guarantee in Theorem 3, the equivalences in Theorem 4, and the matching characterization in Theorem 5 outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The defensible case is narrow: a continuous version of the candidate-selection problem in Theorem 1, rather than a mirror of the whole note.

Theorem 1, cited from Gkatzelis et al. (2020) and recalled here rather than proved here, says: for every election, some candidate \(c\) has a domination graph \(G(c)\) admitting a fractional perfect matching, and such a candidate can be found in polynomial time. The paper contains no named hardness result to anchor a continuization; this is its clearest named algorithmic anchor.

I would call the mirror Continuous Domination-Matching Selection. An instance is a candidate set \(C\) of size \(m\), and a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings, each with rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Write \(\tau=|T|\). Find a candidate \(c\) and nonnegative masses \(x_{t,d}\) such that

\[ \sum_{d\in C} x_{t,d}=\mu_t \quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_{t\in T} x_{t,d}=\frac{1}{m} \quad(d\in C), \]

and \(x_{t,d}=0\) unless type \(t\) ranks \(c\) at least as high as \(d\). The \(x_{t,d}\) are the matching: they assign each type’s mass across candidates it can be matched to in \(G(c)\). A solution is the candidate and a feasible assignment. There is no welfare objective in this result; it is a feasibility-and-selection question. The candidate capacity \(1/m\) is the paper’s capacity \(n/m\), normalized by population size.

The regime is a large electorate whose members are indistinguishable for this problem whenever they have the same ranking: for example, a large public consultation with many voters but relatively few distinct ballot types, so \(n\gg\tau\). Rankings are complete types here because the paper’s domination graph uses rankings alone. This is a genuine population continuization: the mass is voter mass. It does not make outcomes or preferences probabilistic. Aggregation also preserves the matching condition exactly: summing individual voter assignments by ranking gives \(x\), and a feasible \(x\) can be split among the voters of each type.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. For each proposed \(c\), build a flow network from a source to the \(\tau\) types, then to candidates allowed by the ranking condition, then to a sink. Give each type source-edge capacity \(\mu_t\) and each candidate sink-edge capacity \(1/m\). A flow of value \(1\) is a witness for \(c\). Testing all \(m\) candidates takes time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length \(L\). Existence follows from Theorem 1 by realizing rational masses as proportions in a finite election and normalizing its matching; the support-sized flow algorithm gives the compact computation without expanding that election.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it keeps their candidate, their domination relation, and their fractional perfect matching, while representing repeated rankings as mass. Its scope is Theorem 1’s selection result. It does not by itself continuize the paper’s metric-distortion guarantee or the equivalences in Theorem 4. Those motivate a further question: can a continuous model of the plurality-proportional candidate clones recover the paper’s distortion-\(3\) result, and what clone structure should it use? The note also already raises the related question of achieving distortion \(3\) without cloning.

The weakest point is that the discrete matching is already fractional, so this mirror mainly changes the input representation and exposes the high-multiplicity runtime in \(\tau\) rather than \(n\). The anchor is also a result cited from earlier work, not a new complexity result of this note. That makes this a modest positive case, but the high-multiplicity regime is natural for elections and the continuous question is precise and faithful to the theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror adds a new input encoding, but no new structure to the problem. For each candidate \(c\), the matching constraints are exactly the finite domination graph’s constraints with voters of the same ranking aggregated into one row. Every rational distribution \(\mu\) can be expanded into a finite electorate by clearing denominators, and any matching in either representation can be converted exactly into one in the other. The proposed algorithm is therefore the familiar flow test applied to grouped vertices; its running time in \(\tau\) and \(L\) is a useful compression, but the computational question has no objective, pricing problem, or new feasibility phenomenon for mass to reveal. That makes it a modest high-multiplicity reformulation of Theorem 1’s already-fractional matching certificate.

This is a limited case against the mirror, not a decisive one. Theorem 1 is cited from earlier work, which makes it a thin anchor for this note’s contribution, but it is numbered and does assert polynomial-time selection. And the exact aggregation argument cuts both ways: it gives a clear continuous model, a compact algorithm, and a plausible large-election regime. Under ChoCo’s stated criteria, those are legitimate reasons to study it. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists here; the case against rests on the mirror being incremental, and that is weaker than showing the question is not worth asking.
