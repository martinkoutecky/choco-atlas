The continuous mirror question

Given two alternatives \(s,t\), a finite set of role types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_u>0\) summing to \(1\), and an allowed option set \(A_u\subseteq T\cup\{s,t\}\) for each type \(u\), choose a revised mass profile \(\eta_{u,B}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_B\eta_{u,B}=\mu_u\). Its cost is \(\sum_{u\in T}\sum_{B\subseteq T\cup\{s,t\}}\eta_{u,B}|A_u\triangle B|\). Each option targeting a role permits delegation to any member of that role. Is there a revised profile of cost at most a given rational budget \(\beta\), leaving a well-formed election, such that \(s\) wins in every valid delegation, where each agent chooses one allowed option and almost every delegation path ends at an alternative? With two alternatives, majority and plurality coincide.

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents with an atomless mass measure, grouped into finitely many interchangeable role types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_u\). Agents of a role share its allowed delegation options and per-arc edit cost; a mass edit splits each type among revised option sets. The objective is to minimize total edited mass times edit cost, subject to the designated alternative winning under every valid delegation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s Majority-All and Plurality-All Election Bribery results. It leaves the Winner Determination results, One Election Bribery results, and Delegation Bribery results without a proposed mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does All-Mass Election Bribery remain NP-hard when fractional edits are allowed, and can hardness be established for a meaningful mass-budget parameter such as \(\kappa=\lceil\beta/\min_u\mu_u\rceil\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper by a reduction from Clique. It says that Majority-All Election Bribery and Plurality-All Election Bribery are each NP-hard and W[1]-hard with respect to the arc-edit budget \(k\), even with two alternatives and an election graph that is a DAG of maximum outdegree and depth two. The problem is constructive: can we edit at most \(k\) arcs so that a designated alternative wins in every delegation?

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large online consultation or party federation organized around recurring delegation roles. Many members may share the same direct-voting options and be willing to delegate to the same role classes. For example, a large cohort may be willing to delegate to either of two classes of representatives, while each representative class has its own fixed options. If each role has \(q\) members, then the population can be \(q\) times the number of roles while the support consists only of those roles. In a Clique-shaped instance, vertex roles and edge roles can each have many members; each edge-role member can delegate to either endpoint’s vertex role. The type-interaction graph still carries the combinatorics, while the population is large relative to its support.

Here is the mirror I would ask authors to consider: All-Mass Election Bribery. An instance has two alternatives \(C=\{s,t\}\), a finite set of role types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_u>0\) summing to \(1\), and an outgoing option set \(A_u\subseteq T\cup C\) for each type \(u\). An option in \(C\) means direct voting; an option \(v\in T\) means delegating to any member of role \(v\). All members of a role share its options and per-arc edit cost, but may choose their delegation independently.

The input also gives a target \(s\), a rational mass budget \(\beta\), and a rule \(R\in\{\text{majority},\text{plurality}\}\). An edit profile splits each type’s mass among revised option sets: \(\eta_{u,B}\geq0\), with \(\sum_B\eta_{u,B}=\mu_u\). Its cost is

\[ \sum_{u\in T}\sum_{B\subseteq T\cup C}\eta_{u,B}\lvert A_u\triangle B\rvert. \]

The question is whether there is such a profile of cost at most \(\beta\), leaving a well-formed election, for which \(s\) wins under \(R\) in every valid delegation. A valid delegation assigns each voter one allowed option, has no delegation cycles, and sends almost every voter’s path to an alternative. The vote mass of an alternative is the measure of voters whose paths end there. A solution is the revised mass profile; the universal win condition is part of the decision problem. With two alternatives, majority and plurality winners coincide, matching both branches of Theorem 2.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. The Clique reduction’s structure survives the move to mass. In its construction, a vertex type’s arc to \(t\) must be removed completely before its endpoint becomes unusable for a worst-case delegation. Any positive residual subgroup that can still reach \(t\) can receive the votes of an incident edge cohort. Thus fractional edits can contribute their own forced \(s\)-voters, but they do not force an edge cohort to \(s\) unless both endpoint types are fully blocked. The reduction’s threshold then still requires a size-\(\ell\) clique. This suggests NP-hardness persists despite continuous edit amounts. A corresponding parameterized question is whether W[1]-hardness holds for \(\kappa=\lceil\beta/\min_u\mu_u\rceil\); that is a proposed scale-aware parameter, not an automatic identification of the paper’s \(k\).

This mirrors only the paper’s All Election Bribery result. It does not claim a mirror for One Election Bribery, Winner Determination, or Delegation Bribery. Further questions include whether the continuous problem admits useful approximation or support-parameter algorithms, and how its answer maps back to finite elections at high multiplicity. The fluid-mechanics work cited by the paper is relevant background, but its analytic questions do not answer this computational one.

The soft spot is that the paper’s arcs name individual agents, whereas this mirror lets a voter name a recipient role and delegate to any member of it. That is an additional modeling choice. I think it is plausible for large systems that expose delegation by representative class, and the mirror preserves the paper’s central question—whether arc edits can guarantee a winner across all delegations. If the authors require identity-specific recipients, this case becomes substantially weaker.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Election Bribery acts on a network of named people, where each person is both a voter and a possible relay for many other votes. In Theorem 2’s reduction, edge agents point to particular vertex agents; deleting a vertex agent’s arc can change where an entire group’s votes may end up. That makes the arc’s cost and its effect depend on different things: the edit applies to one person, while its consequences can reach a large share of the electorate.

In a faithful population limit, a pivotal representative may have zero mass but still receive positive delegated mass. Charging edits by the mass of the people whose options change then makes that representative’s arc edits cost zero. Charging a fixed amount per representative avoids that collapse, but no longer measures edits by population mass. The proponent’s model avoids the problem by letting voters delegate to a role type and charging for edits to a mass of that type. That is a coherent model, but it changes “delegate to this person” into “delegate to anyone in this class.” The Clique construction depends on endpoint roles and their incidence pattern, so the distinction matters.

This is a real weakness in the proposed mirror, especially for liquid-democracy settings where trust attaches to particular people. But it does not defeat every plausible scenario. A large federation could let members delegate to interchangeable representative roles, and a type-level graph over those roles would retain a computational question that the paper’s analytic liquid-democracy work does not settle. The mirror would be a modeling extension, but the brief allows a co-modified model the authors would recognize.

So I cannot make a convincing universal case against the anchor. The identity-sensitive version risks zero-cost pivotal edits; the role-based version survives that objection and is plausible in some settings. The negative case is therefore weak precisely at its hardest burden: showing that no such setting makes the continuous question worthwhile.
