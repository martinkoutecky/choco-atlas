The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee limit \(k\), a fixed tie-breaking order, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) ballot types \(t=(A_t,D_t)\), find \(T\subseteq C\). For each \(U\subseteq C\), retain disapprovals \(D_t\cap U\), set \(a_\mu(c)=\sum_{t:c\in A_t}\mu_t\) and \(d_{\mu,U}(c)=\sum_{t:c\in D_t\cap U}\mu_t\), and make \(c\) eligible when \(a_\mu(c)>d_{\mu,U}(c)\), at price \(p_U(c)=a_\mu(c)/(a_\mu(c)-d_{\mu,U}(c))\). Run weighted Tax-MES with initial per-unit budgets \(k\) and affordability condition \(\sum_{t:c\in A_t}\mu_t\min(b_t,\rho)=p_U(c)\), updating each \(b_t\) after a purchase. Writing \(W_U\) for the resulting positive committee, require that no candidate outside \(T\) belongs to \(W_T\), and that for every \(c\in T\setminus W_T\), candidate \(c\) belongs to \(W_{T\setminus\{c\}}\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete ternary ballots \(t=(A_t,D_t)\) with mass \(\mu_t\). The decision variable is the retained-veto set \(T\subseteq C\); feasibility requires CVD and IMVD under weighted Tax-MES, with no objective beyond finding a feasible set.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2’s CVD and IMVD veto-set construction, instantiated with Tax-MES on weighted profiles; it leaves the proportionality guarantees, PAV results, and counterfactual instability results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can weighted Tax-MES on rational ballot masses be implemented exactly in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length, yielding an explicit polynomial bound for the Proposition 2 construction?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest computational anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says that for every election and every rule \(R\), there is a veto set \(T\subseteq C\) at which \(R\) satisfies both Counterfactual Veto Deletion (CVD) and Inclusion-Maximal Veto Deletion (IMVD); if \(R\) is polynomial-time computable, a greedy algorithm finds such a \(T\) in polynomial time. CVD means that no candidate outside \(T\) is selected when the election is run with disapprovals restricted to \(T\). IMVD means that for every veto retained in \(T\) whose candidate is not selected, removing that veto would make CVD fail. The proposition is a computational result proved here, though it is conditional on being able to compute \(R\).

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large civic deliberation platform deciding among a fixed slate of proposals. Many participants may submit the same ternary ballot—approve, disapprove, or abstain—so the electorate can have millions of people but a moderate number \(\tau\) of distinct ballot types. That scenario fits the paper’s discussion of participatory budgeting and deliberation platforms. The type is a complete ballot \(t=(A_t,D_t)\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants submitting it, with rational masses summing to \(1\).

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous CVD–IMVD Veto Selection for Tax-MES. The input is a candidate set \(C\), a committee limit \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) ballot types, together with a fixed tie-breaking order. For any proposed veto set \(U\subseteq C\), restrict each type’s disapprovals to \(D_t\cap U\), and run a weighted version of Tax-MES. Its support and opposition for candidate \(c\) are
\[ a_\mu(c)=\sum_{t:c\in A_t}\mu_t,\qquad d_{\mu,U}(c)=\sum_{t:c\in D_t\cap U}\mu_t. \]
As in the paper, candidates with \(a_\mu(c)\le d_{\mu,U}(c)\) are ineligible; each other candidate has price \(a_\mu(c)/(a_\mu(c)-d_{\mu,U}(c))\). In weighted MES, each unit of population mass starts with budget \(k\), and affordability aggregates contributions by mass: a supporter type with current per-unit budget \(b_t\) contributes \(\mu_t\min(b_t,\rho)\). The question is to output \(T\subseteq C\) such that, writing \(W_U\) for the weighted Tax-MES outcome under veto set \(U\):

  • no candidate outside \(T\) belongs to \(W_T\); and
  • for every \(c\in T\setminus W_T\), CVD fails under \(T\setminus\{c\}\).

The output is \(T\); the \(W_U\) outcomes are used to check its properties. This weighted rule preserves the paper’s underlying task: choose which disapprovals to retain so the resulting election has the specified counterfactual justification. The paper’s Appendix A.2 explicitly discusses rules on weighted votes and gradual disapproval removal, which makes this population-based formulation especially plausible to its authors.

I expect this problem to be tractable. For this weighted Tax-MES definition, each round has at most \(k\le m\) selections. Candidate prices and affordability breakpoints are rational functions of the input masses and current budgets; sorting the support types and solving the resulting piecewise-linear affordability equations gives a polynomial-time weighted rule. Proposition 2’s greedy construction then applies with polynomially many calls to that rule. The running time should be stated in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the bit length of the rational masses and other numeric input.

This case covers Proposition 2’s veto-set selection result. It does not turn the paper’s proportionality theorems into computational-complexity results. Natural follow-up questions are whether the weighted Tax-MES implementation has a clean exact bit-complexity bound, and what happens if the decision variable is a fractional veto weight \(x_c\in[0,1]\), as suggested by the appendix’s continuous-removal discussion. The latter would still be a population-continuization project only if the society remains the distribution \(\mu\); fractional veto weights alone would be a different continuity angle.

The soft spot is that Proposition 2 is a generic wrapper around a rule that is already polynomial-time computable. Its continuous version looks tractable largely because the underlying rule can be evaluated on a compact type distribution, so it offers little new optimization structure. Also, although the paper mentions NP-hard PAV winner computation in prose, it gives no numbered hardness result to anchor a continuous hardness-dissolution question. This is therefore a defensible but modest positive case: the authors have a clear weighted-voter foothold, and their veto-selection problem has a precise continuous form, but the paper’s strongest computational anchor is not its central proportionality analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Proposition 2’s algorithmic content sits in the candidate set, not in the population. Its greedy procedure deletes vetoes and calls a rule; the electorate supplies data to those calls but is not itself optimized over. In the proposed mirror, the type masses would affect the rule’s calculations, but the wrapper would still make the same sequence of candidate-level decisions. That makes this a thin continuization: a weighted implementation of an existing rule, followed by a generic finite-set argument.

That is a reason to doubt this is the ChoCo programme’s most revealing mirror, but it does not defeat it. Proposition 2 really is a named computational result, albeit conditional on being able to compute \(R\). And a large deliberation platform with many participants sharing a moderate number of ternary ballots is a credible high-multiplicity regime. A ballot \((A_t,D_t)\) is a complete type for this rule; the model does not need voter identities.

The proposed continuous problem is also coherent after one repair. For Tax-MES, weighted affordability for candidate \(c\) must reach its price \(p(c)\), not \(1\): with per-unit budgets \(b_t\), the condition is \(\sum_t \mu_t\min(b_t,\rho)=p(c)\). With that correction, the weighted rule appears amenable to exact computation from the support types, and Proposition 2’s greedy veto-set construction can be applied to it. Establishing the bit complexity would be real work, but the likely tractability of the mirror is not an objection under the programme’s criteria.

Nor can I make “the result is only a generic wrapper,” or “the continuous answer is probably easy,” carry the negative case. Those points limit how much this anchor says about the paper’s proportionality analysis; they do not make the question ill-posed or unworthy. The paper’s mention of weighted votes and gradual disapproval removal is not already a continuous computational treatment of veto-set selection.

So the honest case against is modest: this mirror may be a low-priority ChoCo result because its main algorithmic step is inherited from Proposition 2, rather than arising from new population-side structure. I cannot defend the stronger claim that there is no worthwhile mirror in any scenario. The anchor survives that challenge.
