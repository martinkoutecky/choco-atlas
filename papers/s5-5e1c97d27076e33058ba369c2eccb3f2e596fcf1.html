What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue covers the support-size statements in Theorems 1 and 3; it leaves Theorem 2’s profile characterization and the geometric propositions without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a weak population-level reading of Theorem 3, proved here: the maximum number of pairwise distinct preferences in a two-dimensional \(\ell_1\)-Euclidean profile over \(m\) candidates is \(\Theta(m^4)\). Imagine a large voting-advice population distributed across a two-dimensional policy space. A type is a complete ranking induced by distance to the candidates; its mass is the fraction of respondents in the region inducing that ranking. One could ask for the largest support size achievable by choosing candidate locations and a distribution over voter locations. A solution would give those locations and a distribution assigning positive mass to every ranking in the support. Theorem 3 gives the same \(\Theta(m^4)\) bound for that support-size question.

But this is a lift of a structural geometry result, not a computational mirror of a named computational result. The distribution’s masses do no work: any nonempty preference region can be given positive mass, and the paper explicitly treats duplicate preferences as irrelevant to realizability. Theorem 1 (proved here), which gives the exact maximum of 19 preferences for four candidates under \(\ell_1\), has the same limitation. Theorem 2 (proved here) characterizes four-candidate \(\ell_2\)-Euclidean profiles as subprofiles of three specified maximal profiles; it likewise does not assert a computational complexity result.

So the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind to anchor a continuous-complexity question. It mentions Peters et al.’s NP-hardness result for recognizing \(\ell_2\)-Euclidean profiles in dimension greater than one, but that is prior work cited in the paper, not a numbered result of this paper. The support-size formulation is the strongest plausible population analogy I see; its weakness is decisive for this assignment: it adds masses to an extremal geometry problem without making population multiplicity or computation part of the question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper proves structural facts about which sets of rankings can arise, not a computational result about a society. Theorem 3 bounds the number of distinct preferences; Theorem 1 gives the exact bound for four candidates; Theorem 2 characterizes the feasible four-candidate \(\ell_2\) profiles. None states a complexity or algorithmic claim. Recognition appears as prior work and as a future direction, not as a result proved here.

Theorem 3 is the strongest proposed anchor, but its statistic is already the number of ranking types in a finite profile. In a continuous society over rankings, assign positive rational mass to each preference in any realizable support. The support size is unchanged, whatever those masses are. Conversely, each profile counted by the theorem gives such a distribution. Thus the proposed \(\Theta(m^4)\) question is exactly the paper’s support-count question with arbitrary weights attached; multiplicity and mass do no work. Theorem 1 has the same issue. This does not make high multiplicity an unsensible regime: it means these particular results discard multiplicities by construction.

Theorem 2 fares no better under the stronger weighted formulation. A weighted society is representable precisely when its positive-mass support is a feasible profile. Its characterization therefore carries over by ignoring the weights and checking whether that support is a subprofile of one of the three maximal profiles. The continuous version adds no new condition or computational task. Likewise, one could ask whether a weighted society is Euclidean, but that is recognition on its support—the recognition problem the paper cites and leaves for future work, with masses irrelevant to the answer.

A mass-sensitive question could be built on top of these feasible supports—for example, about changing an election outcome by moving population mass. That could be worthwhile research, but it adds an objective absent from the paper and is not a mirror of any result the authors prove. The negative case is strongest for the proposed anchors and for recognition as a weighted reformulation. I cannot establish that no useful population-level problem could ever be built around Euclidean preferences; I do not see one in this paper’s results that merits a ChoCo mirror.
