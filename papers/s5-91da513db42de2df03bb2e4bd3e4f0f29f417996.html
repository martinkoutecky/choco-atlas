The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(S\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_s\) summing to \(1\), integer prior cumulative allocations \(a_s\), integer current demands \(d_s\), and common per-user fair share \(f\), compute integer allocations \(x_s\) to every member of type \(s\), subject to \(0\le x_s\le d_s\) and \(\sum_{s\in S}\mu_s x_s\le f\), that maximize \(\min_{s\in S}(a_s+x_s)\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of resource users, grouped by identical prior cumulative allocation and current demand. Each member receives an integer allocation, while total capacity scales to \(f\) per unit mass. For a proposed integer minimum \(z\), feasibility is exactly \(z\le a_s+d_s\) for every type and \(\sum_s\mu_s\max(0,z-a_s)\le f\), giving a scalar threshold problem.

The objection that survived

The minimum can be unstable as a cohort's mass tends to zero, and the paper does not establish small \(\tau\) for its workloads; this bounds the regimes covered but leaves the fixed positive-mass question intact.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 4's one-quantum max-min cumulative-allocation guarantee for \(\alpha=0\) with enough initial credits. It leaves the other numbered guarantees, the empirical evaluations, and the full dynamic and strategic mechanism unmodeled.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the type-compressed guarantee be extended across multiple quanta while preserving the paper's online information constraints?
  • What finite-population approximation best captures the minimum when a cohort's mass tends to zero?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow. This paper has no named result asserting that a computational problem is NP-hard, in \(\mathrm{P}\), or belongs to another complexity class. Theorem 1 concerns Pareto efficiency, Theorem 2 online strategy-proofness, Theorem 3 collusion, Theorem 4 a fairness optimum, and Theorem 5 a worst-case fairness ratio. So there is no qualifying complexity anchor under the requested rule.

The closest named result is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that for any quantum \(q\), once users’ allocations through quantum \(q-1\) are fixed and their demands at \(q\) are given, Karma’s allocation maximizes the minimum total allocation any user has received from quantum \(0\) through \(q\). The paper’s formal guarantees are for \(\alpha=0\), with enough initial credits to prevent users from running out.

A plausible mirror is the Fluid One-Quantum Karma Fairness Problem. Consider a unit-mass population with finite support \(S\). Each type \(s\in S\) has rational mass \(\mu_s>0\), with \(\sum_s\mu_s=1\), and state \((a_s,d_s)\): every member has received \(a_s\) resource slices before the current quantum and now demands \(d_s\) slices. All users have fair share \(f\); the total supply scales with population size, giving capacity \(f\) per unit mass. Choose an integer allocation \(x_s\) for each type, with \(0\le x_s\le d_s\) and \(\sum_s\mu_s x_s\le f\). The goal is to maximize
\[ \min_{s\in S}(a_s+x_s). \]
A solution is an optimal allocation vector \(x\) and its attained minimum. Each individual still receives an integral bundle; the aggregate resource used by a type can be fractional because its mass is fractional.

This is a tractable mirror. For a proposed minimum \(z\), type \(s\) needs at least \(\max(0,z-a_s)\) slices per user. So \(z\) is feasible exactly when \(z\le a_s+d_s\) for every type and
\[ \sum_s \mu_s\max(0,z-a_s)\le f. \]
The largest feasible integer \(z\) can be found by binary search over the input range, using arithmetic polynomial in the support size and bit length. Assigning \(x_s=\max(0,z-a_s)\) then attains it. In the paper’s \(\alpha=0\) setting, credits rank users by their cumulative allocations, so this is the fluid version of the fairness objective Theorem 4 says Karma achieves.

The regime would be a large shared-resource system—such as a cluster with many tenant services or cache workloads—where many users have the same fair share, current demand, and prior cumulative allocation. A cohort of such users is one type; its mass is its fraction of the population. Repeated service templates and discrete slice allocations make this plausible, and the paper reports workloads with thousands of users. But its trace analysis does not establish that the exact state support \(\tau\) is small. The case is strongest for systems where \(n\) is large and repeated workload histories keep \(\tau\) well below \(n\); it is not a claim about every multi-tenant system.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. Further questions include whether the fluid allocation can be iterated over a horizon while preserving an online information model, how the state support evolves, and whether the paper’s strategy-proofness and collusion guarantees have faithful nonatomic versions. This mirror covers Theorem 4’s one-quantum fairness guarantee only; it does not continuize the paper’s full dynamic mechanism or its strategic results.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 4 is an optimality guarantee, not a complexity result for an independently posed hard problem. This mirror captures a fairness subproblem the authors care about, but it does not provide the discrete-hardness question that would let the continuization programme test whether hardness dissolves. The right positive claim is therefore that the paper admits a credible, tractable population mirror for one named guarantee—not that it supplies a strong complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result about computational complexity. Theorems 1–4 establish properties of Karma, and Theorem 5 gives a fairness bound. That leaves ChoCo without a direct complexity result to continuize.

The proponent’s Theorem 4 mirror is the strongest candidate. It is mathematically coherent: if a unit-mass cohort has prior allocation \(a_s\), demand \(d_s\), and mass \(\mu_s\), then a target minimum \(z\) is feasible when \(z\le a_s+d_s\) for every type and \(\sum_s \mu_s\max(0,z-a_s)\le f\). The paper’s type definition and workload setting make such cohorts plausible. I cannot dismiss this as a mistaken model.

Its weak point is that the fairness objective is a minimum over users, while the input is a population distribution. One exceptional user among \(n\) can determine the discrete minimum, yet that user’s mass \(1/n\) vanishes as the population grows. The limiting distribution then loses information the theorem’s objective needs. Keeping that user as a positive-mass type preserves the question for each finite instance, but the optimum can jump when the type’s mass reaches zero. Replacing the minimum with an essential minimum or a quantile would give a more stable population statistic, at the cost of changing the theorem’s fairness criterion.

The proposed mirror also depends on exact prior allocations and current demands. Those state variables can distinguish nearly every user, so the paper gives no evidence that the support size \(\tau\) is small in the traced regimes. Cloned services with identical allocation histories would supply a credible high-multiplicity scenario, though it is narrower than merely having many tenants.

Finally, this is not a computational challenge identified by the paper. The mirror reduces to a scalar feasibility test over type records. That simplicity alone would not make it unworthy, but it does mean the anchor offers little of the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

This is the strongest case against the mirror: its fairness objective is fragile under vanishing-mass populations, and its computational scope is limited. It still does not establish that no worthwhile scenario exists. For fixed positive-mass cohorts with shared histories, the mirror survives, and a thoughtful reader could reasonably regard it as a useful tractable extension of Theorem 4.
