The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no anchor: they identify no numbered computational result and decline to invent one. That is the strongest case against a mirror here. The paper defines fairness metrics, discusses a sampling approach, and reports an empirical ensemble; it does not state a complexity problem or prove an algorithmic result about one. Figure 5’s 19,184 sampled plans are an example, not a complexity guarantee.

The strongest candidate for a mirror is the sampling section. One could replace the finite tract graph with a geographic region \(\Omega\) carrying a population density and partisan preference densities, then ask about connected, equal-mass partitions: how hard is it to optimize a fairness score over them, or to sample valid plans? But that would require a new model of continuous geometry and a new algorithmic question. If the map is fixed, computing district vote shares is just integration; if maps are to be chosen or sampled, the main challenge is the geometry and constraints on partitions. The paper neither formulates nor analyzes that problem. Keeping its graph and legal constraints instead leaves the plan space as partitions of finite geographic units, so making voters continuous does not give the paper a new computational result to mirror.

The symmetry and efficiency-gap sections offer even less of an anchor. Their scores can be applied to fractional vote totals, but that extends the metrics’ inputs; it does not turn the paper’s normative discussion into a complexity question about a continuous society. A spatial continuum model of districting could still be worthwhile as a separate research direction. I do not see a basis for calling it a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results. That is a strong negative case, though not proof that no useful continuous redistricting problem can be formulated.
