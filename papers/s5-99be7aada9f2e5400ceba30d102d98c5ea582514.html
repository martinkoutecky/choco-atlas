What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive scenario I can see is a very large advisory system—say, a regulator’s standing pool of accredited experts—with \(N\) experts but only \(\tau\) recurring classes. Class membership could specify an expert’s reputational weight \(\lambda\) and signal accuracy. For a binary project state \(\theta\), an instance could give the prior, project payoff \(p+\theta\), and conditional masses \(\mu_\theta(t)\) over complete expert types \(t\). The decision rule accepts the project when the mass of “yes” votes reaches quota \(q\). A possible continuous design problem is to find a quota and a Bayesian equilibrium of report-and-vote strategies that maximize expected public payoff.

That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but the decisive difficulty is that an expert in an atomless population has zero mass. Changing one report or vote cannot change the outcome or anyone’s reputation. The incentives to exaggerate or vote strategically—the paper’s central mechanisms—therefore disappear. Making experts pivotal again by using finite committees or positive-mass blocs changes the model in a substantive way.

The closest numbered results are Proposition 4 and Proposition 9, both proved in this paper. Proposition 4 says that in a two-member committee, unanimity best aligns committee and public interests when information cannot be manipulated; when it can, unanimity and simple majority perform equally well. Proposition 9 says that for an \(n\)-member committee with a negative common prior project value, differing reputational concerns, and non-manipulable information, unanimity best promotes the public’s interests. These are equilibrium and voting-rule results. They do not classify the complexity of a computational problem; the paper has no named result asserting membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, fixed-parameter tractability, or a related complexity claim.

So I can offer a concrete continuous extension, but I cannot honestly present it as a continuous mirror anchored to a qualifying result under your rules. Its weakest point is also its central one: the continuum removes the individual pivotality that gives the paper’s strategic behavior its force.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not green either anchor. The strongest objection is that neither Proposition 4 nor Proposition 9 states a computational result: both compare equilibrium outcomes under voting rules in a finite-player Bayesian game. The paper gives no complexity claim for a decision problem that a continuous mirror could carry over. One could ask for an algorithm to compute equilibria or choose an optimal quota, but that would introduce a computational problem the paper does not claim to study.

The more fundamental obstacle is that the propositions rely on an individual member being decisive. In Proposition 4, unanimity makes the less reputation-conscious member pivotal; Proposition 9 extends that role to the least reputation-conscious member in a larger finite committee. In an atomless population, a member’s report or vote has zero effect on the vote share, the project decision, and therefore the market’s posterior based on that decision. Since the member’s payoffs depend on the decision and resulting reputation, changing their own action cannot produce the strategic trade-off these propositions analyze. A mass quota preserves an aggregate voting rule, but removes individual pivotality; interpreting unanimity as “all but a measure-zero set” has the same problem.

There are ways to preserve pivotality, but each changes the strategic unit. Keep a finite committee inside a large pool of potential experts, and the committee still drives the result while the continuous pool is incidental. Treat positive-mass classes of experts as coordinated blocs, and it is the bloc that acts strategically, an assumption absent from the paper. These are possible models, but neither is a continuous-population version of the mechanisms behind Propositions 4 and 9.

The strongest repair is to model a large population of finite committees, with committee composition and reputational parameters defining committee types, then optimize a voting rule over their distribution. That could be a sensible new design problem. But it makes committees, rather than experts in one committee, the continuous population; the propositions’ strategic analysis remains inside each finite committee. The distribution then aggregates outcomes across separate panels rather than continuizing the paper’s decision-making society.

This case is strongest against the proposed anchors and the obvious large-committee repair. It cannot establish that no author would recognize a population-of-panels question as their problem. That possibility is the honest weak point. But it is a distinct extension, and the paper supplies no computational result to make it a mirror of the results cited here.
