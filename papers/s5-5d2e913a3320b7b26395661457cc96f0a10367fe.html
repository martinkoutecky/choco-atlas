The continuous mirror question

Given known criteria and breakpoints, an oracle for a society μ=½δθ₁+½δθ₂ over two unknown UTA preference types, and permitted two-criterion indifference queries, does there exist a finite adaptive strategy that recovers both complete slope tables up to permutation? Each query returns the exact distribution of answers across the society, including mass answering “None.”

The model it lives in

The society is μ=½δθ₁+½δθ₂, where each type θ is a complete UTA preference model specified by its strictly positive marginal slopes on the known intervals, up to normalization. A query asks the same matching question of the whole population; the oracle returns the exact answer distribution, and the objective is to recover the two types up to permutation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s finite identification guarantee and its rectangle-query procedure. It leaves unaddressed query and precision complexity bounds, more than two decision-makers, noisy answers, and unrestricted increasing marginals.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bounds can be proved on the number of queries and the precision required as the number of criteria and breakpoints grow?
  • Can identification be extended to support size τ>2 or to unknown segment masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is narrow: Population UTA Identification with Two Segments. A type is a complete UTA preference model: the strictly positive slopes of its piecewise linear marginal value functions on the known criterion intervals. Normalize each model by fixing one slope to \(1\). A supermarket has a large customer base split equally between two homogeneous preference segments. Its society is \(\mu=\tfrac12\delta_{\theta_1}+\tfrac12\delta_{\theta_2}\), with the two types unknown and identified only up to swapping their names. The number of customers may be large while the support size is \(\tau=2\).

For each permitted two-criterion matching query, ask the whole population for its indifference answer and return only the exact distribution of answers, including any mass answering “None.” Thus the query reveals the unordered pair of segment answers, with each answer carrying mass \(\tfrac12\)—the population version of the paper’s anonymous pair of responses. The problem is: given the known criteria and breakpoints and access to this query oracle, does there exist a finite adaptive strategy that recovers the two complete UTA types? A solution is such a query strategy and a decoder that outputs the two slope tables up to permutation. With a fixed bound on rational parameter encodings, the possible types form a finite universe; the unknown society is specified by its two support types and their masses.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that two piecewise linear additive preference models with known breakpoint structure can be elicited using a finite number of indifference queries even when each query’s two exact answers are anonymous. The authors establish this with their rectangle query patterns and iterative elicitation procedure. This is an algorithmic identifiability result, not an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or other standard complexity classification; the paper does not give a bit-complexity bound for the number or precision of queries.

The regime has a plausible basis in the paper’s own supermarket example: many customers, a small number of homogeneous preference segments, and market shares expressed as fractions rather than headcounts. This mirror covers the paper’s two-model finite-elicitation result, not its broader preference-learning background. I expect the fixed two-segment oracle problem to be tractable in the finite-query sense: its answer structure is exactly the one handled by Theorem 1. That does not establish a polynomial-time Class A result in ChoCo’s complexity-theoretic sense. Natural next questions are whether identification extends to arbitrary known or unknown segment masses, whether it works for support size \(\tau>2\), and what query and precision bounds hold as \(\tau\), the number of criteria, and the encoding length grow.

The soft spot is that this mirror adds little computational distance from the source problem: with two equal-mass segments, the population answer distribution contains precisely the same anonymous pair of values as the paper’s two respondents. The population is genuinely represented by masses, and the market-segmentation setting is credible, but the theorem does not yet show that continuization unlocks new tractability. The stronger research opportunity lies in the larger-support population problem, which this anchor motivates but does not resolve.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor is Theorem 1. The proposed two-segment mirror preserves its transcript exactly: each query returns the same unordered pair of answers, whether the respondents are called decision-makers or customer segments. Giving those segments masses \(1/2\) adds no information or algorithmic parameter. Multiplying the number of customers in each segment changes neither the answers nor the elicitation strategy. So this version is a population story around the paper’s existing two-model problem, rather than a new computational question about population size.

Allowing unequal masses makes the model less degenerate, but the answer distribution reports those masses alongside the answers. Distinct masses can even help match a segment across queries; equal masses return the original anonymity problem. The strongest finite-support extension would use \(\tau>2\) unknown preference types and ask how many queries recover their slope tables. That is a coherent research question, and a large market with a few preference segments is plausible. But its hard parameter is the number of distinct latent models, \(\tau\); copying each segment across a much larger population changes nothing. The question becomes anonymous mixture identification, not a computational problem made tractable by population continuization.

If the mirror instead allows an unrestricted distribution over a continuum of preference types, finite query transcripts generally provide only finitely many aggregate views of that distribution. Without restrictions such as bounded support or a parametric family, there is no general reason those views should determine the underlying distribution. Adding such restrictions brings the proposal back to a finite latent-type learning problem; removing them risks asking for an object the queries cannot identify.

The negative case is not airtight: a careful complexity study of query strategies for finite \(\tau>2\) could be worthwhile, and the paper does not already prove that result. But that would be a new multi-model elicitation programme whose complexity comes from the number and geometry of distinct preference types. The proposed equal two-segment anchor, at least, gains no computational content from making the society continuous.
