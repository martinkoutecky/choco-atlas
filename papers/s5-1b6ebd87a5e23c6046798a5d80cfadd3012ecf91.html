The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), supported initial rankings \(T\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a designated candidate \(p\), and an attainable rational per-capita swap radius \(\rho\), let \(x_{t,\pi}\ge 0\) be the mass moving from initial ranking \(t\) to final ranking \(\pi\in\Pi(C)\). Require \(\sum_{\pi}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,\pi}x_{t,\pi}d_K(t,\pi)=\rho\). Define \(h(x)=-\sum_{t,\pi}x_{t,\pi}\ln(x_{t,\pi}/\mu_t)\), with zero terms interpreted as \(0\); let \(A\) maximize \(h\) over all feasible plans and \(A_p\) maximize it over plans where \(p\)'s final Plurality score is at least every other candidate’s. Compute an \(\epsilon\)-additive approximation to \(I_p=A-A_p\), returning \(+\infty\) if no feasible plan makes \(p\) a winner.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types. A mass-transport table \(x_{t,\pi}\) assigns each type’s mass to final rankings, with average Kendall distance fixed at \(\rho\); the objective is the gap between the maximum conditional entropy over all such transports and the maximum subject to \(p\) winning.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s unit-cost Plurality #Swap-Bribery result; it does not cover the Borda or Shift-Bribery results, experimental findings, or finite-population probabilities beyond their exponential rate.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove that along admissible population scalings \(N\), the finite exact-radius winning probability has exponential rate \(A-A_p\), including integrality, plurality ties, and boundary optima.
  • Give an \(\epsilon\)-approximation algorithm for \(A-A_p\) with running time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length, and the requested precision.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s exact-radius winner probability in a large electorate. I would not claim that an exact count of finite elections becomes a continuous count: the continuous analogue I believe in is the exponential rate at which the probability of a win falls as the population grows.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper by a reduction from \(\#\mathrm{Matching}\). It states that Plurality \(\#\)Swap-Bribery is \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard and is also \(\#\mathrm W[1]\)-hard when parameterized by the swap radius, even with unit swap prices. The paper notes related prior \(\#\mathrm P\)-hardness work, but gives the reduction establishing the theorem here. The counted objects are final elections at *exactly* the given number of adjacent swaps in which the designated candidate wins. This is the best anchor because its hardness result concerns the same random-swap robustness question that motivates the paper.

The plausible regime is a very large election or organization-wide ballot, with a fixed set of candidates and many voters sharing complete rankings. A ranking is a type: with unit swap prices, voters with the same ranking are interchangeable for the model. If there are \(N\) voters but only \(\tau\) distinct rankings, with \(\tau\) much smaller than \(N\), the society is represented by rational masses \(\mu_t\), one for each supported ranking \(t\). The paper’s experiments use only \(100\) voters; this mirror concerns a high-multiplicity regime beyond those experiments.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Plurality Population Swap-Robustness Rate. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a list of supported initial rankings \(T\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a designated candidate \(p\), a rational per-capita swap radius \(\rho\), and a rational accuracy \(\epsilon>0\). Let \(\Pi(C)\) be all rankings of \(C\), and let \(d_K(t,\pi)\) be Kendall swap distance. A mass-transfer plan is a nonnegative array \(x_{t,\pi}\), where \(x_{t,\pi}\) is the fraction of society that starts with ranking \(t\) and ends with ranking \(\pi\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{\pi\in\Pi(C)}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t\in T, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(C)}x_{t,\pi}d_K(t,\pi)=\rho. \]

Write \(s_c(x)=\sum_{t,\pi:\,\pi\text{ ranks }c\text{ first}}x_{t,\pi}\) for candidate \(c\)’s final Plurality score. A plan makes \(p\) a winner when \(s_p(x)\ge s_c(x)\) for every \(c\in C\).

For such a plan define its conditional entropy by

\[ h(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \left( -\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(C)} \frac{x_{t,\pi}}{\mu_t} \ln\frac{x_{t,\pi}}{\mu_t} \right), \]

with \(0\ln 0=0\). Let \(A\) be the maximum of \(h(x)\) over all plans at radius \(\rho\), and \(A_p\) its maximum over those plans where \(p\) wins. The problem asks for an \(\epsilon\)-approximation to \(I_p(\mu,\rho)=A-A_p\), returning \(+\infty\) if no winning plan is feasible. The requested solution is the approximate rate, not a transfer plan listing all \(m!\) possible rankings.

This is the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s own counting experiment. Start with \(N\mu_t\) named voters of each supported ranking and choose uniformly among final elections at exactly \(N\rho\) swaps. For any transition table \(x\) whose entries are multiples of \(1/N\), the number of labeled final elections it describes is

\[ \prod_{t\in T} \frac{(N\mu_t)!}{\prod_{\pi\in\Pi(C)}(Nx_{t,\pi})!}. \]

Its logarithm divided by \(N\) approaches \(h(x)\). Thus the numerator and denominator of the paper’s winning probability have exponential growth rates \(A_p\) and \(A\), respectively, and the win probability has rate \(I_p\): along feasible population scalings, it behaves as \(\exp(-NI_p+o(N))\). The masses are the population; the transport plan is the aggregate perturbation; and the entropy records how many named-voter outcomes realize each aggregate plan. This gives a formal bridge from their finite count to a continuous-population robustness question.

I expect this rate problem to be Class A, though that is a conjecture, not a result of the paper. The entropy maximizations have linear mass, cost, and winner constraints. Their duals need partition sums over rankings, but for a source ranking \(t\) those sums appear manageable without enumerating all permutations. If \(c\) is in position \(j\) of \(t\), then

\[ \sum_{\pi:\,\pi\text{ ranks }c\text{ first}}q^{d_K(t,\pi)} = q^{j-1}\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(1+q+\cdots+q^{i-1}). \]

The factor for the remaining candidates is the inversion-generating function for their permutations. This suggests an approximate convex-optimization algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the rational input bit length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), despite the exponentially large set of final rankings. Establishing that bound, with the boundary cases handled, is the main algorithmic question.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s Plurality \(\#\)Swap-Bribery result only. It preserves the paper’s exact-radius, unit-Kendall-cost perturbation and winner condition, while replacing the finite count by its per-capita exponential rate. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s Borda and Shift-Bribery results or its experimental findings. High-multiplicity election work supports the plausibility of the regime; it is evidence that the regime makes sense, not a collision that makes the question already answered.

The main weakness is the change in what is computed. The rate forgets subexponential factors, so \(I_p=0\) does not tell us whether the winning probability tends to \(1\), to a constant between \(0\) and \(1\), or to \(0\) slowly. A referee could reasonably ask whether that is too coarse to count as the authors’ robustness question. I think it is still a credible mirror because the paper’s probability is defined by precisely these finite counts, and this rate answers whether winning outcomes are exponentially rare in a large society. It also yields natural follow-ups: identify the regions where \(I_p=0\), and refine the analysis there to recover the actual limiting winning probabilities.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 1: Plurality \(\#\)Swap-Bribery is \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard, via a reduction from \(\#\mathrm{Matching}\), even with unit prices. The strongest objection is that the proposed rate is not a continuous version of that counting theorem. The theorem counts exact finite outcomes; the proposed problem keeps only their leading exponential growth as the population scales. That rate can describe exponential rarity, but it discards the factors that distinguish many finite probabilities. In particular, rate zero does not tell us whether the candidate’s win probability approaches \(0\), a positive constant, or \(1\). The paper’s experiments use probabilities and thresholds such as the first swap distance where the winner drops below \(50\%\). The rate alone cannot supply those quantities.

That objection does not dispose of the better mirror, though. The proponent has a direct bridge from the paper’s probability model to the proposed entropy objective: endpoint rankings are counted once per labeled voter, and tables of mass transfers are realized by multinomially many label assignments. Their limiting exponent therefore comes from the paper’s own uniform distribution over exact-radius final elections; it is not an unrelated noise model imposed on the problem. For fixed candidates and rational input masses and radius, the required population scalings are available. The authors could plausibly recognize the resulting question as large-population winner robustness.

Nor can I make a sound case that there is no high-multiplicity scenario. In a large election with many voters sharing complete rankings and unit swap costs, the ranking is a sufficient type for this problem. Neither voter identity nor the reduction’s use of named voters makes mass meaningless. The reduction’s combinatorial purpose also does not rule out a mirror: the question is whether the high-multiplicity version is sensible, not whether it preserves the reduction’s answer.

The proposed rate may be substantially coarser than the paper’s experimental measure, and its entropy optimization may admit a relatively direct convex formulation. Those are real reasons to ask whether it merits a major research effort. They do not establish that it is outside the programme: it studies a computational robustness quantity for a continuous population, and the paper itself motivates robustness through winning probabilities under exact-radius swaps.

So the negative case is weak. I can argue that this mirror answers a narrower asymptotic question than the paper’s finite-population probabilities, but I cannot honestly defeat the improved version the proponent gives. On the programme’s stated scope, Theorem 1 remains a credible anchor for a continuous mirror.
