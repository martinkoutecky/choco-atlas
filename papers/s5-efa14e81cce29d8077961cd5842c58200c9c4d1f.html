The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over types \((x,b)\), with \(b\in[0,B]\), can one compute a weighted median \(y\) of the public location mass such that \(SC_\mu(y)=\sum_{(x,b)}\mu_{x,b}\bigl||x-y|-b\bigr|\le \mathrm{OPT}(\mu)+2B\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over public locations \(x\) and private preferred distances \(b\in[0,B]\). Return a weighted median of the location marginal, ignoring reports of \(b\); evaluate it by per-capita social cost \(SC_\mu(y)=\sum_{(x,b)}\mu_{x,b}\bigl||x-y|-b\bigr|\).

The objection that survived

The broad mass rule-synthesis question lacks an input representation and running-time model; this limits that proposal but does not undercut the weighted-median anchor.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the one-dimensional Median guarantee in Theorem 3 and its support-weighted refinement; it does not turn Theorems 1 and 2 into running-time hardness results or cover the paper’s two-dimensional and \(k\)-facility results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the sharper instance-dependent guarantee extend with \(r=\mu\{(x,b):|x-\mathrm{med}_\mu|<B\}\) and the average preferred distance among that mass?
  • Can mechanisms that use reported preferred-distance types improve the worst-case additive guarantee under a specified positive-mass coalition incentive condition?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a mass version of the paper’s one-dimensional mechanism-design problem. The facility’s location is already continuous in the paper; the continuization here is the population.

Let \(B>0\). An instance is a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over types \((x,b)\), where \(x\) is a publicly known location, \(b\in[0,B]\) is the resident’s private preferred distance, and \(\mu_{x,b}\) is that type’s fraction of the population. Thus \(\tau\) is the number of represented types, with masses and other numerical data given in binary. A facility at \(y\in\mathbb R\) gives type \((x,b)\) cost
\[ c(y;(x,b))=\bigl||x-y|-b\bigr|, \]
so the per-capita social cost is \(SC_\mu(y)=\sum_{x,b}\mu_{x,b}c(y;(x,b))\). A mechanism reads the reported type masses, with locations fixed and residents able to misreport preferred distances, and returns a location or a lottery over locations.

One co-modification is needed for incentives: in a nonatomic population, a single resident has zero mass, so their report cannot affect the outcome. I would require partial group strategyproofness by type: no positive-mass subset of residents with the same true \((x,b)\) can lower its expected true cost by changing its reports together. This is close to the paper’s own Lemma 1, proved there: strategyproofness implies partial group strategyproofness for agents at the same location with the same preferred distance. It also matches the paper’s lower-bound proofs, which use a whole cohort’s misreport. Locations and costs are shared within a type in exactly the high-multiplicity sense.

The plausible regime is a large district with many residents concentrated in a few public-location and preferred-distance categories—for example, known residential clusters and a small number of private preferences about how far a school or firehouse should be. Population size \(n\) can grow while \(\tau\) stays small. In fact, the lower-bound witnesses already use just three types, so the barrier is not an artifact of a large support.

My lead is Randomized Mass-DP Facility Synthesis. Given rational \(B\) and an error threshold \(\epsilon\), ask whether there is a mass-partial-group-strategyproof rule \(F\) that maps every such reported distribution \(\mu\) to a lottery over facility locations and satisfies
\[ \sup_\mu\left(\mathbb E_{Y\sim F(\mu)}[SC_\mu(Y)]-\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\right)\le\epsilon. \]
A solution is either such a rule with its guarantee, or an impossibility proof ruling one out. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for every truthful-in-expectation mechanism in the paper’s one-dimensional doubly-peaked facility-location game, there is an instance with expected social cost at least \(\mathrm{OPT}+nB/300\). After normalizing total population to mass one, the corresponding bound is \(\epsilon\ge B/300\). I consider this the strongest anchor because its obstruction holds even when the mechanism may randomize.

The witness makes the transfer concrete. The paper uses two profiles with three equal-sized cohorts; in the mass model they have mass \(1/3\) each. One profile puts those masses on \((0,B)\), \((-B/4,B/2)\), and \((B/2,3B/4)\); in the other, only the last cohort’s reported preferred distance changes, to \(B/2\). Their optimal per-capita costs are \(B/4\). The paper’s incentive argument against improving both profiles carries over with these rational masses. The expected direction is therefore approximation hardness transfers. That means the stated error guarantee is impossible; it does not mean the facility-location problem is NP-hard.

A second, related anchor is Deterministic Mass-DP Facility Synthesis, the same question restricted to deterministic rules. Its anchor is Theorem 1, also proved in this paper: every deterministic, universally truthful mechanism in the one-dimensional setting has additive approximation error at least \(nB/24\)—for every such mechanism, some instance has cost at least \(\mathrm{OPT}+nB/24\). In normalized masses this gives \(\epsilon\ge B/24\). The same three-cohort witness yields the lower bound. This anchor has the stronger numerical constant, but applies to the narrower deterministic class; Theorem 2 is my lead because it survives randomization.

As a benchmark for these questions, a rule that ignores the private \(b\)-reports and returns a weighted median of the public location mass is immediately truthful and computable from the support. The same triangle-inequality argument gives additive error at most \(2\int b\,d\mu\le2B\). So the mirror has a feasible algorithmic baseline as well as lower bounds. Further questions include determining the exact best additive error for deterministic and randomized mass mechanisms, whether using private-distance reports can improve on that baseline, and how the results extend to two dimensions.

The scope is deliberately narrow: one facility on a line, public locations, private doubly-peaked costs, and the paper’s deterministic and truthful-in-expectation lower bounds. The paper proves both anchors itself. The weakness is that individual truthfulness becomes vacuous at zero mass, so the mirror needs a positive-mass coalition notion. That is a real change, but it is grounded in the paper’s own partial-group result and in the cohorts used in its proofs. A second limitation is that these anchors give approximation barriers, not running-time lower bounds or a P-versus-NP classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are the proponent’s strongest anchors, and their arithmetic really does carry over. On the two profiles with three equal cohorts, normalize the population to mass one. Dividing the paper’s bounds by \(n\) gives additive-error lower bounds \(B/24\) for deterministic rules and \(B/300\) for truthful-in-expectation rules. A mass mechanism with the proposed cohort incentive condition faces the same two deviations as the paper’s proof. I would not attack either anchor by claiming the witnesses fail to survive continuization.

The case against is that these are approximation guarantees for incentive-compatible mechanisms, rather than complexity results about computing an outcome from an encoded instance. The proposed “Mass-DP Facility Synthesis” asks whether a rule exists over an entire domain of distributions. It assigns no representation or running-time bound to that rule, so it has no complexity landscape in \(m,\tau,L\) for the programme to chart. The proofs establish a worst-case quality floor through two profiles; they do not expose a pricing problem, optimization bottleneck, or algorithmic boundary.

The strongest computational version would ask what can be computed from a supplied finite-support distribution. Without incentive constraints, the one-dimensional optimum is easy to compute: \(\sum_{x,b}\mu_{x,b}\lvert |x-y|-b\rvert\) is piecewise linear, with breakpoints among \(x-b,x,x+b\), so sorting and scanning them finds a minimum. A weighted-median rule also gives a direct baseline. Adding truthfulness requires specifying a representation of a rule over the full report domain. Restricting that domain or the rule class could make synthesis computationally concrete, but those restrictions would create a new problem whose complexity the paper’s two lower bounds do not determine.

There is also a modeling cost in making the incentive condition meaningful. With locations and preferred distances ranging continuously, a natural diffuse population has no positive-mass group with exactly the same \((x,b)\). The proposed group condition then imposes no constraints on such a population. The paper’s witness survives by using atoms: cohorts with identical public locations and identical private preferred distances. That is a coherent regime, and apartment blocks or preference categories could motivate it. But it is a narrower model; using broader location or preference categories would change the agents’ costs.

So the negative case is about fit and research value, not a fundamental failure of the mass model. The exact cohort transfer makes the approximation question well-posed, and the paper’s own group-strategyproofness lemma supports its incentive interpretation. The programme’s stated rule against rejecting a mirror merely because its answer is known or dull also rules out treating the immediate lower-bound transfer as decisive. I cannot make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. I can make a narrower case that these two anchors, as stated, belong to mechanism-design approximation theory rather than the computational-complexity programme, and that a computational reformulation would need additional choices the paper does not supply.
