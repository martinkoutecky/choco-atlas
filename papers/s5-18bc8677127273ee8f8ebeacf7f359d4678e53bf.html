What the mirror covers

The proposed scale-design extension draws on Theorems 4.3 and 4.7 and Proposition 4.9, but none is a qualifying computational anchor; the experimental results and the paper's other incentive analyses are not mirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population setting is a large peer-grading or annotation job where many workers share a small number of complete worker types. A type \(\theta\) would include the worker’s effort costs and belief model over answers and peers; its mass \(\mu_\theta\) is its share of the workforce. The society is a rational distribution over these types, with many workers but relatively few types. Keep the answer set and the paper’s batch of \(n\) tasks finite: the proposed continuation changes the population, not the answers or task space.

The closest named result is Theorem 4.7, proved in this paper. It says that if, for every worker \(w\) and answer \(x\), the expected honest payoff \(\bar{\tau}_w(\alpha)\) exceeds the effort-cost difference \(c_w(e_1)-c_w(e_0)\) divided by
\[ 1-\frac{(n-1)P_p(x)(1-P_p(x))^{n-2}}{1-(1-P_p(x))^{n-1}}, \]
and that quantity is at least \(w\)’s self-predictor, then honest reporting is a strict equilibrium of RPTSC and gives workers maximum profit. This is a strong anchor for the *incentive question*, but it does not assert a computational complexity result. The same issue applies to Theorem 4.3 and Proposition 4.9: they establish incentive properties, not that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. So this paper has no qualifying named computational anchor under the criterion in the prompt.

A plausible follow-on is Type-Mass RPTSC Scale Design. Given a rational distribution over finitely many worker types, each type’s rational costs and belief data, a fixed task batch size \(n\), and a budget bound \(B\), find the smallest common scale \(\alpha\le B\) for which the RPTSC honest, high-effort profile is a strict equilibrium and maximizes profit for every represented type. A solution is \(\alpha\), together with the per-type incentive certificates and the resulting mass-weighted expected payment. Theorem 4.7 supplies a sufficient route to such a design; with its conditions as the target, the scale appears tractable by checking the finite type-answer support. This is a credible extension of the authors’ problem, but it is not a computational result the paper proves. For this robust design, the masses mostly weight the payment objective; the hardest incentive constraint depends on which types are present, not their relative shares. A richer population-sensitive question—such as selecting which types participate under a task-capacity constraint—would need additional modeling.

That is the weak point: the continuous worker population is plausible, especially for large MOOCs or standardized annotation, and the mechanism is recognizably the paper’s. But the paper’s named results do not anchor a computational mirror. The proposed scale-design problem should therefore be presented as a new research lead, not as a continuization result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.7 and Theorem 4.3 establish incentive conditions; Proposition 4.9 establishes an equilibrium of a scale elicitation protocol. None defines a problem of finding an algorithm, classifying complexity, or computing an optimum. The reward mechanism does compute empirical frequencies, but that is not a complexity result. Under the programme’s stated gate, there is no computational anchor here.

The best population setting is still plausible: a large peer grading or annotation job with a small number of complete worker types, where each type includes its effort cost and belief model. That clears the high multiplicity test. The problem is that, for the proposed scale design, Theorem 4.7 gives finitely many constraints over types and answers. The reward scales linearly with \(\alpha\), so using those conditions amounts to evaluating the type-specific thresholds and taking their maximum. Strictness means the threshold is generally an infimum, not an attained smallest scale. Masses can weight aggregate reports or total payments, but they do not create a search problem. If the design instead selects a fraction of types, the basic extension is to rank their thresholds and accumulate their masses.

The other named results fare similarly. Theorem 4.3 supplies another sufficient condition for an honest equilibrium. Proposition 4.9 uses a quantile of reported scales; with finitely many represented types, its mass version is a weighted quantile calculation. A richer problem involving entry, task capacity, or endogenous population strategy could become interesting, but those ingredients would define a new mechanism design problem. They do not supply a computational question already anchored in this paper.

I would not argue that crowdsourcing cannot have a meaningful continuous population, or that such a model is already covered by continuous prior work. The paper’s large worker pools make the regime credible. The negative case is narrower: the named results concern incentives, and the most direct mass-based repair reduces to finite threshold or quantile calculations. I cannot establish the literal universal claim that no worthwhile population-continuum question could arise from any extension of this setting; I can argue that this paper offers no strong computational mirror for ChoCo to prioritize.
