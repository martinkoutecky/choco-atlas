What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the closest-monotone-ranking optimization over strict rankings; it leaves the weak-order Pareto-front procedure and Proposition 1's structural characterization without a computational mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the optimization over candidate rankings be solved efficiently, or does its ranking combinatorics make the problem hard?
  • What pricing or separation problem results from requiring the output mass distribution to be realizable by rankings?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s ranking procedure, but it has a serious anchor problem: the paper contains no named computational result of the kind your instructions require. Its only relevant numbered result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. For a profile of \(r\) voters over \(k\) candidates, it says the induced pair-distance counts satisfy completeness (for each ordered pair, the counts over all nonzero rank gaps sum to \(r\)); reciprocity (\(D_i(a,b)=D_{-i}(b,a)\)); stability (for each nonzero gap \(i\), the sum over ordered pairs is \(r(k-|i|)\)); and peakedness (for each candidate, its incoming and outgoing counts at positive gap \(i\) are at most those at gap \(i-1\), and at negative gap \(i\) are at most those at gap \(i+1\)). These are structural feasibility properties, not a complexity or algorithmic result. The paper’s ILP is unnumbered, and its case study computes examples without stating a named complexity result. So Proposition 1 cannot honestly serve as a qualifying anchor.

The substantive mirror I would propose is this. Let the candidates be \(C\), and let each voter type be a complete ranking \(\sigma\) of \(C\). The input is a rational society \(\mu\) over rankings, given by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types. For nonzero rank gap \(i\), define
\[ D_\mu^i(a,b)=\sum_{\sigma:\,r_\sigma(a)-r_\sigma(b)=-i}\mu_\sigma. \]
Choose an output society \(\nu\) over rankings and a proposed strict ranking \(\succ\). Require \(D_\nu\) to satisfy the paper’s monotonicity constraints for \(\succ\), using cumulative masses in place of voter counts. Among such pairs, minimize
\[ d_\succ(D_\mu,D_\nu) =\sum_{a\succ b}\ \sum_{\substack{j=1-k\\j\ne0}}^{k-1} \left|\sum_{\substack{i=1-k\\i\ne0}}^j \bigl(D_\mu^i(a,b)-D_\nu^i(a,b)\bigr)\right|. \]
The solution is a closest monotone society \(\nu\) and its optimal ranking \(\succ\). Equivalently, the decision variable can include a mass transfer \(x_{\sigma,\sigma'}\) from input ranking types to output ranking types, with row sums \(\mu_\sigma\) and column sums \(\nu_{\sigma'}\). Because \(\nu\) is an actual distribution over rankings, its induced statistics satisfy the normalized feasibility properties automatically.

A plausible regime is a large public consultation on a fixed set of policy plans: many participants submit rankings, while repeated ballots leave \(\tau\) distinct ranking types far fewer than the population size. The paper’s 31-stakeholder example is a small committee, so it does not itself establish this regime. Still, the underlying input is already a distribution of rankings, and the model assigns no individual-specific prices or costs. The population mirror keeps the paper’s central question—how close the observed ranking data are to data monotone with respect to a candidate order—intact.

For a fixed \(\succ\), the proposed problem is an LP over output ranking masses, with absolute values linearized. Across all \(\succ\), it has an outer optimization over candidate rankings and potentially exponentially many ranking types. My provisional expectation is Class B: the combinatorics over alternatives remain, and the continuous population does not obviously remove them. The decisive open question is whether the required pricing or separation over rankings is tractable, or whether hardness can be proved. The paper itself answers neither question. A further issue is whether its listed feasibility constraints alone capture realizable ranking distributions; the formulation above requires realizability by an actual \(\nu\).

That missing named computational anchor is the weakest point of the positive case. I would present this as a plausible continuous analogue of the paper’s computed ranking problem, not as a mirror anchored in one of its numbered computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that it fails the programme’s required anchor. The proponent identifies the problem clearly: Proposition 1 gives feasibility properties of the induced distributions, but it does not assert a computational result. The paper’s ILP, enumeration of weak orders, and case study also provide no named complexity result. So the proposed mass-distribution optimization may be a sensible new question, but it is not a continuization of a computational result the paper establishes.

That objection survives a better formulation of the mirror: let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over ranking types, require the repaired distribution \(\nu\) to be realizable by actual rankings, and minimize the paper’s normalized distance while choosing a best candidate order. None of those repairs supplies the missing result to mirror. Earlier work gives the monotonicity-repair method a computational lineage—the paper says its predecessor uses a flow network for the fixed-ranking problem, and Rademaker and De Baets had already studied stochastic-monotonicity restoration with cumulative-frequency loss functions ([2011 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ins.2010.10.026)). That makes a new population formulation less clearly a continuation of this paper’s computational contribution, though it does not answer the continuous-population question.

The model objections do not carry much weight. A large consultation over policy plans is a credible high-multiplicity regime; rankings are complete voter types, and the objective depends on aggregate distributions rather than named voters. Normalizing the distance by population size need not make it vanish. Requiring \(\nu\) to be realizable is also a reasonable strengthening, and the resulting optimization could have a genuine pricing problem over rankings.

So I would reject this paper as an anchored mirror under the stated rule: it has no qualifying named computational result. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The broader ranking problem remains a plausible, potentially useful new computational question; the negative case is strongest on eligibility, and weak on the substance of that reformulated model.
