The continuous mirror question

Given an explicit outcome set \(X=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\{0,\ldots,m_i-1\}\), a target \(z\in X\), and a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) with support \(\tau\) over strict rankings of \(X\), with pairwise majority ties excluded, compute the set of all \(x\in X\) from which some objects scheme admits a maximal sequence of best-neighbor moves ending at \(z\) under the majority rule induced by \(\mu\).

The model it lives in

The population consists of ranking types \(t\), each with mass \(\mu_t\). An object is a nonempty subset of features; it permits moves that change only those features, and a move must go to the most-preferred feasible outcome under the majority social rule. The output is the exact universal basin of \(z\), with no cost objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror instantiates Theorem 5.1 for social rules induced by weighted majority; it leaves the paper’s probability calculations, local-optimum counts, and numerical statistics outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can membership in the universal basin be decided faster than computing the whole basin?
  • Can the computation use small \(\tau\) or a succinct representation of the outcome set?
  • How do majority ties or other anonymous aggregation rules affect basin computation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s universal-basin computation, not a claim that its continuous version reveals hidden hardness.

Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper, says that for a social rule on \(M\) social outcomes and a specified outcome \(z\), Algorithm ComputeUniversalBasin computes the universal basin \(\Psi(z)\) in \(O(M^3\log M)\) time. Here \(\Psi(z)\) is the set of starting outcomes from which some objects scheme permits a maximal sequence of best-neighbor moves ending at \(z\). This is the paper’s clearest named computational result. The paper does not give a named hardness result, so I would use this single algorithmic anchor rather than pad the case with its structural theorems.

A concrete continuous problem is Majority-Continuum Universal-Basin. Let the outcomes be bundles of features,
\[ X=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\{0,\ldots,m_i-1\}. \]
An instance gives a target \(z\in X\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) with support \(\tau\) over strict rankings of \(X\). Each ranking is a voter type, and its mass is the fraction of the population with that ranking. Require pairwise majority to be tie-free; then define \(x\succ_\mu y\) exactly when more than half the mass ranks \(x\) above \(y\). This produces the strict social rule needed by the paper.

The question is: compute every \(x\in X\) from which the authority can reach \(z\) by object construction and best-neighbor moves under \(\succ_\mu\). An object is a nonempty subset \(I\) of features. From outcome \(x\), it permits moves to outcomes that agree with \(x\) outside \(I\); a move must be to a best neighbor under the induced social rule. An objects scheme is a collection of such subsets covering all features, with overlaps allowed. The desired answer is the exact set \(\Psi_\mu(z)\), the union of the basins over all schemes. Equivalently, for a specified starting outcome \(x\), the decision question is whether \(x\in\Psi_\mu(z)\); a positive answer has a scheme and a path as its witness. There is no cost-minimization objective in this anchor: the task is reachability.

The population regime is a large electorate choosing among combinations of a fixed menu of policy features. For example, many residents may share rankings over bundles of transit, housing, and budget choices, while an authority decides which features to consider together during successive revisions. The number of people \(N\) can be very large while the number of distinct rankings \(\tau\) is much smaller. Since rankings are the only voter characteristics used here, grouping agents with the same ranking preserves the model’s relevant information. If \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), it is also the direct high-multiplicity representation of a finite electorate.

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem because their setup starts with agents’ preferences over feature bundles, turns those preferences into a social rule, and studies object construction and the resulting paths. Pairwise majority is a concrete choice of aggregation rule that yields the tournament their analysis uses; it is not a claim that the paper mandates majority rule. The mirror covers Theorem 5.1’s universal-basin computation under this aggregation rule, not the paper’s full range of probability and local-optimum results.

The expected answer is tractable (Class A). Computing all pairwise majority comparisons takes \(O(\tau M^2)\) weighted comparisons, followed by Theorem 5.1’s \(O(M^3\log M)\) algorithm. With binary-rational masses, exact arithmetic adds polynomial factors in the input bit length \(L\). Thus the continuous problem is polynomial in \(\tau\), \(M\), and \(L\), for an explicitly represented outcome set. When \(\mu\) comes from finite voter counts and uses the same tie convention, the induced social rule—and hence the basin—is exactly the same as in the finite model.

The weakest point is that the theorem itself takes a social rule as input; it says nothing about population multiplicity. In this mirror, \(\mu\) supplies that rule through weighted majority, after which the paper’s algorithm applies unchanged. This is a faithful continuous-population formulation, but it does not show that continuization makes a hard problem easy: the anchor is already polynomial for an explicit tournament. Also, \(M=\prod_i m_i\) can be exponential in the number of features, so the theorem does not establish polynomial time for a succinctly encoded feature space.

The mirror raises natural follow-up questions: can membership in \(\Psi_\mu(z)\) be decided faster than computing the whole basin; can the algorithm exploit small \(\tau\) or a succinct outcome representation; and how do majority ties or other anonymous aggregation rules affect the basin computation?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that Theorem 5.1 takes an explicit social rule on \(M\) outcomes as input, not a voter population. In the proposed majority mirror, the distribution \(\mu\) matters only through the \(M^2\) pairwise majority comparisons: any two distributions inducing the same tournament have the same universal basin. After those comparisons, the paper’s algorithm applies unchanged. This gives a population-sensitive input format, but no population-sensitive computation; the complexity work is entirely over the outcome graph.

That critique does not defeat the mirror under the programme’s stated standard. A distribution over complete rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity society, and pairwise majority is a natural way to turn it into the strict social rule the paper studies. For a large electorate choosing among bundles of policy features, ranking types and their masses are a plausible model. The exact basin is then well-defined, and Theorem 5.1 supplies a polynomial-time solution in the explicit-outcome model. A Class A result that turns out to follow from an existing algorithm is still a meaningful answer; the fact that the computation discards everything about \(\mu\) beyond its tournament does not invalidate the question.

The compact-feature caveat is real: since \(M=\prod_i m_i\), polynomial time in \(M\) need not be polynomial in a succinct encoding of the features. But the paper itself measures the theorem’s running time in \(M\), and an explicit-outcome mirror remains faithful to that problem. I therefore cannot honestly make the case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The objection identifies a limit on what this mirror teaches about population structure; it does not sink the anchor.
