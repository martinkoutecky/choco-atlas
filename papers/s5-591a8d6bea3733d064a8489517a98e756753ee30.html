The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_\alpha,\mu_\beta\) with \(\mu_\alpha+\mu_\beta=1\), rational additive values \(v_\alpha(g),v_\beta(g)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\) of indivisible copies of each item kind \(g\), output a finite-support rational mass allocation \(\lambda_{r,b}\) over \(r\in\{\alpha,\beta\}\) and integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^m\) satisfying \(\sum_b\lambda_{r,b}=\mu_r\), \(\sum_{r,b}b_g\lambda_{r,b}=q_g\), and \(v_r(a)\ge v_r(b-e_g)\) for every positive-mass pair \((r,a),(s,b)\) and every \(g\) with \(b_g>0\).

The model it lives in

A two-type high-multiplicity indivisible-goods model: \(\mu_r\) is the mass of valuation type \(r\), \(q_g\) is per-capita supply, each agent receives an integral bundle, and \(\lambda_{r,b}\) records bundle-class mass; the objective is EFX feasibility/search.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the two-valuation-type complete-EFX existence result in Theorem 1 and is informed by its constructive augmentation method. It does not establish a mass analogue of Theorem 6, nor does it cover the paper's general-case open problems or cited related-work results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the continuous EFX search problem be solved in time polynomial in \(m\) and the bit length of \(\mu,q,v\), without expanding a denominator-cleared population, while producing polynomial-size support?
  • Does a positive-mass analogue of Theorem 6 preserve EFX and Pareto domination under bundle-class mass transfers with finite support?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness, P, FPT, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its strongest computationally meaningful anchor is therefore Theorem 1, proved in this paper: complete EFX allocations always exist when every agent has one of two given additive valuation functions, with agents of the same valuation type identical. The theorem is uniform in the number of agents and items, and places no bound on the number of distinct item values. Its proof is constructive, although it does not establish polynomial running time.

The natural mirror is a high-multiplicity course- or goods-allocation problem. Let \(G=\{g_1,\ldots,g_m\}\) be item kinds, with two complete agent types \(\alpha,\beta\), having additive valuations \(v_\alpha,v_\beta\). Let \(\mu_\alpha,\mu_\beta\) be their population masses, with \(\mu_\alpha+\mu_\beta=1\). Item kind \(g\) has \(q_g\) indivisible copies per unit population. Thus a society of size \(N\) has \(N\mu_r\) agents of type \(r\) and \(Nq_g\) copies of \(g\).

The action variable is a population allocation \(\lambda\), where \(\lambda_{r,b}\) is the mass of type-\(r\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^m\). Here \(b_g\) is the number of copies of \(g\) in the bundle and

\[ v_r(b)=\sum_{g\in G} b_gv_r(g). \]

A complete allocation satisfies

\[ \sum_b\lambda_{r,b}=\mu_r \]

for each \(r\in\{\alpha,\beta\}\), and

\[ \sum_{r\in\{\alpha,\beta\}}\sum_b b_g\lambda_{r,b}=q_g \]

for every item kind \(g\). It is EFX when, whenever \(\lambda_{r,a}>0\) and \(\lambda_{s,b}>0\),

\[ v_r(a)\ge v_r(b-e_g) \]

for every \(g\) with \(b_g>0\). The continuous problem is therefore:

Given \(\mu_\alpha,\mu_\beta\), \(v_\alpha,v_\beta\), and per-capita item supplies \(q\), output a complete EFX population allocation \(\lambda\).

This is a feasibility/search objective, not a welfare maximization problem. The population is continuous, but every unit of population receives an integral bundle; \(\lambda\) is only the histogram of those bundles, not a lottery over fractional items.

The regime is plausible in a large course-allocation system: perhaps hundreds of thousands of students, but only two standardized valuation profiles, with course seats supplied proportionally to cohort size. The two valuation types are complete types because students sharing a type agree on every feature used by the problem. Thus \(n\gg\tau=2\), precisely the high-multiplicity situation the programme targets.

The bridge to the paper is exact for rational data. Choose \(N\) clearing all denominators. Create \(N\mu_r\) agents of type \(r\) and \(Nq_g\) distinct indivisible copies of each \(g\). Theorem 1 gives a complete EFX allocation of this finite instance. Grouping agents with the same type and bundle yields \(\lambda\). Conversely, a rational \(\lambda\) can be expanded into such a finite allocation. This is a genuine discrete-to-continuous identity, not a collision: the finite objects remain indivisible, while the limiting society is represented by masses.

The paper’s internal mechanism reinforces the computational interpretation. Theorem 6, also proved here, states that from any partial EFX allocation and one unallocated item, one can construct an EFX allocation that Pareto dominates the previous one. The proof repeatedly exchanges bundles of the least-well-off agents of the two valuation types and then removes items to eliminate newly created EFX envy. That exchange logic is exactly what one would try to lift to mass transfers between bundle classes.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A in the programme’s sense: population multiplicity itself creates no obstruction, and feasibility follows from the two-type structural theorem. A genuinely compressed algorithm—polynomial in \(m\) and the encoding length of \(\mu\) and \(q\), without expanding the enormous finite population—remains an interesting question. Any hardness is more likely to arise from the bundle-support problem as \(m\) grows than from the number of agents.

The weakest point is that scaling item supplies with population is a co-modification. If one insists on keeping the paper’s one-off finite item set while sending the population to a continuum, almost everyone receives nothing and the mirror is useless. The proposed course-cohort regime avoids that degeneration, and the programme explicitly permits per-capita supplies when population continuization forces them. It also preserves the paper’s central object—EFX for indivisible bundles—rather than replacing it with divisible-resource fairness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at the programme’s anchor gate: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 1 is an existence theorem, not a complexity, algorithmic, approximation, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Theorem 6 is likewise an existential exchange lemma. The remarks about polynomial-time EF1 and APX-hard Nash welfare occur only in related work and cannot serve as anchors.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 1 is mathematically coherent, but that is not enough. For rational \(\mu\) and \(q\), clearing denominators expands the continuous instance into a finite instance with \(N\mu_r\) agents and \(Nq_g\) item copies. Applying Theorem 1 and taking the bundle histogram gives \(\lambda\). Conversely, a rational \(\lambda\) expands back to a finite allocation. Thus the continuous feasibility statement is essentially an immediate encoding of the finite existence theorem. It does not expose a computational question proved by the paper.

The genuinely interesting version would ask for a succinct algorithm for the configuration

\[ \lambda_{r,b}, \]

perhaps polynomial in \(m\) and the bit length of \(\mu,q\), without expanding the denominator-cleared population. But that is a new configuration-support problem. The paper gives no running-time bound, no compact representation, and no pricing or separation result for the bundle configurations. Its termination argument only increases a potential over finitely many allocations; the number of iterations may be enormous, and the argument provides no bound polynomial in the continuous input encoding. Adding welfare optimization or a compressed-output requirement would make a worthwhile new project, but it would no longer be a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 6 does not rescue the case. Its proof relies on a named finite agent, a single unallocated item, an envy graph on individual agents, and strict improvement of

\[ \phi(X)=\sum_i v_i(X_i). \]

After normalization, one item copy and one agent have mass \(1/N\), so the proof’s progress becomes infinitesimal. In the mass formulation, \(\phi\) ranges over continuously many values and the finite-potential termination argument disappears. Aggregating equal bundle classes might produce a valid mass-transfer theorem, but proving that aggregation preserves the champion and minimum-preferred-set logic, gives finite support, and terminates efficiently would be entirely new work. Theorem 6 itself supplies none of this.

The per-capita supply modification is permissible and the course-allocation story is the strongest possible rescue. I would not reject it merely because items are replicated, nor because high-multiplicity work already exists. The honest objection is narrower: the resulting problem is a plausible new high-multiplicity fair-division problem, but this paper supplies no computational anchor for it. Its central contribution is finite existence under two valuation types, and the proposed continuum inherits that existence by denominator clearing rather than revealing a computational phenomenon about continuous societies.

So, if the formal anchor requirement is applied, there is no worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper for ChoCo. If that requirement is relaxed, the proponent’s model is admissible—but it should be presented as a new configuration-algorithm problem inspired by the theorem, not as a computational continuization established by the paper.
