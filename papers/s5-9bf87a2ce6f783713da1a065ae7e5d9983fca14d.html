The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: the paper’s main contribution is about mechanism axioms and approximation, and it has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its best computational anchor is the approximation result below. I would count that as an algorithmic result, while being clear that it is not a complexity-class classification.

Lead mirror: Weighted Percentile Radius Evaluation. Consider a large city choosing \(m\ge2\) uncapacitated service facilities in the plane. A type is a resident’s location \(t\in\mathbb Q^2\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents at that location. For example, millions of residents might be represented by the locations of thousands of residential buildings. Location is a complete type for this problem: distance costs depend only on it.

An instance consists of a finite support \(T\subset\mathbb Q^2\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a number of facilities \(m\), and fixed percentile parameters \(\mathbf p=(p^x_j,p^y_j)_{j=1}^m\). Define \(Q^x_a(\mu)\) as the weighted \(a\)-quantile of the residents’ \(x\)-coordinates, with \(Q^x_0\) the minimum; define \(Q^y_a\) similarly. The percentile rule places facility \(j\) at \(z_j^{\mathbf p}=(Q^x_{p^x_j}(\mu),Q^y_{p^y_j}(\mu))\). For a facility tuple \(Z\), let

\[ D_\mu(Z)=\max_{t\in T}\min_{z\in Z}\|t-z\|_2,\qquad R_m^*(\mu)=\min_{w_1,\ldots,w_m\in\mathbb R^2}D_\mu(\{w_1,\ldots,w_m\}). \]

The task is to return the percentile placement, an optimal radius and an attaining facility tuple, and the resulting approximation ratio \(D_\mu(Z^{\mathbf p})/R_m^*(\mu)\). If the optimum is zero but the percentile rule’s radius is positive, the ratio is infinite. The objective remains the paper’s maximum travel distance; the population distribution is what has changed.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for any two or more facilities in two or more dimensions of Euclidean space, no multidimensional PERCENTILE mechanism has a finite worst-case approximation ratio for maximum distance. The proof gives the two-facility, two-dimensional cases and then generalizes. If neither facility is at the coordinatewise maximum, put \(N-1\) agents at \((0,0)\) and one at \((1,1)\); if a facility is at that maximum and another is not at the minimum, reverse the large and small groups; if the facilities take the minimum and maximum corners, use the diagonal points \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\). Each instance has zero optimal radius and positive radius under the percentile rule. The paper also invokes an earlier total-distance result by Sui, Boutilier, and Sandholm, but Theorem 4’s maximum-distance claim is proved here.

Those examples give a direct high-multiplicity mirror: the masses can be rational, often \((N-1)/N\) and \(1/N\), so they are valid continuous societies with only two supported types. The weighted-quantile rule is the natural version of the paper’s coordinate-percentile mechanism on those masses. The authors would plausibly recognize this as their same facility-location and maximum-distance question in a population regime with \(N\) residents and \(\tau\ll N\) distinct locations.

For the mirror’s direction, Theorem 4’s negative guarantee carries over: aggregation does not give this percentile family a finite uniform ratio. For computing the exact optimum radius when \(m\) is an input, I expect hardness to transfer, since uniform mass on a finite set of points is already an instance of the same facility-placement problem. That is a prediction about the mirror, not a complexity result proved by Theorem 4. Further questions include the complexity in \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length; whether support-size restrictions yield useful approximation guarantees; and how to model incentives without making individual deviations have zero mass.

The weakest point is strategic: the paper studies strategy-proof mechanisms, but individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous for infinitesimal agents in a nonatomic society. This mirror deliberately covers Theorem 4’s approximation question and weighted percentile rule, not the paper’s axiomatic impossibility results or its strategic claims. The paper’s facilities and Euclidean distances were already continuous; the proposed mirror continuizes only the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I grant the proponent’s strongest modeling point: residents grouped by location form a plausible high-multiplicity society. The obstacle is the anchor. Theorem 4 proves a worst-case approximation guarantee for a family of mechanisms; it states no computational problem, runtime bound, or algorithm. “Weighted Percentile Radius Evaluation” adds a task the paper never states: compute the optimal radius, then divide. That is a possible new question, rather than a computational result from Theorem 4 carried into a continuous population.

The added task also splits cleanly. Weighted coordinate quantiles depend on the masses and can be computed by sorting the \(\tau\) supported locations and accumulating their rational weights. The optimal radius \(R_m^*(\mu)\) depends only on the support: it is the planar \(m\)-center problem of covering those locations with equal-radius disks. That problem has its own computational-geometry literature; the variable-\(m\) version is NP-complete, and parameterized approximation algorithms have been studied. [Tan’s planar \(p\)-center paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00224-025-10228-9), [Bandyapadhyay, Friggstad, and Mousavi](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/20305). This prior art is not itself a novelty collision under the programme’s rules. It does show that the hard optimization part of the proposed task comes from the support geometry, while changing multiplicities to masses leaves that part intact.

A stronger rescue might ask for distribution-sensitive or additive guarantees, or define meaningful group deviations. Those could be worthwhile ChoCo questions, but they require new questions beyond Theorem 4. In the stated continuum model, a unilateral agent has zero mass, so strategy-proofness of a percentile rule becomes vacuous; treating a positive-mass type as a deviator changes the strategic unit to a bloc.

So I find the negative case strong against this anchor, while the universal claim remains less secure: the proposed population scenario is sensible, and a carefully chosen mass-sensitive question could still be valuable. It would be a new research direction rather than a computational mirror of the paper’s named result.
