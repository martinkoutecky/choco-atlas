The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named results concern equilibrium and stability, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. For example, Theorem 3 (proved here) says that an \(sd\)-Nash equilibrium in truncations has either a singleton outcome support or no supported matching that every worker ranks weakly below every other supported matching. Proposition 3 (also proved here) establishes existence of such an equilibrium for admissible initial matchings. Those are substantial game-theoretic results, but neither gives an algorithm or a complexity classification. Theorem 1 is cited from Pais (2008), and likewise is not a computational result.

There is a conceivable high-multiplicity setting: many firms and workers fall into a small number of cohorts with identical preferences, and the initial matching is represented by a flow of mass between cohorts. But the paper’s game depends on individual firms taking turns, workers responding to particular offers, and agents’ histories. A continuum version would need new rules for how offer turns and responses work for masses. That might be a worthwhile new model, but I can’t claim the authors would recognize it as the continuous form of a computational problem established here.

So the honest positive case is limited: senior-level matching offers a plausible setting for studying a continuum of firm and worker types. This paper, however, supplies no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror questions. The main weakness is that preserving its strategic dynamics in a continuum would itself require substantial modeling choices; a static flow model would not capture the results the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 3 is a structural statement about the support of an equilibrium outcome; Proposition 3 establishes equilibrium existence under an admissibility condition. Neither poses an algorithmic problem or gives a complexity classification. Theorem 1 is cited from earlier work and is also about stability, not computation. So the proponent’s own named anchors do not supply the kind of result ChoCo is meant to mirror.

One could propose a better question: given a population with repeated preference types and an initial matching represented by mass flows, compute an equilibrium or test whether an equilibrium has a specified support property. But that requires defining a new nonatomic game. In this paper, a firm makes an individual offer at a particular turn, a worker accepts or rejects it based on her current history, and a deviation is evaluated against the outcomes of particular sequences of named firms. In an atomless model, one agent’s deviation has zero mass; replacing that deviation with a positive-mass cohort deviation changes the equilibrium test. Replacing the turn sequence with aggregate rates or a flow process changes the stochastic game as well. Those choices might produce a worthwhile research project, but they do not yield a canonical continuous version of Theorem 3 or Proposition 3.

A cohort setting for senior-level matching is conceivable, so the case against multiplicity in every possible labor market would be too broad. The narrower conclusion is stronger: this paper offers a strategic matching model, but no computational question whose high-multiplicity version it establishes. The negative case is persuasive for a ChoCo mirror of this paper; it is weaker against the possibility of a separate continuous matching-game project.
