The continuous mirror question

Given a rational finite support \(T\) of repeated two-member collaboration types, each with parameters \((V_{H,t},V_{L,t},a_t,c_t,D_t)\) in Theorem 1's valuation regime, determine each type's EA equilibrium profile \((d_H,d_L,e_H,e_L)\), the mass of each profile, and the exact mass-weighted cohort accuracy and welfare.

The model it lives in

A distribution over matched-pair types with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Each type retains the paper's two-member game, separate incentive pool, and binary contribution and effort actions; compute its equilibrium and weight its accuracy and welfare by \(\mu_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the EA equilibrium profiles and their cohort-level accuracy and welfare. It leaves the OA and SV equilibrium analyses, the cross-mechanism comparison in Theorem 4, and the numerical observations outside this anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • For cohorts whose pair types span valuation regimes, characterize equilibrium profile masses under OA and SV and determine whether the paper's accuracy and welfare ordering persists.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is a large cohort of repeated two-person collaborations: for example, an online course with many pairs working on unverifiable binary-answer tasks. The paper itself motivates its two-member model with course projects and research collaboration, so this keeps its strategic setting intact while making the *population of collaborators* large.

A type \(t\) describes a complete, repeatedly occurring pair configuration: its high- and low-valuation roles and their parameters \((V_{H,t},V_{L,t},a_t,c_t,D_t)\). The population is a finite support \(T\) of such configurations with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to one; \(\tau=|T|\) is small compared with the number of pairs. Each configuration represents matched masses of H- and L-role members. The pairing is part of the instance, since the paper’s incentives and decisions operate within each pair. Each pair still has its own incentive pool and binary effort decisions; the mass records its share of the cohort. Thus this continuizes the collaborating population, while preserving the paper’s binary outcome and two-stage game.

The strongest lead is the following Continuum Mechanism Audit. Given \(T\), its masses, and each type’s exact rational parameters, compute for every allocation mechanism \(z\in\{EA,OA,SV\}\) a paper-style equilibrium profile \(s_z(t)=(d_H,d_L,e_H,e_L)\) for each supported type. Return the profile masses and the exact cohort averages \(\bar p_z=\sum_t\mu_t P_{T,t}(e_z(t))\) and \(\bar w_z=\sum_t\mu_t[U_{H,t}(d_z(t),e_z(t))+U_{L,t}(d_z(t),e_z(t))]\); identify the mechanisms that maximize both measures. The actions remain those of the paper: \(d_m\in\{0,D_t\}\), \(e_m\in\{0,1\}\). The answer is a type-to-equilibrium map and its weighted outcome totals, not a model that replaces each pair’s game with a fractional action.

Its named anchor is Theorem 4 (Team Equilibrium Performance Comparison), proved in this paper. In the sufficiently heterogeneous valuation regime studied there, at equilibrium, \(p_T^{*SV}\ge p_T^{*OA}\ge p_T^{*EA}\) and \(w^{*SV}\ge w^{*OA}\ge w^{*EA}\). The paper’s focus is \(V_H/V_L>\max\{(14a-5)/(6a-1),(2a+1)/(3-2a)\}\). Apply that condition to each pair type. Since the theorem’s inequalities hold type by type, weighting them by \(\mu_t\) gives the same ordering for the cohort averages. This is a direct, useful population-level question: which incentive mechanism performs best across a large, heterogeneous cohort of the very collaborations the paper models?

A second, more explicitly constructive question is the EA Continuum Equilibrium Census: on the same kind of finite-support instance, compute the EA equilibrium profile for every type and return the mass of each profile and the resulting cohort accuracy and welfare. Its anchor is Theorem 1 (Equilibrium under EA), proved in this paper. In the paper’s studied valuation regime, with \(c_L=(2a-1)V_L/4\) and \(c_H=(2a-1)V_H/4\), the equilibrium always has \(d^*=(0,0)\). If \(0<c\le c_L\), then \(e^*=(1,1)\); if \(c_L<c\le c_H\), then \(e^*=(1,0)\); and if \(c>c_H\), then \(e^*=(0,0)\), for every \(D>0\). This gives a precise typewise rule for the census. For rational input parameters, evaluating the thresholds for \(\tau\) types and summing their masses yields a polynomial-time algorithm in the input bit length and \(\tau\). That complexity statement is an inference from the theorem’s formula, not a complexity classification printed in the paper.

The paper contains no named complexity-theoretic result: no theorem classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. These two theorems are the strongest constructive and performance anchors available, but they do not support a hardness-transfer claim or the usual question of whether discrete hardness disappears in a continuum.

The soft spot is that this is a cohort of independent dyads, not one large team whose members’ actions interact through a shared continuum-scale game. The pairing structure is essential, and the continuum mainly lets us compute cohort-level results from a small support of pair types; it does not create new mean-field strategic behavior. Still, the scenario is plausible, the paper itself studies exactly these two-person collaborations, and the outcomes remain binary. That makes this a modest but credible population mirror of its equilibrium and mechanism-performance results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibria in a two-player game, and Theorem 4 compares their performance. Neither asserts the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic classification. The paper’s results are about equilibrium behavior and mechanism design; turning them into computational social choice questions requires adding a new task.

For Theorem 4, a cohort of repeated two-person collaborations is plausible, and treating their configurations as types is a legitimate way to make the population continuous. But each pair’s game remains independent of every other pair. The cohort’s accuracy and welfare are weighted averages of pair-level results. Since Theorem 4 already gives a pointwise ordering under its heterogeneity condition, the cohort ordering follows by summing with nonnegative masses. This is a valid population-level observation, but the “mechanism audit” is supplied by the analyst: the paper does not study a shared population decision, and the mass introduces no strategic or computational interaction.

Theorem 1’s equilibrium census has the same limitation. For each type, apply the published thresholds and add its mass to the corresponding profile. That is a straightforward batch evaluation of a closed-form characterization. Calling it an algorithmic problem does not make the paper’s result a computational result; the complexity claim is an inference about a wrapper around the theorem. A version with varying parameters across types is plausible, but it extends the paper’s homogeneous ability and effort-cost assumptions rather than revealing a computational structure in the paper.

A stronger alternative would put many heterogeneous members into one shared team, with a common incentive pool and an aggregate majority outcome. That would create real population-level coupling. But the named results here depend on exactly two members: the OA probabilities and the Shapley allocations are derived for that game. In an atomless team, an individual’s marginal effect on aggregate accuracy may vanish, so a nontrivial Shapley mechanism would need a new scaling or definition. Such a model may be worth studying, but it would be a new multi-member game, not a continuous mirror of Theorems 1 or 4.

The negative case is weakest on whether the repeated-dyad cohort is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: it plainly can be. The objection is narrower. The proposed mirrors aggregate independent copies of an analytical two-player result; the more coupled version changes the game that the paper analyzes. On the paper’s results, neither route supplies the computational question ChoCo is looking for.
