Why no mirror

Corollary 1 is a computational result, but the proposed BMSE asks whether whole cohorts can profitably exchange positive mass. The paper’s equilibrium rules out profitable swaps by pairs of individual agents; in a population limit, an individual swap has infinitesimal effect on neighborhood averages, so the theorem does not establish the proposed condition. The BMSE may be easy to solve, but it is a different equilibrium problem.

What the mirror covers

The proposal covers only Corollary 1's equilibrium-existence and efficient-computability claim for bipartite graphs at \(\Lambda=1/2\); it leaves the Price of Anarchy and Price of Stability bounds and the other existence and finite-improvement results alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite \(K\)-fold blow-ups of a bipartite template, do limits of individual-swap equilibria satisfy a well-defined nonatomic pairwise-swap condition, and can such limit states be computed efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is algorithmic. The paper gives no named NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result, but Corollary 1, proved here from Theorem 4, says: for \(\Lambda=\tfrac12\), every game on a bipartite graph admits a swap equilibrium, and one can be computed efficiently. That is the result I would mirror.

My scenario is a large development built from many interchangeable copies of a finite set of residential roles. Let \(H=(V,E)\) be a connected bipartite template with no isolated vertices. Replace each role \(v\) by \(K\) residents, and connect every resident in role \(v\) to every resident in each adjacent role. As \(K\) grows, the population grows while there are still only two intrinsic agent types, blue and red; residents of a given color have the same utility function and available swaps. The role graph describes the neighborhood structure, and the society is a distribution of resident mass across those roles. This is a population limit, not a fractional outcome or a lottery over finite profiles.

Here is a precise mirror, Bipartite Mass-Swap Equilibrium (BMSE). An instance consists of \(H\) and a rational minority share \(\mu_B\le\tfrac12\). For each role \(v\), let \(x_{v,B},x_{v,R}\in[0,1]\) be the fractions of its resident mass of each color, with \(x_{v,B}+x_{v,R}=1\) and \(\sum_v x_{v,B}=|V|\mu_B\). In the large-\(K\) limit, the same-color neighborhood fraction for color \(c\) at role \(v\) is
\[ f_c(v;x)=\frac{1}{d(v)}\sum_{u\in N(v)}x_{u,c}. \]
This is the limit of the paper’s closed-neighborhood fraction as each role’s degree scales with \(K\); the single resident’s self-contribution becomes negligible.

A mass swap chooses distinct roles \(v,w\) and an amount \(\varepsilon>0\), moves blue mass \(\varepsilon\) from \(v\) to \(w\), and moves red mass \(\varepsilon\) from \(w\) to \(v\). It is profitable if, in the resulting allocation \(x'\), both moved cohorts strictly gain: \(p(f_B(w;x'))>p(f_B(v;x))\) and \(p(f_R(v;x'))>p(f_R(w;x))\). A solution is a feasible allocation with no profitable mass swap. The computational question is: given \(H\) and \(\mu_B\), find such an allocation, with rational output of polynomial bit length.

I expect BMSE to be Class A. In fact, the uniform allocation \(x_{v,B}=\mu_B\), \(x_{v,R}=1-\mu_B\) for every \(v\) is a solution. After a swap from \(v\) to \(w\), the blue fraction in \(w\)’s neighborhood either stays at \(\mu_B\) or decreases, since \(v\) loses blue mass. Because \(\mu_B\le\tfrac12\) and \(p\) is increasing up to \(\tfrac12\), the blue cohort cannot strictly improve. The swap therefore cannot be profitable for both colors. This gives a direct polynomial-time output and, in this model, even works beyond bipartite templates.

The scope is just Corollary 1’s equilibrium existence and computation result. I would not claim a mirror of the paper’s Price of Anarchy or Price of Stability results from this construction. The natural next question is whether the mass equilibrium has a useful two-way relationship with equilibria in the finite \(K\) blow-ups: does it round to an exact or approximate finite equilibrium, and do finite equilibria converge to mass equilibria?

My weakest point is the change in strategic granularity. In the paper, each node has one resident, so a profile is a binary coloring and an agent’s self-contribution remains part of its utility. Here a role contains both colors, and the self-contribution vanishes in the limit. The uniform solution depends on that change; it is not the independent-set construction behind the paper’s corollary. The case for the mirror is that dense, repeated neighborhood templates are a plausible high-multiplicity residential regime, and the mass-swap question retains the paper’s colors, graph-based neighborhood utility, and reciprocal swap stability. If the authors regard that regime as too different from their atomic network game, this anchor would not survive.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 1 is a genuine computational anchor: it guarantees and efficiently constructs a swap equilibrium for \(\Lambda=\tfrac12\) on bipartite graphs. The strongest objection is that the proposed BMSE changes what is being equilibrated. In the paper, a profile is a coloring of individual vertices, and a move exchanges two agents at specific positions. Each agent’s utility depends on the exact coloring of that position’s closed neighborhood. In BMSE, a positive mass moves at once, changing the neighborhood composition faced by whole cohorts. That is a collective deviation; the paper’s equilibrium rules out profitable swaps by pairs of individuals.

The proposed dense block construction makes that change especially consequential. Once neighborhood fractions are determined by role averages, the uniform allocation \(x_{v,B}=\mu_B\) is stable on any template: if two roles are nonadjacent, a swap leaves the moving cohorts’ neighborhood fractions unchanged; if they are adjacent, moving blue mass lowers blue’s same-color fraction, which cannot help when \(\mu_B\le\tfrac12\). The independent-set structure behind Corollary 1 has disappeared. This is a plausible nonatomic exchange game, but it does not preserve the paper’s pairwise equilibrium question.

A sparse repeated-neighborhood model would preserve more of the paper’s local structure. But role-level masses alone would lose the correlations among neighboring colors that determine utilities and profitable swaps. Restoring those correlations means tracking distributions of colored local configurations, or even whole module configurations. That may still yield a useful continuous model in a carefully chosen repeated-block regime, but it is no longer the simple low-support mirror proposed here.

So the case against this particular BMSE is substantial: it either smooths away the graph structure that supports the theorem or requires a richer state that makes the compression unclear. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case stick, though. Large residential blocks with repeated neighborhood roles are plausible, and the paper’s fraction-based utilities make a population limit natural. If the authors recognize that regime as their problem, its possibly easy or topology-independent answer is not grounds to reject it. The negative case is therefore weak against the claim that *some* worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
