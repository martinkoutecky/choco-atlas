The continuous mirror question

Given \(a\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\) and a rational polynomial \(p\) in coefficient-list form, consider a society with type \(A\) of mass \(a\) and null type \(Z\) of mass \(1-a\). For coalition masses \((x_A,x_Z)\in[0,a]\times[0,1-a]\), let worth be \(V(x_A,x_Z)=x_Ap(x_A)\). Compute each type's exact Shapley payoff density, defined as the limit of its finite Shapley payoff multiplied by population size along replications with \(qa\) type-\(A\) members. Is that density \(p(a)\) for \(A\) and \(0\) for \(Z\)?

The model it lives in

Use two interchangeable types, \(A\) and \(Z\), with masses \(a\) and \(1-a\). A coalition is represented by its type masses \((x_A,x_Z)\), has worth \(x_Ap(x_A)\), and receives type-level Shapley payoff densities defined by the finite-replica limit; there is no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 1's deterministic, unweighted bipartition case; it does not cover Propositions 2 and 3's randomized settings or the discussion of weighted Shapley values.

Open questions for a prover

  • For multi-type coalition worth functions, which succinct input representations make exact Shapley-density computation nontrivial?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Bipartition Mass-Shapley Density, anchored on Proposition 1 (proved in this paper). It says that for a fixed participation vector \(B\in\{0,1\}^n\), with \(\kappa_S=\sum_{i\in S}B_i\) and \(\kappa=\kappa_N\), if \(v(S)=\kappa_S f(\kappa_S)\) for \(f:\{0,\ldots,n\}\to\mathbb{R}\), then \(\phi_i(v)=B_i f(\kappa)\). Thus every participating player receives \(f(\kappa)\), every null player receives zero, and the total worth \(\kappa f(\kappa)\) is divided equally among participants. The paper’s accompanying discussion gives the computational consequence: evaluate \(f(\kappa)\), then produce the \(n\) allocations, for \(O(n)\) computations.

A credible high-multiplicity setting is a large cooperative purchasing pool with standardized members: coalition surplus depends on how many enrolled members it contains, and members with the same contract are interchangeable. Let the population have mass \(a\) of participating members, type \(A\), and mass \(1-a\) of null members, type \(Z\), where \(a\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\). The number of individuals can be very large while the number of types is two. This retains the paper’s substantive premise: coalition worth depends on the amount of participating membership in the coalition. The continuous object is the population; mass-normalizing worth is the natural extensive scaling that keeps total worth finite.

Precisely, take as input \(a\) and a rational polynomial \(p\in\mathbb{Q}[x]\), represented by its coefficient list. A coalition is described by masses \((x_A,x_Z)\in[0,a]\times[0,1-a]\), and its worth is \(V(x_A,x_Z)=x_Ap(x_A)\). The task is to compute the exact Shapley payoff density for each type, where the density is the limit of the finite Shapley payoff per individual multiplied by the number of individuals.

To define that limit, take population sizes \(q\) divisible by the denominator of \(a\), with \(qa\) type-\(A\) individuals and \(q(1-a)\) type-\(Z\) individuals. Give a coalition \(S\) worth \(v_q(S)=\frac{|S\cap A_q|}{q}p\!\left(\frac{|S\cap A_q|}{q}\right)\). Proposition 1 applies with \(f_q(k)=p(k/q)/q\). It gives each type-\(A\) individual Shapley value \(p(a)/q\), and each type-\(Z\) individual value zero. Therefore the continuous payoff densities are \(\Phi_A=p(a)\) and \(\Phi_Z=0\); their mass-weighted total is \(a p(a)=V(a,1-a)\). This is an exact, well-defined solution, computable by one polynomial evaluation (polynomial time in the dense input’s bit length). I expect this mirror to be tractable.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem at high multiplicity: it preserves the coalition worth rule and the Shapley allocation, replacing participating-player count with participating mass. It does not claim that the paper already studies a continuous population. I would not use Propositions 2 or 3 as anchors here: they extend the finite-player result to randomized participation or characteristic functions, but randomization is not population continuization.

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s deterministic, unweighted bipartition case only. A natural follow-up is whether similarly exact densities exist when participating agents split into multiple consequential types and worth depends on their vector of coalition masses.

The weak point is that a continuum of agents has no uniquely canonical Shapley value. This question resolves that by defining the density through the paper’s own finite Shapley values, so the limit is forced here rather than chosen by a new axiom. Still, this is a narrow, easy Class A mirror: its continuous solution follows directly from the proposition, and it does not by itself establish a broad complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The purchasing-pool scenario passes the high-multiplicity test: a large population with two interchangeable types is plausible, and Proposition 1 is a real computational result. I can’t honestly argue that the mirror fails as a model. The stronger case against it is that continuization removes the only complexity parameter this result studies.

For \(q\) replicas, the proposed construction gives each participating person \(p(a)/q\). The limiting payoff density is \(p(a)\), so the continuous computation takes \(a\) and \(p\), evaluates \(p(a)\), and returns two type-level values. The paper’s \(O(n)\) computation produces an allocation for each of \(n\) named players; after aggregation, \(q\) no longer appears in the algorithm or its running time. With a dense polynomial input, the remaining cost is polynomial evaluation. Any harder problem would come from choosing a more elaborate representation of \(p\), rather than from the population continuum or Proposition 1’s Shapley computation.

The best broader mirror would allow \(\tau\) active types and worth \(V(x)\) as a function of their coalition masses. For smooth \(V\), the finite-replica limit gives the Aumann–Shapley density \(\Phi_j(x)=\int_0^1 \partial_j V(tx)\,dt\). This continuous Shapley framework is established, including its derivation as a limit of discrete models; for polynomial \(V\), the integral can be computed directly from its coefficients. [Moulin and Sprumont’s survey](https://www.ruf.rice.edu/~econ/papers/2005papers/moulin%2009.pdf) describes both the discrete and continuous formulations. That analytic literature does not itself settle a computational-complexity question, so I would not call it a novelty collision. It does show, however, that the natural extension supplies a formula rather than a new algorithmic problem. More difficult encodings could manufacture one, but their complexity would be about the encoding of \(V\).

This is a limited negative case, not a universal proof. The proposed mirror is well-posed and has a credible population story. Its weakness as a ChoCo project is that a result already requiring only linear work becomes one evaluation once identical agents are represented by mass; the continuum offers little complexity landscape to chart.
