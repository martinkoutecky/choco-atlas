The continuous mirror question

Given item classes \(G\), roles \(U\), binary-rational supplies \(q_g\), and a finite support \(T\) of team types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) and additive values \(v_t(g)\), where each class \(g\) has an eligibility set in \(U\), promise that \(q\) can be realized by a distribution over feasible integral bundles. A bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\) is feasible if its item copies can be matched injectively to roles. Does there exist an algorithm polynomial in \(|G|, |U|, |T|\), and input bit length \(L\) that outputs a polynomial-size sparse rational list \(x_{t,b}>0\), with \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=q_g\), such that every pair of bundles \(b,b'\) used with positive mass satisfies EF1 for the recipient of \(b\): either \(b'\) is empty or some \(g\) with \(b'_g>0\) has \(\sum_h v_t(h)b_h\ge\sum_h v_t(h)(b'_h-\mathbf{1}_{h=g})\)?

The model it lives in

A unit mass of teams is divided among finitely many complete valuation types \(t\), each with mass \(\mu_t\). Decision variables \(x_{t,b}\) assign type mass to integral, role-feasible bundles \(b\); class supplies \(q_g\) scale with population mass, and every pair in the positive support must satisfy EF1. There is no welfare objective: the task is to construct a sparse rational feasible allocation.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3 states no runtime or complexity bound, so the mirror’s polynomial-time construction from succinct rational input is a new algorithmic claim rather than a bound established by the paper.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population-scale anchor is Theorem 3 in Section 4, rather than the paper’s headline three-agent theorem. Theorem 3 is proved here by combining cited matroid-reduction and partition-matroid allocation results. It says that for \(n\) agents with additive valuations, a complete, feasible EF1 allocation exists if the common matroid is \(n\)-colorable and transversal; if it is \(\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor\)-colorable and graphic; or if it is \(\lfloor(n+2)/2\rfloor\)-colorable and a gammoid. The proof is constructive, though the theorem statement does not give a runtime bound for a succinct, rational-mass input. I would build the mirror around its transversal case.

Call it Transversal Mass-EF1 Allocation. There is a finite set \(G\) of athlete or task classes and a finite set \(U\) of roles. Each class \(g\in G\) has an eligibility set in \(U\); a bundle is feasible for one team if its copies can be matched injectively to roles. The input gives binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and a finite support \(T\) of team types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type is a complete additive valuation vector \(v_t(g)\) over the classes. All teams share the same role-feasibility constraint.

The instance is promised feasible: there is a distribution \(z\) over feasible integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\) such that \(\sum_b z_b=1\) and \(\sum_b z_b b_g=q_g\) for every class \(g\). The question is to find a mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,b}\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(b\). It must satisfy \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=q_g\), and use only feasible bundles. It must also be EF1 across the entire support: whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\) and \(x_{t',b'}>0\), either \(b'\) is empty or there is a class \(g\) with \(b'_g>0\) such that
\[ v_t(b)\ge v_t(b'-e_g), \]
where \(e_g\) removes one copy of class \(g\). The solution is this rational mass vector \(x\); individual teams still receive integral bundles. Fractionality appears only in how mass is distributed across bundle types.

This has a credible regime: a large league or regional program forms many teams from a cohort with standardized role eligibility, while coaches fall into relatively few complete valuation types. The number of teams can be much larger than \(\tau=|T|\), and athlete supplies scale with the population. That is the paper’s own team-and-role example lifted to repeated cohorts, not a change to expected fairness or fractional bundles.

There is a strong exact bridge from the anchor to this mirror. Since the bundle set is finite and the feasibility constraints are rational, a feasible \(z\) can be chosen rational. Clear the denominators of \(z\), the \(\mu_t\), and the \(q_g\) with a common integer \(N\). This gives a finite instance with \(N\) teams, \(N\mu_t\) teams of each type, and \(Nq_g\) physical items of each class. The scaled bundles partition those items into feasible rosters, so the transversal matroid is \(N\)-colorable. Theorem 3 supplies a finite EF1 allocation; counting its type-and-bundle pairs and dividing by \(N\) gives an exact \(x\) satisfying the continuous conditions. EF1 survives aggregation because every positive-mass pair came from a pair of teams that satisfied EF1.

So the rational-mass existence question is already answered positively by this paper’s general-\(n\) theorem. The computational mirror worth pursuing is constructing a compact \(x\) directly from binary-rational data. I expect Class A, at least with a fixed or parameterized number of role and good classes: the finite theorem supplies the structural guarantee, and a configuration-based method could seek the bundle masses without expanding the population to \(N\) named teams. The further question is whether that construction can run in time polynomial in \(|G|,|U|,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. EF1 imposes pairwise compatibility on bundles used by positive mass, so the support choice is not automatically an LP; finding a compact formulation or an efficient support-selection method is part of the problem.

This mirror covers only Theorem 3’s transversal case. Its graphic and gammoid cases have different colorability assumptions, and I would leave them out of this first question.

The weakest point is also the main computational opportunity: the paper’s theorem already transfers existence to rational mass instances, so the mirror does not create a new existence theorem. Its value rests on whether the succinct mass-input search problem has a useful algorithmic treatment. The scenario also needs genuinely repeated team and athlete profiles; if every coach and every athlete has a distinct role or valuation pattern, the small-support premise disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is an anchor mismatch. The Section 4 transversal result is the paper’s only result with an unbounded number of agents, but it states an existence guarantee without a running-time bound or complexity classification. The paper’s explicit polynomial-time result is for \(n=3\). So the paper has a growing-population result without a stated computational result, and a computational result without a growing population axis. Under ChoCo’s requirement for a named computational anchor, the proposed direct algorithm for rational masses is not a continuization of a complexity result the paper establishes.

The proposed model itself is coherent. Repeated cohorts of teams, finitely many coach valuation types, and per-capita supplies give a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Keeping each team’s bundle integral also preserves the paper’s indivisible-goods problem. The proponent’s denominator-clearing argument is sound for existence: it lifts a rational mass instance to a finite transversal-matroid instance and aggregates a finite EF1 allocation back to mass. I would not reject the mirror on identity, indivisibility, or scenario grounds.

That bridge leaves a possible algorithmic project: compute a mass allocation directly, without expanding to the potentially enormous clone population implied by the input denominators. But the paper does not analyze that succinct-input problem. The proposed phrase “compact \(x\)” also needs a concrete output representation: the feasible-bundle universe can be exponential in \(|G|\), and EF1 constrains which type-and-bundle pairs may coexist in the support. Defining and solving that problem could be worthwhile; it would be a new high-multiplicity algorithmic question built around the paper’s existence theorem, rather than a mirror of its stated complexity result.

The three-agent polynomial-time theorem does not repair the gap: its agent count is fixed, so treating its agents as population masses changes the object being allocated and the meaning of EF1. Moving to many agents takes us back to the Section 4 existence result.

This is the best negative case I can make, and it does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The transversal mirror survives as a plausible research lead. The case against it is that its plausible computational target is not anchored in a named computational result of this paper, which weakens it under ChoCo’s stated screening standard.
