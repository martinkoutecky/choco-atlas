The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow positive case, anchored by Lemma 1. It is the only named result here that can qualify under the formulation exception; the paper states no named result about NP-hardness, polynomial time, or parameterized complexity.

Lemma 1, cited from Baïou and Balinski [5] rather than proved in this paper, says that in a many-to-one market with strict worker preferences and \(q\)-responsive firm preferences, a matching is stable exactly when its incidence vector is an integer solution of the polytope \(SCP\). That polytope imposes firm quotas, at most one assignment per worker, nonnegativity, zero assignments for unacceptable pairs, and, for every acceptable pair \((f,w)\), the inequality
\[ \sum_{j\succ_f w}x_{f,j} +q_f\sum_{i\succ_w f}x_{i,w} +q_fx_{f,w}\ge q_f. \]
The paper uses this formulation to define stable fractional matchings and to work through an example, so Lemma 1 is a reasonable formulation anchor. It does not itself claim that a linear program finds an integer stable matching.

A plausible mirror is Continuous Type-Stable Assignment. Imagine a large annual hiring or placement market: many applicants seek positions at a finite set \(F\) of employers, but applicants fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of complete types. A type records the applicant’s ranking of employers and the attributes employers use to rank applicants. Each employer ranks types and has capacity proportional to cohort size. Thus \(N\) can be very large while \(\tau\) stays modest. An assignment remains integral for each person; the variable \(x_{f,t}\) records the mass of type \(t\) assigned to employer \(f\).

The instance is \(F\), the type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), employer capacities \(\kappa_f\) per capita, and strict rankings over acceptable employer–type pairs. A solution is an exact rational assignment \(x\) with
\[ x_{f,t}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_f x_{f,t}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{f,t}\le \kappa_f, \]
and no blocking type–employer pair. In the natural mass version of stability, that condition is
\[ \left(\kappa_f-\sum_{t'\succeq_f t}x_{f,t'}\right) \left(\mu_t-\sum_{g\succeq_t f}x_{g,t}\right)=0 \]
for every acceptable \((f,t)\): there cannot be both employer capacity available for type \(t\) and positive mass of type \(t\) who would prefer \(f\) to their current assignment. The task is to find such an assignment; there is no welfare objective in the anchored problem. Its natural complexity parameters are \(|F|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

I would expect this problem to be tractable, in Class A, through a type-level stable-matching procedure or an equivalent optimization method. That is a forecast for the mirror, not a result established by this paper. The mirror covers Lemma 1’s stable-assignment layer. The paper’s Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 give structural decompositions and characterizations of strongly stable fractional matchings; they do not assert a computational complexity result. Extending those characterizations to mass assignments would be a further question: does the connected-set/convex-hull description survive, and how does it relate to mixtures of stable assignments in large finite cohorts?

The weakest point is the preference model. The paper ranks individual workers strictly, whereas a credible continuous type model treats workers of one type as indistinguishable to each employer. That requires rankings over types and per-capita capacities, and the authors would need to accept these as natural features of a high-multiplicity market. The case is strongest for cohort hiring or placement with shared applicant categories; it does not extend automatically to markets where employers’ decisions depend on individual identities.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 1 does not give this paper a computational result to mirror. It is Baïou and Balinski’s cited characterization of stable matchings as integer solutions of \(SCP\). The paper’s own Theorems 1 and 2 characterize strongly stable fractional matchings; they do not ask for an algorithm, an optimization objective, or a complexity classification. The proposed task—find a stable assignment for a society of types—is therefore a new problem attached to the paper’s setting, not a continuous version of a computation the paper studied.

The hiring scenario is plausible, and I would not reject it for lack of multiplicity. But the proposed assignment \(x_{f,t}\) changes what the paper’s fractional variables mean. In the paper, \(x_{f,w}\) can represent a lottery or time share for a particular named worker. In the mass model, \(x_{f,t}\) is the share of a type assigned to a firm: different people receive different integral assignments. Then the paper’s convex combinations of stable matchings become population splits within a type, rather than lotteries over assignments to the same people. The natural mass blocking condition,

\[ \left(\kappa_f-\sum_{t'\succeq_f t}x_{f,t'}\right) \left(\mu_t-\sum_{g\succeq_t f}x_{g,t}\right)=0, \]

is coherent, but it defines a continuum stability problem; it does not preserve the paper’s distinction between stable lotteries and strongly stable fractional matchings.

There is also a modeling cost hidden by the type definition. The paper assumes strict firm preferences over workers. If two applicants share a type in every respect the model uses, a firm must either rank them differently—making that difference part of their types and potentially destroying the small support—or be indifferent between them, which changes the paper’s assumptions. Tied priority categories are a natural extension, but they are an extension on which the paper’s characterizations would need to be re-established.

Continuum stable matching is already a well-developed neighboring model: Azevedo and Leshno study a continuum of students matched to finitely many colleges, with stable outcomes characterized by supply and demand; Arnosti gives a framework with finite capacities that includes continuum models and a deferred-acceptance analogue. These works are analytic, so I do not count them as having already answered the paper’s computational question. They do show, however, that the proposed stable-mass-assignment task is not a distinctive computational gap opened by this paper’s results. [Azevedo and Leshno](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/687476), [Arnosti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881).

My negative case is strongest against the actual Lemma 1 anchor: the proponent has supplied a sensible continuous matching model, but not a computational result from this paper whose mirror it is. It is weaker as a universal claim. A type-mass version of strong stability, especially with tied priority classes, could motivate a new structural or algorithmic project around Theorem 2. That possibility prevents me from claiming no worthwhile mirror can exist; it does not rescue the submitted anchor as a continuization of a computational result in this paper.
