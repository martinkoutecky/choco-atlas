The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational candidate set \(C\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported types \(t=(x_t,p_t)\), where \(p_t\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\), does weighted Conditional-Median, using a fixed clone-compatible weighted-median rule, satisfy \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(M(\mu))\le 11\min_{y_1,y_2\in C,\;y_1\ne y_2}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y_1,y_2)\), where \(c_t(y_1,y_2)=\max_{j:p_{tj}=1}|x_t-y_j|\) and \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y_1,y_2)=\sum_t\mu_t c_t(y_1,y_2)\)?

The model it lives in

Types are location-approval pairs \(t=(x_t,p_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\). The decision is the pair of distinct candidate sites chosen by Conditional-Median using masses and weighted medians, and the objective is \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the \(11\)-approximation guarantee of Theorem 2.2 for weighted social cost; leaves the \(5\)-approximation guarantee, the strategyproofness claim, and the sum-variant result outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does weighted Conditional-Median retain the \(11\)-approximation for social cost on every rational type distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a weighted-cohort version of the paper’s truthful facility-location problem. A plausible regime is a city with many residents, where a small number \(\tau\) of cohorts share the same ideal location and public approval set. For example, millions of residents might be represented by a few hundred neighborhood-and-service-preference types. Each cohort’s mass is its fraction of the population.

Formally, let \(C\subset\mathbb{Q}\) be a finite set of candidate sites and let the supported types be \(t=(x_t,p_t)\), where \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}\) and \(p_t\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\). The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with support size \(\tau\). A solution is a deterministic rule mapping reported distributions to a pair of distinct sites \((y_1,y_2)\in C^2\). A type’s cost is
\[ c_t(y_1,y_2)=\max_{j:p_{tj}=1}|x_t-y_j|. \]
The social cost is \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)=\sum_t\mu_t c_t(y)\), and the maximum cost is \(\mathrm{MC}_\mu(y)=\max_{t:\mu_t>0}c_t(y)\).

For incentives, the non-vacuous continuous version treats each supported type as a cohort: a cohort may report a different location for its entire mass, while its approval set and true location remain fixed when evaluating its cost. The rule is mass-cohort strategyproof if no such cohort can lower its cost by changing its report. The weighted Conditional-Median rule is the paper’s rule with cohort counts replaced by masses and medians by weighted medians, using a fixed tie-breaking convention.

My lead is Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper: Conditional-Median has social-cost approximation ratio at most \(11\) for the max-variant problem. Its continuous mirror is to establish, or refute, that for every rational \((C,\mu)\), the weighted Conditional-Median rule is mass-cohort strategyproof and
\[ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(M(\mu))\le 11\min_{y\in C^2,\;y_1\ne y_2}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y). \]
A solution is a proof of both properties for the rule, or a counterexample. I expect this to be tractable: the paper’s social-cost bounds use median and triangle-inequality arguments that appear to carry over when sums and cardinality comparisons become mass-weighted sums and comparisons. The cohort incentive claim needs its own proof.

A second mirror is Theorem 2.4, also proved here: Conditional-Median has maximum-cost approximation ratio at most \(5\). Ask whether the same weighted rule is mass-cohort strategyproof and satisfies
\[ \mathrm{MC}_\mu(M(\mu))\le 5\min_{y\in C^2,\;y_1\ne y_2}\mathrm{MC}_\mu(y) \]
for every such input. Again, a proof or counterexample is the solution; I expect the approximation bound to be tractable because the paper’s distance inequalities can be applied type by type.

The incentive foundation is Theorem 2.1, proved here: Conditional-Median is strategyproof for individual agents in the finite model. Its continuous question is whether the weighted-median version is strategyproof when a whole type cohort can change its reported location. I expect this to be tractable as well, but it is not a direct consequence of Theorem 2.1: a cohort with positive mass can move the weighted median more than one individual can move an ordinary median.

The scope here is the paper’s max-variant problem and its simultaneous approximation guarantees. The paper contains no named hardness, completeness, FPT, or polynomial-time complexity result to anchor a hardness-transfer claim. Further questions include the best simultaneous social- and maximum-cost bounds under mass-cohort truthfulness, and whether the weighted rule has tight bounds for arbitrary rational masses.

The soft spot is the change in incentive notion. If the continuum consists of nonatomic individuals, one person’s report has zero effect on the distribution, making individual strategyproofness vacuous. Treating a positive-mass cohort as the strategic unit gives a meaningful continuous question, but it is stronger than the paper’s individual truthfulness claim, and the authors might reject it as a different mechanism-design problem. There is also less computational bite than in an exponential-variable LP: with finite \(C\), unconstrained optimal placement can be found by checking candidate pairs. The mirror’s substance is the truthful-mechanism constraint and its approximation guarantees, not a new tractability result for the underlying placement objective.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about fit with ChoCo’s computational question, not whether a city can sensibly be represented by masses over location and approval types. It can. The paper’s three proposed anchors, however, concern a mechanism’s guarantees: Theorem 2.1 proves individual strategyproofness, while Theorems 2.2 and 2.4 bound its social and maximum cost. They do not classify the complexity of a problem or study an algorithm for finding an optimal intervention. The placement problem itself is small: with finite \(C\), one can evaluate all \(|C|(|C|-1)\) facility pairs, and the proposed mechanism uses medians and nearest available sites. There is no pricing problem or hidden optimization over exponentially many outcomes for a continuous model to expose.

The two approximation anchors are especially hard to make into new questions. For rational masses, choose a common denominator \(D\) and replace each type \(t\) by \(D\mu_t\) identical agents. For every facility pair \(y\),
\[ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)=\frac{\mathrm{SC}_{D}(y)}{D}, \qquad \mathrm{MC}_\mu(y)=\mathrm{MC}_{D}(y). \]
With clone-compatible weighted medians, the continuous instance produces the same outcome as this finite instance. Thus the factors \(11\) and \(5\) follow directly from the paper’s theorems. The clone expansion need not be materialized: weighted medians and candidate comparisons can be computed from the \(\tau\) supported types. That gives a compact implementation, but not a new optimization result or complexity landscape.

Theorem 2.1 has a similar fork. For a genuinely atomless society, one person’s report changes no mass and cannot change the outcome, so individual strategyproofness is vacuous. If each person retains positive mass, an individual deviation is a single-clone deviation and is already covered by the finite theorem after scaling. The proponent’s mass-cohort version avoids vacuity only by letting an entire cohort coordinate its reports. That is a real question, but it asks for group strategyproofness, a stronger incentive property than the paper proves. A question about the best guarantees among all such cohort-strategyproof mechanisms would be a further mechanism-design problem, without an algorithmic anchor in this paper.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight. The cohort scenario is plausible, and group-strategyproofness for this weighted median rule may be a worthwhile extension for mechanism-design researchers. But its likely contribution is a weighted or coalition-level theorem, not a continuous-computation result of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart. I would reject the paper as a strong ChoCo mirror while acknowledging that this is a case against its programme fit, not proof that no worthwhile continuous research question can be built from it.
