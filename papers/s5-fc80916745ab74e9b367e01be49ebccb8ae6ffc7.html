What the mirror covers

The proposed task computes LENDRECOUP’s allocation and credit trajectories over type masses; it does not establish continuum versions of the paper’s incentive, credit-fairness, or efficiency guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is for aggregating and executing LENDRECOUP in a high-multiplicity resource pool. It is a narrow case: the paper has no numbered theorem establishing a standard complexity classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. Its closest named computational-mechanism anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: LENDRECOUP is online strategy-proof. In full, for any round \(t\), agent \(i\), true demand history, and reports by the other agents, if \(i\)’s reports agree with their true demands through round \(t-1\), then truthful reporting in round \(t\) gives at least as much utility in that round as any misreport in that round.

A natural regime is a large shared compute pool with many tenants following a small number of recurring workload templates. Define a type \(\theta\) by its endowment \(e_\theta\) and its complete demand sequence over an explicit horizon \(H\). Tenants of the same type have identical endowments and demand histories. The input is a finite support \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\), summing to one. A type’s mass is its share of tenants; \(\tau\) may be far smaller than the population. Let \(\bar E=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta e_\theta\) be supply per unit mass. The decision variables are each type’s per-capita allocation \(a_{\theta,t}\) and credit \(c_{\theta,t}\); total allocation is measured by \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta a_{\theta,t}\). Allocations remain expected resource amounts, as in the paper. The continuity here is in the population representation.

Mass-LENDRECOUP Execution is the concrete mirror: given these types, masses, and demand reports for \(H\) rounds, compute the exact allocation and credit trajectories prescribed by the LENDRECOUP rules. There is no separate welfare objective in the paper; the mechanism determines the outcome. Its PSWC step becomes a weighted water-filling computation: find \(\lambda\) so that
\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta \operatorname{clip}(\lambda e_\theta;m_\theta,\ell_\theta)=A, \]
then assign each type \(a_\theta=\operatorname{clip}(\lambda e_\theta;m_\theta,\ell_\theta)\). Here \(A\) is the stage’s resource total, and \(m_\theta,\ell_\theta\) are its bounds. The shortage branches use the same credit-adjusted caps and cumulative-allocation bounds as Algorithm 1, replacing sums over agents with mass-weighted sums over types; credits update by \(c_{\theta,t+1}=c_{\theta,t}+e_\theta-a_{\theta,t}\). The output is the resulting exact rational allocation and credit trajectory.

For this finite-support execution problem, I expect Class A: sort the \(O(\tau)\) waterline breakpoints at each stage and compute the threshold, giving \(O(H\tau\log\tau)\) arithmetic operations and polynomial bit complexity in the explicit input length. This running-time claim is a derived property of the weighted execution problem, not a theorem claimed by the paper. The paper’s Proposition 2 establishes that LENDRECOUP is Pareto efficient, and Theorem 5 establishes that it satisfies credit fairness; these make it meaningful to compute the mechanism’s prescribed outcomes at scale. Theorem 6 supplies the incentive guarantee of the mechanism being executed.

The mirror covers the LENDRECOUP allocation rule and raises a natural follow-up: does its online incentive guarantee persist in a continuum model with individual deviations? It should not be assumed. In an atomless population, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot change aggregate water-filling thresholds; moreover, credit fairness condition CF2 tracks whether an individual was pivotal, a notion that may become vacuous under a direct measure-theoretic translation. A careful individual-versus-cohort incentive model is needed before claiming that Theorem 6 or the credit-fairness result transfers.

I would not use Theorem 3—proved here, it rules out an anonymous mechanism satisfying strategy-proofness, credit fairness, and Pareto efficiency—as a second anchor. Its finite-agent counterexample depends on a unilateral report changing allocations through the population profile. Turning the continuous analogue into a cohort’s collective deviation changes individual strategy-proofness into a group incentive requirement, which the paper does not prove. This is also the weakest point of the positive case overall: the workload-template regime is plausible but not established by the paper’s 50-agent experiments, and the execution problem is a modest aggregation of an existing algorithm rather than a new complexity theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper proves no complexity classification for a computational problem. Theorem 6 establishes an incentive property; Theorem 3 gives an incompatibility among mechanism axioms; Proposition 2 and Theorem 5 establish properties of a particular mechanism. The experiments compare outcomes. The proposed mass-execution task is therefore a new computational question attached to the paper, rather than a continuous version of one of its computational results.

The workload-template scenario is plausible: many tenants could share an endowment and a recurring demand path. In that regime, Mass-LENDRECOUP is well-defined. But its PSWC step is weighted water-filling: sort at most \(2\tau\) breakpoints and solve for one threshold. Across \(H\) rounds, that gives \(O(H\tau\log\tau)\) arithmetic operations, with exact rational arithmetic. The paper already cites an \(O(n\log n)\) implementation of the same primitive. When \(\tau\ll n\), grouping identical tenants could be useful in a resource system, but it is a direct compression of an explicit algorithm, with output size \(H\tau\). Theorem 6 contributes nothing to that computation, and there is no pricing problem or hidden combinatorial search to expose.

Theorem 6’s content is a named individual’s unilateral report. With a finite population, a deviating member of a cohort can be represented by splitting off a mass \(1/N\); the resulting instance is just the finite mechanism on \(N\) copies. In an atomless model, that person has zero mass and cannot move the population waterline. Their own allocation might still depend on their report, but modeling that requires a tagged price-taking individual outside the type-mass state. A positive-mass report instead describes a cohort deviation, which changes the incentive question. Either way, the execution problem does not mirror the theorem’s computational content.

There is a related obstacle for Theorem 5. Under a direct atomless translation, credit condition CF2 loses its pivotal-agent force: removing one individual changes aggregate supply by zero, while Pareto efficiency bounds the others’ total valued allocation by total supply \(E\). The strict condition that triggers CF2 therefore cannot hold. A type-cohort credit ledger could restore a meaningful accounting condition by weighting credits and supply by cohort mass, but that would be a new fairness axiom about cohorts. Proposition 2’s efficiency guarantee may transfer cleanly; that still gives a property of the allocation rule, not a computational problem. Theorem 3’s finite unilateral-deviation witness likewise does not establish a continuum impossibility: a cohort’s collective deviation is different from the individual deviation in the theorem.

So I would not call the negative case airtight. Repeated workload templates make high multiplicity sensible, and mass execution could be a useful systems result. But on the paper’s actual anchors, the continuous questions either reduce to routine weighted water-filling or require replacing individual incentive and credit claims with new cohort-level questions. I see no substantial computational landscape here for ChoCo to chart.
