What the mirror covers

The candidate distributional model restates scoring-rule winner selection; it does not mirror either theorem as a computational result. The paper's axiomatic characterizations remain outside the programme's computational target.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: no theorem says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which characterizes scoring rules by Universal Domain, Ballot Anonymity, Neutrality, and Positive Responsiveness; and Theorem 2, proved here, which characterizes qualified scoring rules by Universal Domain, Ballot Anonymity, Positive Responsiveness, General Abstention, and Coalition Permanency. Both are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results.

The strongest nearby continuous model is straightforward: fix alternatives \(A\) and a balloting procedure, let each type \(t\) be one permitted ballot combination, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of society with that type. Given the score \(s(t,a)\) that type \(t\) gives alternative \(a\), return
\[ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in A}\sum_t \mu_t s(t,a). \]
A large organization with standardized ballot options could plausibly have many voters but relatively few distinct ballot types. The authors would likely recognize this as continuous-population aggregation. Winner determination takes \(O(\tau |A|)\) arithmetic operations.

But this is not a valid anchored case under your criteria. The model only restates the scoring-rule aggregation operation over a distribution; it does not mirror a computational result of the paper. Making Theorem 1 or 2 into the anchor would continuize their axiomatic characterizations, while the programme’s target is computational complexity. And this simple winner problem offers no discrete hardness whose fate in the continuum is at stake. My positive case is therefore weak: a natural population mirror exists, but the paper provides no eligible computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the level of the paper’s actual results. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results. A continuous version could restate their axioms over a population distribution, but that would continue the axiomatic analysis; it would not chart the computational landscape ChoCo targets. The paper itself emphasizes that distinction: it studies aggregation properties, while strategic voting is beyond its scope.

The proposed society of ballot types is sensible. Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass using ballot type \(t\). Then the winner is determined by maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t s(t,a)\). That is a faithful continuous form of the scoring rule, but it supplies no computational result from the paper to investigate. Winner determination is not posed as a complexity problem in the source. Making it one would add a question rather than mirror a result.

The best rescue is to add a costed intervention: choose mass transfers between ballot types to make a target alternative win. With fixed transfer costs, the scoring constraints are linear, so this gives a plausible continuous optimization problem. But the paper specifies no ballot changes, costs, campaigning, or robustness objective. Those additions import the computational problem wholesale; the characterizations do not select or motivate one over many possible extensions. The same applies to Theorem 2’s weighted scoring rules.

So I would not count either theorem, or winner computation, as a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper for ChoCo. The limit of this negative case is that it cannot show no worthwhile adjacent project exists: a continuous study of costed ballot changes could be sensible. It can show that such a project would be new work built around scoring rules, rather than a continuization of this paper’s results.
