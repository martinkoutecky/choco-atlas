The continuous mirror question

Given finite resident types \(I\), house types \(J\), binary approvals \(u_{ij}\), rational type masses \(\mu_i>0\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\geq 0\) with \(\sum_j q_j\geq 1\), and a rational threshold \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), decide whether there is an assignment of mass \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq q_j\), for which \(E(x)=\sum_i\sum_{j:u_{ij}=0}x_{ij}\mathbf{1}[v_i(x)>0]\leq\alpha\), where \(v_i(x)=\sum_{i'}\sum_{j:u_{ij}=1}x_{i'j}\). Equivalently, minimize \(E(x)\) over feasible assignments.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete binary approval types; house types have per-capita supplies \(q_j\). The decision variable \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type-\(i\) residents receiving one whole house of type \(j\), and the objective is the mass assigned a disapproved house when positive mass receives a house that type approves.

The objection that survived

A vanishing positive mass assigned to approved houses can make a fixed mass of residents envious, so the objective is not stable under ordinary convergence of allocations; the proposed case notes the support trigger but does not directly address this topological caveat.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a population mirror of binary Optimal House Allocation (OHA). The anchor is Theorem 4.8: \([0/1]\)-OHA is NP-complete even when every agent approves at most two houses. The paper proves this via a reduction from Clique on regular graphs; the reduction is here, while the hardness of that restricted Clique problem is cited from elsewhere. [The published paper, Theorem 4.8](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/355342/2/355342.pdf)

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large housing allocation in which residents fall into repeated approval profiles: for example, cohorts that value the same kinds of units. Group houses by which resident types approve them. A resident type is thus a complete binary approval profile over house types; a house type has the same approval pattern across all resident types. If there are \(\tau\) resident types, any rational type distribution can be realized by taking sufficiently many copies of every type, so the number of residents can greatly exceed \(\tau\). Let \(\mu_i\) be the fraction of residents of type \(i\), and let \(q_j\) be the supply of house type \(j\) per resident. Then \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\) and \(\sum_j q_j\geq1\). The per-capita house supply is the co-modification needed to keep the allocation problem meaningful as the population grows.

Call the resulting decision problem Mass-OHA. Its input is finite sets of resident types \(I\) and house types \(J\), binary approvals \(u_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), rational supplies \(q_j\), and a rational threshold \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). A solution is a nonnegative assignment matrix \(x\) with
\[ \sum_{j\in J}x_{ij}=\mu_i \quad(i\in I),\qquad \sum_{i\in I}x_{ij}\leq q_j \quad(j\in J). \]
Here \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type-\(i\) residents assigned one whole house of type \(j\). The houses and each resident’s bundle remain indivisible; only the aggregate assignment is measured by mass.

For each resident type \(i\), let
\[ v_i(x)=\sum_{i'\in I}\sum_{j\in J:\,u_{ij}=1}x_{i'j}, \]
the mass of residents receiving a house that type \(i\) approves. The total mass of envious residents is
\[ E(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j:\,u_{ij}=0}x_{ij}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[v_i(x)>0\right]. \]
Mass-OHA asks whether a feasible \(x\) has \(E(x)\leq\alpha\), with \(x\) as its witness; its optimization version minimizes \(E(x)\). This is the direct population analogue of OHA: a resident assigned a disapproved house envies residents assigned houses they approve, and the objective measures the mass of residents who envy anyone.

The analogy has a useful high-multiplicity dictionary. In a finite instance with \(n_i\) residents of type \(i\) and \(m_j\) houses of type \(j\), set \(\mu_i=n_i/n\) and \(q_j=m_j/n\). A discrete allocation gives \(x_{ij}\) in multiples of \(1/n\), with \(E(x)=\kappa^\#(\Phi)/n\). Conversely, a rational mass assignment can be realized as an ordinary allocation after taking sufficiently many copies so its entries and supplies become integral. The paper already groups binary-valuation agents and houses into types for its formulations, so this is a natural extension of its own model. The requested mirror changes the population scale while preserving the underlying question of who receives a house and who envies whom.

I would expect unrestricted Mass-OHA to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C), though Theorem 4.8 does not prove that. The source reduction relies on indivisible choices among individual agents and houses, so hardness does not automatically transfer when a type’s mass can split. But the mass objective retains a combinatorial trigger: if any positive mass of a type-approved house is allocated, all residents of that type who receive disapproved houses count as envious. That support condition may preserve difficult choices even when assignment variables are fractional. The concrete open questions are whether this trigger can be handled by a polynomial algorithm, whether Mass-OHA remains hard when each type approves at most two house types, and whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in the number of resident or house types.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 4.8’s binary OHA hardness, not the paper’s general-ranking, egalitarian, utilitarian, or welfare results. The weakest point is that the paper’s Clique reduction constructs individual agents whose choices are integral, whereas Mass-OHA permits each resident type to divide across house types. That could dissolve the reduction’s combinatorics. The mirror still seems worth asking: it is precisely the population-scaled version of the authors’ envy-minimization problem, and its support-based objective gives a specific, testable reason why hardness might survive.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is the paper’s NP-completeness result for binary OHA when each agent approves at most two houses. My strongest objection is that the proposed mass objective has no stable limit under ordinary convergence of allocations. If type \(i\) has mass \(a_i\) assigned to disapproved houses and any positive mass \(b_i\) receives houses it approves, its contribution is \(a_i\mathbf{1}[b_i>0]\). A finite society can have \(b_i=1/N\), so one person’s assignment makes all \(a_i\) envious; as \(N\) grows, that approved mass vanishes, and the limiting allocation with \(b_i=0\) incurs no envy. A single person can therefore change the normalized objective by a fixed amount even as their share tends to zero.

Repairs expose the modeling choice rather than remove it. Requiring \(b_i\geq\epsilon\) introduces a threshold that changes the finite problem; replacing the trigger with a smooth pairwise measure changes OHA into a different envy objective. The exact positive-mass trigger is still a clear, computable support-selection problem, but it is not a topology-stable limit of the paper’s individual-envy objective. That weakens the claim that this is the uniquely natural continuization.

It does not, however, defeat the mirror. A housing authority could serve large cohorts with repeated approval profiles and standardized house types, with supplies scaled per resident. The paper itself defines agent and house types and gives type-parameterized algorithms, which supports the idea that repeated profiles are sensible here. The programme also permits the co-scaling of house supply, and the exact rational-mass model has a discrete high-multiplicity dictionary.

So the negative case is weak: the discontinuity is a real modeling caveat, but the authors could reasonably accept the positive-mass definition as their question. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile scenario exists, or defeat this anchor under the programme’s stated standard.
