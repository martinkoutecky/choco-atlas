The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \(m\) houses, a true ranking \(t^\star\) with mass at least \(\alpha>0\), and truthful reports by the rest of society, choose a common report \(\rho\) for a cohort of mass \(\alpha\) from \(t^\star\). After moving that mass to \(\rho\), run PS with unit supply of each house and mass-weighted eating rates; return a report maximizing the cohort's per-agent allocation lexicographically according to \(t^\star\).

The model it lives in

Types are strict rankings, with rational masses \(\mu_t\); a cohort of mass \(\alpha\) moves from its true type \(t^\star\) to report \(\rho\). Each type consumes at aggregate rate equal to its mass, each house has unit supply, and the cohort evaluates its per-agent PS allocation by the DL order induced by \(t^\star\).

The objection that survived

The theorem concerns a unilateral deviation: a fixed positive mass \(\alpha\) acts as a coalition, while the exact finite-agent correspondence uses \(\alpha=1/N\), which vanishes as \(N\) grows.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.9's DL best-response algorithm. It leaves the expected-utility best-response result, the cycling result, the Nash-equilibrium existence result, and the two-agent equilibrium algorithms aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the stingy-ordering construction behind Theorem 4.9 extend to mass-weighted PS with running time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • How do optimal reports in the mass model relate to finite instances on rational mass grids, especially when the deviating cohort's mass tends to zero?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s DL best-response problem. In a large public placement lottery, for example, many applicants may rank the same standardized menu of scarce places in the same order. Let a type be a strict ranking of the \(m\) houses, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of applicants with ranking \(t\). Suppose \(\mu\) is given by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and binary rational masses. Mark a cohort of mass \(\alpha>0\) within type \(t^\star\), where \(\mu_{t^\star}\ge\alpha\). Its members share true ranking \(h_1\succ^\star\cdots\succ^\star h_m\); the rest of society reports truthfully.

Call the mirror \(\textsc{DL-Mass-BestResponse-PS}\). For any report \(\rho\), move mass \(\alpha\) from \(t^\star\) to \(\rho\), run probabilistic serial with unit supply of each house, and let \(x^\rho(h)\) be the marked cohort’s per-person allocation. In this fluid version, each unit of mass eats at unit speed, so a type of mass \(\lambda\) consumes at aggregate rate \(\lambda\). The task is to return a report \(\rho\) whose vector \((x^\rho(h_1),\ldots,x^\rho(h_m))\) is lexicographically maximal. That is exactly the DL best-response objective: no other report gives the cohort a DL-preferred allocation. The masses and \(\alpha\) are rational; the input size is measured in \(m,\tau\), and their bit length \(L\).

The anchor is Theorem 4.9, proved in this paper: \(\textsc{DLBESTRESPONSE}\) can be solved in \(O(nm^3)\) time. Its input is an \(n\)-agent, \(m\)-house assignment problem with strict preferences and a designated agent; it returns a report for that agent such that no alternative report gives a DL-preferred allocation under their true ranking. The authors prove it by constructing a best response through successive partial preference lists and PS simulations.

There is an exact bridge to the anchor. If the masses lie on a \(1/N\) grid and \(\alpha=1/N\), the marked cohort is one agent in a population of \(N\). The mass-PS process reproduces that finite PS profile after scaling each agent’s allocation by \(N\); this common positive scaling does not change DL comparisons. The continuous instance then compresses \(N\) named applicants into \(\tau\) ranking types. In a regime with \(N\gg\tau\), the natural target is an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), rather than in the expanded applicant count.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, though that is a conjecture to prove, not a result of the paper. The paper’s algorithm depends on an event-based simulation of PS and a structural ordering of houses in a best-response list. In the mass model, each event’s eating rate is a sum of type masses, so the simulation can operate on \(\tau\) types using rational arithmetic. The central question is whether the paper’s structural lemmas still hold for these weighted rates; if they do, they could yield a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\).

The soft spot is that arbitrary \(\alpha\) makes this a coordinated cohort deviation, whereas the theorem concerns one agent. The \(1/N\)-mass case preserves the paper’s unilateral problem exactly, but extending to arbitrary mass asks whether the authors would accept a cohort best response as their problem’s continuous form. I think it is a plausible extension because it preserves PS, the true DL objective, and the report space, while replacing repeated identical agents with their mass. It is less secure as a mirror than a result whose underlying actor is already a population or coalition.

This case covers only Theorem 4.9. It does not extend the paper’s Nash-equilibrium, cycling, or expected-utility results. The fractional lottery outcome in PS is already part of the original rule; the mirror’s new continuous object is the population distribution over rankings. The paper gives no comparable hardness result, so the algorithmic theorem is the appropriate anchor. The further questions are whether the weighted version of the stingy-ordering argument proves a \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bound, and how its solutions relate to finite profiles on \(1/N\) grids.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only computational anchor is Theorem 4.9, the polynomial-time algorithm for one agent’s DL best response. Its main difficulty as a continuous mirror is strategic: in an atomless society, one agent has zero mass, so changing that agent’s report does not change the PS population flow. Under literal mass accounting, the individual’s allocation is not represented at all. To recover it, one needs a separate tagged-agent model, such as a first-order account of the allocation as the agent’s mass tends to zero. That may be a useful model, but it is not the paper’s ordinary PS instance specified by a distribution over types.

The proponent’s positive-mass repair keeps the feedback, but it changes the action. A cohort of mass \(\alpha>0\) that jointly switches reports performs a coalition manipulation. The case \(\alpha=1/N\) reproduces a finite individual’s deviation, but along a high-multiplicity sequence that cohort’s mass vanishes; arbitrary positive \(\alpha\) instead represents a nontrivial coalition. Scaling supplies can preserve a meaningful per-capita allocation, as the programme permits, but it does not remove this distinction between an individual deviation and a bloc changing the aggregate eating rates.

That mismatch is not enough to rule out the mirror. The authors explicitly list coalitional manipulations as future work, and a large cohort of applicants with the same preferences is a plausible bloc. The resulting mass-coalition best-response problem could therefore be one they recognize, even though it is not the unilateral problem proved in Theorem 4.9. Nor does the theorem’s \(O(nm^3)\) algorithm settle the compressed-input question: expanding rational masses may take time exponential in their bit length.

So the strongest negative case is that the exact unilateral mirror degenerates in a genuinely atomless model, while the nondegenerate version changes the problem to coalition manipulation. But the paper itself makes that change particularly defensible. I can’t honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives; this is a real objection to the anchor’s interpretation, but a weak case for the universal negative.
