The continuous mirror question

Given a finite graph \(H=(T,E)\) and positive rational role masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute exactly \(\alpha_\infty(H,\mu)=\min_{p\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T},\ p_u+p_v\ge 1\ \forall uv\in E}\ \max_{I\subseteq T,\ I\text{ independent in }H}\sum_{t\in I}\mu_t p_t\), and output an optimal vector \(p\).

The model it lives in

Each \(t\in T\) is a cohort of interchangeable members with population share \(\mu_t\). Members of linked types form minimal winning pairs, so \(p_u+p_v\ge 1\) for every \(uv\in E\); independent sets of types describe losing coalitions. Minimize the largest losing-coalition payoff per population member, \(\max_I\sum_{t\in I}\mu_t p_t\).

The objection that survived

Winning still has an atomic pair witness, so the model is a normalized role-cohort blow-up rather than a fully atomless cooperative game; this limits the interpretation but leaves the high-multiplicity question intact.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact threshold computation for graphic games, including the arbitrary-graph hardness and bipartite polynomial-time results. It leaves the paper’s structural bounds, complete-game bound, and separate fixed-threshold result unclaimed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the exact complexity of computing \(\alpha_\infty(H,\mu)\) for rational masses, including whether the arbitrary-graph hardness reduction and bipartite algorithm extend with running time polynomial in \(|T|\) and the mass encoding length.
  • Does a useful atomless coalition semantics preserve the same critical-threshold question, or is the exact high-multiplicity blow-up model the appropriate continuous formulation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is the paper’s graphic-game problem in a high-multiplicity regime. Consider a large federation with many interchangeable members in each of \(\tau\) role classes. Each member has the same interactions as every other member of their class, and a coalition wins as soon as it contains a pair of members from two linked classes. The classes form a graph \(H=(T,E)\); their population shares are rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\). The number of members \(M\) can be much larger than \(\tau\).

My lead question is Mass-Critical-Threshold. Given \(H\) and \(\mu\), compute the normalized critical threshold
\[ \alpha_\infty(H,\mu) = \min_{\substack{p\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T}\\ p_u+p_v\ge 1\ \forall\,uv\in E}} \ \max_{\substack{I\subseteq T\\ I\text{ independent in }H}} \sum_{t\in I}\mu_t p_t . \]
Here \(p_t\) is the payoff assigned to each member of type \(t\). The edge constraints ensure that every minimal winning pair receives at least \(1\); an independent set describes the types that can jointly form a losing coalition. A solution is an optimal per-type payoff vector \(p\) and the exact value \(\alpha_\infty\).

This is the population mirror of the paper’s computation, with an exact finite-instance dictionary. For any \(M\) such that \(M\mu_t\) is an integer for every type, replace each type \(t\) by \(M\mu_t\) identical players, and connect every member of type \(u\) to every member of type \(v\) whenever \(uv\in E\). The resulting graph is still a graphic simple game. Symmetry averaging permits an optimal payoff constant within each type, and then
\[ \frac{\alpha(\text{blow-up of }H)}{M}=\alpha_\infty(H,\mu). \]
Thus mass is the fraction of the population in a role class, and the objective is the worst losing coalition’s payoff per population member. This is a high-multiplicity version of the authors’ own graphic-game problem, not a change to the outcome space.

The lead anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper: computing \(\alpha_G\) for arbitrary graphs \(G\) is NP-hard. Their reduction starts with a graph \(G\) whose maximum independent set has size \(k\), makes two copies, and adds cross edges between copies exactly for identical or adjacent original vertices. For the resulting graph \(G^*\), they prove \(\alpha_{G^*}=k/2\). [The paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.08841)

The mirror inherits that hardness. Give every type in \(G^*\) mass \(1/|V(G^*)|\). Then \(\alpha_\infty=\alpha_{G^*}/|V(G^*)|=k/(4|V(G)|)\), so an exact answer recovers \(k\). I expect hardness to transfer: the reduction’s combinatorics remain in the graph of role types, even when each role contains many members. This is the strongest anchor because the continuous problem poses precisely the programme’s question of whether discrete hardness survives population continuization.

A useful secondary question is Bipartite Mass-Critical-Threshold: compute the same value and an optimal \(p\), restricted to bipartite \(H\). Its anchor is Proposition 1, also proved here: computing \(\alpha_G\) for bipartite graphs is polynomial-time solvable. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.08841) The paper’s ellipsoid argument carries over: for candidate payoffs \(p\), separation requires finding a maximum-weight independent set with weights \(\mu_t p_t\), which is polynomial-time solvable on bipartite graphs. I therefore expect this mirror to be tractable, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass encoding length \(L\). These two anchors give a clear boundary: the general problem remains hard, while the bipartite type graph admits efficient optimization.

The mirror covers the paper’s Section 4 computational results on graphic simple games. It does not claim a computational mirror for the \(n/4\) bound or the complete-game asymptotic bound.

The soft spot is that winning still depends on an atomic pair of members; only the sizes of the role cohorts become continuous. A referee could regard this as a normalized blow-up family rather than a new atomless coalition semantics. The case survives because every such blow-up is a valid instance of the paper’s graphic simple-game problem, and the identity above makes the mass formulation its exact per-capita high-multiplicity form. Further questions include whether fixed-threshold algorithms extend to arbitrary rational masses, and which graph parameters yield FPT or approximation algorithms beyond the bipartite case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only computational anchors are the two graphic-game results in Section 4. The strongest objection to both is the meaning of a continuous coalition. In the paper, an edge makes a pair of individual players a minimal winning coalition. In an atomless population, that pair has zero mass. If winning instead requires positive mass from both endpoint types, a coalition can be winning with arbitrarily small mass. With additive payoff densities, its total payoff can then be made arbitrarily small, so no bounded payoff vector can enforce the paper’s unit threshold. Treating even a null set of linked individuals as enough to win makes winning depend on zero-mass details that the distribution \(\mu\) does not record.

The proposed model keeps the constraints \(p_u+p_v\geq 1\), which preserve those individual winning witnesses, while weighting losing coalitions by type mass. That is a coherent optimization problem. Its semantics are a hybrid: winning is witnessed by two individuals, while the objective is a population average. The exact blow-up identity confirms the hybrid’s finite meaning. It does not supply an atomless coalition interpretation. Requiring whole cohorts to act together avoids the tiny-coalition problem, but makes each cohort a bloc-player; imposing a minimum participating mass introduces a cutoff absent from the paper.

This challenges the general-graph hardness anchor directly. The reduction to arbitrary \(H\) remains in the graph of types: with uniform masses, \(\alpha_\infty(H,\mu)=\alpha(H)/|T|\). Replicating every type preserves the reduction and only scales the original threshold. That is valid evidence for a high-multiplicity version, so the scaling itself is not an objection. The objection is that the paper’s pairwise winning condition has not become a mass-based condition in the limit; the hard problem survives in the finite interaction structure among types.

The bipartite algorithm has the same limitation. Weighted independent-set separation gives a polynomial algorithm for the proposed mass functional, but it uses the same type-level edge constraints. Bipartiteness does not resolve the zero-mass-witness issue. A bloc interpretation or a minimum-mass rule would again define a different game.

This is the strongest case against the mirrors, and it does not finish the job. Under ChoCo’s stated high-multiplicity standard, the exact normalized blow-up may itself be enough: a large federation of interchangeable members in each role class is a sensible regime, and the weighted graph problem is well-posed. I can challenge its claim to be an atomless continuation of the paper’s game; I cannot honestly rule out its value as a high-multiplicity computational mirror. The negative case is therefore weak against the programme’s own criterion.
