The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the required kind. Theorem 1 proves existence of a stable outcome; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize negative assortativity; Theorems 4 and 5 give uniqueness properties; and Theorem 6 proves existence, uniqueness, and negative assortativity for the roommate model. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or states an algorithmic running time. I therefore have no qualifying anchor, and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover. Treating Theorem 2 as an “in P” result would overstate what it proves: a structural characterization is not a complexity bound for computing the matching and sharing rules.

The nearest unanchored route is a continuum version of the paper’s risk-sharing market. Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be distributions over finite sets of complete male and female utility types, with the common income-risk assumptions of the paper. A solution would be a mass coupling \(\pi\) between types and, for each matched type pair, an efficient sharing rule. Ask for a stable outcome: matched agents prefer their assigned rule to remaining single, and no male–female type pair can choose a sharing rule that both strictly prefer. A large risk-sharing community with many people but relatively few utility types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; Theorems 2 and 6 motivate negative assortativity as a property to investigate.

That is a plausible population continuization of the economic model, but it is not anchored in a named computational finding from this paper. The weak point is substantial: the paper’s results are analytic, and its general utility functions do not come with a finite computational encoding. The proposed continuum question would need additional representation choices before it became a computational-complexity problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor, and that is the strongest case against a mirror here. Theorem 1 proves existence; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize stable matches; Theorems 4 and 5 concern uniqueness; Theorem 6 treats the roommate model. None gives a computational complexity result or an algorithm with a running time. In particular, Theorem 2’s negative assortativity is a structural characterization, not a claim about how hard it is to compute a matching.

The best replacement is a mass version of the risk-sharing market: give each side rational masses of complete utility types, then ask for a stable mass coupling and an efficient sharing rule for each matched type pair. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. With finitely represented utilities and finite-state income risk, it could be made into a computational problem. But that would be a new algorithmic formulation: the paper allows general utility functions and income distributions without specifying finite input encodings or how to represent an output sharing rule. Supplying those choices might produce worthwhile research, but it cannot supply a computational result for this paper to mirror.

I would not argue that the population cannot sensibly be continuized. The paper’s assumptions allow repeated complete types, and cohort-style risk-sharing groups are plausible. The negative case is narrower: no named computational result survives as an anchor, and the strongest replacement asks a new computational question built on the paper’s economic model. If ChoCo treats that as sufficient for a mirror, the case against is weak; under the stated anchor standard, there is no worthwhile mirror of this paper.
