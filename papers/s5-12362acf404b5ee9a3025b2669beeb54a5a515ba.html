The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support distribution \(\nu\) over claim-award pairs \((v_i,x_i)\), with rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), positive claims, integral awards, and per-authority supply \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i=\kappa\), consider the strict-order regime \(v_1>\cdots>v_\tau\) and \(x_1>\cdots>x_\tau\). Classify whether every pair of positive-mass population groups \(A,B\), with every award in \(A\) greater than every award in \(B\), satisfies \(\frac{\int_A x\,d\nu}{\int_A v\,d\nu}>\frac{\int_B x\,d\nu}{\int_B v\,d\nu}\), satisfies the reverse inequality throughout, or satisfies neither uniformly.

The model it lives in

Each authority receives an integral award \(x\); its claim is \(v\), and the population is represented by mass \(\mu\) over claim-award pairs. The total supply scales with the population at per-authority average \(\kappa\). The task classifies the supplied apportionment; it has no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

A per-type average allotment can hide which authorities received which integral award, so a faithful input must retain the joint mass of claims and awards.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Statement 2’s strict-order test for total favoring in a supplied apportionment. It does not cover the simulated favoring frequencies or comparisons among Hamilton, Sainte-Laguë, d’Hondt, Huntington–Hill, and Adapted Sainte-Laguë.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the adjacent-ratio characterization extend to tied awards or claim-award supports whose claims and awards are not strictly ordered together?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Statement 2, proved in this paper. The authors call it a “Statement,” not a theorem, and do not state a standard complexity classification. Its computational content is clear, though: assuming \(V_1\ge\cdots\ge V_n\) and \(x_1>\cdots>x_n\), the exponentially many subset inequalities defining total favoring of large beneficiaries all hold if and only if \(x_1/V_1>\cdots>x_n/V_n\). For small beneficiaries, they all hold if and only if the ratios increase instead. Thus a check that could involve \(K_n\) conditions reduces to \(n-1\) comparisons. Statement 1 gives the recurrence for \(K_n\), which exceeds two million at \(n=20\); Statement 2 is what makes the paper’s simulations cheaper. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result.

A plausible mirror is a large federation of local authorities dividing a pool of indivisible seats, permits, or service units in proportion to their populations or needs. The beneficiaries are the authorities, not the residents. A type records an authority’s claim \(v_t\) and any other feature the apportionment problem uses; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of authorities of that type. The regime is one with many authorities, \(n\gg\tau\), and relatively few distinct types. The total supply grows with the number of authorities, with \(M/n\) approaching a fixed per-authority supply \(\kappa\). Each authority still receives an integral bundle; a type’s average allotment can be fractional when its mass represents authorities receiving different integral bundles.

Call the mirror Continuous Total-Favoring Recognition. Its instance is a finite-support society \((T,\mu)\), positive claims \(v_t\), and a proposed apportionment with per-authority type averages \(a_t\), satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=\kappa\). In the strict-order case corresponding to Statement 2, order the types so that \(a_{t_1}>\cdots>a_{t_\tau}\), with claims ordered consistently with proportional apportionment. Ask whether every pair of positive-mass groups \(A,B\), where every type in \(A\) receives more than every type in \(B\), satisfies
\[ \frac{\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t a_t}{\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t v_t} > \frac{\sum_{t\in B}\mu_t a_t}{\sum_{t\in B}\mu_t v_t}, \]
or whether every such comparison goes in the reverse direction; otherwise return neither. The output is a classification of the supplied apportionment, not an optimized allocation. In the strict-order case, the mass-weighted group comparisons reduce to checking the adjacent ratios \(a_{t_i}/v_{t_i}\). That gives a tractable mirror, with a sorting and comparison algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

This is a close match to the paper’s question of whether a particular apportionment totally favors large or small beneficiaries. It does not replace the population with a continuous outcome: individual awards remain integral, while the type-level record captures their aggregate. It also keeps the mirror to the result it can support. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s simulation estimates comparing Hamilton, Sainte-Laguë, d’Hondt, Huntington–Hill, and Adapted Sainte-Laguë.

The main weakness is that Statement 2 is a compact recognition criterion, not a complexity classification for computing apportionments or for estimating a method’s overall favoring. The paper’s method-level conclusions come from finite simulations, with \(n\le15\); this mirror alone does not establish their continuous limits. Extending the analysis to ask whether those methods induce well-defined limiting allocations and limiting favoring frequencies would be a further question. My case rests on Statement 2 as the paper’s named structural algorithmic result; if “anchor” is restricted literally to a Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition explicitly asserting a complexity class, then the paper has no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Statement 2 is the only credible anchor, but it is a weaker computational result than the proponent’s framing suggests. It gives an iff test that replaces many subset comparisons with adjacent ratio comparisons, and therefore implies a fast recognizer. The paper does not formulate a complexity problem or give a complexity classification, however; the reduction’s stated purpose is to make its simulations cheaper. If ChoCo requires a named result about the complexity of a computational problem, this paper has no qualifying anchor.

The proposed federation of similar local authorities is plausible, so lack of a high-multiplicity regime is not a strong objection. But type-average allocations can erase distinctions the paper’s criterion uses. If identical authorities receive different integer allotments, their mean allotment does not reveal which individuals have more than others. A faithful mass model could record the joint mass of claim types and integer allotments. In the strict-order regime covered by Statement 2, the resulting test still reduces to sorting and adjacent weighted-ratio comparisons, in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length.

That repair makes a sensible continuous question, but its computation is a compact restatement of the paper’s algebraic test. The population limit introduces no optimization, pricing problem, or new complexity issue. The paper’s more substantial empirical conclusions concern frequencies across sampled finite instances. A continuous limit for those frequencies would require choosing a distribution over claims and a scaling of supply—choices the paper does not make—and would be a further statistical study rather than a mirror of Statement 2.

So the negative case is strongest as an argument about ChoCo’s priorities: the paper offers little to add to a programme charting computational complexity. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A high-multiplicity apportionment regime is credible, and a broad reading of “computational result” could accept Statement 2’s reduction as an anchor.
