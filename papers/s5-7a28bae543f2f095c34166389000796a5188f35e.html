The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a directed type-approval relation, and a rational threshold \(\alpha\), decide whether an atomless society of those types admits two matchings whose per-capita PAV score is at least \(\alpha\). Each person may be paired at most once in each matching, and approval between types applies uniformly to their members.

The model it lives in

Use type-state masses \(z_{t,S}\), where \(S\subseteq\{1,2\}\) records the shifts in which that mass approves its partner, and pairing-flow variables \(y^j_{q,r}\) between type-state classes for each shift \(j\). Maximize \(\sum_{t,S}z_{t,S}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[S\ne\varnothing]+\tfrac12\mathbf{1}[S=\{1,2\}]\bigr)\), subject to mass conservation, pairing feasibility, and the type-approval relation.

What the mirror covers

Covers the general matching-election Thiele score-threshold problem for \(k=2\); leaves the sequential-rule algorithms, other winner-determination results, axiomatic guarantees, and axiom-checking results alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the stated type-state flow formulation be proved exactly equivalent to feasible pairs of atomless matchings for every directed type-approval relation?
  • How does the problem change when the number of matchings \(k\) is part of the input, and can the \(2^k\) approval-history dependence be improved?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s PAV hardness result. Its anchor is the restatable theorem labelled th:PAV-hard; the published proceedings version prints it as Theorem 3 (the supplied extract omits the numeral at that statement). It proves that, for any Thiele weight sequence with \(w_1>w_2>0\), deciding whether a matching election has a size-\(k\) committee with score at least \(\alpha\) is NP-complete even when \(k=2\) and each agent approves at most three others. The paper proves this by reduction from finding two edge-disjoint perfect matchings in a 3-regular graph, citing Holyer for the source hardness. ([Proceedings version](https://comsoc-community.org/assets/proceedings/comsoc-2021/BoehmerEtAl-2.pdf))

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large organization assigning the same workforce to two rounds of paired shifts. A type records an agent’s complete role and approval profile: every member of type \(t\) approves the same partner types, and those approval relations apply uniformly between members of the relevant types. There may be many workers but relatively few such types, so \(n\gg\tau\). In each shift an individual still has one actual partner or is unmatched; the continuous object is the population distribution and the resulting mass of pairings.

I would call the mirror Atomless Two-Matching Thiele Threshold. Its input is a finite set \(T\) of types, positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), the directed type-approval relation, fixed rational weights \(w_1=1>w_2>0\), and a rational threshold \(\alpha\). The society is atomless within each type. The question is whether it admits two matchings whose per-capita Thiele score is at least \(\alpha\). For an individual \(x\), let \(h(x)\in\{0,1,2\}\) count the shifts in which \(x\) is matched to an approved partner. The score is

\[ \int \left(w_1\mathbf{1}[h(x)\geq 1]+w_2\mathbf{1}[h(x)=2]\right)\,d\mu(x). \]

Each matching is a feasible pairing of individuals, with every pair having at least one approving endpoint. Pareto-optimal representatives can be used: a strict increase in the set of approving agents increases the score because both weights are positive.

There is a compact LP for this fixed-\(k\) mirror. Let \(z_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) whose members approve exactly the shifts in \(S\subseteq\{1,2\}\), with \(\sum_S z_{t,S}=\mu_t\). For each shift \(j\), let \(y^j_{q,r}\) be the mass paired between state classes \(q=(t,S)\) and \(r=(u,S')\). Permit that variable only when the type approvals make the bits in \(S,S'\) consistent with pairing \(t\) to \(u\), and at least one endpoint approves. If \(d_j(q)\) is the paired mass incident to \(q\), counting a within-class pair twice, require \(d_j(q)=z_q\) when \(j\in S\), and \(d_j(q)\leq z_q\) when \(j\notin S\). Maximize

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,2\}} z_{t,S} \left(w_1\mathbf{1}[S\ne\varnothing]+w_2\mathbf{1}[S=\{1,2\}]\right). \]

These constraints preserve each person’s binary approval history across the two matchings. Atomlessness lets each type-state class be split among the prescribed partner classes in each shift. The LP has polynomial size in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, so I expect this threshold problem to be in Class A for two matchings. The hard discrete theorem’s graph combinatorics may dissolve when cohorts can split across pairings.

The mirror covers only the paper’s general matching-election winner-determination result for \(k=2\). It does not claim continuations of the paper’s sequential-rule algorithms or its EJR, PJR, and core-stability results. A useful next question is whether the state formulation can be made fixed-parameter tractable, or polynomial, when \(k\) is part of the input; the direct version has \(2^k\) approval-history states. Another is how well rational mass flows round back to large finite high-multiplicity elections.

My main reservation is that the reduction’s sparse graph uses individually identified agents and edges. A positive-mass type that approves a partner type represents many compatible partners, so the theorem’s “at most three approved agents” restriction does not carry over verbatim as “at most three individual partners.” The type-level analogue would bound each type’s approved partner classes, which is a different restriction. Still, the objective and the underlying task—choosing multiple matchings to maximize the same per-agent Thiele score—are intact; the changed feasibility is the natural consequence of replacing the population with type masses. The paper’s cited lottery-over-matchings approach is adjacent, but it treats distributions over outcomes rather than a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is the paper’s PAV hardness result. The strongest objection is that its hard instances depend on a sparse graph of approvals between named agents. The reduction’s cubic graph and “at most three approvals” condition rely on exactly which individual is adjacent to which. In a type-level model, an approval from type \(t\) to type \(u\) applies across a whole block of agents. Each sparse edge becomes broad compatibility between populations. To preserve an arbitrary graph’s neighborhoods, one would need many distinct types; with few types, the reduction’s incidence structure disappears.

That makes the proposed mirror a poor continuation of the *hardness phenomenon*: it changes the domain to block-structured, divisible matching. For fixed \(k=2\), the proposed state-and-flow LP appears to capture that domain directly and plausibly gives a polynomial algorithm. This is nondegenerate and mathematically well posed, but may be a fairly immediate consequence of allowing atomless populations to split across partner classes. It also would not show that the paper’s graph-based hardness was caused by population multiplicity.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror, though. A role-based workforce with repeated shifts and compatibility determined by role is plausible, and the LP asks a recognizable version of the paper’s question: how to choose two matchings to maximize per-capita Thiele score. Existing lotteries over matchings do not answer it, since they randomize outcomes rather than make the population continuous. The LP’s answer being easy is not, under ChoCo’s stated scope, a reason to dismiss the question.

So the negative case is weak. The type-block model does not preserve the reduction’s sparse identity-level structure, but it remains a credible continuous-population problem in a plausible regime. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
