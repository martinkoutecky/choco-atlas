What the mirror covers

Proposition 5 is the only proposed anchor; the other named results characterize grading rules or rankings and establish structural properties.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is the paper’s threshold-selection mechanism. It already treats agents as voters choosing among sufficientarian grading rules, so a population of stakeholders with repeated preference types is a natural place to make the population continuous.

The closest named anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper using Lemmas 1 and 2 and results cited from Dilworth (1960), Savaglio and Vannucci (2019), and Vannucci (2019). It says that when the number of voters is odd, simple majority aggregation of their proposed sufficientarian grading rules is strategy-proof on the domain of preferences that are single-peaked over threshold antichains under the paper’s metric betweenness. The aggregation is implemented by transferring the proposals through the isomorphism between grading rules and antichains, applying a suitable aggregation rule there, and mapping the result back.

Mass Threshold Selection is a plausible continuous counterpart. Let \(X=\prod_{r=1}^{m}X_r\) be the paper’s finite capability space. An instance gives a distribution with rational masses \(\mu_j\) over \(\tau\) stakeholder types. Each type is a complete single-peaked preference over threshold antichains, with peak antichain \(A_j\); its mass is the share of stakeholders with that preference. Write \(U_j=\{x\in X: a\leq x\text{ for some }a\in A_j\}\) for the upward filter represented by that peak. For each capability type \(x\), let its support be \(\sum_{j:x\in U_j}\mu_j\). The selected threshold system is the set of minimal elements of
\[ U_\mu=\left\{x\in X:\sum_{j:x\in U_j}\mu_j>\frac12\right\}. \]
The solution is that antichain. With equal masses and an odd electorate, this recovers the pointwise simple-majority rule. A scenario would be a large public consultation on a multidimensional sufficiency benchmark, where many stakeholders share the same complete ranking of candidate thresholds and \(n\) is much larger than \(\tau\).

For an explicitly listed \(X\), computing the majority filter and its minimal elements is polynomial in the input size. If \(X\) is represented compactly as a product and \(m\) is the parameter of interest, that running-time claim does not follow; the succinct version needs its own analysis. Further questions include whether the rule retains strategy-proofness for unequal-mass blocs, how to handle exact ties, and what the complexity is under a compact product representation. The paper’s odd-electorate result does not answer those questions.

There is a strict limitation: Proposition 5 is not a computational-complexity result. Nor are the paper’s other named results: they characterize grading rules and preorders, establish a lattice structure, or prove mechanism properties. None asserts that an underlying problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. So Mass Threshold Selection is a credible continuation of the paper’s threshold-selection problem, but it is not anchored to a result that meets your stated computational-anchor rule.

That is the weak point in the positive case. The proposed regime is plausible and close to Section 4, but the paper’s main contributions are axiomatic and mechanism-design results, not computational questions. If the anchor gate is applied literally, this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Proposition 5, and the paper does not give the kind of result ChoCo is looking for: it proves a strategy-proofness property, not a complexity bound or an algorithmic classification. The other named results are characterizations of grading rules and rankings, or structural results about the lattice of thresholds. So the proposed Mass Threshold Selection is a reasonable continuous extension of the paper’s mechanism, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in it.

The most charitable version of that extension is clean: represent stakeholder preferences by a distribution over types, take the strict-majority support set, and return its minimal elements as the selected threshold system. This genuinely continuizes the population, and I would not object that repeated preference types are implausible. But the paper poses no computational task about selecting that antichain. Turning it into one requires choices the paper does not make, such as a compact encoding of \(X\), of preference types, and of their peaks. A question about computing the output under those encodings could be worthwhile in its own right; it would be a new computational problem, not a mirror of Proposition 5.

There is also a tension in preserving Proposition 5’s main content. If the continuous population is atomless, a single stakeholder has zero mass, so unilateral strategy-proofness becomes vacuous: an individual report cannot change the aggregate. If instead each positive-mass preference type acts as a strategic player, the model treats whole blocs as agents. One could study coalition manipulation or the minimum mass needed to change the selected antichain, but those are new questions with a different strategic unit. Neither rescues the proposed anchor as stated.

That leaves a real limitation in the negative case: the paper’s setting admits a sensible continuous population, and a mass-based threshold-selection rule is natural. I cannot honestly claim that no useful research could grow from it. The stronger claim I can support is narrower: none of the paper’s named results supplies a computational problem for ChoCo to continuize, and the best proposed anchor either changes the task into a new complexity question or loses the strategy-proofness content that made the result distinctive.
