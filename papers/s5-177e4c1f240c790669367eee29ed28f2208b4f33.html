The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is narrow: continue the agent population into a distribution over reward types, while keeping the bandit’s finite feedback sample explicit. The paper already makes the policy \(p\in\Delta^K\) continuous; that is not the continuization I’m claiming.

A type \(t\) is the complete vector of reward laws \((D_{t,1},\ldots,D_{t,K})\), not merely a vector of means. Let \(\mu_t\) be its population share, with support size \(\tau\). For a finite population of size \(n\), take \(n\mu_t\) agents of each type. At each round, the learner chooses a policy \(p\), an arm is drawn from \(p\), and agents receive independent rewards from their type’s law for that arm. The learner observes the rewards, which can be aggregated by type. If \(\theta_{t,j}\) is the mean reward for type \(t\) on arm \(j\), then \(u_t(p)=\sum_j p_j\theta_{t,j}\). The source-faithful welfare is \(W_{n,\mu}(p)=\prod_t u_t(p)^{n\mu_t}\), exactly the paper’s Nash social welfare when \(n\mu_t\) is integral.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says Explore-First has two alternative expected-regret bounds at horizon \(T\): with \(L=\Theta(N^{2/3}K^{-2/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(NKT))\), its regret is \(O(N^{2/3}K^{1/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(NKT))\); with \(L=\Theta(N^{1/3}K^{-1/3}T^{2/3}\log^{2/3}(NKT))\), it is \(O(N^{1/3}K^{2/3}T^{2/3}\log^{2/3}(NKT))\). The theorem is a learning-performance result, not a named complexity classification. The paper has no named theorem establishing that a problem is in P or NP-hard, so I would not present it as a direct complexity-theory anchor.

The corresponding prover question is Type-Mass Explore-First Regret. An instance gives \(K\) arms, horizon \(H\), a rational type distribution \(\mu\) with support \(\tau\), population size \(n\) with \(n\mu_t\in\mathbb{N}\), and unknown reward laws \(D_{t,j}\subseteq[0,1]\). In each round the learner chooses \(p^r\in\Delta^K\), receives the arm’s rewards from the finite population, and incurs regret \(W_{n,\mu}(p^*)-W_{n,\mu}(p^r)\), where \(p^*\) maximizes \(W_{n,\mu}\) for the true means. The task is to give a horizon-dependent online algorithm, with running time polynomial in the explicit parameters and input size, and prove its worst-case expected cumulative-regret guarantee. Explore-First’s concrete target is either of Theorem 1’s two bounds with \(N=n\), ideally improved using \(\tau\) and the type masses. A solution is the algorithm and its guarantee, not just the optimized policy for known means.

The regime is a large organization, platform, or conference where many people face the same alternatives and fall into a small number of reward-law profiles—for example, a large workforce with a modest number of stable employee archetypes. The high-multiplicity structure is \(n\gg\tau\). The mirror is plausible because agents within a type are indistinguishable for everything the model uses, and the type counts provide repeated observations. Explore each arm, estimate each type’s means from its observations, then choose a policy maximizing estimated type-mass Nash welfare. That optimization has a concave log objective, \(n\sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(p)\), as in the paper’s policy computation. The discrete high-multiplicity instances reproduce the paper’s objective and feedback exactly; extending the mass vector beyond those empirical proportions is the continuous question.

I expect the type-mass version to be tractable as an online-learning problem: the paper’s exploration-and-estimation method and concave policy optimization carry over, with room to exploit repeated types. In ChoCo’s stricter computational trichotomy, however, this is not yet a Class A result: Theorem 1 bounds regret, rather than classifying the complexity of a computational problem. It prompts useful follow-ups about regret rates in \((n,\tau,\mu,H,K)\), and whether a meaningful finite-population guarantee has a nontrivial limit as \(n\) grows.

My weakest point is exactly that finite-feedback qualification. With a literal infinite population, if the learner observes rewards from everyone, each arm pull reveals each positive-mass type’s mean exactly by averaging across its infinitely many members. The paper’s statistical learning problem then collapses after exploring the arms. Keeping \(n\) explicit preserves the paper’s experiment and makes \(\mu\) the population’s type-mass description, but it means the cleanest mirror is the high-multiplicity family and its finite-batch limit, not a claim that the literal continuum has the same regret behavior. Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor for that case; I would not use Proposition 2’s \(\Omega(\sqrt{KT})\) lower bound as a continuous hardness result, because its statistical uncertainty need not survive that limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a weak anchor for ChoCo’s purpose: it proves a regret bound for a learning algorithm, not the complexity of a computational social choice problem. The policy step it relies on—maximizing Nash social welfare over \(\Delta^K\)—is already described in the paper as polynomial-time solvable. A type-mass restatement therefore offers no new pricing problem or complexity boundary for the programme to chart.

The direct type-mass mapping also loses the paper’s statistical challenge in the literal continuum. If type \(t\) has share \(\mu_t\) in a finite population of size \(n\), then it contains \(n\mu_t\) agents, and one pull of arm \(j\) gives that many independent observations from \(D_{t,j}\). This is a valid high-multiplicity family, but its feedback still depends on integer counts. With an infinite population and the paper’s independent-by-agent rewards, one pull reveals each positive-mass type’s mean exactly by aggregation. Explore-First can then try each arm once and choose the optimal policy. The learning uncertainty that Theorem 1 bounds has disappeared.

The welfare scaling creates a further problem for carrying over the theorem. Writing \(u_t(p)=\sum_j p_j\theta_{t,j}\), the source objective for that finite population is
\[ W_n(p)=\prod_t u_t(p)^{n\mu_t} =\left(\prod_t u_t(p)^{\mu_t}\right)^n. \]
As \(n\) grows, this source-scaled welfare and its additive regret can collapse toward zero. Using the per-capita geometric mean \(\prod_t u_t(p)^{\mu_t}\) avoids that collapse and preserves the optimizer, but it changes the regret being bounded; Theorem 1’s rates do not transfer just by replacing \(N\) with type masses.

A stronger rescue would keep a continuous population distribution but let the learner observe only a finite sample of agents each round. That is a coherent bandit model, and it could be worth studying. It adds a sampling constraint, though, to prevent the paper’s all-agents feedback from revealing type means in the limit. The resulting statistical question may be valuable, but it is not the population-only mirror of Theorem 1.

So the best negative case is that the faithful finite-population version remains the paper’s discrete experiment with repeated types, while the literal continuous version removes its learning difficulty. I cannot make the universal claim airtight: a sample-limited population model is plausible. Its appeal depends on accepting a changed feedback model, and its natural contribution would be a bandit regret analysis rather than the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to build.
