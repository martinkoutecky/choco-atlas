The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many worker types \(t\), with population shares \(\mu_t\), response qualities \(q_t\in[1/2,1)\), costs \(c_t\ge 0\), prior \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and per-capita budget \(\beta\), choose recruited masses \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t c_tx_t\le\beta\). For finite juries with type counts asymptotic to \(Nx_t\), find an allocation maximizing the BV error exponent \(E(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}-N^{-1}\ln(1-\mathrm{JQ}(J_N,\mathrm{BV},\alpha))\).

The model it lives in

A large candidate pool is represented by worker types \(t=(q_t,c_t)\) with shares \(\mu_t\). Choose masses \(x_t\) under type-capacity constraints \(x_t\le\mu_t\) and budget \(\sum_t c_tx_t\le\beta\), maximizing \(E(x)=\sum_t x_t[-\ln(2\sqrt{q_t(1-q_t)})]\). This is a linear optimization problem over the type masses.

The objection that survived

The exponent does not capture subexponential differences between allocations with equal exponents, so it does not fully order juries by the paper’s exact JQ objective.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the jury-selection hardness of Theorem 4 and the fixed-jury evaluation question of Theorem 2 through their asymptotic error-exponent counterparts. It leaves the paper’s exact finite-jury JQ approximation guarantee, simulated-annealing heuristic, and extensions to multiclass tasks and confusion-matrix worker models outside its stated scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Derive finite-\(N\) bounds relating exponent-maximizing allocations to allocations maximizing exact JQ.
  • Characterize how to compare allocations with equal error exponents when their subexponential error terms differ.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is for an asymptotic jury-selection mirror: a crowdsourcing platform has a very large pool of workers, grouped into a modest number of exact quality-and-price types, and selects a growing cohort to answer the same binary task. This is a plausible regime for a tiered platform: many workers share the same calibrated reliability and reward rate, so the pool has \(N\) workers but only \(\tau\) distinct types, with \(\tau\ll N\). A type is \(t=(q_t,c_t)\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the candidate pool with that quality and cost. This preserves the paper’s independence assumption, prior, Bayesian Voting (BV), and notion of Jury Quality (JQ).

The lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved by the authors, with its detailed proof in their technical report [15]. It says that JSP is NP-hard: given candidate workers with known qualities \(q_i\), costs \(c_i\), and a budget \(B\), selecting a subset whose BV Jury Quality is maximal is NP-hard. The paper’s results establish BV as the optimal voting strategy, so this is the hardness of selecting a jury under that strategy.

Here is a continuous counterpart. The input is the type masses \(\mu_t\), qualities \(q_t\in[1/2,1)\), costs \(c_t\ge0\), a prior \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and a per-capita budget \(\beta\). Choose masses \(x_t\) of workers to recruit, with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t c_tx_t\le\beta\). In a finite approximation of population size \(N\), this means recruiting \(Nx_t\) workers of type \(t\) and spending at most \(N\beta\). The optimization target is the exponential rate at which their BV error falls:
\[ E(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N}\ln\!\left(1-\mathrm{JQ}(J_N,\mathrm{BV},\alpha)\right), \]
where \(J_N\) is the corresponding finite jury.

Mass-JSP asks for a feasible \(x\) maximizing \(E(x)\), and returns that allocation. For these binary symmetric worker types, the rate is
\[ E(x)=\sum_t x_t\bigl[-\ln(2\sqrt{q_t(1-q_t)})\bigr]. \]
Thus the continuous selection problem is a linear optimization over \(\tau\) type masses, with a capacity bound and a budget bound. I expect this mirror to be Class A. Its tractability comes from the large-cohort limit: Bayesian evidence accumulates additively by type, and finite jury subsets become fractional allocations. If perfect workers with \(q_t=1\) are allowed, recruiting positive mass of one gives zero error and infinite rate; that boundary case can be handled separately.

The first-order limit of JQ alone would be too coarse: it is \(1\) whenever any positive mass of informative workers is selected. The error exponent retains the distinction the finite objective makes between juries that are all highly accurate. For two allocations with different positive exponents, the one with the larger exponent eventually has higher JQ as the cohort grows. The exponent is therefore a natural asymptotic refinement of the paper’s objective, rather than an unrelated substitute.

A second anchor, also proved by the authors with details in [15], is Theorem 2: given a prior \(\alpha\) and a fixed jury \(J\), computing \(\mathrm{JQ}(J,\mathrm{BV},\alpha)\) is NP-hard. The authors give a reduction from Partition for \(\alpha=0.5\), then extend to general priors. Its continuous counterpart is Mass-BV Evaluation: given \(\mu\) and a recruited type-mass vector \(x\), compute the asymptotic BV error exponent \(E(x)\). The same displayed sum computes it directly from the support, so I expect this evaluation problem to be tractable as well. This is a separate mirror of the fixed-jury computation result; it does not rely on the selection optimization.

The authors have a good reason to recognize these as their problems: both retain their binary decision task, independent worker responses, worker quality and cost, prior, Bayesian decision rule, jury-quality objective, and budgeted selection. The population is what changes. Their Theorem 1 supplies useful support: it identifies BV as optimal for JQ, and this mirror keeps that decision rule.

The weak point is the regime. The mirror assumes the jury grows with the candidate pool and the budget scales per capita. That is natural for very large adjudication panels, but the paper’s experiments use much smaller pools; a referee focused on selecting a fixed handful of workers could reasonably say this asymptotic question does not answer their operational problem. The exponent also refines JQ rather than equaling its pointwise limit, which saturates. I would present the case as a credible mirror for growing crowds, not as a continuation of every finite-JSP result. It also points to useful follow-up questions: finite-\(N\) error bounds for the exponent approximation, exact treatment of equal exponents, and the corresponding rate and selection problems for multiclass tasks and confusion-matrix worker models.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the finite quantity these results study does not survive the population limit. With independent responses, any positive mass of workers with \(q_t>1/2\) drives the Bayes error to zero as the jury grows. The limiting Jury Quality is therefore \(1\) for any such selection; with no informative mass, it is just the better prior guess. That makes a direct continuous version of Theorem 2—evaluating exact JQ—degenerate. The NP-hard finite computation depends on distinguishing likelihoods across exponentially many vote vectors, including near-cancellations that vanish from the limit.

The proposed Mass-BV Evaluation avoids that degeneration by computing the error exponent \(E(x)\). That is a coherent asymptotic statistic, but it is not the continuous value of the paper’s JQ computation: it replaces a probability of correctness with its exponential decay rate. Keeping exact JQ instead requires a finite jury size and integer worker counts, or extra sampling assumptions such as Poissonization. Those could yield worthwhile variants, but they change the model rather than preserve Theorem 2’s computational question.

The same objection reaches Theorem 4: maximizing the limiting JQ over masses is trivial once any informative mass is feasible. Mass-JSP rescues the optimization by maximizing the exponent. Its allocation model is plausible—many workers can share a quality and reward tier, and the authors’ independence assumption makes repeated workers of a tier interchangeable. But the rescued problem is an asymptotic reliability and budget-planning question. It no longer asks for the finite jury with highest expected correctness; it asks which type masses give the fastest decay of error. That is a meaningful distinction, not merely a technical restatement.

Theorem 1 does not close that gap. It justifies using BV for a fixed jury under the paper’s assumptions; it does not make the error exponent identical to JQ or establish that the exponent is the authors’ preferred continuous objective. A real negative case can therefore say that neither anchor transfers *as stated*, and that the exponent formulation is a different, more specialized question.

But that case cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A platform with a very large worker pool, a modest number of quality-and-price tiers, and a growing jury is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. For that regime, maximizing the error exponent is a defensible way to retain distinctions after JQ saturates, and it is a computational question about a distribution over worker types. Theorem 4’s anchor survives in this stronger asymptotic form. The argument against it is that it changes the objective and narrows the regime, not that the mirror is unsound or unworthy.
