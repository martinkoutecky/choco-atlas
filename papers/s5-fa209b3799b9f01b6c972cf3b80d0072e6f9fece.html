Why no mirror

A mass-based committee feasibility problem is a sensible continuous version of the paper’s committee conditions. But the named results establish probability limits and an existential nonexistence witness; the polynomial-time constructions use latent scores rather than the stated ranking-profile input, so no anchor has the required computational character.

The case FOR (proponent)

I do not find a named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor. The closest results are Theorem 3.1, proved here, which says that for each fixed \(k,\alpha\), and all sufficiently large fixed \(n\), the probability under \(\mathrm{IC}(n,m)\) of an \(\alpha\)-winning \(k\)-committee tends to \(1\) as \(m\to\infty\) when \(\alpha<(k-1)/k\), and to \(0\) when \(\alpha>(k-1)/k\); and Theorem 4.1, also proved here, which gives the same probability limits for \(\alpha\)-dominating committees with threshold \((k-1)/(2k)\). Corollary 1.3, proved here from that analysis, says that for every \(k\) and \(\alpha>(k-1)/(2k)\), some preference profile has no \(\alpha\)-dominating \(k\)-committee. These are probabilistic and existential results, not statements that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The paper says its positive constructions are polynomial-time computable, but it does not formalize that as a numbered complexity result with a specified input model.

The closest continuous computational question is a direct mass version of the paper’s committee definitions. Let the types be complete rankings of \(m\) candidates, and let the input be a finite support of rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), together with \(k\) and \(\alpha\). Ask whether there is a size-\(k\) committee \(S\) such that, for every \(a\notin S\), either
\[ \sum_{t:\,\exists b\in S,\ b\succ_t a}\mu_t\ge\alpha \]
for the winning version, or there is a \(b\in S\) with
\[ \sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\mu_t\ge\alpha \]
for the dominating version. A solution is such a committee \(S\); the optimization form is to maximize its achievable \(\alpha\). This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime when a large electorate shares a relatively small set of complete ballot types, with population size much greater than support size \(\tau\). Normalizing any finite profile’s type counts gives an exact rational-mass instance, so Corollary 1.3 also supplies continuous instances with no qualifying committee.

That is a credible mirror of the underlying *committee condition*, but it does not carry the paper’s central threshold result into the continuum. Under the continuous uniform distribution over all rankings, every size-\(k\) committee wins against an outsider for mass \(k/(k+1)\), and each committee member beats that outsider for mass \(1/2\). Those thresholds match the paper’s fixed-\(m\), \(n\to\infty\) Proposition A.1. The main theorems instead fix \(n\) and send \(m\to\infty\), obtaining different thresholds. In that regime, voters typically have distinct rankings, so the support can be as large as the population; it offers little evidence for high multiplicity.

I would leave the continuous feasibility problem’s complexity unclassified on the evidence here. Its exact embedding of finite profiles means any hardness of the corresponding discrete feasibility problem would transfer, but this paper establishes no such hardness or tractability result. The strongest honest positive case is therefore a sensible mass-based reformulation of the committee definitions and an exact continuous counterpart to Corollary 1.3. Its weakest point is decisive for the requested computational case: none of the paper’s named results supplies a qualifying complexity anchor, and the main asymptotic regime is unlike a high-multiplicity population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that none of the three anchors is a computational-complexity result. Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.1 establish probability limits under \(\mathrm{IC}(n,m)\), with fixed \(n\) and \(m\to\infty\); Corollary 1.3 establishes that some finite profile lacks a qualifying committee. The paper mentions polynomial-time constructions on the positive sides, but the named results do not specify a computational input model or classify a search problem’s complexity. In the main regime, \(n\) is fixed and full rankings are typically all distinct, so the result itself offers little evidence about a high-multiplicity population.

The best mass-based question is still clear. For a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, ask for a size-\(k\) committee maximizing its winning or dominating threshold:
\[ \max_{|S|=k}\ \min_{a\notin S}\sum_{t:\exists b\in S,\ b\succ_t a}\mu_t, \qquad \max_{|S|=k}\ \min_{a\notin S}\max_{b\in S}\sum_{t:b\succ_t a}\mu_t. \]
That is a sensible computational problem, and rankings are complete types for these conditions: aggregating voters with the same ranking loses no relevant information. But it is a new arbitrary-input optimization problem, not the question answered by either probability limit. For the uniform distribution over all rankings, the thresholds reduce directly to \(k/(k+1)\) and \(1/2\), matching the paper’s fixed-\(m\), \(n\to\infty\) appendix; that does not recover its fixed-\(n\), \(m\to\infty\) phenomenon.

Corollary 1.3 is the closest bridge to a mass instance: normalizing its finite profile gives rational masses and the same nonexistence witness. That is an exact translation, but the corollary establishes only that a witness exists. Turning it into a problem of finding an optimal committee or deciding feasibility over arbitrary mass inputs adds a new computational question; the corollary supplies no complexity result for it. The same distinction applies to Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.1: their existence statements can be translated into mass language, but that translation does not make their probabilistic thresholds algorithmic results about continuous societies.

So the negative case is strongest as a judgment about this paper’s contribution: its central results concern randomness over finite electorates and a growing candidate set, not computational complexity over a continuous population. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A high-multiplicity ranking society is a plausible model, and the optimization questions above merit study on their own. The honest weakness is that the paper does not provide a named computational result to anchor that study.
