The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is that a high multiplicity regime is entirely natural here. A large school or dormitory intake could have two room types in proportions \(p\) and \(1-p\), with a fraction \(q\) of students preferring the first. The paper itself models exactly this kind of society as a continuum. The relevant population has only two preference types, so its support size is \(\tau=2\).

But that foothold also exposes the problem: the paper has already continuized the population. Its results compare preferences over compound lotteries and allocation procedures; they do not study the computational complexity of a discrete problem and its continuous mirror. A direct mirror would restate the paper’s existing continuum model.

I find no eligible computational anchor. Propositions 1–3 give conditions under which agents prefer one mechanism to another, or neither endpoint mechanism is optimal. Claim 1 establishes an identity for reduced probabilities. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. I can’t honestly turn one of these into the required anchor.

The nearest computational follow-up is the paper’s open question about optimal Priority Groups design: given rational \(p>q\), a specified recursive-preference functional \(V\), and a number \(K\) of groups, choose group shares \(r_i\) and trade probabilities \(s_i\) to maximize the agent’s value of the induced compound lottery, subject to
\[ \sum_i r_i=1,\qquad 1=s_1\ge \cdots \ge s_K\ge 0,\qquad \sum_i r_i s_i=\frac{q(1-p)}{(1-q)p}. \]
That is a plausible computational extension of their stated open question, but it is not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. So there is no defensible per-anchor problem or complexity classification to offer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest feature for ChoCo is also the strongest case against a *mirror*: it already takes the population to a continuum. Section II assumes a \([0,1]\) society and states the allocation problem in terms of the fractions \(p\) and \(q\). The two preference types are a sensible high multiplicity regime, but there is no missing move from named agents to masses for a mirror to make.

There is also no computational result among the paper’s named results. Claim 1 and Propositions 1–3 establish identities and conditions for preferring mechanisms; their conclusions concern recursive preferences and lottery distributions, not the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Recasting those results over a continuous society would restate the model they already use. This is a collision on the population-continuum question, even though the paper does not provide a continuous *complexity* treatment.

The strongest salvage is the open question about designing Priority Groups: for a given number \(K\) of groups, choose their shares \(r_i\) and trade probabilities \(s_i\) to maximize \(V\), subject to the supply constraint. That is recognizably the authors’ problem and could motivate a computational project. But it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper allows a broad preference functional \(V\), and even its rank-dependent treatment specifies a differentiable weighting function rather than a finite input encoding. To ask a complexity question, one must first choose how \(V\) is represented. With an explicit representation, the decision variables are the \(K\) mechanism tiers; if \(K\) varies, it is the mechanism architecture that grows. The society still enters through \(p\) and \(q\), however large the intake.

Changing the setting to schools, dorms, or hiring does not restore the missing bridge: the population is already continuous and the results already use aggregate proportions. Expanding the type space to include heterogeneous recursive preferences would change the model’s assumptions and raise new questions about whose mechanism preference to optimize; it would not mirror Propositions 1–3.

So I would not argue that the high multiplicity regime is implausible, or that a computational study of Priority Groups could not be worthwhile. The stronger negative case is narrower: the best candidate is a new optimization problem about designing a continuous mechanism, not a computational mirror of a discrete result in this paper. That case is strong under ChoCo’s stated mirror criterion, though weaker against the broader possibility of building a worthwhile adjacent project.
