The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has substantial mathematical and numerical computation, but it has no named result asserting a computational complexity classification such as polynomial time, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. So it supplies no anchor that meets the programme’s stated requirement.

The nearest conceptual anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. It characterizes when voters \(i<j\) are symmetric: either \(w_j\ge q\); or \(w_i+w_{S_p}<q\), where \(S_p\) is the largest-weight subset of the other voters; or some consecutive pair in the weight ranking of those subsets straddles the quota, with \(w_j+w_{S_k}\ge q\) and \(w_i+w_{S_{k-1}}<q\). Proposition 9, also proved here, says every weighted form with three or four voters and strictly distinct weights is inconsistent for every quota. Proposition 15 gives a probability limit: as total weight \(w\) grows and \(q/w\to r\in(0,1]\), the probability of inconsistent weighting is \(1\) for \(n=3,4\). These are structural and probability results, not complexity classifications. Proposition 15 also sends total weight to infinity while holding the number of voters fixed; it does not continuize the population.

The most plausible population-level follow-up would use a large federation of member institutions grouped into a small number \(\tau\) of statutory voting-weight classes. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), its fraction of members, and per-member weight \(a_t\); the quota is \(q\). For a rational exchange size \(\varepsilon>0\), ask whether there are distinct-weight types \(t,u\) such that exchanging an \(\varepsilon\)-mass cohort of type \(t\) for one of type \(u\) preserves the outcome for every feasible background coalition. A solution is such a pair \(t,u\), or a certificate that every unequal-weight pair can be distinguished by some background coalition. This “mass-exchange symmetry” question appears tractable: for each pair, feasible background weight ranges over an interval, so symmetry can be checked by interval comparisons in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

That is a concrete candidate question, but it is a weak positive case. It replaces the paper’s exchange of two individual voters with exchange of a positive-mass bloc. As \(\varepsilon\) shrinks toward an individual’s vanishing population share, the finite subset gaps that Proposition 1 relies on disappear. The scenario is plausible for a large institution with repeated voting classes, and the aggregate model is sensible; the connection to the paper’s individual-symmetry results is the soft spot. I would present it as a new follow-up prompted by Proposition 1, not as a continuous mirror anchored by a qualifying result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper studies finite players, while its continuous version loses the object its main results measure: whether two individual voters are interchangeable in every coalition. The paper has no named result about an algorithmic problem’s complexity. Propositions 1 and 9 characterize finite weighted games; Propositions 11–16 count finite weight representations and study limits of those counts. The use of Barvinok’s algorithm is computational machinery for those results, not a complexity classification in \(n\), support size, and bit length. That leaves no direct computational result to mirror.

Proposition 1 is the best structural anchor, but its criterion depends on gaps between attainable subset weights. In a society with type masses \(\mu_t\) and per-member weights \(a_t\), a coalition is a vector \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), and it wins when \(\sum_t a_tx_t\ge q\). A single individual has zero mass, so adding either of two individuals leaves that sum unchanged. Individual symmetry then holds automatically, whatever their weights; “inconsistent weighting” follows whenever distinct-weight types are present. The finite symmetry test has become vacuous.

A bloc version can avoid that degeneracy by comparing cohorts of mass \(\delta>0\). But \(\delta\) becomes part of the question. For distinct weights \(a_t>a_u\), the two cohorts can be distinguished whenever a background coalition has weight in the interval from \(q-\delta a_t\) to \(q-\delta a_u\). In the continuum, feasible background weights fill an interval, so at an interior quota sufficiently small cohorts are distinguishable. Cohort symmetry can persist at boundary quotas or for selected cohort sizes, but that is a new bloc-substitution property whose answer depends on a chosen scale. It does not preserve Proposition 1’s individual symmetry question.

Proposition 9 has the same obstacle. Its claim concerns games with exactly three or four voters. Recasting those voters as types with many members preserves the number of weight classes, but the continuum then has the vacuous individual symmetry above. Switching to positive-mass blocs changes the property and introduces the cohort-size choice. The paper’s applications mostly involve a small number of institutional bodies; one could construct a large association or federation with repeated voting classes, but the more plausible population regime does not restore the proposition’s finite subset structure.

Proposition 15 does not supply the missing population limit. It sends total weight \(w\) to infinity while holding the number of voters at \(n=3\) or \(n=4\). The continuum there is a limit over normalized weight representations. The paper’s probability space consists of integer weight assignments for a fixed number of voters; a population limit would instead need a model for distributions over voter types and a definition of the corresponding symmetry event. There is no unique population distribution inherited from the paper’s IAC assumption.

A mass-level study of bloc symmetry, or a new probability model over type distributions, could be worthwhile on its own. The honest negative case is that neither is a continuous mirror of the named results here: the individual property collapses in an atomless society, and rescuing it requires a scale-dependent replacement. I cannot establish that no such extension would interest the authors, especially for a large federation with repeated voting classes. The case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror is strong; the universal claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile follow-up is weaker.
