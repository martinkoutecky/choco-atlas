The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), a committee size \(K\), and \(\tau\) approval types \(A_t\subseteq C\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), find a committee \(S\subseteq C\) of size \(K\) such that, for every \(Q\subseteq C\), \(\sum_{t:\,|A_t\cap Q|>|A_t\cap S|}\mu_t < 3.651|Q|/K\). Can this be done in expected time polynomial in \(|C|\), \(\tau\), and the mass encoding length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A population distribution over complete approval types \(A_t\), with mass \(\mu_t\); the decision is an integral committee \(S\) of size \(K\), evaluated by the mass of types that strictly prefer each competing committee.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(3.651\)-approximation and algorithmic guarantee in Theorems 1.5 and 4.10. It does not address exact core existence or the paper's other stability notions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the algorithm be implemented in expected time polynomial in \(|C|\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), without expanding rational masses into individual voters?
  • Can the Lindahl computation, sampling, and recursive steps be carried out with bit complexity polynomial in the weighted input size?
  • Can the \(3.651\) approximation factor be improved for rational-mass inputs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s approximate-stability problem for a committee, with the electorate represented directly as approval mass. The paper’s main named computational result is algorithmic rather than a hardness result: Theorem 1.5, proved here, says that for every ABC election and committee size \(K\), a \(3.651\)-approximately stable committee exists and can be computed in randomized polynomial time. The paper’s Theorem 4.10 gives the detailed algorithmic statement: its algorithm has expected polynomial running time and returns such a committee for any given \(K\). There is no named hardness result in the paper to anchor instead.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous \(3.651\)-Approximate Stability for Approval Masses. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), an integer \(1\le K\le |C|\), and a list of \(\tau\) distinct approval types \(A_t\subseteq C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each type’s complete preference over committees is the paper’s approval utility, \(u_t(S)=|A_t\cap S|\). The task is to output a committee \(S\subseteq C\) of size \(K\) such that, for every competing committee \(Q\subseteq C\),

\[ \sum_{t:\,|A_t\cap Q|>|A_t\cap S|}\mu_t < \frac{3.651\,|Q|}{K}. \]

The left side is precisely the mass of agents who strictly prefer \(Q\). Equivalently, the problem is to construct a committee whose worst proportional blocking-mass ratio is at most the paper’s guarantee. Its input parameters are \(|C|\), \(\tau\), and the mass encoding length \(L\); the algorithmic question is whether the committee can be found in expected time polynomial in \((|C|,\tau,L)\).

The regime is a large membership organization or platform choosing a committee from a fixed slate. For example, millions of members may approve candidates according to a few hundred recurring stakeholder or professional-group profiles. A type is an approval set, and its mass is the fraction of the membership with that set. This grouping is exact for the problem: agents with the same approval set evaluate every possible committee identically. The relevant regime is therefore many members and relatively few distinct approval types—not an assumption that all large elections have few types.

The mirror is close to the paper’s own question: it keeps the same candidates, committee size, approval preferences, and definition of a blocking group, replacing voter counts by mass. Only the population becomes continuous. The committee remains an integral \(K\)-subset; the paper’s fractional Lindahl equilibrium and randomized rounding are tools used to find it, not a continuization of the outcome. A finite election with counts \(n_t\) gives masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Conversely, any rational mass profile can be expanded to a finite electorate by taking a common denominator. Thus the stability condition has an exact two-way dictionary for rational profiles.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable, through a weighted-type version of the paper’s method. Its algorithm already reasons about approval utilities, voter groups with particular satisfaction levels, and how many voters support a candidate. In the mirror, those quantities become mass sums over types. The Lindahl step can be expressed with one price vector per type: with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a fractional committee \(x\) of mass \(k\) has type budgets \(p_t\cdot y\le k\) and candidate supply constraints \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{ti}\le1\). The rest of the algorithm’s sampling and committee construction concerns candidates, not individual voters. This makes a running time polynomial in \((|C|,\tau,L)\) a credible target. Further questions include whether the \(3.651\) factor can be improved for mass inputs, whether a deterministic algorithm is possible, and how the continuous exact-core question relates to the paper’s open question about core nonemptiness.

The important qualification is that Theorem 1.5 itself gives polynomial time for the paper’s finite election input; it does not state a running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). Expanding rational masses into individual voters proves the guarantee’s existence, but a common denominator can be exponentially large in \(L\), so expansion alone does not give the desired continuous algorithm. A weighted implementation of the Lindahl and recursive steps, with its bit complexity proved, is needed. That is the mirror’s weakest point—but it is also a concrete computational question, and the paper’s algorithm gives a strong reason to expect a positive answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that its stability condition is exactly the finite condition with voter counts replaced by weighted counts. For rational masses with common denominator \(D\), clone each type into \(D\mu_t\) voters. The blocking comparisons and their thresholds are unchanged. So the paper’s theorem already gives the \(3.651\) guarantee for every rational mass profile; the guarantee itself reveals no new phenomenon.

That does not settle the stronger computational question the proponent states. The clone construction may take time polynomial in \(D\), which can be exponential in the masses’ bit length \(L\). A mirror requiring time polynomial in \((|C|,\tau,L)\) still needs a weighted algorithm. But this paper looks unusually close to admitting one: voters with the same approval set are interchangeable, the recursive steps use approval counts that can become mass sums, and the rounding distribution is over candidates. The main technical obligation is to establish a weighted, bit-efficient Lindahl computation and carry exact rational arithmetic through the recursion. That may be a narrow extension rather than a new complexity landscape, but it is still a well-posed continuous-population question.

There is no stronger objection here. The paper has named algorithmic results to mirror; approval types are complete for the preferences it studies; and a large organization with many members and few recurring approval profiles is a credible high-multiplicity regime. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor or its better weighted version. My negative case is weak: it questions how much new theory the mirror is likely to require, but it does not show that the question is unworthy of the programme’s time.
