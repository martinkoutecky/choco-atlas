The continuous mirror question

Given \(N\), a finite union \(F\) of rational closed intervals in \([0,1]\), and a support \(\{(x_j,\mu_j)\}_{j=1}^{\tau}\) with \(\mu_j=n_j/N\), \(\sum_j n_j=N\), find a deterministic facility rule \(M_N(\mu,F)\in F\), computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of intervals, and input bit length, that is strategyproof when one resident changes location and moves mass \(1/N\), and guarantees \(W_\mu(M_N(\mu,F))\ge \operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)/3\), where \(W_\mu(y)=\sum_j\mu_j(1-|x_j-y|)\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)=\max_{y\in F}W_\mu(y)\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity population of \(N\) residents, grouped by location type \(x_j\) with mass \(\mu_j=n_j/N\); choose \(y\in F\) to serve them. Per-capita welfare is \(W_\mu(y)=\sum_j\mu_j(1-|x_j-y|)\), and a resident’s report changes the distribution by \(1/N\). The weighted median projected to the nearest feasible point with a fixed tie rule is the candidate mechanism.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s single-facility utilitarian-welfare guarantee. It leaves the other single-facility objectives and the two-facility results aside; Theorem 4’s lower bound is not established for a large-population-only restriction.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the factor-3 lower bound persist when the domain is restricted to \(N\ge N_0\) with \(N/\tau\) large? Theorem 4’s two-agent witness does not settle that restricted regime.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity facility-location mechanism for one public facility along a corridor. A type is a resident’s location \(x\in[0,1]\); mass is the share of residents at that location. In a plausible regime, \(N\) residents may be spread across \(\tau\) residential complexes, with \(N\gg\tau\), and everyone in a complex has the same location type. The feasible facility set \(F\) is a finite union of closed intervals in \([0,1]\).

For this mirror, the society is \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}(n_j/N)\delta_{x_j}\), where the \(x_j\) and the endpoints of \(F\) are binary rationals, \(n_j\) are nonnegative integers, and \(\sum_j n_j=N\). The facility location \(y\in F\) is the decision. Per-capita utilitarian welfare is
\[ W_\mu(y)=\int (1-|x-y|)\,d\mu(x), \qquad \operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)=\max_{y\in F}W_\mu(y). \]
A mechanism is a deterministic rule \(M_N(\mu,F)\in F\), defined across all such societies. To retain the paper’s incentive question, a resident has mass \(1/N\): a report from \(x\) to \(r\) changes the distribution to \(\mu'=\mu-\frac1N\delta_x+\frac1N\delta_r\). Strategyproofness means \(|x-M_N(\mu,F)|\le |x-M_N(\mu',F)|\) for every resident and every report \(r\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: for a single facility with limited feasible locations, the \(\mathrm{MEDIAN}^*\) mechanism is strategyproof and 3-approximates the optimal utilitarian welfare. In this mirror, the result asks for an efficiently computable strategyproof mechanism \(M_N\) such that
\[ W_\mu(M_N(\mu,F))\ge \tfrac13\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F) \]
for every \(N,F,\mu\). A solution is the rule, its strategyproofness and approximation proofs, and a runtime bound in \(\tau\), the number of feasible intervals, and the input bit length. The weighted median of the reported locations, projected to the nearest feasible point with a fixed tie rule, supplies the corresponding construction. This is a tractable, Class A question: the weighted median and projection can be computed from the support and interval list. The theorem’s bound is stated for total welfare; dividing by \(N\) leaves the approximation ratio unchanged.

A second, closely matched anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here: for a single facility with limited locations, no deterministic strategyproof mechanism can achieve a better than 3-approximation to optimal utilitarian welfare. It anchors the companion problem of determining the best uniform factor \(\rho\) over this mechanism class:
\[ \inf_M\ \sup_{\mu,F} \frac{\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)}{W_\mu(M_N(\mu,F))}, \]
where \(M\) must be strategyproof for every \(N\) in the domain. Theorem 4 gives \(\rho\ge 3\), and Theorem 3 gives \(\rho\le 3\), so the factor is exactly 3 on that full domain. This is an incentive-approximation lower bound, not an NP-hardness result; the programme’s hardness trichotomy does not apply to it in the usual way.

These are unusually direct mirrors: the type, feasible set, facility decision, welfare objective, reports and manipulation test all retain the paper’s meaning. Representing reports by a weighted distribution is the high-multiplicity description of the same population, and \(\mathrm{MEDIAN}^*\) is itself a weighted-profile rule. The mirror covers only the paper’s single-facility utilitarian-welfare results; it makes no claim about the other welfare measures, satisfaction objectives or two-facility results.

The main follow-up is whether the factor-3 lower bound persists when the domain is restricted to genuinely large populations with \(N\ge N_0\). Theorem 4’s proof uses a two-agent instance, so it establishes the bound for the all-\(N\) family, not for a high-multiplicity-only slice. One could also ask whether randomized mechanisms improve the factor in that slice.

That is the weakest point, along with a modelling choice needed to preserve strategyproofness. In a strict nonatomic model, an individual has zero mass, so a unilateral report does not change \(\mu\) and strategyproofness becomes vacuous. I therefore retain \(N\) and the \(1/N\) report change while giving the mechanism only the aggregated distribution. This is a defensible high-multiplicity mirror, but it is not the strict limit in which every individual is infinitesimal. The positive case rests most securely on Theorem 3: it gives a meaningful and efficient mechanism for large populations represented by type masses, even though Theorem 4’s lower-bound example does not by itself show that the sharp factor is forced in that regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that strategyproofness and a genuinely nonatomic population do not coexist in the way this paper needs. If a resident has measure zero, changing their reported location leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Every mechanism that depends on \(\mu\) is then strategyproof: the incentive condition has become vacuous. One can choose a facility that maximizes \(W_\mu(y)\) over \(F\), attaining optimal welfare directly. With finite support and finitely many feasible intervals, that objective is piecewise linear and can be optimized from the support and interval endpoints. In this strict continuum, Theorem 3’s factor of 3 no longer captures a trade-off between incentives and welfare.

The proponent’s repair—keep \(N\) finite and let one resident’s report move mass \(1/N\)—does preserve that trade-off. But with rational masses, this is exactly a finite profile summarized by its location counts, with agent names discarded. That is a useful compressed representation, especially when many residents share locations, but for Theorem 3 it does not create a new continuum problem: the weighted median and projection give the same mechanism on the same finite profiles. The proposed polynomial-time question is therefore a straightforward implementation of the paper’s result, rather than a new computational landscape opened by continuization.

Theorem 4 has a sharper mismatch with the claimed high-multiplicity regime. Its proposed all-\(N\) factor question includes the two-agent instance that proves the lower bound. The exact factor of 3 on that domain says nothing about whether the bound persists when \(N\) is large compared with the number \(\tau\) of distinct locations. A better mirror would restrict to such large populations. But that requires a separate finite-\(N\) incentive model: if residents retain influence \(1/N\), the question remains about discrete individuals; if \(N\) is taken to infinity, individual incentives vanish. Studying how the best factor changes with \(N\) could be worthwhile, but it is a new asymptotic mechanism-design question, not the continuous version of Theorem 4 as stated.

So the negative case is that these two anchors offer no evident nontrivial middle ground: preserve individual incentives and the “continuous” input is a compressed discrete profile; take a true nonatomic limit and strategyproofness imposes no constraint. The large-population finite-\(N\) variant is the point where this case is weakest. A large community concentrated at a few locations is plausible, and ChoCo explicitly treats high multiplicity as relevant evidence. That leaves a defensible research question, even though the paper’s two anchors do not, by themselves, establish a strong case for a continuous mirror.
