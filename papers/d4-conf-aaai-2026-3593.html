The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its numbered items are examples; its account of responsibility defines concepts and illustrates them on finite games. The computational questions appear in “What’s Next?” as future work—checking responsibility properties, computing strategies, and minimizing gaps or diffusion. Those are worthwhile questions, but the paper gives no algorithmic problem with a defined input and output whose continuous version could be compared. Under ChoCo’s mirror standard, that is decisive.

The best rescue would be a population of repeated decision settings—say, many factories with finitely many capacities and action options. Represent each factory type by its mass, let total emissions determine whether the fish die, and ask how to compute or minimize the mass of outcomes with responsibility gaps. That fits the paper’s interest in collective accountability, and the authors might recognize it as a useful extension.

But it changes what responsibility means. In the paper, responsibility is attributed to an agent because of that agent’s available choices, knowledge, or control at a point in the game. In an atomless population, one person’s unilateral action has measure zero and cannot change an outcome determined by aggregate action shares. Counterfactual responsibility can therefore disappear, as can responsibility for seeing to it. To recover a nontrivial attribution, the model must treat a positive-mass cohort as a responsible party. The paper mentions collective and institutional responsibility, so that is a plausible research direction; it is still a change from attributing responsibility to individual agents.

The alternative is to preserve individual responsibility by modeling a continuum of independent copies of finite games, such as many separate car interactions. Then each copy retains the paper’s original notion of responsibility, but the continuum merely weights the results across copies. Without a shared aggregate mechanism linking their actions, the population representation adds no new computational question. Adding such a link creates a different game model.

So the negative case is strong for a *mirror of this paper*, though not for continuous accountability research in general. Its weak point is the natural mass or group responsibility extension: it is close to the paper’s stated interests, and its authors might well pursue it. The case against is that it would be a new computational programme built from the paper’s future-work agenda, rather than a continuous version of a result the paper establishes.
