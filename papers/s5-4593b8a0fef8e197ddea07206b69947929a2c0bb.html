The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational result. Theorems 1, 3, 5, and 7 are proved here, but they characterize Nash or defection-proof equilibria; none asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithmic result.

The strongest near-miss is Theorem 3. It says that under plurality, a strategy profile is a defection-proof Nash equilibrium exactly when party sizes differ by at most one and either there are two parties, with the minimum adjacent distance greater than \(1/3\), or there are three parties, equally spaced around the circle. That sharp geometric characterization suggests a possible large-population limit for politicians, but turning it into a mass model would be a new analytical game, not a computational mirror anchored by this paper.

The scope problem is also real: the paper already models voters as a uniform continuum, while the strategic decision-makers whose party formation it studies are finite in number. A continuum of politicians is conceivable, but the paper’s results do not establish that this is a sensible high-multiplicity regime or pose a computational question about it. The honest positive-side conclusion is therefore limited: there is a suggestive equilibrium result, but no named computational anchor from which to formulate the requested prover-ready mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1, 3, 5, and 7 characterize equilibria; they do not classify the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The proponent’s anchor, Theorem 3, is the clearest candidate, but it is a geometric equilibrium characterization: in the natural large-population limit, its party-size condition becomes equal party masses, alongside the stated two- or three-party agenda geometry. That gives a sensible continuous *analytic* question, but not a computational one.

The best mass-based version would model many identical politicians, define each party by its agenda and mass, and measure utility per unit mass. It still has a problem at the individual level: a zero-mass deviation cannot change the distribution, while allowing a zero-mass politician to create a party leaves the mass model unable to record that party or define its per-capita payoff. One can repair this by defining deviations that move positive mass. That is a coherent new game, but its coalition and credibility rules need to be specified; they are not supplied by the finite-player theorem. For the symmetric cases in Theorem 3, the resulting question appears to reduce to the same simple geometry and equal-mass conditions, not to a complexity landscape.

There is an additional limit for the proportional-rule results: Theorem 5 bounds each party at two politicians, so as the population grows every party’s mass vanishes. That makes a mass distribution over parties a poor representation of the party structure the theorem characterizes.

So the negative case is strong as an argument that this paper does not offer a computational mirror for ChoCo. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population model can be built: Theorem 3 has a plausible equal-mass limit. The case against is that this limit is a new analytic game, and no computational question follows from the paper without adding substantial structure.
