The continuous mirror question

Given a finite valuation-type set \(\mathcal T\subseteq[0,1]^m\) and rational per-capita item supplies \(q_j\), consider finite markets of size \(N\) whose type counts approximate \(N\mu\) and whose supplies are \(Nq_j\). Determine whether an anonymous Groves rebate functional \(R\), defined as the limit of finite-market rebates on the background type distribution, can attain a specified worst-case limiting ratio of welfare retained after payments to efficient welfare over all \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal T)\); if so, compute an optimal \(R\) and its tagged-agent allocation and payment limits, subject to dominant-strategy truthfulness, individual rationality, and weak budget balance.

The model it lives in

A unit-demand market with finitely many valuation types carrying masses \(\mu\), integral bundles for individuals, and supplies scaled by population size. The mechanism chooses a per-agent rebate functional \(R(\mu)\) and the limiting tagged-agent VCG allocation and payment; the objective is to maximize the worst-case welfare ratio subject to truthfulness, individual rationality, and weak budget balance.

The objection that survived

A tagged agent has zero mass in the limit, so the allocation, VCG externality, and limiting DSIC, IR, and WBB constraints need a tie-aware definition that neither theorem supplies.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the LP-based optimization of rebates for welfare-maximizing, unit-demand heterogeneous-item allocation under individual rationality and weak budget balance. It leaves the paper’s geometric framework and applications to other objectives or domains without a continuous guarantee.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a tie-aware tagged-agent limit of finite Groves mechanisms define allocation and VCG payments consistently for every \(\mu\), while preserving truthfulness, individual rationality, and weak budget balance?
  • When consistent polyhedral subdivisions are given compactly, what are the LP size and algorithmic complexity as functions of \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length, and the number of cells?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time, or parameterized-hard. Its strongest computational anchors are Theorems 1 and 2, both proved here: they establish LP-based ways to optimize Groves rebates. My lead is Theorem 1, because it gives an exact optimum under a structural condition.

A plausible mirror is a large market for heterogeneous items. Let the possible valuation types be \(\mathcal T=\{v^1,\ldots,v^\tau\}\subseteq[0,1]^m\), with a society described by masses \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal T)\). Each agent wants at most one item, and item class \(j\) has supply \(q_j\) per capita. The supply scales with population size so the market remains nondegenerate as the population grows. Allocation is still integral for each agent; an allocation variable \(x_{tj}\) records what fraction of type-\(t\) mass receives item \(j\). Efficient allocation maximizes \(\sum_{t,j}\mu_t v^t_jx_{tj}\), subject to unit-demand and supply constraints.

The mechanism-design question is to choose an anonymous Groves rebate rule based on the distribution of other agents’ reports. In the limit, that rule is a functional \(R\) of the type-mass distribution. Define it as the limit of the finite-market rebate over VCG payments; require individual rationality and weak budget balance at every society \(\mu\). The objective is to maximize the worst-case fraction of efficient welfare retained by participants after payments. A solution must give \(R\), the induced efficient allocation and payments, and establish that they are limits of feasible finite-population Groves mechanisms. This keeps the paper’s central choice—optimizing payments within the efficient, dominant-strategy Groves class—while making the population itself continuous.

Theorem 1 says that, for a finite-agent mechanism-design problem with consistent subdivisions \(P_V,P_W\), the restricted LP on the value-space extreme points yields an optimal rebate at those points; affine interpolation over each cell of \(P_W\) then gives an optimum for the full problem. It is proved here as a multi-parameter generalization of earlier single-parameter work. Its mirror is the exact high-multiplicity rebate problem above: given \(\mathcal T\) and \(q\), decide whether a guarantee \(\rho_0\) is achievable, and, if so, return an optimal mass-based rebate functional. The conjectured route is a finite LP over extreme points when the society simplex has suitable consistent polyhedral subdivisions. I’d expect Class A when those subdivisions are supplied and compact; finding them, or handling growing \(\tau\), may be much harder. The follow-on question is whether the partition geometry has a succinct description or efficient separation oracle.

Theorem 2 says that, for any rebate-space subdivision \(P_W\) and initial value-space subdivision \(P_V^I\), the paper’s LinearRebates LP finds a feasible rebate function affine on \(P_W\). It is proved here; its objective is optimal within that affine subclass, providing a lower bound on the unrestricted optimum. The mirror question is: given \(\mathcal T,q\), a finite polyhedral partition \(P\) of \(\Delta(\mathcal T)\), and threshold \(\rho_0\), compute the best feasible Groves rebate functional affine on each cell of \(P\), or decide whether it achieves \(\rho_0\). This is again Class A for an explicit manageable partition. The broader search over partitions is open; the useful further question is how approximation quality and LP size depend on the number of cells and on \(\tau\).

The positive case is that the paper’s allocation and payment constraints are already piecewise linear, while a high-multiplicity society gives the rebate domain a natural aggregate representation: a distribution over complete valuation types. Theorem 1’s extreme-point argument and Theorem 2’s feasible-LP construction therefore suggest concrete continuous-optimization questions, rather than a vague analogy to “large markets.”

The soft spot is substantial: the paper has \(n\) individually pivotal agents and a fixed supply of \(m\) items. My mirror scales supply with population and takes a leave-one-agent limit of the Groves payments. Those choices preserve a sensible heterogeneous-item market, but neither theorem proves that truthfulness and rebate feasibility survive this limit, or that the authors would regard the scaled-supply model as their problem. The mirror is credible as a large-market extension of their payment-optimization question; it is not already established by their results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that their finite-agent structure is doing essential work that a distribution over types does not capture by itself. Theorem 1 interpolates rebates across cells of the profile space, and Theorem 2 uses those cells to produce feasible rebates. Both concern a rebate \(h(v_{-i})\) and constraints on each individual’s allocation and payment. They are genuine computational results, so “the paper has nothing computational to mirror” would be the wrong objection. But neither theorem shows that its construction survives when the population becomes a distribution.

In the proposed mirror, changing one agent’s report changes \(\mu\) by zero. A rebate \(R(\mu)\) therefore cannot encode that agent’s unilateral report, and the aggregate allocation variables \(x_{tj}\) say what fraction of type \(t\) receives item \(j\), not what a particular agent gets or pays. Without more structure, individual incentive compatibility and individual rationality are not defined by the proposed aggregate object. This is a substantive gap in both mirrors: Theorem 1’s exact interpolation argument and Theorem 2’s feasibility guarantee apply to finite profile constraints, not automatically to a mass-based mechanism.

A stronger mirror can repair the gap by retaining a tagged infinitesimal agent against a background distribution \(\mu\), and defining the agent’s allocation and VCG externality through a first-order perturbation of the efficient allocation problem. But that requires specifying how to select allocations and payments at ties, and which limiting finite-market mechanisms the derivative is meant to represent. Neither theorem supplies that limit or proves that its feasible rebates converge. This is the best objection to the anchors as stated: the LPs are not yet algorithms for the continuous problem the proponent proposes.

It does not, however, defeat the best version of the mirror. With finitely many valuation types and per-capita supply, a tagged-agent model using marginal welfare or shadow prices looks coherent. The resulting constraints may still admit a polyhedral partition and LP treatment. I cannot honestly claim that no such scenario is worthwhile, or that authors working on Groves payment optimization would reject it. The negative case is therefore weak: it exposes missing model and limit arguments in the proposed mirrors, but it does not rule out a meaningful continuous-population problem behind either theorem.
