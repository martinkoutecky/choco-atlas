Why no mirror

Proposition 4 is a named, explicit equilibrium calculation, so the paper has a computational anchor. But the proposed continuum makes each caucus a single strategic actor; with independently acting members, each member’s influence on recognition vanishes in the limit, and Proposition 4’s incentives do not transfer. The weighted-caucus model may be a plausible related bargaining game, but its masses are voting weights on strategic blocs rather than a high-multiplicity population.

What the mirror covers

The proposed model covers Proposition 4’s temporary-rights equilibrium formulas; it leaves Proposition 3’s permanent-rights analysis and the experimental findings outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large legislature or standards body organized into a few cohesive caucuses. Each caucus contains many members with the same preferences, costs, and discount factor. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its share of the population and its voting weight. A caucus chooses a common campaign effort \(e_t\); its aggregate effort determines its chance of winning proposal rights. After a rejection, temporary rights expire and the next proposer is sampled in proportion to caucus mass. The bargaining rounds remain discrete, while the population and voting weights are continuous.

One precise question is Temporary-Rights Continuum Bargaining Equilibrium. An instance gives binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a fund \(F\), discount factor \(\delta\), a majority or unanimity threshold, and each caucus’s preferences and effort cost. Caucus \(t\) chooses aggregate effort \(e_t\), so its first-round recognition probability is \(e_t/\sum_s e_s\). A proposal allocates accounts \(a_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_t a_t=F\), divided equally among the members of each caucus. Members of a caucus vote together; a proposal passes when the mass voting yes meets the threshold. After rejection, bargaining continues with probability \(\delta\), and the next proposer is drawn with probability \(\mu_t\). The problem is to compute a stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium: the effort profile and the proposal and voting strategies, or a certificate that no pure-strategy effort equilibrium exists. Its output includes exact continuation values and the resulting allocation rules.

The lead anchor is Proposition 4, proved in this paper. For the three-player game with temporary proposal rights, it says that under majority rule the optimal effort is \(e^*=2F(1-\delta/2)/9\), and the proposer offers one other player \(\delta F/3\) and keeps \(F(1-\delta/3)\). Under unanimity, the optimal effort is \(e^*=2F(1-\delta)/9\), and the proposer offers each other player \(\delta F/3\) and keeps \(F(1-2\delta/3)\). With three equal-mass caucuses, the proposed model recovers this game: after a rejection, each caucus has recognition probability \(1/3\), and a majority requires two of the three caucuses. Proposition 4 gives an exact equilibrium calculation for that slice. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, FPT, or other formal complexity classification; Proposition 4 is a closed-form computational foothold, not a complexity theorem.

I would expect the general typed problem to be Class A, plausibly FPT in \(\tau\). For a fixed number of types, the bargaining continuation conditions have finitely many type-level coalition patterns, while masses allow fractional voting weights and shares. Enumerating those patterns and solving the resulting continuous subproblems looks like a credible route to an algorithm. That is a conjecture about the proposed extension; Proposition 4 alone establishes no runtime bound. It raises further questions about exact versus approximate equilibrium computation, uniqueness under unequal masses, and how permanent proposal rights change the problem. A discrete-to-continuous convergence result for electorates with rational type proportions would also be a valuable bridge.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem in a large committee or standard-setting organization: their paper discusses those settings, and says the game generalizes to more players and contest success functions. The mirror covers Proposition 4’s temporary-rights equilibrium predictions only. It does not claim to mirror the experimental findings about effort, fairness, efficiency, or replication.

The weakest point is the coordinated-caucus assumption. If each member instead chooses effort independently in a nonatomic population, an individual’s influence on aggregate recognition vanishes, and the contest can collapse. The mirror therefore needs caucuses that act as strategic units, with members sharing their type’s preferences and costs. That is plausible for organized constituencies, though it is a real modeling addition to the paper’s three-individual experiment.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The real obstacle is that Proposition 4 is about a *person* winning proposal rights. Each of the three players chooses costly effort, and the lottery gives that person a chance to make a proposal and keep the residual. Its formulas, including \(e^*=2F(1-\delta/2)/9\) under majority rule, come from those individual incentives.

In an atomless population, a person’s effort change has zero effect on the aggregate contest. With a fixed fund, that person’s chance of winning also shrinks as the population grows, so the private return that drives Proposition 4 disappears. Scaling the fund with population can keep the finite-population incentive meaningful, but then the limit must preserve the effect of one infinitesimal person on a prize of population scale. That is a singular, two-scale model with a distinguished winner, not the ordinary distribution over types that the continuization programme studies. The bargaining vote can sensibly become a mass threshold; the individual prize of being proposer does not pass into that limit as directly.

The caucus version repairs this only by making each type a joint strategic actor. But identical preferences and costs do not make members choose effort together or pool the benefit of proposal rights. If the caucus shares its proceeds evenly, no individual gets the proposer’s premium; if its chair gets that premium, the chair is again a distinguished individual. A binding caucus could be a reasonable institution, but it adds collective action and pooled payoffs to the game. That may be a worthwhile weighted-bloc bargaining model; it is not a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s individual contest.

So the three equal-mass caucus case does recover Proposition 4, but only because it retains three strategic actors and assigns them voting weights. The masses describe the blocs’ weights, not a large population of agents whose multiplicity the model handles. The proposed FPT-in-\(\tau\) route likewise treats each type as a strategic bloc; its finite coalition patterns do not establish an algorithm for the atomless individual game. And Proposition 4 itself is a closed-form equilibrium characterization, not a complexity result. The paper does use numerical search for a finite effort grid elsewhere, but extending that computation to weighted blocs would be a new equilibrium-computation problem, not a continuous mirror of the result the authors proved.

This is the strongest negative case, but it is not airtight: a legislature with genuinely binding caucuses could make the weighted-bloc model recognizable to these authors. The case against is that the mirror’s tractability and its continuous masses come from replacing individual proposers with collective actors. If that institutional change is accepted, the mirror may survive; without it, the central contest incentive has no ordinary continuous-population counterpart.
