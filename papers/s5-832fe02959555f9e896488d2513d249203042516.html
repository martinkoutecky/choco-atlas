The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 characterizes a family of allocation rules by axioms; Proposition 5.1 identifies an average of those rules with the equal-distance allocation. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The sequential procedure is a definition of the rules, not a complexity analysis.

The best population scenario is a large workforce receiving whole-hour shifts, with types given by workers’ single-peaked preferences and the total number of hours scaling with workforce size. A possible mirror would ask how to compute the aggregate allocation by award level for each type. That is a sensible population model, but the paper does not pose this computational question or provide a complexity result whose population version could be studied. Asking instead for a characterization of aggregate rules would turn Theorem 4.1 into an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

There is also a modeling obstacle to making that question a faithful mirror. The theorem ranges over arbitrary monotonic standards that order pairs of a named agent and an integer. A distribution over preference types alone does not capture those priorities. Adding priority to the type can preserve the information, but may make the support grow with the workforce. Restricting to a symmetric or parametrically encoded priority rule could restore a compact population model; it would also add assumptions and a computational problem absent from the paper.

Proposition 5.1 offers a better-looking route: ask for the aggregate expected allocation under a distribution of standards. But the paper’s expectation is over the distinct standards for a finite named-agent problem, and its result connects the average to the equal-distance allocation. Extending that expectation to a continuum of workers requires specifying a new distribution over priorities. Evaluating the resulting allocation would still be a new algorithmic question, not a continuous complexity result from the paper. The paper’s discussion of a divisible task concerns continuity of the outcome with a finite agent set; it does not supply the continuous-population computation ChoCo studies.
