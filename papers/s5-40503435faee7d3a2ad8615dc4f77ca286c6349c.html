The proposed positive-mass deviation restores consent by replacing individual moves with coordinated bloc moves; meanwhile, pairwise stranger resolutions mean role masses alone do not determine realized utilities. What the mirror covers

The only proposed anchor is Theorem 6, on N-IS existence for asymmetric FOHGS and EOHGS; the verification results, other stability notions, and nonexistence examples are not mirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the existence problem in Theorem 6 (identified as Theorem 6 in Appendix B; proved here via a reduction from Exact Cover by 3 Sets). The paper’s result is: for asymmetric FOHGS and asymmetric EOHGS, deciding whether there exists a necessarily individually stable partition is NP-complete. “Necessarily” means stable under every resolution of the strangers; individual stability means no agent can move to another coalition, or become a singleton, in a way that strictly benefits them while every member of the destination coalition weakly agrees.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large organization or project pool with many people in a small number \(\tau\) of recurring roles. People in the same role have the same preferences and relation labels toward each role, but those labels can be asymmetric: role \(t\) may view role \(s\) as a friend while \(s\) views \(t\) as an enemy or stranger. The society is the rational mass vector \(\mu\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here the number of people can be much larger than \(\tau\); complexity should be measured in \(\tau\), the input’s bit length \(L\), and any other stated parameters. The stranger resolution can still vary across individual pairs.

My proposed problem is Necessary Mass-IS Existence for FOHGS/EOHGS. An instance gives a finite role set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), a directed friend/enemy/stranger label for each ordered role pair, and whether preferences are friend-oriented or enemy-oriented. A candidate solution is a measurable partition of the population into coalitions. Its decision variables are the masses \(x_t^A\) of each type \(t\) in each coalition \(A\), with \(\sum_A x_t^A=\mu_t\). For a resolution \(r\), a type-\(t\) agent in \(A\) evaluates the mass of friends and enemies in \(A\). To preserve the paper’s ordinal preferences under normalization, FOHGS compares \((\text{friend mass},-\text{enemy mass})\) lexicographically; EOHGS compares \((-\text{enemy mass},\text{friend mass})\). These orders match the discrete utility priorities: one additional friend outweighs all possible enemy differences in FOHG, and one fewer enemy outweighs all possible friend differences in EOHG.

The question is whether there exists such a partition that is stable under every stranger resolution when any positive mass of one type may move to another coalition or to a singleton. A move is blocked if the moving mass strictly gains and all members of its destination coalition weakly consent. This is a feasibility problem: the solution is the partition, not a minimum-cost value. Treating a positive mass of interchangeable people as the deviating unit is the co-modification forced by continuizing the population; a single person has zero mass in the limit.

I think authors working on necessary stability in stranger-based coalition formation could recognize this as their problem’s population analogue: it retains their coalition decision, asymmetry, friend/enemy utilities, uncertainty, and stability test. I would tentatively expect Class C—continuum-specific hardness—because the hard relation structure can live across the \(\tau\) roles even when many people share each role. But Theorem 6 alone does not establish that: divisible masses may erase the integrality used by the EC3 reduction. Whether hardness survives, or a finite-dimensional optimization method gives Class A, is exactly what a prover should investigate.

The weakest point is the move rule. The theorem concerns single-agent deviations, while a zero-mass person cannot change a coalition’s composition in a continuum. Replacing that with positive-mass deviations is a natural continuum co-modification, but it can change the stability concept; an author or referee could reasonably insist on a different limiting treatment. A first follow-up question is whether stable partitions have a finite-support representation bounded in \(\tau\), which would make the existence problem computationally concrete. This mirror covers Theorem 6 only; the paper’s other stability notions and its nonexistence examples remain outside it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is a real computational result to mirror: N-IS existence is NP-complete for asymmetric FOHGS and EOHGS. I would not argue that the paper lacks a computational question, or that high multiplicity is inherently implausible. Large organizations with recurring roles are a credible setting. My objection is that the proposed mirror has trouble preserving what makes this particular result about individual stability.

In the atomless limit, a single person has zero mass. If that person joins a coalition, its type masses—and thus its members’ utilities—do not change. Their consent is automatic, so individual stability loses its distinctive permission condition and collapses toward Nash stability. That may define a useful continuum problem, but it no longer mirrors Theorem 6’s N-IS question.

The proposed fix is to let any positive mass move. That retains a meaningful effect on the destination coalition, but changes the deviation from one agent moving to a bloc moving together. It also makes the choice of deviation size part of the model: a tiny mass, a type’s whole mass, or any amount in between can face different consent constraints. Calling this a natural co-modification is plausible; it is not forced by continuizing the population in the way that normalizing a per-capita quantity is. This mirror needs an additional stability axiom, and its connection to the paper’s N-IS existence result is therefore uncertain.

There is a second modeling gap around strangers. The proposed role-level utilities assume a stranger relationship resolves uniformly for everyone in a pair of roles. In the paper, stranger relations resolve pair by pair. Under those resolutions, people initially assigned the same role can end up with different realized neighborhoods. Coalition masses \(x_t^A\) alone then do not determine each person’s utility or whether a particular person can block. Preserving pairwise resolutions seems to require tracking neighborhood variation within roles; imposing one shared resolution per role pair instead changes the uncertainty model.

These objections do not show that no useful continuous model exists. A bloc-deviation version with role-level uncertainty could be a coherent research question, and authors might recognize it as a worthwhile extension. But that possibility is also the weakness in the negative case: I cannot rule out their accepting those changes. The strongest argument against this anchor is that its most tractable formulation seems to alter both the deviation unit and the stranger-resolution semantics, while a more faithful formulation no longer has the proposed finite type-mass description.
