What the mirror covers

The mass-weighted query covers winner determination for a fixed scoring rule; it leaves the paper’s characterizations and axiom-independence results without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large consultation about a fixed set of alternatives—for example, many app users reporting partial rankings, with a relatively small number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings. A type would be a strict partial order, and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of users with type \(t\). For a fixed scoring rule \(s\), the natural population question is to return the alternatives maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t s_t(a)\).

That gives a sensible continuous-population object, but this paper has no named computational result to anchor it. Its closest candidates are Theorem 1, which characterizes positional scoring rules by four axioms, and Theorems 6 and 13, which characterize the Uniform Plurality and Uniform Anti-Plurality rules by their respective axioms. These are axiomatic results, not claims about complexity or algorithms. The remaining named results likewise characterize rule classes or establish axiom independence.

Winner determination from an explicitly listed support appears to take \(O(m\tau)\) score evaluations for a fixed, evaluable scoring rule. That is an observation about the natural mirror, not a computational result asserted by the paper. Under ChoCo’s scope, I would not present it—or the axiomatic theorems—as a qualifying anchor. So there is a plausible population model, but no valid per-anchor continuous problem for this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The high-multiplicity setting is plausible: a large app survey could have many users sharing a small number of partial-order ballots, and representing each ballot type by its population share is natural. That does not, however, give the paper a computational result to mirror. Its Theorems 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 11, and 13 characterize rule classes through axioms; the corollaries do the same for approval rules. The propositions establish axiom independence. None states the complexity of a computational problem.

The proposed task—given masses \(\mu_t\), find the alternatives maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t s_t(a)\)—is well-defined. But it is an operationalization of the rules, not a result this paper asks or answers. The stronger version, replacing finite profiles with points in the population simplex and restating the characterizations for mixtures, remains axiomatic. Winner robustness or minimum-mass campaigning could yield computational questions, but they add problems and actions absent from the paper; they do not rescue an anchor to one of its named results.

So the strongest case against is that this is a poor paper to mirror for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme, not that its population model is implausible. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could grow from it goes further than the evidence supports: adjacent computational questions may be worthwhile, but they would be new questions rather than mirrors of this paper.
