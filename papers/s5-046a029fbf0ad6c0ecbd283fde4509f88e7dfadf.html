Why no mirror

The named results are structural or axiomatic: Proposition 1 proves a support property, Proposition 3 characterizes priority lotteries, and Theorem 1 characterizes a mechanism class. Although the paper specifies those mechanisms recursively, it gives no computational result such as a complexity bound or hardness classification; the proposed mass-assignment problem adds that computational question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is the allocation problem, not the paper’s mechanism characterization. Imagine a large placement market with \(M\) applicants and \(M\) indivisible positions, grouped into a fixed set \(B\) of position categories with per-capita supplies. If applicants have only \(\tau\) recurring strict rankings over those categories, then \(M\) can grow while \(\tau\) stays small. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: the society is described by ranking masses \(\mu_t\), while each person still receives a lottery over indivisible positions.

A concrete exploratory problem is Unambiguous Mass Assignment. An instance consists of categories \(B\), ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), supplies \(q_b\) with \(\sum_b q_b=1\), and a feasible allocation \(x\), where \(x_{tb}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned category \(b\), \(\sum_b x_{tb}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{tb}=q_b\). For \(\mu_t>0\), type \(t\)’s per-person lottery is \(\pi_{tb}=x_{tb}/\mu_t\). The question is whether \(x\) is unambiguously efficient: whether there is no other feasible \(x'\ne x\) whose per-person lotteries, for every type, are either weakly stochastic-dominating or incomparable with that type’s current lottery. A witness \(x'\) solves a “no” instance.

This is a population-continuous question with a natural connection to the paper, but it is not anchored by a result of the required kind. The paper proves Proposition 1: unambiguous efficiency is strongly combinatorial, so shrinking an allocation’s support while keeping it feasible preserves unambiguous efficiency. It also proves Proposition 3: adjacent-2 sets are maximal for unambiguous efficiency when randomizing over serial-dictatorship orders. Neither result asserts a complexity classification.

The closest main result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says a mechanism is unambiguously efficient, strategy-proof, non-bossy, neutral, and boundedly invariant if and only if it is a hierarchy of monarchies and diarchies. Such a hierarchy recursively allocates to one agent or a pair; it must use a monarchy when residual supply is fractional, and its choices must agree across histories with the same length, assigned-agent identities at each step, and allocation-integrality pattern. The paper does not give a running-time result for a computational problem, and its recursive construction is not a tractability theorem. So, under the stated anchor rule, there is no qualifying anchor and no justified A/B/C prediction to attach to a continuous mirror.

The soft spot is the identity structure. Theorem 1’s hierarchies select named agents and may treat agents with identical rankings differently. In a mass model, averaging those agents into one ranking type can erase that distinction; preserving every identity-dependent role can make the number of types grow with \(M\). The authors might well recognize Unambiguous Mass Assignment as a useful continuous version of their allocation-efficiency question. They would have less reason to accept it as a mirror of Theorem 1 without a new incentive notion and new results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes mechanisms by axioms; Propositions 1 and 3 describe which allocations are efficient and which priority lotteries preserve efficiency. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm whose high-multiplicity version can be compared with it. So there is no paper-grounded Class A, B, or C question here. One could formulate a computational problem around the paper’s ideas, but that would be a new research agenda, not a continuous mirror of a computational contribution.

The proposed Unambiguous Mass Assignment is plausible in a large placement market, but it adds the computational question. In the paper, unambiguous efficiency is a normative property of an allocation, not a decision or optimization problem whose computational cost the authors study. The proposed model also aggregates named agents into ranking types and replaces distinct objects with categories and per-capita supplies. Those choices may make sense for an application, but they require a new finite-to-continuous dictionary. Requiring every member of a type to receive the same lottery would supply one possible dictionary; it also imposes an anonymity restriction absent from the paper. Thus this could be useful work in its own right, but its value cannot be credited as a mirror of a result in this paper.

Proposition 1 is the best possible anchor for that efficiency question, and it still does not provide the missing computational link. Strong combinatoriality says that efficiency is invariant to changes in allocation probabilities that preserve support. Aggregating individuals by type can erase the individual support pattern the proposition concerns. Requiring a common lottery within each type repairs that loss by restricting the model, but then the surviving question is about a new type-uniform assignment problem. Proposition 1 gives no complexity result for it.

Proposition 3 and Theorem 1 have a more direct obstacle to population aggregation. Proposition 3 concerns lotteries over permutations of named agents. In an atomless population, swapping two named agents changes zero mass; randomizing over positive-mass blocks or priority schedules would be a different object. Theorem 1’s hierarchies likewise select named agents and may condition later choices on who was previously assigned. Encoding those roles as types makes the number of types grow with the population; imposing anonymity instead changes the mechanism class being characterized. Either route leaves a new axiomatic question, not a computational mirror.

This makes the negative case strong on fit with ChoCo, but weak as a universal claim that no useful continuous question could arise in any scenario. The paper’s allocation model could inspire worthwhile computational work, including on an anonymous population version. The honest conclusion is narrower: none of the anchors raised supports a worthwhile continuous *mirror of this paper’s results* under the programme’s computational remit. Absolute impossibility would go beyond the evidence.
