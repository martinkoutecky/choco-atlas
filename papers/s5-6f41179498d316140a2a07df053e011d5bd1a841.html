What the mirror covers

The CP/IP characterization, implementable cost families, discounted-cost impossibility, and individual-rationality results receive no continuous computational mirror; the Hungarian-method mention and transfer formulas are computational ingredients, not numbered complexity results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 2.1, proved here, says that a general queueing model is first-best implementable exactly when its cost domain satisfies the combinatorial property (CP) and the independence property (IP). Proposition 5.5, also proved here, says that the discounted-cost model is not first-best implementable. These are results about whether suitable truthful, efficient, budget-balanced mechanisms exist. Neither asserts that a computational problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Calling the impossibility result “hardness” would confuse mechanism existence with computational hardness.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the paper: a large service system with many people sharing a small number of waiting-cost schedules and service benefits. The schedule and benefit would be the type; mass would be the fraction of the cohort with that type. Since service positions scale with population size, a natural model would also scale a person’s position \(k\) to a normalized rank \(r\in[0,1]\). One could then ask whether efficient assignments of types to ranks admit dominant-strategy truthful, exactly budget-balanced transfers. That is a credible *new mechanism-design question*, and the queueing setting makes the population regime plausible.

But it is not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The paper’s central contribution is an economic characterization of first-best implementability, not a complexity landscape for an algorithmic problem. So I can’t honestly provide the required per-anchor computational question or assign it a tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness outcome. My positive case is limited to the natural high-multiplicity queueing scenario; its weakest point is that the paper supplies no computational anchor for ChoCo’s programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is the proponent’s own concession: the paper has no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 2.1 characterizes when a truthful, efficient, balanced mechanism exists; Proposition 5.5 gives an impossibility result of the same kind. Neither studies an algorithmic problem’s complexity. The paper does mention the Hungarian method for computing an efficient queue and gives explicit transfers for some cost families, but those are computational ingredients, not a complexity result whose continuous counterpart this programme could chart.

The proposed large service system is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But turning it into a continuum mechanism requires specifying how an individual’s report affects their own service position and payment when the allocation is expressed as mass across ranks. If the rule depends only on the population distribution, one person’s report does not change that distribution. If the rule instead assigns each report a personal rank lottery, it becomes a different mechanism model from the paper’s permutation of named individuals. Either way, the paper’s CP/IP characterization does not directly provide a computational question over the continuous society.

One could aggregate a finite queue by type and compute its assignment with a transportation LP, or study continuous rank distributions and truthful menus. Those may be coherent research directions. But the former is an algorithmic reformulation of finite assignment; the latter is a new mechanism-design problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The negative case is weakest precisely here: a researcher could find the continuum mechanism question worthwhile in its own right. What the paper does not supply is an anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme.
