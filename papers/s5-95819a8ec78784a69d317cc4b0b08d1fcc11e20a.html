The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is narrow: the paper’s one-player problem of designing a truthful menu whose allocation changes gently when a person changes their report.

Picture a large platform running the same \(m\)-item allocation process for many claimants, each with a separate per-capita copy of the items. A claimant’s complete type is their additive valuation vector \(\theta\); two claimants with the same vector face the same menu and payment rules. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^m\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of claimants of type \(t\), \(N\gg\tau=|T|\), and per-capita supply fixed as \(N\) grows. Each claimant still receives an integral bundle \(a\in\{0,1\}^m\), though average allocations across the population may be fractional.

The lead question is: given \(m\) and \(\mu\), choose a deterministic DSIC menu \(p:\{0,1\}^m\to\mathbb{R}\), with each report \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^m\) assigned a utility-maximizing bundle \(f(\theta)\), to minimize worst-case local change in bundle cardinality. Write \(Q_a=\operatorname{cl}\{\theta:f(\theta)=a\}\); the objective is
\[ S_c(f)=\max_{a,b:\,Q_a\cap Q_b\ne\varnothing} \left|\|a\|_1-\|b\|_1\right|. \]
A solution gives both the minimum value and a menu attaining it.

The anchor is Proposition 12, proved in this paper: for every \(m\), the minimum cardinality sensitivity of a one-player DSIC auction is \(\mu_c(m)=1\). The paper gives a witnessing menu with \(p(a)=\|a\|_1^2\). Its objective and individual bundles are exactly those of the proposed mirror; repeating the same local mechanism across a high-multiplicity claimant population gives it a plausible population setting. I expect this mirror to be Class A: the paper supplies an explicit succinct menu and the optimal value.

A second, less complete question replaces cardinality distance with Hamming distance \(d_H(a,b)=\|a-b\|_1\), and asks for the exact minimum of
\[ S_H(f)=\max_{a,b:\,Q_a\cap Q_b\ne\varnothing}d_H(a,b) \]
and a menu attaining it. Proposition 13, also proved here, establishes \(2\le\mu_h(m)\le m-1\) for \(m\ge3\), using regular subdivisions to construct the upper-bound mechanisms. I would tentatively expect the exact problem to be Class A, though with much less confidence: the paper supplies useful geometric structure, but not an exact answer or an algorithm for finding one. A first question for a prover is whether those bounds can be tightened to an exact formula in \(m\).

This scope covers the paper’s one-player sensitivity results, not its full classification of truthful mechanisms or its multi-player affine-maximizer extension. The main weakness is that \(\mu\) does not affect either sensitivity objective: these are worst-case guarantees for a common individual menu, so population frequencies drop out. The separate-copy scenario preserves the paper’s one-player question, which its authors should recognize, but it does not capture competition for shared auction supply. Whether a continuous population with shared supply admits a similarly faithful sensitivity problem remains open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The weak point in both proposed mirrors is that the paper’s continuum is a single player’s valuation space, not a continuous society. Propositions 12 and 13 optimize worst-case changes in one player’s bundle over truthful menus. Their inputs are the number of items \(m\) and the geometry of the cube; no population distribution or support size enters. More broadly, the paper gives geometric characterizations and bounds, not a complexity result on instances of a computational problem.

Repeating the same auction for many claimants, with each claimant given a separate per-capita supply, is a possible high-multiplicity story. But it leaves the propositions unchanged: the sensitivity objective ranges over all reports, whether or not anyone in \(\mu\) has those reports. Changing the distribution cannot change the optimum. That makes the population a dummy input, not the continuous object whose computational consequences ChoCo is meant to study.

The stronger shared-supply version runs into a different problem. With fixed total supply in an atomless society, one person has zero mass; an individual report change does not perturb the population distribution. To study a change in positive mass, the natural object becomes aggregate allocations or type-level shares, rather than the individual bundle changes measured by these propositions. Scaling supply with population can preserve individual bundles, but then meaningful capacity constraints require a new multi-agent mechanism-design problem. Avoiding a trivial mechanism that allocates nothing also requires adding an objective or allocation constraint. That could be worthwhile research, but these results do not establish or analyze it.

So I would reject Propositions 12 and 13 as anchors for a continuous-population mirror: keeping their individual sensitivity objectives makes the population irrelevant; making the population consequential changes the problem. This is not a proof that no related continuum market-design question is worth asking. It is a strong case that the proponent’s mirrors, including the natural shared-supply repair, do not carry the paper’s results into ChoCo’s population-continuization programme.
