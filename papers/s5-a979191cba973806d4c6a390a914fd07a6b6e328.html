The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that a natural population mirror exists, but this paper fails ChoCo’s computational-anchor requirement.

The paper’s strongest structural anchor is Proposition 8, proved by the authors and supported by the supplied appendix. It says that, under state-dependent single-peaked preferences and the consensus procedure, there exist \(p_l<0\) and \(p_r>0\), independent of the finite policy set \(X\), such that the equilibrium of the exogenous-status-quo game with biases \(b+p\) is also an equilibrium of the endogenous-status-quo game with biases \(b\). Proposition 7, also proved by the authors, says that under Assumptions 1 and 2 all equilibria are partisan, and that an equilibrium exists when \(\Theta_1\) is finite. These are substantive results about equilibrium existence and structure. The article’s subject is indeed dynamic bargaining with an endogenous status quo. [Published article](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/686747)

But neither proposition is computational. The paper contains no named theorem, proposition, lemma, or corollary asserting membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, an approximation algorithm, or a computational encoding. The appendix’s Claims 1–3 and Propositions 5–8 are likewise equilibrium and fixed-point arguments. References to numerical solution or to the possible “intractability” of earlier models are motivation, not computational results proved here. Thus, strictly speaking, this paper has no qualifying anchor and cannot support a compliant positive mirror under the stated rubric.

The best substantive mirror would be a large-legislature version of the endogenous-status-quo game. Let

\[ X=\{x_1<\cdots<x_N\} \]

be ordered policy alternatives, let \(\Theta\) be a finite set of economic states with rational Markov transition matrix \(K\), and let \(T\) be a finite set of complete legislator types. A type includes its state-dependent utility \(U_t(\theta,x)\), ideological bias, voting costs, and bargaining role. The society is a rational distribution

\[ \mu\in\Delta(T), \]

where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of legislators of type \(t\). The regime is a large legislature or repeated committee system: many members share a small number of party, constituency, and preference types, with \(n\gg |T|\). The common economic shock \(\theta\) remains an environmental state; the population being continuized is \(\mu\), not the state process.

At each state \((\theta,q)\), where \(q\in X\) is the current status quo, each type uses a type-symmetric Markov strategy \(\sigma_t(\theta,q)\) for proposing, accepting, or vetoing adjacent changes. The next policy is determined by the \(\mu\)-weighted mass of votes, using a quota \(\kappa\). Setting \(\kappa=1\) and using two cohesive positive-mass caucuses recovers the paper’s unanimity logic; choosing \(\kappa\in(1/2,1)\) gives the more genuinely population-sensitive large-legislature version.

I would call the resulting problem Continuous Endogenous-Status-Quo Equilibrium:

Given \(X,\Theta,K,T,\mu,\delta,U,\kappa\), an initial state \((\theta_0,q_0)\), a target \(x^\star\), and a rational threshold \(\eta\), decide whether there exists a type-symmetric Markov equilibrium \(\sigma\) such that \(x^\star\) is implemented in the next bargaining round with probability at least \(\eta\). A solution consists of \(\sigma\), the induced aggregate transition kernel, and certificates that each type’s prescribed actions are best responses and that the kernel is generated by the \(\mu\)-weighted votes.

A natural optimization variant maximizes aggregate discounted welfare,

\[ \mathbb E\left[\sum_{r\ge 0}\delta^r \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t U_t(\theta_r,x_r)\right] \]

over all such equilibria.

This would be recognizable to the authors as an extension of their problem: the status quo remains endogenous, policy changes are still negotiated sequentially, and the central question remains how strategic anticipation creates polarization and inertia. Proposition 8 suggests a tractable single-peaked subcase: one could ask whether the bias shift \(p\) survives after replacing the two players by finitely many mass types, and whether it remains independent of \(X\). A plausible Class A result might follow for cutoff strategies and fixed \(N\), while the general equilibrium-selection problem could have continuum-specific hardness. That classification, however, would be new work, not something established by this paper.

The weakest point is decisive: the original model has only two strategic players, \(l\) and \(r\). If one merely clones them into large populations, \(\mu\) may be largely inert; if one introduces weighted population voting, one changes the within-period bargaining institution. The mirror is therefore substantively plausible for a large legislature, but not an exact high-multiplicity relaxation of a computational problem already solved or classified in the paper.

So my positive-side report would be: natural continuous-population extension, but no qualifying named computational anchor; do not count this paper as a successful ChoCo mirror under the strict rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no computational anchor at all. Proposition 7 and Proposition 8 are equilibrium-existence and equilibrium-structure results. They contain no encoded computational problem, complexity classification, algorithm, approximation guarantee, or parameterized analysis. Thus there is no paper result whose continuous-population counterpart could enter ChoCo’s complexity landscape. A new computational model inspired by the paper might be worthwhile, but it would not be a mirror of this paper in the programme’s sense.

Proposition 8 is the strongest-looking candidate, but it is not a population statement. It concerns two strategic players, \(l\) and \(r\), and says that an equilibrium under an endogenous status quo can be represented by an equilibrium of an exogenous-status-quo game after applying player-specific bias shifts \(p_l\le 0\) and \(p_r\ge 0\). The theorem’s objects are individual continuation payoffs, individual deviations, and the two players’ bargaining roles. There is no electorate, multiplicity parameter, or aggregate statistic to be replaced by a distribution.

The proposed large-legislature version does not repair this. If many legislators are merely clones of \(l\) and \(r\), their masses are inert: the game remains one between two cohesive blocs, so the continuous society contributes no new object. If legislators vote according to a quota \(\kappa\), then the model has changed its institution. Pivotality, continuation values, proposal power, and the meaning of the bias shift all change. The resulting type-distribution game could be interesting, but it is a new collective-bargaining model, not a high-multiplicity relaxation of Proposition 8. Asking whether some analogue of the bias-shift equivalence survives would be new theory, not a computational question already present in the paper.

Proposition 7 fails for the same reason. “All equilibria are partisan” and “an equilibrium exists when \(\Theta_1\) is finite” are qualitative statements about the two-player dynamic game. They do not specify a computational task. In a mass model, “partisan” would also need a new definition: it could mean that every type uses a pure directional strategy, that almost every voter does so, or merely that aggregate support is polarized. These are materially different population models. Retaining the original meaning leaves only two strategic agents; replacing it with quota-based aggregate voting creates a different game.

The proponent’s optimization variant—maximizing aggregate discounted welfare over equilibria—makes the gap still clearer. Aggregate welfare is not the paper’s objective, and equilibrium selection is not one of its computational problems. It may be a sensible ChoCo problem in its own right, but its motivation comes from inventing a large legislature around the paper, not from continuizing a computational result in the paper.

The honest limitation is that the universal modelling claim is not mathematically airtight: one can certainly imagine a plausible repeated legislature with many members sharing finitely many preference and party types. What cannot be obtained is a worthwhile *mirror of this paper under the stated gate*. The paper studies a two-player dynamic bargaining game and proves analytic equilibrium results. A continuous population model would either collapse to the same two-player game, or introduce an aggregation institution, objective, and computational task that the paper never contains. That is enough to reject it as a ChoCo mirror, even though the newly invented large-legislature model might deserve separate study.
