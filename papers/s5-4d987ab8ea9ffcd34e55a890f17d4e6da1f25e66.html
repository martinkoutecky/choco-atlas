The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu\) and a finite-description monotone complete rule \(V\) whose class-aggregate SSI or PBI limits exist under integer replications, find nonnegative class weights and a quota minimizing the distance between \(V\)'s limiting power distribution and that of a weighted rule.

The model it lives in

A society has finitely many interchangeable voting types with masses \(\mu_t\); a binary rule acts on each type’s fraction of yes votes. Replicate each type proportionally, aggregate voters’ SSI or PBI within type, and take the limit. Approximate that limiting distribution with a weighted rule, using Manhattan distance or the proposed per-capita-scaled Chebyshev distance.

What the mirror covers

Covers the best-weighted-approximation objective underlying Proposition 1 for fixed-class populations; it does not reproduce the proposition’s exact finite-\(n\) worst-case values or the lower bounds in Proposition 2.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which regularity conditions ensure that the replicated SSI and PBI limits exist and are independent of the replication sequence?
  • What are the complexity of computing those limiting indices and the complexity of finding the nearest weighted rule?
  • Do worst-case power-approximation gaps persist as the population grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a continuous version of the paper’s best weighted approximation problem. There is an important limitation up front: the paper states no named P/NP/FPT-style result. Proposition 1 is a named computational result, proved here by exhaustive enumeration, so it is the closest available anchor—but it is not a complexity classification.

Proposition 1 gives the exact worst-case distances between the power distribution of a complete simple game and that of its closest weighted game for \(n=7,8\). For SSI, the Manhattan distance is \(0.0666667\) at both sizes; the Chebyshev distance is \(0.0166667\) for \(n=7\) and \(0.0154762\) for \(n=8\). For PBI, the Manhattan distances are \(0.0599700\) and \(0.0567084\), and the Chebyshev distances are \(0.0173913\) and \(0.0139124\), respectively. The continuous question I would anchor on is the best-approximation optimization that these worst-case values are built from.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large cooperative or shareholder body with a few legally defined membership classes. Members of a class have identical voting rights and are interchangeable under the decision rule; there may be millions of members but only a small number \(\tau\) of classes. Let \(T\) be the class set, and let \(\mu_t\) be class \(t\)’s fraction of the population. A voting profile is \(x\in[0,1]^T\), where \(x_t\) is the fraction of class \(t\) voting yes. A continuous complete rule \(V:[0,1]^T\to\{0,1\}\) is monotone, and has an ordering of classes such that replacing equal mass of a less desirable class’s yes votes with a more desirable class’s yes votes cannot turn a winning profile into a losing one.

For a precise bridge to the paper’s indices, replicate the society by factors \(r\) for which every \(r\mu_t\) is an integer. Lift \(V\) to a finite game by applying it to the class participation fractions of each coalition. When the limits exist, define \(P_{p,t}(V)\) as the limiting aggregate \(p\)-power of class \(t\), summing its members’ SSI or PBI before taking the limit. A weighted continuous rule has the form \(W_{a,q}(x)=1\) exactly when \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t x_t\ge q\), for nonnegative class weights \(a_t\) and quota \(q>0\).

Call the resulting problem Continuous Weighted Power Approximation: given rational masses \(\mu\), a finite description of a continuous complete rule \(V\), an index \(p\in\{\mathrm{SSI},\mathrm{PBI}\}\), and a distance, find \(a,q\) minimizing the distance between \(P_p(V)\) and \(P_p(W_{a,q})\). Use \(d_1(P,Q)=\sum_t|P_t-Q_t|\); for the Chebyshev version use the per-capita density distance \(\max_t |P_t-Q_t|/\mu_t\). That rescaling keeps the intensive comparison meaningful as each member’s individual power tends to zero. A solution is a minimizing weighted rule, or an arbitrarily close one if the infimum is not attained, together with its distance. This mirrors the inner approximation task in Proposition 1; it does not claim to reproduce the proposition’s particular \(n=7,8\) constants.

This is a plausible object for the authors to recognize: the rule remains a binary voting rule, the comparison remains between complete and weighted rules, and the objective remains approximation of SSI or PBI power. Aggregating within types is natural when class members are truly interchangeable. The paper’s broader story that weighted games can closely approximate richer rules gives the question a clear motivation. I would expect the optimization to be open, with continuum-specific hardness a real possibility: the weighted candidates have only \(\tau\) weights and a quota, but the complete-rule side ranges over monotone decision regions and its limiting power measures may be difficult to compute. That is a forecast, not a hardness claim. Further questions include whether these limits exist independently of the replication sequence, whether worst-case gaps persist as population size grows, and whether the weighted search has a tractable pricing or separation formulation.

The weak point is substantial: SSI and PBI are defined for atomic voters, and the continuum power measure above requires a replication limit that the paper neither defines nor studies. Some rules may not have a well-behaved limit, and type-level aggregation plus the intensive Chebyshev metric are genuine model choices. The paper’s enumerated committees are also small, so its exact values are evidence for the underlying approximation question, not evidence that this high-multiplicity regime is already present in the paper. The case survives as a recognizable continuous analogue of the paper’s optimization objective, but it does not supply the complexity anchor the continuization programme most wants.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Proposition 1: its exact worst-case distances are built from the distance between a complete game’s power distribution and its best weighted approximation. That is a real computational result, but it is an enumeration of values for \(n=7,8\), not a complexity result. The paper’s central claim is about how closely weighted games approximate richer rules, not about the complexity of computing that approximation. So there is no complexity classification here for a continuous mirror to extend.

The proposed mirror also changes the object in an important way. The inner problem, “given \(V\), find the closest weighted rule,” is only one part of Proposition 1. A closer counterpart would be something like
\[ \sup_{V\in\mathcal C(\mu)}\inf_{W\in\mathcal W(\mu)} d\!\left(P^\infty_p(V),P^\infty_p(W)\right), \]
where \(P^\infty_p\) is a limiting power measure. But the paper supplies neither that measure nor a class of continuous rules over which to take the supremum. To define the measure, one must lift a mass rule to finite replicated games and aggregate and normalize their individual indices. In a nonatomic society, an individual’s marginal effect is zero, so the ordinary SSI and PBI do not themselves give the proposed quantity. A replication limit may be a sensible new index, but it needs its own definition and regularity conditions; different restrictions on which rules or sequences are allowed can change the worst case.

There is a real high-multiplicity setting one could study, such as a large shareholder body with a few classes of identical voting rights. With a fixed, modest number of classes, the rule can have meaningful nonweighted structure, and a limiting power-approximation question is plausible. But that studies a restricted family of block-symmetric games. If the aim is to retain the paper’s full range of complete games, the number of distinct types may grow with the electorate, removing the support-size advantage. And if the aim is to retain the paper’s worst-case result, one must show that the finite extremal games belong to a well-behaved family of replicated rules; the paper does not establish that.

Thus the best objection is not that large voting bodies or aggregated power are incoherent. It is that the proposed question is a newly constructed asymptotic power-index problem, rather than a continuous formulation of the result the paper actually proves. The negative case is only moderate: a fixed-class replication study could be worthwhile if the authors accept the limiting index and restricted rule family. The paper alone, however, gives little reason to prioritize it as a continuization-complexity problem.
