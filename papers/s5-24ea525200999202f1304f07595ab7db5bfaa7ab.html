The continuous mirror question

For two homogeneous types with masses \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), suppose the unknown cost is a coverage function and each sample is the limiting mass vector \(x=(1/4,1/4)\) obtained by including each clone independently with probability \(1/2\); the input also gives \(C(\mu)\). Can any estimator using polynomially many such cost observations approximate both types’ clone-limit aggregate Shapley shares within a fixed constant factor for every such coverage cost? In the hard pair, the two costs swap feature weights \(1\) and \(M\), yet agree at every observed vector and at \(\mu\), while their type shares are \((1,M)\) and \((M,1)\).

The model it lives in

A continuous society has two types, \(A\) and \(B\), each of mass \(1/2\); members of a type share the same coverage features. A fractional coalition is \(x\in[0,\mu]\), and its cost charges a feature’s weight once when positive mass from a type covering it is present. The target for each type is its aggregate Shapley share in the limit of finite clone populations. The feature assignment is hidden; the data consist of coalition-mass and cost observations, including the full-population cost.

The objection that survived

The target aggregates player-level Shapley shares by type using a clone-limit definition, so the mirror does not establish a canonical Shapley value for every nonatomic cost game; this limits its scope but leaves the stated coverage-game question intact.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 6’s sample-based Shapley inapproximability result and Theorem 3’s probably stable core algorithm. They leave the approximate-core results, curvature approximation and lower bound, and data-dependent Shapley axioms and estimation results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which distributions over fractional coalitions, particularly ones that assign positive probability to coalitions omitting a type, can type-aggregate Shapley shares be estimated within a constant factor?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is a high-multiplicity cost-sharing model: a large population of service users is divided into a small number \(\tau\) of cohorts whose members are identical in every cost-relevant respect. A type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), and a coalition is described by the mass vector \(x\), where \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). Its service cost is \(C(x)\).

A type-level share density \(p_t\) charges a coalition of mass \(x\) the amount \(\sum_t x_t p_t\). The full population pays \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\). This is a plausible setting for pooled infrastructure or services: many clients have the same demand profile, while the observed coalitions and costs vary. It makes the population continuous; the samples of coalitions remain a separate part of the model.

Theorem 6 is my lead hardness anchor. Proved in this paper, it says that no constant \(\alpha>0\) suffices to approximate the traditional Shapley value of coverage functions from samples drawn from the uniform distribution over coalitions. The proof gives two coverage functions that agree on polynomially many samples with high probability, yet some player’s Shapley values differ by a factor large enough to rule out the claimed approximation.

A continuous version can use the finite-population limit of Shapley shares, aggregated by type. Take two types \(A,B\), each of mass \(1/2\). In one coverage game, every \(A\)-member covers a feature of weight \(1\), and every \(B\)-member covers features of total weight \(M\); in the other game, these roles are reversed. All members of a type are identical within each game. In a finite clone population, the total Shapley share of each type converges to \(\Phi_A,\Phi_B\): the feature weight is divided among the types that cover it in proportion to their masses. The two games therefore have limiting type shares \((1,M)\) and \((M,1)\).

Define Continuum Type-Aggregate Shapley Estimation as follows: the instance gives the two type masses and polynomially many cost observations \((x,C(x))\), but not the coverage representation. Each sample is the limit of including every finite-population agent independently with probability \(1/2\), so the observed mass vector is \((1/4,1/4)\). Both games give the same cost there, \(1+M\), and also the same full-population cost. The question is whether the samples suffice to estimate both type shares within a fixed multiplicative factor. Choosing \(M\) large enough defeats any proposed constant factor: the observations cannot reveal which type receives which share. I expect this mirror to be hard for continuum-specific, information-theoretic reasons. It is not a claim of NP-hardness or a running-time lower bound. The sample law has lost the coalitions that distinguish the games. This is a natural two-type regime with an enormous population and only two types, not a construction that needs many types.

Theorem 3 is the strongest case that the authors would recognize a continuous mirror as their problem. Proved in this paper, it says that for any class of games with a nonempty core, cost shares in the probably stable core are efficiently computable with sample complexity \(O((n+\log(1/\Delta))/\delta^2)\). Here “probably stable” means the shares satisfy the core inequality on a fresh sample with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and \(\Delta\) is the algorithm’s failure probability.

Its continuous counterpart is Continuum Probably Stable Core from Samples. The instance consists of rational type masses \(\mu_t\), the full-population cost \(C(\mu)\), and samples \((x^j,C(x^j))\) from a distribution over fractional coalitions \(x\in[0,\mu]\). The cost function is unknown beyond those observations, and the instance promises a nonempty continuous core. The task is to output share densities \(p_t\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=C(\mu)\) and \(\Pr_{x}[\sum_t x_t p_t\le C(x)]\ge1-\delta\). The sample constraints and balance condition form a linear program with \(\tau\) share variables. The paper’s halfspace generalization argument points to an algorithm using \(O((\tau+\log(1/\Delta))/\delta^2)\) samples, with polynomial running time in the sample and input bit lengths. I expect this mirror to be tractable. This is my strongest admissibility case: it keeps the paper’s core question, replaces equal shares for named players with a cost density for each homogeneous cohort, and reduces the relevant dimension from the number of players to the number of types.

Theorem 3 also raises the natural exact-versus-probable question: can one efficiently find shares satisfying the core inequalities for *all* fractional coalitions, and when does the answer transfer back to the finite clone population? For Theorem 6, the next question is whether sampling laws that give meaningful probability to coalitions with a type absent restore learnability with polynomially many samples.

The mirrors cover only the paper’s probably stable core result and its sample-based Shapley lower bound. I would not extend this case to every result in the paper.

The soft spot is real: Theorem 6’s continuous sampling law is degenerate, and its target uses the limit of type-aggregated finite Shapley shares. A skeptical author could question whether that is their Shapley question. The limit gives a precise answer to that objection, but it does not erase the modeling choice. The core mirror is less exposed: its main added choice is to define costs on fractional coalitions, which is plausible for pooled services but is not uniquely determined by every finite game. Taken together, the two anchors make a defensible case for a continuous population model while also identifying where the sample-based Shapley question can become impossible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that the paper’s main difficulty is statistical identification, not the combinatorial optimization that ChoCo’s continuous toolbox is meant to expose. For Theorem 3, a type-mass version is almost automatic: share densities \(p_t\) give affine sample constraints \(\sum_t x_t p_t\le C(x)\), balance is another linear constraint, and the corresponding halfspace class has VC dimension \(O(\tau)\). The same empirical-feasibility LP and generalization proof go through. That is a clean extension, but it looks more like replacing \(n\) by the number of types in a standard learning argument than opening a new continuous-computation problem.

A stronger core mirror would ask for inequalities on every fractional coalition, or derive them as a limit of finite clone games. Neither follows from Theorem 3’s samples. A finite game specifies costs only on a lattice of clone counts; it does not determine costs between lattice points. The all-fractional version therefore needs a continuous cost model or a chosen limiting sequence. Both are plausible, but either adds substantive structure beyond the theorem’s statistical core problem. This makes Theorem 3 a weak reason to prioritize the mirror, though not a reason to call it ill-posed.

Theorem 6 has a sharper vulnerability: a nonatomic population has no uniform random permutation, so its “Shapley value” needs a definition. The proposed type aggregate, taken as the limit of finite clone Shapley shares, is sensible, but it is a modeling choice. The paper’s Shapley value is assigned to named players; the mirror’s target is a type-level aggregate or share density. That distinction deserves scrutiny.

It does not, however, defeat the strongest version of the mirror. In the paper’s coverage construction, the two types can be genuinely homogeneous, and their limiting aggregate shares are well-defined: \((1,1/\alpha^2)\) in one game and \((1/\alpha^2,1)\) in the other. Under independent inclusion of each clone with probability \(1/2\), the mass vector converges to \((1/4,1/4)\). Both games have the same cost there; distinguishing coalitions require one type to be absent, an event whose probability vanishes in the limit. Thus the proposed sample lower bound survives the natural finite-clone limit. Calling the sample law degenerate identifies a feature of the mirror, but does not make it an invalid population model.

So I would argue that Theorem 3 is a modest, nearly routine extension and that the Shapley mirror changes the target’s interpretation. But I cannot honestly make the universal negative case: the two-type clone-limit construction is a coherent, recognizable question about statistical cost sharing over a continuous population. The objection to Theorem 6 is a genuine caveat, not a defeat; under the prompt’s standard, that anchor survives.
