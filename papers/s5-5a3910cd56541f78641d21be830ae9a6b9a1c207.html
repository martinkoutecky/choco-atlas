The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: this paper has no named computational result that can serve as an anchor. Its numbered propositions establish structural facts, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. For example, Proposition 3.8 proves that merging agents from different path-connected components increases the new agent’s viability beyond the sum of their former viabilities; Proposition 3.10 proves that such a merge increases stability; and Proposition 3.13 proves that, when a dominated agent delegates to another, the recipient’s viability does not decrease. These are proved here, but they are not computational results. Proposition 3.12 relates embedded and closure Betti numbers to path-connectedness and larger hyperedges; it, too, is structural rather than a complexity result.

There is a plausible setting for a *future* continuous extension: a large political organization with many interchangeable members in a small number of roles. A type could specify a member’s complete coalition and delegation behavior, and its mass could be that role’s fraction of the organization. But role masses alone do not preserve the paper’s object: the hypergraph records which particular subsets form viable coalitions, and the same role frequencies can correspond to different coalition structures. A continuous intervention question—such as how much role mass must be delegated to reach a target stability level—would also need a principled continuum definition of viability or stability. The paper does not supply one; its stability measure counts subsets of a finite agent set.

So I cannot give a precise continuous problem *per anchor* without inventing a new problem and misrepresenting it as a mirror of a result in this paper. The paper’s geometric realization of a hypergraph does not itself continuize the population. My weakest point is therefore decisive: although a high-multiplicity political setting is imaginable, the paper provides neither a named computational result to mirror nor a canonical continuous version of its coalition measures.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper gives no computational problem to mirror. Propositions 3.8 and 3.10 compare viability and stability before and after a specified merge; Proposition 3.13 compares viability before and after a specified delegation; Proposition 3.12 relates Betti numbers to connectivity. These are structural claims about finite hypergraphs, not complexity results. A continuous theorem extending them could be mathematically interesting, but it would not chart the complexity of a problem this paper formulates. A question such as “what minimum mass must be delegated to reach a stability threshold?” would first require inventing the intervention choices, costs, and objective.

The strongest modeling rescue is a large organization with many members in a few roles, with coalitions represented by a continuous hypergraph or a measurable interaction rule. That could be a sensible continuous population model. But role masses alone lose which members form coalitions; preserving that information requires a richer object than a distribution over individual types. Even granting that co-modification, the paper supplies no computational result to transfer. Its operation on a single agent also has no direct atomless analogue: one agent has zero mass, while delegating a positive mass requires a new bloc-level rule.

There is a further, weaker obstacle: the paper’s global viability and stability divide coalition counts by quantities exponential in the number of agents. In many sparse large-organization models those measures tend to zero; keeping them informative may require exponentially many viable coalitions. Local viability may avoid that problem in some regimes, so this is not a general impossibility.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s central gate—there is no named computational anchor here—and weaker as a universal claim about every plausible continuous model. A continuous hypergraph model could motivate new computational questions, but calling them mirrors of this paper would overstate the connection.
