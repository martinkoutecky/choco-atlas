The continuous mirror question

Given candidates with fixed positions in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), a norm from \(\{\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_\infty\}\), and a finite support \(S\) of realizable strict-ranking types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), compute a ranking \(\pi\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\,\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\succ_t)\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings realizable from ideal points in the fixed two-dimensional candidate geometry. Each type has mass \(\mu_t\); the decision variable is a consensus ranking, and its cost is the mass-weighted Kendall tau distance.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Kemeny hardness in Theorem 1 and the Slater hardness in Corollary 1; Theorem 2 supplies the tournament-inducibility construction behind those results rather than a separate continuous complexity question.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the complexity vary with the number \(\tau\) of supported ranking types or with restrictions on the geometry of those types?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a strong Class B case: the population mirror is natural, and the paper’s hardness survives it. The Kemeny result is the lead; the Slater result is a separate, credible second anchor.

Picture a large electorate whose members rank candidates by distance from their ideal points in a two-dimensional policy space. Fix the candidate positions and a norm. A voter type is a strict ranking induced by an ideal point; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the share of the population with that ranking. Ideal points in the same ranking region are the same type for these aggregation problems, since the objective uses their rankings, not their exact distances. If there are \(n\) people but only \(\tau\) occupied ranking regions, the model records \(\tau\) rational masses rather than \(n\) ballots. For fixed candidate positions under \(\ell_2\), the pairwise bisectors partition the plane into at most \(O(m^4)\) regions, independent of electorate size.

Lead: Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Under each of the norms \(\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_\infty\), Kemeny ranking on two-dimensional Euclidean preferences is NP-hard, even when the input includes a two-dimensional representation of the preferences. The paper also states that the decision version is NP-complete.

The continuous question is Continuous 2D-Euclidean Kemeny Ranking. An instance gives a candidate set \(C\), a fixed norm from \(\{\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_\infty\}\), candidate positions, and a finite support \(S\) of voter-ranking types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). The instance includes a Euclidean representation for those rankings, using the same representation convention as the paper. The task is to find a ranking \(\pi\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\,\mathrm{KT}(\pi,\succ_t). \]
Its decision version asks, given rational \(B\), whether some \(\pi\) has objective at most \(B\).

This is a particularly direct high-multiplicity mirror. Given a discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), where \(n=\sum_t n_t\). The continuous objective is then exactly the discrete Kemeny cost divided by \(n\); a discrete threshold \(K\) becomes \(K/n\). The Euclidean representation is retained. Thus the paper’s hard instances embed in the continuous question, with polynomial-size rational masses. I expect hardness transfers, not a tractability gain.

The paper’s Theorem 2 supplies the structural reason this hardness survives the geometric restriction: it proves that every weighted tournament with weights of the same parity is inducible by a two-dimensional \(\ell_2\)-Euclidean profile, and every weighted bipartite tournament with even weights is inducible under \(\ell_1\) and \(\ell_\infty\), by polynomial-time constructions. This is a supporting result rather than a computational-complexity anchor. It explains why the geometry leaves ample room for the combinatorics behind Kemeny hardness.

Second anchor: Corollary 1, proved in this paper. Under \(\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_\infty\), Slater ranking on two-dimensional Euclidean preferences is NP-hard, even when a two-dimensional representation is given.

The corresponding Continuous 2D-Euclidean Slater Ranking instance uses the same candidates, represented ranking types, and rational masses. Define the population majority margin
\[ \Delta_\mu(c,d)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t \bigl(\mathbf{1}[c\succ_t d]-\mathbf{1}[d\succ_t c]\bigr). \]
The task is to find a ranking minimizing the number of pairs with \(\Delta_\mu(c,d)>0\) that it orders as \(d\) above \(c\), plus pairs with \(\Delta_\mu(c,d)<0\) that it orders as \(c\) above \(d\). Pairs with zero margin contribute no disagreement. This mirrors Slater ranking’s majority-comparison objective directly. For a discrete profile, dividing all margins by \(n\) preserves their signs, so its Slater objective is unchanged. The expected direction is again hardness transfers. Further questions include how support size or restrictions on the geometry of the ranking regions affect the problem, and whether useful approximation guarantees are possible.

Both questions make the society continuous through its distribution over deterministic preference types. The masses are population shares, not uncertainty about a ballot or a lottery over outcomes. The mirror covers Theorem 1 and Corollary 1; it does not need to reinterpret the rest of the paper.

The weakest point is that this mirror does not show a new algorithmic benefit from continuity: the hard discrete profiles are already inside it after normalization. That makes the continuous formulation look close to a weighted re-expression of the original problem. I still think the mirror is sound. High multiplicity is precisely the regime in which a population distribution is the natural input, and the paper’s results give a clear answer for that regime: here, continuization does not dissolve the hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a strong case against the Kemeny mirror. The paper has a named computational result, and its inputs already treat voters with identical rankings identically. A population distribution over those ranking types is therefore a natural instance regime, not a forced reinterpretation. For discrete multiplicities \(n_t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) divides every Kemeny score by \(n\) and preserves the supplied Euclidean representation. The objective remains nontrivial: it is a weighted sum of pairwise disagreements, bounded by \(\binom{m}{2}\), rather than a cost that disappears in the limit.

The stronger spatial version also holds up. Fix candidate positions and distribute an atomless population over ideal points. Each strict ranking occupies an open region of the plane; assigning rational mass to those regions gives the same type distribution and the same Kemeny objective. Thus the mirror need not put positive mass on individual ideal points or discard the geometry. Neither individual identity nor a degenerating limit blocks it.

The Slater anchor survives for the same reason. Its continuous majority margin is
\[ \Delta_\mu(c,d)=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[c\succ_t d]-\mathbf{1}[d\succ_t c]\bigr). \]
Normalizing a discrete profile preserves each margin’s sign and any tie, so it preserves the Slater objective. Slater is not an independent second structural case—it uses the same majority-tournament backbone—but the brief explicitly allows one good mirror to suffice.

The best negative point is that these mirrors may yield little new structure: both objectives can be expressed through weighted pairwise comparisons, and the discrete hardness instances embed directly after normalization. But that is not a sound objection here. A high-multiplicity regime is part of the programme’s target, and learning that hardness persists is a valid result. The honest negative case is therefore weak: I find no fundamental obstacle to either mirror, and the Kemeny one alone survives it.
