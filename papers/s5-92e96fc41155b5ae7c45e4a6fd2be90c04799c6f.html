The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is one mirror, anchored in the Kemeny hardness result. The 2021 paper has no numbered complexity theorem of its own; in §3.1 it cites Theorem 2 of Bartholdi, Tovey, and Trick. That cited theorem says that Kemeny Score is NP-complete, while computing a Kemeny ranking and deciding whether a candidate is a Kemeny winner are NP-hard. The score problem asks, given strict voter rankings and a threshold, whether some consensus ranking has total Kemeny score at most that threshold. The theorem is cited, not proved in this paper; its hardness reduction comes from Feedback Arc Set. [Bartholdi et al., Theorem 2](https://rohitvaish.in/data/Papers/%5BBartholdi%2B%5D%20Voting%20Schemes%20for%20which%20it%20can%20be%20Difficult%20to%20tell%20Who%20Won%20the%20Election.pdf)

Lead mirror: Continuous Kemeny Score. An instance has a candidate set \(C\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) strict rankings, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\). For rankings \(s,t\), let \(d_K(s,t)\) count the pairs of candidates ordered differently. The question is whether

\[ \min_{s\in S_m}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_K(s,t)\le q. \]

A yes-instance has a ranking \(s\) witnessing that bound; an exact optimizer can return the minimum value and a ranking attaining it. This is a question about the underlying aggregation problem, not an encoding of it.

A plausible regime is a large cooperative, professional association, or civic consultation where many members rank the same fixed menu of proposals. Each complete ranking is a type, and its mass is that fraction of members. Standardized options and recurring preference patterns can make \(N\), the membership, very large while \(\tau\), the number of observed ranking types, is much smaller. The decision is the consensus ranking minimizing a random member’s expected number of pairwise disagreements.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: they already compress a finite profile into distinct rankings with integer voter counts, and minimize total distance to those rankings. Replacing counts \(n_t\) by shares \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) makes the objective the per-capita version of theirs, so the minimizing rankings are unchanged. This is population continuization; neither the ranking outcome nor the voters’ preferences have been made fractional.

The expected classification is Class B: hardness transfers. Given a discrete profile with \(N\) voters, set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). The continuous objective is the discrete Kemeny score divided by \(N\), or by \(2N\) under the original theorem’s convention of charging two points per inversion. Scale the threshold by the same factor. The cited reduction has polynomially many voters, so these rational masses and the scaled threshold have polynomial bit length. The continuous score decision is also in NP: a ranking is a certificate, and its rational objective can be computed and compared with \(q\) in polynomial time. The reduction’s combinatorics lie in the alternatives and their pairwise majority cycles, so a continuum of population mass does not remove the hardness.

This scope is deliberately limited to the paper’s underlying Kemeny optimization and its cited hardness result. It does not claim that the Borda-seeded branch-and-bound method or its measured runtime improvement has a continuous counterpart. Further questions include whether Continuous Kemeny Score is tractable for fixed \(\tau\), and whether useful approximation or parameterized algorithms exist for structured supports.

The soft spot is that for rational inputs this is almost a normalization of the discrete problem, and it offers no expected tractability gain: it is a Class B mirror, not a Class A result. But the discrete profile in the paper is still finite, with integer counts; the continuous population is a sensible high-multiplicity model, and establishing that the hardness survives is itself a meaningful boundary for the continuization programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed Continuous Kemeny Score is a good ChoCo problem, but a weak mirror of *this paper’s contribution*. The paper cites Bartholdi et al.’s hardness result; it does not prove a complexity theorem of its own. Its new result is an experimental claim: Borda initialization reduced the average running time of a particular branch-and-bound implementation on a finite set of profiles. A continuous version of that experiment would be a benchmark study, not a new complexity question with an evident place in the programme’s tractability landscape.

The best version of that mirror would use rational masses on ranking types, initialize the search with the mass-weighted Borda ranking, and study how performance depends on support size and the distribution of mass. That is coherent, and the association or consultation scenario is credible. But the paper does not identify a structural question about continuous societies that this experiment would answer; it reports runtimes for one implementation on profiles with 8–12 alternatives. The mirror would need to add its own research agenda rather than carry over a result the paper established.

That objection has a clear limit: Continuous Kemeny Score remains a sensible population-level computational question, and the paper’s objective gives the authors a direct reason to recognize it. The ranking types are complete agent types, and large groups sharing rankings make multiplicity meaningful. So I cannot honestly defeat that anchor on modelling grounds. My negative case is only that it does not mirror the paper’s contribution; if the reader accepts a cited, underlying problem as an anchor, the proposed mirror survives, and the case against it is weak.
