The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), groups \(m_t\), scores \(s_t\), per-capita capacity \(q\), increasing \(h\), and rational piecewise-linear concave utilities \(u_m\), compute an allocation \(a_t\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t=q\) that maximizes \(W(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_th(s_t)+\sum_{m\in M}u_m(x_m)\), where \(x_m=\sum_{t:m_t=m}\mu_ta_t\), and provide a certificate \(\pi_t\in h(s_t)+\partial u_{m_t}(x_{m_t})\) implementing the allocation by adaptive priorities.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity single-authority admissions or medical-allocation market with policy-complete types carrying masses \(\mu_t\). Each individual receives an integral resource unit, while \(a_t\) records the admitted fraction of cohort \(t\); the authority maximizes score value plus concave group-diversity utility, and \(A^\ast\) supplies marginal group bonuses.

The objection that survived

The opponent's best point is that Theorem 5 gives no explicit input encoding or running-time bound, so its computational status depends on treating the defined \(A^\ast\) allocation rule as a constructive computation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5's single-authority discrete first-best allocation and its \(A^\ast\) marginal-priority certificate. It leaves Theorem 6's priority/quota classification, the multi-authority stability, dominance, and efficiency results, and the empirical benchmarking untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact bit complexity of computing an optimal \(a\) and a valid \(A^\ast\) certificate for rational piecewise-linear \(h\) and \(u_m\)?
  • How does the algorithm depend on the number of supported types \(|T|\), groups \(|M|\), and utility breakpoints?
  • Do analogous polynomial-time mass formulations and certificates extend to the paper's multi-authority stability and dominance results?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case uses Appendix E, not the paper’s main model. Sections 2–5 already make the population continuous, so presenting Theorem 1 or Proposition 2 as a new continuization would be a genuine collision. Appendix E, however, returns to discrete agents and gives a legitimate high-multiplicity target.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in Appendix E.2: “If \(\mu\) is implemented by \(A^\ast\), then \(\mu\) is an optimal matching.” Here the discrete authority has \(q\) resource units, agents have scores and group labels, utility is
\[ \xi(\mu)=\sum_{\theta\text{ assigned}}h(s(\theta))+\sum_{m\in M}u_m(x_m), \]
with \(h\) increasing and each \(u_m\) concave, and
\[ A_m^\ast(y,s)=h(s)+u_m(y)-u_m(y-1). \]
The theorem says that every allocation selected by this adaptive priority rule is welfare-optimal; Lemma 3 establishes that all allocations selected by \(A^\ast\) have the same utility.

A precise mirror is the following.

High-Multiplicity First-Best APM. An instance consists of a finite set of complete types \(T\), where each type \(t\) has group \(m_t\), score \(s_t\), and rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); a per-capita capacity \(q\in[0,1]\); an increasing score function \(h\); and concave, rational piecewise-linear group utilities \(u_m\). Choose allocation fractions \(a_t\in[0,1]\), interpreted as assigning an integral resource to fraction \(a_t\) of the large cohort of type \(t\), subject to
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t=q. \]
Writing
\[ x_m=\sum_{t:m_t=m}\mu_ta_t, \]
maximize
\[ W(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t h(s_t)+\sum_{m\in M}u_m(x_m). \]

A solution is an optimal mass allocation \(a\), together with the adaptive-priority certificate
\[ \pi_t\in h(s_t)+\partial u_{m_t}(x_{m_t}), \]
such that types with larger \(\pi_t\) are assigned first, with a threshold tie split if necessary to fill capacity. This is the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s underlying welfare problem, not a relaxation of the resource: each individual still receives either zero or one unit, while \(a_t\) records the aggregate fraction admitted.

The natural regime is large school-admission or medical-allocation markets: millions of applicants, but perhaps only a few hundred score-band/group types. Agents sharing a type have the same policy-relevant score, group, and authority-relevant characteristics. The resource also scales with population size, so \(q\) is a fraction of the population rather than a fixed number of seats.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. Its finite-type formulation is a concave optimization problem over a simplex; with piecewise-linear utilities it reduces to a linear program, and with standard evaluation oracles it admits polynomial-time approximation. The paper’s adaptive-priority characterization supplies the KKT-style structure explaining why the optimum can be implemented by marginal group bonuses. Further questions include exact bit-complexity bounds, dependence on the number of supported types, and whether multi-authority stability and dominance remain efficiently computable under the same type compression.

The weakest point is that Theorem 5 is an optimality/correctness theorem, not an NP-hardness or explicit polynomial-time complexity theorem. Moreover, the paper already proves the corresponding continuum theory in its main sections. Thus this is a defensible high-multiplicity mirror of the discrete appendix, but a modest Class-A boundary case rather than a new complexity landscape. If the anchor rule is restricted to named complexity classifications only, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor; Theorem 5 is the strongest available rescue because it is the correctness theorem for a computed allocation rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with the anchor itself: Theorem 5 is not a computational-complexity result. It proves a welfare characterization—allocations implementing \(A^\ast\) are optimal—but gives no decision problem, encoding, running-time bound, approximation guarantee, or parameterized classification. The paper’s other theorems likewise concern optimality, stability, dominance, and efficiency, not computational complexity. Under ChoCo’s gate, there is therefore no named computational result to mirror.

The proposed high-multiplicity formulation is mathematically legitimate, but it is not actually Theorem 5’s high-multiplicity version. Appendix E has individually listed agents with arbitrary scores and explicitly assumes that no two scores coincide. Its priority rule uses discrete marginal increments,
\[ h(s)+u_m(y)-u_m(y-1), \]
whereas the proposed mass model uses derivatives or subgradients,
\[ h(s)+\partial u_m(x_m). \]
That is the natural continuum model from Sections 3–4, not a computational consequence of Appendix E. Indeed, the paper already states the corresponding continuum rule in Theorem 1.

Nor does this version expose the kind of computational structure ChoCo is meant to study. With an explicit finite type set \(T\), rational scores, and piecewise-linear concave \(u_m\), the proposed problem is simply a compact linear program in the variables \(a_t\) and \(x_m\). There is no implicit exponential type space, pricing problem, or population-driven combinatorial barrier. If \(h\) and \(u_m\) retain the paper’s arbitrary continuous-function formulation, then the computational problem lacks a finite input representation. Thus the best-typed version is routine LP, while the literal version has no well-defined complexity model.

The stronger alternatives do not rescue the anchor. One could ask for computation of the continuum stable matching from Theorem 3, or of the dominant APM outcome in Theorem 4, but those are already the paper’s own continuum model and still lack a stated computational theorem. A finite-support reformulation would be a new algorithmic paper, not a mirror of a named result here. Proposition 7 similarly gives a small concave allocation problem, not a complexity claim.

The honest limitation is that school admissions and medical allocation can certainly have sensible high-multiplicity regimes. The objection is not that masses are meaningless, identities matter, or the continuous model degenerates. A coherent continuous optimization paper could be built from this material. The stronger negative claim is narrower: this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and its most plausible proposed mirror either restates the paper’s existing continuum theory or reduces immediately to a standard compact LP. Treating that as a worthwhile ChoCo mirror would require inventing the computational contribution rather than extracting it from the paper.
