What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is an unanchored one: the paper has a natural population model, but no named computational result for it to mirror. Theorems 1–4 are proved in the paper and characterize indicators by axioms: Theorem 1 characterizes the subscriber-proportional indicator using composition and non-manipulability; Theorem 2 characterizes the proportional indicator using sharing proofness and non-manipulability; Theorem 3 characterizes the uniform indicator using symmetry, homogeneity, and consumption sensitivity; and Theorem 4 characterizes the subscriber-uniform indicator using composition, nullity, and strong symmetry. Lemmas 1–6 give comparisons and preference conditions for those indicators. None asserts a complexity classification or algorithmic result. The characterizations are therefore not eligible anchors under the requested standard, and continuizing them as axiomatic questions would be outside ChoCo’s scope.

There is still a plausible continuous-population object in the paper’s underlying model. A type could be a subscriber’s complete profile \(t=(p_t,c_{1t},\ldots,c_{mt})\): subscription price plus consumption of each of the \(m\) services. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of subscribers of type \(t\), with finite support of size \(\tau\). This is sensible in a large platform with many subscribers but relatively few repeated price-and-usage profiles. The paper’s Twitch example has millions of viewers and only 19 streamers, though it does not establish that viewers have a small number of distinct complete profiles.

For instance, a Continuous User-Centric Attribution Evaluation problem could take rational \(\mu_t,p_t,c_{it}\) as input and ask for the per-capita revenue allocation vector
\[ \rho_i=\sum_t \mu_t p_t\frac{c_{it}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{jt}} \]
for each service \(i\), assuming each type has positive total consumption. The output is the allocation; for a population of size \(n\), multiply it by \(n\). This preserves what the subscriber-proportional indicator measures, and it has a straightforward polynomial-time evaluation in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. Theorem 1 supplies a nearby named *axiomatic* result—the subscriber-proportional indicator is uniquely characterized by composition and non-manipulability—but it does not establish that computational result. So this is a possible new task suggested by the model, not a continuous problem anchored in a computational theorem of the paper.

The weakest point is that exact profiles include the full consumption vector, so the number of distinct types \(\tau\) could be close to the number of subscribers. Grouping merely similar profiles would change the model, and the paper provides no basis for assuming that approximation. More fundamentally, the proposed evaluation problem is easy and is not a hardness question whose difficulty might dissolve under continuization. Thus there is a defensible population mirror of the attribution model, but no qualifying computational anchor—and no strong ChoCo case—here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–4 characterize indicators by axioms; Lemmas 1–6 compare indicators and their effects. None poses an algorithmic problem or claims a complexity bound. In particular, Theorem 1 is a uniqueness result about composition and non-manipulability, not a result about computing an allocation.

The proposed user-centric evaluation is a sensible population-level calculation, but it is a new computational task rather than a mirror of that theorem. Given finitely many subscriber types, it evaluates
\[ \rho_i=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t p_t\frac{c_{it}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{jt}} \]
by summing over the input support. The paper already gives this indicator as an explicit formula. Calling its evaluation a computational problem does not supply the missing complexity question.

A stronger type-space choice makes the population story more plausible: for the subscriber-uniform indicator, a type can be a set \(A_t\) of services consumed, together with a price from a fixed finite set. Many subscribers could share such a type. Theorem 4 characterizes that indicator, and its population calculation is simply
\[ \rho_i=\sum_{t:\,i\in A_t}\mu_t\frac{p_t}{|A_t|}. \]
This is a credible high-multiplicity regime, but it remains direct aggregation. For the richer user-centric indicator, exact consumption vectors are part of the type, so viewing-time differences can make the support nearly as large as the audience. A continuous distribution over arbitrary vectors would require its own finite encoding before it became a computational input.

To get a more substantial algorithmic question, one would have to add a decision or optimization task—such as selecting an indicator under extra constraints or optimizing revenue allocation. That could be worthwhile research, but it would add a problem the paper does not study. So the case against a worthwhile *mirror of this paper* is strong: its contribution is axiomatic characterization, not computation over a population. The universal claim is less secure: repeated subscriber profiles are plausible, and a neighboring computational problem could be developed. The paper itself, though, gives no computational anchor for it.
