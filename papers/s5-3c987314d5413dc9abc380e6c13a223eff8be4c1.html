The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(M\), \(\tau\) bidder types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational valuations \(v_t(S)\), bundle-size bound \(C\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), does the mass-based CCA achieve either \(R=\Omega(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu/(C^2\log(2+\tau)\log^2 m))\) or \(W=\Omega(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu/\log(2+\tau))\)? If not, what dependence on the smallest positive type mass or its bit length is necessary?

The model it lives in

Each type demands a utility-maximizing integral bundle at each clock round; prices rise in proportion to the mass demanding each item. Historical bids are allocated by choosing masses \(x_{t,r}\ge0\) to maximize revenue, subject to \(\sum_r x_{t,r}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,r:j\in S_t^r}x_{t,r}\le q_j\). Compare the resulting revenue and welfare with \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\), the maximum welfare attainable with those per-capita supplies and integral bundles.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 7's welfare guarantee for bounded-size bids and general valuations. It leaves the paper's lower-bound constructions, unit-demand analysis, and bad examples for other price increments or stopping rules outside this anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the welfare guarantee depend on \(\tau\) alone, or must it also depend on the smallest positive type mass or its encoding length?
  • Does the almost-everywhere version of the CCA stopping condition yield the same welfare guarantee when type mass can be split among historical bids?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s welfare guarantee for the CCA. This is a boundary case for ChoCo: the paper contains no numbered P/NP/FPT result. Its closest named computational anchor is Theorem 7, proved in the paper. It says that when every bidder’s bids are bundles of size at most \(C\), either CCA revenue is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/(480C^2\log n\log^2m)\), or CCA welfare is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/(24\log n)\). Thus the CCA welfare ratio is \(O(C^2\log n\log^2m)\). The theorem makes no restrictions on bidders’ valuation functions beyond the bundle-size limit.

I would name the mirror Continuous-Population CCA Welfare. Its instance is a finite item set \(M\), a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) bidder types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational per-capita item supplies \(q_j\), and a rational valuation \(v_t(S)\) for each type and bundle, with bids limited to \(|S|\le C\). A type includes its complete valuation and tie-breaking behavior, so its agents are indistinguishable for everything the auction uses.

At each clock round, all mass of type \(t\) demands a utility-maximizing bundle \(S_t^r\). Prices rise according to per-capita demand. The auction’s historical bids are allocated by the continuous counterpart of CCA’s revenue maximization: choose \(x_{t,r}\ge0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned its round-\(r\) bundle, to maximize total bid revenue, subject to \(\sum_r x_{t,r}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,r:j\in S_t^r}x_{t,r}\le q_j\). Each assigned unit of mass still receives an integral bundle; only the amount of mass assigned to a bundle can be fractional. Stop when current demand fits supply and the revenue-maximizing historical allocation satisfies the paper’s no-conflict condition, interpreted almost everywhere over agents. Compare its welfare and revenue with \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\), the maximum-welfare mass allocation under those same supplies and integral bundles.

The question is whether this auction always achieves a support-based analogue of Theorem 7: either \(R_{\mathrm{CCA}}=\Omega(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu/(C^2\log\tau\log^2m))\), or \(W_{\mathrm{CCA}}=\Omega(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu/\log\tau)\). A solution would be a proof of that guarantee or a counterexample showing which additional parameter—perhaps the smallest positive type mass or its bit length—is needed. I expect an A-like outcome: a meaningful guarantee should survive for a finite set of aggregate bidder trajectories, possibly with dependence on mass encoding as well as \(\tau\). This is a prediction about welfare analysis, not a claim that the problem is in P.

A plausible regime is a large marketplace for standardized regional infrastructure or spectrum packages, with many small operators whose valuations fall into a few coverage and technology profiles. There, the number of participants can greatly exceed the number of complete bidder types. Per-capita supplies represent repeated or scaled-up regional markets. The clock, package bids, and revenue-maximizing allocation remain recognizable CCA features, so the authors could plausibly accept this as their welfare question in a high-multiplicity regime.

My weakest point is that the mirror needs a material model co-modification: supplies scale with population, and price increments are interpreted per capita. A single spectrum auction with a fixed number of indivisible licences and a few large firms does not naturally have this limit. Also, Theorem 7 is an economic performance result, not a complexity classification; the paper does not provide a named hardness result that this mirror could test for dissolution. This case is strongest as a mirror of the paper’s CCA welfare analysis, and weaker as an example of ChoCo’s complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 7 is not a computational-complexity result in ChoCo’s sense. It bounds the CCA’s welfare relative to optimal welfare; it gives no running time, complexity class, or hardness result for a computational problem. The paper calls winner determination notoriously hard to approximate, but treats its practical computation as a separate implementation issue. Replacing \(n\) by support size \(\tau\) would pose an interesting question about a continuum auction’s economic performance. It would not continue the paper’s complexity analysis, because the paper has no such analysis to continue.

A stronger computational mirror might ask how hard it is to run the CCA or choose its final allocation when bidders are represented by types and masses. But that is a new target, rather than a continuous version of Theorem 7. Given a finite auction history, the proposed mass allocation has variables for type-round pairs and constraints for type mass and item supply. The clock process that generates that history, meanwhile, requires choosing how masses affect price increments and how supply scales. Those choices may define a coherent mechanism, but the resulting computational question is not anchored by a result in this paper.

The proposed replicated-market scenario is plausible, and I would not reject it just because real spectrum auctions have a fixed number of licences. Scaling supply is an allowed co-modification here. Still, it changes the setting enough that the mirror’s strongest justification is a new welfare analysis for a replicated CCA, rather than a computational mirror of the paper’s result. Keeping licence supply fixed instead makes per-capita supply vanish as the population grows, so a nondegenerate limit would need a different normalization.

So I can make a strong case that this is a poor fit for ChoCo’s computational programme, but not an airtight case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The support-based welfare question is well-posed, and an author might recognize it as a useful auction-theory question. The negative case is weakest precisely there: it argues against the programme fit, not against the mathematical or economic value of studying that model.
