Why no mirror

Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 provide named computational guarantees about the finite-agent switch process, so bit (a) holds. The proposed mass-template stability question is precise, but it depends on exact type-pair exchangeability, a coalition-size cap, and a chosen mass update convention that the spatial model does not establish. Whether that capped, homogeneous network remains a problem the authors would recognize as theirs is the unresolved point.

fails bit b-changes — the continuum limit changes the question

Judge caveat

It is unsettled whether an exchangeable, capped RSU network with exact type-pair traffic and contact rates is a credible instance regime for this spatial coalition game; repeated road layouts alone do not ensure that symmetry. A concrete network family where those rates are uniform across each type pair and the cap is operationally justified would settle whether the Proposition 1 mass-stability question is a recognizable mirror.

What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers mass analogues of the coalition stability and switching results; it leaves the revenue simulations, exhaustive-search comparison, and periodic adaptation results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact capped-template stability problem be solved in polynomial time in the explicit support size and input bit length when \(B\) is part of the input?
  • Does a maximal-batch mass-switch process over these templates and history states always terminate at a mass-Nash-stable partition?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. It says that every final partition produced by the coalition-formation stage is Nash-stable, and therefore individually stable. Theorem 1, also proved here, is the natural dynamic companion: from any initial coalition structure, the proposed sequence of switch operations converges to a final partition. Neither result classifies a problem as NP-hard, in P, or in another standard complexity class; the paper has no named hardness result to anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be a large, planned roadside network built from many repeated local road-cell designs. The agents remain the RSUs, not the vehicles. A type must specify an RSU’s traffic loads, packet volume, coordination cost, and its interaction rates with each other type. For the mirror to work, RSUs of the same type must be interchangeable, including in their pairwise V2V contact rates. That is plausible on a sufficiently regular road grid using the paper’s average traffic and meeting rates, but it is a real assumption about the network. The regime would have \(N\) large and the number \(\tau\) of supported RSU types modest.

Here is the model I would hand to a prover. Let \(A\) be the finite set of base RSU types, with rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\). Each RSU still joins an ordinary finite coalition and chooses an ordinary data class; only the population of RSUs is continuous. Impose a fixed coalition-size cap \(B\), representing a local coordination limit. A coalition template \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{A}\), with \(1\le |q|\le B\), records how many RSUs of each type it contains. Let \(\lambda_q\) be the number of coalitions with template \(q\), divided by total RSU population. Feasibility means \(\sum_q q_a\lambda_q=\mu_a\) for each \(a\).

Compute \(u(q)\) by applying the paper’s revenue maximization to a coalition with composition \(q\), using rational type-pair traffic and V2V contact rates and optimizing over the members’ data-class choices. Set \(v(q)=u(q)-c(q)\), with the paper’s coordination cost, and let \(v_a=v(\{a\})\). The paper’s egalitarian payoff rule then gives each type-\(a\) member of \(q\) payoff
\[ \phi_a(q)=v_a+\frac{v(q)-\sum_{b\in A}q_bv_b}{|q|}. \]
To preserve the algorithm’s anti-revisit rule, attach to each RSU a history \(H\) of the coalition templates it has previously left. The possible histories are finite under the cap \(B\), so they can be tracked as state types.

Continuous RSU Nash-Stability Certification (mirror of Proposition 1): Given the rational type data and a feasible mass partition \(\lambda\), including the current history states, determine whether it is Nash-stable. A certificate of “no” is a positive-mass source cohort of type \(a\) in template \(q\) and a present destination template \(p\) (or the empty destination) such that the mover has not previously left \(p+e_a\), the move fits within \(B\), \(\phi_a(p+e_a)>\phi_a(q)\), and every incumbent in \(p\) weakly prefers \(p+e_a\) to \(p\). A certificate of “yes” is that no such positive-mass switch exists. This captures the paper’s Nash-stability condition at population scale: a deviation matters when it is available to a positive fraction of interchangeable RSUs. I expect this checking problem to be tractable once coalition values are computed: it checks the finitely many supported source and destination templates and types. Computing all values from the raw data is finite and parameterized by \(B\), \(\tau\), and the number of data classes.

Continuous RSU Mass-Switch Convergence (mirror of Theorem 1): Given the same type data and an initial mass partition with all histories empty, determine whether every fair sequence of admissible mass switches terminates after finitely many operations at a Nash-stable mass partition. One operation batches the same individual switch across a maximal feasible amount of repeated source and destination coalition instances; this represents many indistinguishable RSUs making the paper’s one-agent move. A “yes” solution gives a finite termination bound, and a “no” solution gives an infinite fair sequence. I expect a continuum-specific obstacle here: the paper’s termination proof uses the finite number of discrete partitions of an \(N\)-agent set, while \(\lambda\) can take infinitely many distinct rational values even with a finite set of templates. The history rule limits each agent’s revisits, but it does not immediately bound the number of aggregate mass moves. A finite potential or a counterexample is a further question; I would not claim either from this paper.

This mirror covers the coalition-formation and stability results, not the simulated revenue improvements, the exhaustive-search comparison with an optimal partition, or the periodic adaptation to changing traffic. The society being continuized is the RSU population; neither continuous data outcomes nor time or uncertainty is the goal.

My weakest point is the regime. The paper’s examples use small networks, and actual RSUs can have distinctive locations and pairwise traffic relationships. The mirror needs many repeated RSUs, a type-pair interaction table, and a coalition-size cap that the paper does not impose. If those assumptions erase the meaningful geography or coalition choices, the authors may reject it. But in a regular, high-multiplicity deployment, the question remains recognizably theirs: do RSUs form stable coalitions, and does the distributed switching process reach one?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither anchor is a complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to chart. Theorem 1 proves that the paper’s switch process terminates; Proposition 1 says its terminal partition is Nash-stable. Their proofs rely on a finite set of labelled partitions and the agents’ histories. The paper gives a cost for checking a candidate switch, but no complexity classification for finding a stable partition or for the full process. A continuous question about those guarantees would therefore need a new computational problem and a specified population dynamic.

The type reduction is particularly strained here. An RSU’s payoff depends on its links to particular other RSUs: \(K_{ij}\), \(d_{ij}\), and \(m_{ij}(d_{ij})\) determine the revenue of a coalition. A type-pair table preserves those payoffs only if interactions are uniform across every pair of RSUs of the corresponding types. In a spatial network, coalition value depends on which RSUs are grouped together, not just how many of each type are present. Averaging links to get a finite type table can change the coalition values, switch incentives, and incumbent-consent checks the paper studies. Exact compression would require highly symmetric interaction classes; ordinary repetition of road layouts does not suffice when relative position still matters.

The proponent’s fixed coalition-size cap \(B\) makes template enumeration finite, but the paper imposes no such cap. Its increasing coordination cost can discourage large coalitions without ruling them out. Dropping the cap to retain the paper’s model leaves unbounded coalition templates. There is also a history issue: the algorithm records which *identified coalition* an RSU has left. Recording only a coalition’s type composition merges distinct coalitions and changes which moves are allowed. Keeping identities preserves the paper’s state but defeats the proposed finite type-state compression, except under strong symmetry assumptions.

For Proposition 1, a mass version of “no profitable switch exists” is a reasonable stability question, but it is not what the proposition proves: the proposition certifies the output of a particular finite-agent process. Asking whether a given mass partition is stable replaces that output guarantee with a new decision problem. Asking for a stable mass partition is a stronger new search problem. Either might be interesting, but the continuous population alone does not supply a faithful finite-type input when link identities matter.

For Theorem 1, the difficulty is sharper. A single RSU’s move changes a finite partition, while its mass tends to zero in a continuum model. If switches are treated as individual moves, the aggregate state may not change; if they are batched into positive-mass moves, that is a different update rule. The proposed maximal batching convention is one possible choice, not a limit forced by the paper’s random sequential updates. A flow or rate-based model could ask whether the population approaches a stable distribution, but that replaces finite-step termination with a new dynamical question whose timing and fairness rules must be chosen.

The case against is weakest at exactly the point the proponent identifies: a deliberately homogeneous, repeated RSU environment could make type-pair interactions exact and support a recognizable mass version of stability and switching. I would not claim that no conceivable scenario can do so. But the paper itself gives little reason to expect that regime, and its named results do not define a complexity landscape to transfer. The strongest defensible negative verdict is that these two anchors do not yet justify ChoCo’s time: the plausible mirrors depend on added symmetry and update assumptions, while the faithful versions retain the paper’s labelled network and finite-agent dynamics.
