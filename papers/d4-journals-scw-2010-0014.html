The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of voting-rights types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive integer weights \(a_t\), and a rational quota per capita \(q\), form each admissible replicated weighted voting game with \(N\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\) and quota \(Nq\). Determine whether the type aggregates \(b_t(N)=\sum_{i\text{ of type }t}PBI_i^{(N)}\) converge as \(N\) grows through the specified admissible values and, if so, compute their exact limits.

The model it lives in

A society is specified by the type distribution \(\mu\), with each type carrying a common voting weight \(a_t\); the quota scales with population as \(Nq\). Replicate each type according to its mass, then return the limit vector of total normalized Banzhaf power by type.

The objection that survived

The type-level limit may depend on the admissible replication sequence and quota lattice, so the tuple \((T,\mu,a,q)\) may not determine a canonical atomless Banzhaf index; the case specifies admissible replications but does not establish sequence independence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the Banzhaf calculation in Theorem 3.1 for weighted voting games and has a parallel Shapley–Shubik limit from Theorem 3.2; it leaves arbitrary monotone games, other power indices, and the paper’s broader power-profile enumeration aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which \((T,\mu,a,q)\) do the type aggregates converge, and can the limits depend on the admissible replication sequence?
  • Can the exact limit vector be computed efficiently as a function of the number of types and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror I see is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s weighted-voting calculations. Consider a large membership organization with a few voting-rights classes: many members may share the same vote weight, while the number of classes stays small. A type is a class with the same voting weight and voting rights; \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the membership. This makes the society continuous while keeping the paper’s central object—how much decision-making power voters have.

Take \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive integer weights \(a_t\), and a rational quota \(q\) per unit of population. The continuous input is \((T,\mu,a,q)\). Its finite approximations have \(N\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), and a coalition wins when its total weight is at least \(Nq\). Let \(N\) grow through admissible values for which the counts and quota are integral and the quota aligns with the weight lattice. The mass is thus the population share of each voting-rights class; the decision rule remains whether a proposal passes.

There is no optimization variable or objective here: this is a power-index calculation problem. For each finite approximation, aggregate each individual’s index by type. The lead question, Continuum-Type Banzhaf Limit, asks for the exact vector
\[ b_t=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i\text{ of type }t}PBI_i^{(N)}. \]
A solution gives the vector and proves the limits exist. Its anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. For an electorate of \(n\) voters with minimal winning coalitions \(V_1,\ldots,V_h\), it expresses the Banzhaf score of voter \(w\) as an alternating sum over nonempty subfamilies \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,h\}\): each term is \(2^{n-|\cup_{i\in I}V_i|}\) if \(w\in\cup_{i\in I}V_i\), and zero otherwise. This is a formula for calculating the index from the MWC set, not a claim of polynomial-time complexity.

A second, closely related question is Continuum-Type Shapley–Shubik Limit: on the same input and replication sequence, compute
\[ s_t=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i\text{ of type }t}SSI_i^{(N)}, \]
again giving the exact vector with a proof that the limits exist. Its anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper. It gives the individual Shapley–Shubik index as an alternating sum over the same MWC subfamilies, with each term \(1/|\cup_{i\in I}V_i|\) when that union contains \(w\), and zero otherwise. The Banzhaf question is my lead; the Shapley–Shubik question is a genuine companion, since the paper explicitly develops both measures.

These are credible mirrors because they retain the paper’s voting rules and quantities, while aggregating voters who are indistinguishable by the parameters the game uses. Weighted voting is a real part of the paper’s scope, illustrated by its EEC example, and large organizations with many members in a small number of voting-rights classes give a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The authors could reasonably recognize type-level power limits as a continuous-population extension of calculating individual power. The mirror covers their power-index calculations for this regime, not their broader ambition to enumerate every possible power profile of an arbitrary voting system.

I would initially expect the limit questions to be Class A candidates when \(\tau\) is fixed, using generating functions and asymptotic analysis of the type counts. Whether they admit algorithms polynomial in variable \(\tau\) and the binary input length is open; the paper itself establishes no such complexity bound. Natural follow-ups include convergence rates, quota regimes where limits change, and whether analogous limits exist for arbitrary monotone voting rules given through their minimal winning coalitions.

The weak point is that the paper measures an individual voter’s pivotality in a finite game, whereas the mirror asks for the limiting distribution of total power across growing voter classes. That aggregation and limiting operation can wash out finite-electorate effects, and the paper’s MWC formulas do not directly supply the limit. Still, the underlying questions remain about the very indices the authors compute, in a regime where type-level power is an intelligible quantity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s two named theorems are calculation identities, not complexity results. Theorem 3.1 expresses a finite electorate’s Banzhaf score by inclusion–exclusion over its minimal winning coalitions; Theorem 3.2 does the same for Shapley–Shubik. The paper explicitly says its aim is not to improve calculation time and that the method may take longer. So the proposed limits do not continue a complexity question the paper established: they introduce a new asymptotic power-index problem.

For Banzhaf, there is a deeper mismatch. A voter’s decisiveness is a one-person boundary event. In an atomless society, adding or removing one voter changes no coalition mass, so direct decisiveness vanishes; normalizing those zero scores does not define a type-level index. Cloning voters repairs this, but then the proposed quantity is driven by finite-size boundary fluctuations. For type \(t\), the finite-game swing probability is a probability that the other voters’ weight lands in a narrow interval just below quota. Under random coalitions, the type masses concentrate around half their totals: at a central quota the relevant swings come from shrinking fluctuations, and away from it they come from rare deviations. The continuous mass distribution alone does not encode those events. Fixing a replication and lattice convention, as the proponent does, can make the limit well-posed; it also makes the question an asymptotic analysis of finite games, rather than a power index defined on the continuous society. That is the strongest attack on the Banzhaf anchor, but it does not show that the resulting asymptotic question is uninteresting.

Shapley–Shubik has a similar problem with a more visible degeneration. Its finite-game meaning uses a uniformly random ordering and asks which individual crosses quota. An atomless society has no canonical uniform random ordering of its members, and a single crossing individual has zero mass. In a clone limit, the aggregate type value is determined by the local overshoot at the quota crossing; for fixed positive weights and an interior quota, that local law is typically size-biased by weight. It discards most of the finite-game structure the theorem calculates. Recovering more detail requires retaining finite-size fluctuations, which again makes the mirror an asymptotic problem rather than a direct continuum calculation.

This does not support a confident universal negative. Many members of an organization sharing a voting weight are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and a type-level limit of Banzhaf power is a coherent question. I can argue that the two proposed mirrors fit ChoCo’s complexity programme poorly and that the Shapley–Shubik limit loses much of the finite game’s content. I cannot honestly rule out the Banzhaf clone-limit as a worthwhile research question the authors would recognize; it remains the negative case’s weak point.
