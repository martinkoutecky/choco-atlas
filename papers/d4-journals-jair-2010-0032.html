The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of requirement types \(t\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), type-specific GAI valuations \(u_t(\theta)=\sum_r f_{t,r}(\theta_r)\), common cluster domains and current cluster prices \(p_r(\alpha)\), and a rational tolerance \(\epsilon\), compute for each type the set \(M_{t,r}=\{\alpha:\exists\theta,\ \theta_r=\alpha,\ u_t(\theta)-\sum_r p_r(\theta_r)\ge\max_{\theta'}(u_t(\theta')-\sum_r p_r(\theta'))-\epsilon\}\), and aggregate possible demand as \(\nu_{r,\alpha}=\sum_{t:\alpha\in M_{t,r}}\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support of requirement types, each with a GAI valuation over the same discrete configuration space and cluster structure. At fixed cluster prices, compute each type's near-optimal cluster assignments and the mass of types for which each assignment is possible; this is an aggregate demand correspondence, not a capacity-constrained allocation.

The objection that survived

The aggregated masses count types with a possible near-optimal assignment, not allocated demand; the typewise calls are independent, and the proposed feasible allocation adds no coupling.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 15's buyer-preferred-set computation at fixed prices. It leaves the auction's allocation, welfare, and incentive results, including Theorems 10, 12, and 14, outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s buyer-demand calculation. Imagine a large annual procurement of standardized equipment for a university system or public agency. There may be millions of purchase units but relatively few distinct requirement profiles. A type is a complete GAI valuation description for one unit, including its attribute domains, GAI clusters, and local value tables. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) supported requirement types. Each unit still receives an integral configuration; mass records how much of the cohort receives each configuration.

At a given auction round, take the paper’s cluster prices \(p_r(\alpha)\) and discount \(\Delta\) as given. For type \(t\), let \(u_t(\theta)=\sum_r f_{t,r}(\theta_r)\), and let \(\pi_t(\theta)=u_t(\theta)-(\sum_r p_r(\theta_r)-\Delta)\). The continuous question is to compute the full \(\epsilon\)-near-optimal demand correspondence for every type:
\[ E_t^\epsilon=\{\theta:\pi_t(\theta)\ge \max_{\theta'}\pi_t(\theta')-\epsilon\},\qquad M_{t,r}=\{\alpha:\exists\theta\in E_t^\epsilon,\ \theta_r=\alpha\}. \]
It also returns the aggregate mass \(\nu_{r,\alpha}=\sum_{t:\alpha\in M_{t,r}}\mu_t\) whose near-optimal configurations include cluster assignment \(\alpha\), and a feasible mass allocation \(y_{t,\theta}\) with \(\sum_\theta y_{t,\theta}=\mu_t\), supported on \(E_t^\epsilon\). Thus the population is continuous while each unit’s bundle remains discrete.

My anchor is Theorem 15, proved in this paper. It states that the buyer-preferred set \(M_t\) can be computed in time \(O(g|\mathcal I|^2)\), and that the total time for this computation throughout the auction is \(O(g|\mathcal I|(|\mathcal I|+T))\). Here \(g\) is the number of GAI elements, \(\mathcal I\) is the collection of sub-configurations, and \(T\) is the number of Phase A rounds. The bound depends on the compact cluster representation rather than enumerating the full configuration space. Applying the per-round calculation independently to the \(\tau\) types gives \(O(\tau g|\mathcal I|^2)\) time for the typewise correspondences, plus the cost of aggregating their binary-rational masses. With bounded attribute domains and bounded maximum cluster size, this is polynomial in the number of attributes, supported types, and input bit length.

I expect this named mirror problem, Continuous GAI Near-Optimal Demand, to be tractable in that bounded-cluster regime. It keeps the paper’s underlying question—what configurations are close to maximizing willingness-to-pay minus the current cluster-structured price—and extends its preferred-set output to a high-multiplicity population. The authors would likely recognize that as a direct extension of their demand oracle. A next question is whether a full market with supplier-type capacities can clear these typewise demands efficiently, perhaps using a configuration LP and GAI pricing oracles. Whether such an extended auction converges with analogous welfare and incentive guarantees is another question; Theorem 15 alone does not answer it.

This mirror covers the preferred-set computation, not the paper’s entire single-buyer auction or its efficiency and equilibrium theorems. The paper provides no comparable named NP-hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question. My weakest point is that the paper models one buyer procuring one good, whereas this mirror has many procurement units and a distribution of buyer types. That is a meaningful repeated-procurement extension, but it is still a co-modification of the market model. The positive case rests on the shared GAI demand problem and the paper’s explicit computation of its near-optimal cluster choices, not on a claim that the paper already studies continuous populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the Theorem 15 mirror is that it lifts a per-buyer demand calculation, rather than the auction’s allocation problem. The paper has one buyer choosing one complete configuration and competing sellers. The theorem computes buyer-preferred sub-configurations at given prices. Treating purchase units as buyer types changes that model into a population of buyers.

The proposed outputs also separate into two different tasks. \(M_{t,r}\) records which cluster assignments appear in some near-optimal configuration; \(\nu_{r,\alpha}\) therefore counts *possible* demand and can count the same type’s mass for several assignments. It is not an allocation. The \(y_{t,\theta}\) constraints do define a feasible mass allocation, but with no supplier capacities or other coupling, they form independent simplices: any near-optimal configuration can receive each type’s mass. Theorem 15 supplies the substantive computation; the population adds weighted aggregation.

A realistic large procurement portfolio makes the weakness clearer. If units have interdependent values, the paper’s single-configuration GAI model does not describe the portfolio without adding cross-unit structure. If units are independent, the proposed calculation is a batch of typewise calls, running in \(O(\tau g|\mathcal I|^2)\), followed by rational weighted sums. Adding capacities and market clearing could make the population interact, but that would introduce a larger allocation problem beyond Theorem 15’s result.

That critique limits the mirror’s scope and likely its novelty. It does not defeat the anchor under the programme’s stated standard. A distribution over repeated requirement profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; the mass-weighted demand correspondence is well-defined, and per-type computation with aggregate output is still a population-level computational question. The units can receive integral configurations while their type-level averages are fractional. Nor does this paper’s existing auction count as a continuous-population treatment.

So the best negative case is weak: it warns that the proponent mirrors a demand subroutine, not the auction’s welfare or incentive guarantees. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile mirror survives. Theorem 15 remains a credible anchor, though the proposed question should be described narrowly as aggregate near-optimal demand at fixed prices.
