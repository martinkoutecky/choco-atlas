Why no mirror

The paper’s named propositions establish statistical identification, not a computational result eligible to anchor a mirror. What the mirror covers

No continuous computational mirror is anchored; Propositions 3.4, 3.6, and 3.7 and Appendix results B.6 and B.7 establish identification, while the estimation and counterfactual computations are not named complexity results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest affirmative point is that this paper already treats the population as continuous: it models a measure \(Q\) of students, finitely many colleges, and capacities measured as fractions of the student population. A plausible large-market regime would be repeated admissions cohorts with many students sharing complete matching-relevant profiles. A type could encode a student’s ranking of colleges and how each college ranks that student; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of students of type \(t\). One could ask for an allocation \(x_{tc}\) of each type’s mass that respects capacities and individual rationality and has no blocking pair. That is a recognizable continuous matching problem, though it is close to the paper’s existing continuum model.

I cannot make that the required anchored case. The paper has no named computational result asserting a complexity or algorithmic bound. Propositions 3.4, 3.6, and 3.7, proved in the paper, establish identification results; Appendix results B.6 and B.7 do so for a nonseparable model. They do not establish that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise tractable. The Gibbs sampler and Gale–Shapley computations are described as methods, but are not named computational results.

So the paper offers strong evidence that a high-multiplicity matching regime is sensible, but no eligible anchor from which to derive a continuous computational question under your criteria. The weakest point is also the central one: a stable-allocation problem over type masses would be natural, but the paper’s stated results concern identification and estimation, not the computational complexity of finding that allocation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 3.4, 3.6, and 3.7 establish identification: they say which utility derivatives, cutoffs, and distributions can be recovered under stated conditions. Their constructive steps solve systems of equations, but the paper gives no computational problem with an input encoding and complexity bound. The Gibbs sampler and deferred-acceptance simulations are methods used in estimation and counterfactuals, not formal results about worst-case complexity. So none supplies the required anchor.

The proposed stable-allocation problem is the strongest computational alternative, but it changes the paper’s question. The paper assumes a stable matching is observed and asks whether preferences can be recovered from it. Computing a stable allocation from known preferences reverses that direction. It is also already a continuum-population model: students are a measure space, capacities are population fractions, and matching outcomes are characterized by cutoffs. A finite distribution over complete student and school preference types could make a coherent computational problem, but it would study the computation of an equilibrium object in a model the paper already formulates—not the continuous mirror of one of its identification results.

A more faithful attempt would ask whether preferences are identified from a finite-support, rational-mass matching instance, or ask for the identified set. But the paper’s identification results rely on variation over continuous covariate supports, derivatives of conditional match probabilities, and—in Proposition 3.6—large-support arguments. Replacing those with a finite table changes the identification problem; retaining the original objects leaves function-valued distributions and primitives without the finite encoding needed for a standard complexity question. Either way, one must invent a new computational task rather than continuize a result in the paper.

The paper’s continuum setting and its large-market matching application do show that high-multiplicity matching is a sensible regime. That is evidence in favor of the regime, not a novelty collision or an objection. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no eligible computational result, and its most natural algorithmic problem is already posed inside its continuum model but is not the paper’s contribution. I cannot rule out a worthwhile separate complexity project on matching with finitely many types; the case against is strongest for calling that project a continuous mirror of this paper.
