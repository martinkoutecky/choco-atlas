The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an admissible positive case under the anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Its numbered results are proved here, but none classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest anchors are analytic. Theorem 1 proves that, under assumptions (A1)–(A4), a finite jury with \(n\) jurors and a rule requiring \(k\) votes to convict has a unique symmetric, responsive cutoff equilibrium among cutoff strategies. If the likelihood ratio is strictly decreasing at the equilibrium cutoff, that equilibrium is also unique almost everywhere among all symmetric, responsive equilibria, including mixed strategies. Theorem 2 gives a sufficient condition on the ratio of the innocent and guilty hazard rates under which unanimity has a higher ratio of false convictions to correct acquittals than every less demanding voting rule. Theorem 3 proves that, when the hazard-rate ratio is bounded above and away from zero, the sum of the two error probabilities stays bounded away from zero under unanimity, while it tends to zero for every fixed quota \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). These are substantial results, but they concern equilibrium characterization, comparative error rates, and asymptotics—not computational complexity.

There is a real population-continuum angle nearby: large juries with a common objective and private evidence signals drawn from state-dependent distributions. The paper’s continuous signals do not already make its population continuous; the jury still has finite size \(n\). So this is not a prior-art collision on that ground. But a direct continuum of voters undermines the game the paper studies: an infinitesimal juror cannot change whether the mass of conviction votes reaches the quota. The pivotal-vote incentives that produce the cutoff equilibrium disappear, leaving the strategic question substantially changed. And in the paper’s continuous-signal model, exact signal realizations are typically all distinct, so a fixed, small set of high-multiplicity types is not built into its setup.

One could ask for an algorithm that computes Theorem 1’s cutoff from encoded signal distributions. That would be a computational question about the paper’s finite-jury model, not a continuous-population mirror; Theorem 1 also does not itself assert an algorithmic complexity bound. I therefore can’t supply a defensible prover-ready continuous problem per qualifying anchor. The strongest positive observation is that large juries provide a plausible setting for population continuization. The weakest point—and the decisive one for this assignment—is that the paper offers no required computational anchor, while its strategic mechanism relies on finite-agent pivotality.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a mirror starts at the anchor rule: none of Theorems 1–3 states a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes a symmetric equilibrium, Theorem 2 gives a hazard-rate condition for a comparative error result, and Theorem 3 proves an asymptotic jury theorem. None defines a computational problem or classifies its complexity. Computing Theorem 1’s cutoff from encoded distributions would be a plausible algorithmic question, but it keeps the finite jury size \(n\); it supplies no continuous-population mirror.

The most promising setting is a large jury, and the paper itself makes that setting plausible. Its continuous signals do not make its population continuous: there are still \(n\) jurors, each with a private signal. More fundamentally, an individual juror’s action matters through the chance of being pivotal. In an atomless population, one juror has zero mass and cannot change whether the vote share reaches the quota. Individual best responses then cease to select the cutoff equilibrium of Theorem 1. Treating positive-mass cohorts as pivotal players could restore strategic influence, but it changes the players from individual jurors to blocs. Binning signals has a related problem: under a strict monotone likelihood ratio, jurors with different signals are not identical in the way the equilibrium analysis uses them.

That pivotality problem also defeats the strongest versions of the other two anchors. Theorem 2 compares finite-jury error probabilities generated by pivotal voting; replacing the jury with a deterministic mass vote changes the object being compared. Theorem 3 is the closest approach to a population limit, but it is an analytic asymptotic result, not a computational result about a society represented by type masses. Computing a convergence rate or optimizing a quota could be worthwhile work, but it would be a new statistical or algorithmic question rather than a mirror of the theorem.

The negative case is strongest as a recommendation about this paper: it offers no computational theorem to mirror, and its central strategic mechanism is singular at the atomless limit. Its weaker point is the universal claim. A carefully chosen algorithmic extension might be valuable; the evidence here supports saying that it would be an extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
