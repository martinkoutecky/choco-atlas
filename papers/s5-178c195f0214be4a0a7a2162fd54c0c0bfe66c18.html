The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest case with the required computational anchor: this paper has no numbered result asserting a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

Its three numbered theorems are existence results, all proved in the paper. Theorem 1 says that under the restrictive non-crossing condition, the steady bargaining set contains an efficient payoff profile for at least one coalition structure. Theorem 2 gives the corresponding existence claim for the strong steady bargaining set, adding strict \(x\)-comprehensiveness. Theorem 3 says the precise bargaining set contains at least one payoff profile for at least one coalition structure; it does not guarantee efficiency. The unnumbered corollary extends nonemptiness to the Mas-Colell and natural bargaining sets. None supplies an algorithm or a complexity bound. The appendix’s finite-stage construction does not establish one: the paper specifies no computational input model or runtime.

There is a plausible *population-continuum* angle: for example, a large labor market with workers and employers grouped into complete preference-and-technology types, where mass is each type’s share and the outcome assigns agents to coalitions and payoffs. One could ask whether an efficient, bargaining-stable measurable coalition formation exists. But that would be an analytic existence question, not a computational mirror of a named result in this paper. Nor do its real-valued payoff vectors make its population continuous: the player set remains finite. So I would not present that angle as an anchor or claim the authors’ computational question has been mirrored.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent offers no computational anchor. Theorem 1 establishes existence of an efficient outcome in the steady bargaining set under restrictive non-crossing; Theorem 2 adds strict \(x\)-comprehensiveness; Theorem 3 establishes nonemptiness of the precise bargaining set. None classifies a computational problem.

Theorem 3’s finite-stage construction is the strongest candidate for an algorithmic reading: each coalition can be selected at most once. But the proof does not give a computational input model or a runtime. Its steps require selecting a \(P\)-justified objection and reasoning about feasible payoff sets that may be arbitrary continuous sets. Restricting those sets to rational polyhedra and asking for an algorithm would create a new computational problem; the theorem itself supplies no complexity result to mirror. The fixed-point proofs of Theorems 1 and 2 likewise establish existence, not an algorithm.

The labor-market scenario is plausible as a high-multiplicity model: agents could be grouped by worker or employer type, with coalition feasibility depending on group composition. A continuous version would need payoff functions and measurable coalition partitions, or additional restrictions such as a common payoff per type to make the input finite. That could support a worthwhile computational project. It still would be a computationalization of an analytic existence theory, rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper also notes Mas-Colell’s atomless bargaining-set treatment; that is relevant analytic prior work, not a computational answer under ChoCo’s scope.

So the case against this paper as a mirror candidate is strong under the programme’s computational gate: there is no named computational result to anchor. The broader claim that no related continuous computational question could be worthwhile is weaker. A typed labor-market model could make sense, but it would start a new project rather than continue a computational one this paper formulated.
