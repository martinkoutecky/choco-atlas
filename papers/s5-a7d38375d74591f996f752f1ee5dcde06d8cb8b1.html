The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete ranking types, and a target \(d\), choose nonnegative masses \(x_\pi\) on complete manipulator rankings \(\pi\) with \(d\) first to minimize \(\sum_\pi x_\pi\), subject to \(\mathrm{sc}_\mu(c)+\sum_\pi x_\pi s(\pi,c)\leq \mathrm{sc}_\mu(d)+\sum_\pi x_\pi s(\pi,d)\) for every \(c\ne d\).

The model it lives in

The baseline society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types; each decision variable \(x_\pi\) is manipulator mass casting ranking \(\pi\), with the target \(d\) ranked first. Minimize total manipulator mass while making \(d\) a Borda winner.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.1 starts from a fixed finite \(n\times m\) score matrix and does not analyze minimum coalition size, so the proposed mass optimization extends the paper’s core task rather than directly continuing that theorem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers minimum-coalition Borda manipulation over a distributional electorate. It leaves aside the greedy algorithms’ finite-instance performance results, the integrality of the discrete coalition, and the paper’s empirical comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the worst-case integrality gap between the continuous optimum and the discrete minimum coalition size as a function of \(m\) and \(\tau\)?
  • Can the additive \(m-1\) bound from rounding a sparse continuous solution be improved?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result establishing that a manipulation problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Its best anchor for a continuous mirror is Theorem 3.1, proved here: if an \(n\times m\) matrix \(B\) contains each value from \(0\) through \(m-1\) exactly \(n\) times, then there is another matrix \(A\) with the same entries and the same column sums in which every row contains each value exactly once. The paper uses this structural result to turn assignments of Borda scores to candidates into valid manipulator votes. It is not a complexity theorem; if the anchor rule requires one, this paper has no eligible anchor.

The mirror I find convincing is Borda Mass Manipulation. The input is a baseline society \(\mu\), a rational distribution over ranking types with support size \(\tau\), and a target candidate \(d\). Mass is measured relative to one unit of baseline electorate. For each ranking \(\pi\) with \(d\) first, choose \(x_\pi\geq0\), the mass of manipulators who cast that ranking. Ask for a minimum-mass assignment such that \(d\) wins:

\[ \min \sum_{\pi:\,d\text{ first in }\pi} x_\pi \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathrm{sc}_\mu(c)+\sum_\pi x_\pi s(\pi,c) \leq \mathrm{sc}_\mu(d)+\sum_\pi x_\pi s(\pi,d) \quad\text{for every }c\ne d. \]

A solution is the minimum mass and a list of rankings with their assigned masses. Putting \(d\) first is without loss under Borda: moving \(d\) to the top improves its score against every other candidate.

This asks the paper’s underlying question—how large a coalition must be to make its preferred candidate win—while making coalition size continuous. It does not make outcomes fractional: every unit of manipulator mass still casts a complete ranking. The regime is a large online poll, association election, or similar vote with millions of members but comparatively few distinct rankings; the coordinating bloc’s members share the same objective and voting options. The relevant comparison is \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the electorate size.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. It is an LP with a variable for each ranking, but its dual pricing step optimizes a weighted Borda-score expression over rankings with \(d\) first. That is a weighted assignment problem, solvable in polynomial time. Thus the ellipsoid/separation approach gives an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. An optimal solution can be chosen with at most \(m-1\) positive ranking masses, since there are \(m-1\) winner constraints.

The high-multiplicity bridge is concrete. For a discrete election with \(N\) non-manipulators, set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Any discrete manipulation gives a feasible continuous solution, so the continuous optimum is a lower bound on the required coalition as a fraction of \(N\). Conversely, scale a sparse continuous solution by \(N\) and round each positive ranking mass up to an integer number of voters. Each added vote ranks \(d\) first and can only improve \(d\)’s margin, so this gives a discrete manipulation using at most \(N\) times the continuous optimum plus \(m-1\) voters. This makes the mirror useful for lower bounds and additive approximation, even when the exact discrete optimum still matters.

The weakest point is also the source of the tractability: fractional coalition mass relaxes the paper’s integer number of manipulators. Theorem 3.1 supports the aggregate score representation, but the paper does not establish that its discrete hardness dissolves in this relaxation. The next questions are whether the additive \(m-1\) rounding bound can be improved, and what the worst-case integrality gap is as a function of \(m\) and \(\tau\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The claimed anchor does not support the proposed mirror. Theorem 3.1 is a finite matrix-rearrangement lemma: it turns a multiset of scores with suitable column sums into valid manipulator ballots. It says nothing about the complexity of finding a minimum coalition. Its continuous analogue would be a routine fractional assignment or coupling statement, not a continuation of a computational result. The paper’s other named results likewise concern greedy-algorithm performance; its main comparison is empirical, tied to particular random election generators.

That is the strongest case against a *result-anchored* mirror, but it does not defeat the better version of the proposal. The paper defines a minimum-coalition Borda manipulation problem, and the proposed mass formulation is a coherent way to ask it for a society with many voters and few ballot types. The manipulators’ reports remain complete rankings; only the mass assigned to each ranking becomes divisible. A large poll or association election is a plausible regime for that model.

I therefore cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The anchor to Theorem 3.1 fails; the stronger mirror of the underlying manipulation problem survives. Calling that mirror unworthy would require an objection the brief rules out—such as dismissing it because its answer may be an LP or because the paper did not prove a complexity theorem. The negative case is weak at precisely this point.
