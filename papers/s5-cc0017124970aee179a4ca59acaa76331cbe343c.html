The continuous mirror question

Given finite item classes \(I\), bidder types \(\Theta\), rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\), rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_i\), and bundle bids \(b_\theta(S)\), compute an efficient mass allocation \(y^\star_{\theta,S}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_S y^\star_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S:i\in S}y^\star_{\theta,S}\le \sigma_i\). Then compute type- and package-symmetric payment rates \(p_{\theta,S}\) minimizing \(P=\sum_{\theta,S}p_{\theta,S}y^\star_{\theta,S}\), subject to no feasible coalition transfer \(r_{\theta,S,R}\) with \(0\le\sum_R r_{\theta,S,R}\le y^\star_{\theta,S}\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S,R:i\in R}r_{\theta,S,R}\le \sigma_i\) obtaining seller revenue above \(P\) while preserving each selected agent's utility \(b_\theta(S)-p_{\theta,S}\); among minimizers, return a bidder-Pareto-optimal payment schedule.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic high-multiplicity combinatorial auction with \(\mu_\theta\) mass of identical bidder types and per-capita item supplies \(\sigma_i\); \(y_{\theta,S}\) assigns mass to integral packages, \(p_{\theta,S}\) are payment rates, and \(r_{\theta,S,R}\) gives the coalition-separation LP. The objective is lexicographic minimization of total core payments followed by bidder-Pareto selection.

The objection that survived

The paper does not establish that type- and package-symmetric payment rates are the canonical limit of bidder-Pareto optimality; allowing within-type payment discrimination would change the payment space.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.2's core-payment constraint generation and conceptually supports Corollary 2.2's total-payment objective. It leaves the individual-deviation bound in Theorem 2.1, the collusion and Nash-bargaining propositions, and the empirical comparisons unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuous core-payment problem admit a polynomial-time separation oracle for additive, gross-substitute, or otherwise efficiently priceable bundle bids?
  • Can rational mass instances and finite replicated auctions be related by an exact or additive correspondence for payments and bidder-Pareto optimality?
  • For unrestricted or succinct XOR valuations, does computational hardness survive mass aggregation, or is the remaining difficulty only bundle pricing?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a mirror is qualified but real. The paper’s best anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper. It states that, if the separation value \(z(\pi^t)\) equals the previous restricted LP objective \(\theta^{t-1}\), then the solution of \(BPO_{t-1}\) is a bidder-Pareto-optimal core-payment vector. The proof establishes that the payments are unblocked, Pareto-efficient among core payments, and that the constraint-generation process terminates because only finitely many coalition constraints exist and the pay-as-bid vector remains feasible.

I would mirror it with the following problem.

Call it High-Multiplicity Core-Payment Selection, and treat this as the lead mirror. There are finitely many item classes \(I=[N]\), finitely many bidder types \(\Theta\), a rational population distribution \(\mu=(\mu_\theta)_{\theta\in\Theta}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_i\) for the item classes. A type \(\theta\) is a complete bundle-bid function \(b_\theta(S)\) for \(S\subseteq I\), together with any eligibility or payment constraints. Thus two bidders have the same type exactly when the auction uses them identically.

An allocation is a mass assignment \(y_{\theta,S}\ge 0\), where

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq I} y_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta \]

and

\[ \sum_{\theta}\sum_{S\ni i}y_{\theta,S}\le \sigma_i \]

for every item class \(i\). Each infinitesimal bidder still receives an integral package \(S\); only the mass of identical bidders receiving each package is divisible. The efficient allocation \(y^\star\) maximizes

\[ \sum_{\theta,S}b_\theta(S)y_{\theta,S}. \]

A payment schedule assigns a per-unit payment \(p_{\theta,S}\) to mass of type \(\theta\) receiving \(S\), with total revenue

\[ P(y,p)=\sum_{\theta,S}p_{\theta,S}y_{\theta,S}. \]

The core condition is expressed directly through continuous coalitions. A coalition may select any submass \(\lambda_{\theta,S}\le y_{\theta,S}\), reassign it to packages \(R\), and respect the same item capacities. If \(u_{\theta,S}=b_\theta(S)-p_{\theta,S}\) is the current utility rate, define \(Z(y,p)\) as the maximum seller revenue obtainable by such a coalition while giving every selected agent at least its current utility. Formally, with \(r_{\theta,S,R}\) denoting the mass moved from current cell \((\theta,S)\) to alternative package \(R\),

\[ Z(y,p)= \max \sum_{\theta,S,R} \bigl(b_\theta(R)-u_{\theta,S}\bigr)r_{\theta,S,R}, \]

subject to the coalition-mass, reassignment, and item-capacity constraints. The payment schedule is core-feasible exactly when

\[ Z(y,p)\le P(y,p). \]

The problem is: given \(y^\star\), output a rational payment schedule \(p\) that is core-feasible, minimizes \(P(y^\star,p)\), and is bidder-Pareto-optimal among the total-payment minimizers. The full auction version also computes \(y^\star\) first.

This is recognizably the paper’s problem: the allocation remains welfare-maximizing, packages remain combinatorial, blocking coalitions remain the core notion, and the objective is precisely the paper’s primary objective of minimizing total core payments. The only population-level change is that repeated bidders become mass of bidder types. If \(\mu_\theta\) and \(\sigma_i\) are rational, clearing denominators gives a finite auction with many repeated bidders and repeated item copies. Conversely, normalizing such a finite high-multiplicity auction gives this continuous instance.

A plausible regime is a public auction for many standardized spectrum licences, landing slots, or service contracts. There may be millions of small operators but only a few dozen recurring valuation and eligibility types: for example, operators with the same geographic coverage profile and the same package complementarities. The number of bidders is then much larger than \(|\Theta|\), while supplies scale with population. This is a genuine high-multiplicity setting, not merely a fractional outcome model.

The positive computational conjecture is also concrete. In the continuous model, coalition membership is mass-valued rather than binary. The separation problem becomes a configuration LP over \(r_{\theta,S,R}\) and \(\lambda_{\theta,S}\), rather than the paper’s binary integer program \(SEP_t\). With additive, gross-substitute, or otherwise efficiently priceable bundle bids, column generation or ellipsoid-with-separation methods should yield a Class A algorithm whose dependence is polynomial in \(N\), \(|\Theta|\), and the encoding length, assuming the bundle-pricing oracle is polynomial. The paper’s constraint-generation logic is exactly the right starting point.

For unrestricted XOR/package bids, I would not claim that continuization makes the problem easy. The paper itself says that winner determination is NP-hard, citing Rothkopf et al. rather than proving that fact here. That package-level hardness should remain in the continuous mirror and may give a Class B result. The interesting question is whether hardness caused specifically by the number of named bidders disappears when bidders collapse into repeated types, while hardness caused by the item agenda remains. That is precisely the population-versus-agenda distinction the ChoCo programme wants to study.

Corollary 2.2, also proved here, supports the objective: within any feasible payment region bounded below by VCG payments, minimizing total potential gains from unilateral deviation is equivalent to minimizing total payments. I would use it as motivation rather than as a second anchor. The paper’s Theorem 2.1 and Corollary 2.2 do not transfer verbatim to an atomless population, because an individual’s infinitesimal VCG externality can collapse to zero; the mirror should therefore focus on total core-payment selection, not claim a continuous VCG theorem for free.

The weakest point is formal eligibility. The paper contains no numbered theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or a comparable complexity classification. Theorem 3.2 is an algorithmic convergence theorem, but it does not prove polynomial running time; its separation subproblem can still be NP-hard and the number of generated constraints may be exponential. Thus, under a strict anchor rule, this is not a clean computational-complexity anchor. Substantively, however, it is a strong continuous mirror: it preserves the paper’s auction, allocation, coalition, and payment questions while making repeated bidder populations continuous and exposing exactly where the constraint-generation method gains or fails.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is weaker than the proponent admits. Theorem 3.2 is not a complexity result: it is a conditional correctness statement for a finite constraint-generation procedure. It gives no polynomial bound, and its procedure repeatedly invokes \(SEP_t\), which the paper explicitly formulates as an integer winner-determination problem. The number of generated constraints may also be exponential. Thus the proposed continuous problem is not a continuization of a computational classification in the paper; it is a new high-multiplicity auction problem inspired by the paper’s formulation. The conjectured column-generation algorithm is precisely the new result that would have to be proved.

There is also a genuine degeneracy in the direct limit. The paper has \(N\) indivisible items and \(M\) bidders, so at most \(N\) bidders can win. If bidder mass is normalized to one while \(N\) stays fixed, the winning mass is at most \(N/M\), which tends to zero. Per-capita payments, welfare, and the population-level core objective then vanish. The VCG externality of an individual also disappears: one atomless bidder cannot affect an allocation or price. Hence the paper’s strategic motivation—bounding unilateral gains and selecting payments close to VCG—has no nontrivial population limit.

The proponent repairs this by introducing item classes with per-capita supplies. That is a permissible co-modification under ChoCo, but it changes the object substantially: the result is now a nonatomic combinatorial market with replicated supplies, not the finite-item auction to which Theorem 3.2 applies. The repair may be a worthwhile research problem, but it cannot be presented as evidence that this paper already contains a strong computational anchor for continuization.

The payment concept also does not pass cleanly to the limit. In the paper, \(\pi_j\) is a payment of a named winner and bidder-Pareto optimality compares the utilities of those named bidders. In the proposed model, identical type mass may be split among several packages and assigned different payments. If one imposes a common \(p_{\theta,S}\), one adds an anonymity restriction absent from the paper. If one allows arbitrary payments within a type’s mass, the payment object becomes a measurable function or distribution, and finite type compression no longer captures the original Pareto problem. The best continuous formulation therefore either changes the payment-selection problem or abandons finite-dimensionality.

Nor does replacing coalitions by mass restore the original theorem. The finite proof relies on a finite family of named coalitions and on permanently adding one constraint for a blocking coalition. A nonatomic core has measurable subcoalitions, generally an uncountable family. One can replace finite termination by an ellipsoid or separation-oracle result, but that is a new algorithmic theory, not Theorem 3.2 surviving the limit. The proponent’s \(Z(y,p)\) formulation is a reasonable starting point, especially after correcting it to preserve the allocations of noncoalition mass, but its tractability is conjectural and depends on valuation restrictions not assumed in the paper.

I would therefore reject this as a strong paper-anchored mirror: the named theorem supplies no complexity statement, the literal population limit degenerates, and the nondegenerate repair changes the item and payment model enough that the interesting computational theorem would be newly invented. I would not claim that this proves no worthwhile continuous problem exists. The standardized-licence scenario is plausible, and a carefully defined nonatomic core problem could be valuable to ChoCo. The honest negative conclusion is narrower: this paper does not itself provide a sufficiently sharp computational anchor for that programme.
