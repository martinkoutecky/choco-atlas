What the mirror covers

The mirror captures rule by rule score, ranking, grade profile, and rank reversal comparisons for a fixed population distribution. It does not capture how the paper estimates that distribution from its sample or establish its explanation of strategic behavior.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible anchors in this paper: it has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition, and makes no named computational-complexity claim. Its results are empirical comparisons. So I cannot honestly restate a qualifying anchor or give a compliant one-question-per-anchor case.

The strongest positive lead, explicitly not an anchor, is a continuous version of its fixed-electorate comparison. Fix an experimental scale and let \(C\) be the ten candidates. A type \(t\) records a voter’s complete response on the ballots being compared: their official first-round choice \(o_t\), approval choices \(a_t(c)\), and evaluative grades \(e_t(c)\). Give each type a rational population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t \mu_t=1\). The question is: given the support and masses, compute the candidate rankings and rank reversals under the official first-round vote, approval voting, and evaluative voting. The solution is the exact aggregate score for each candidate under each rule, together with the resulting rankings. The underlying choices include strategic behavior as recorded; the model does not try to infer sincere preferences or predict how behavior would change under a different rule.

A national electorate of roughly 43 million voters and a finite set of complete ballot-response types is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, though the paper does not establish that the actual support size \(\tau\) is small. The authors could recognize this as a population-level version of their “who is favored?” comparison, with fixed political supply and observed responses. Its expected direction is tractable by direct aggregation, in \(O(m\tau)\) score operations. The weak point is that this is a simple computational restatement of the empirical comparison, not a mirror of a named complexity result; it does not capture the paper’s weighting procedure or establish its explanation of strategic behavior. A more demanding follow-up would ask how robust the rankings are across population distributions consistent with the observed sample and calibration margins.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that this paper offers no eligible anchor: it reports empirical comparisons and their interpretation, not a theorem about computational complexity. So there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The fixed-electorate comparison is a sensible high-multiplicity description. A type can record a voter’s official choice and their approval and evaluative ballots, with mass \(\mu_t\). For each rule \(r\), the score is \(\mathrm{sc}_r(c)=\sum_t\mu_t s_r(t,c)\). The rankings, grade profiles, and most of the paper’s candidate comparisons then follow by direct aggregation in \(O(m\tau)\) operations. That restates the descriptive analysis; it creates no evident optimization problem, pricing problem, or complexity boundary. A national electorate makes the population large, but size alone does not give this mirror a computational question.

The strongest upgrade is the proponent’s suggestion to test whether the reported rankings survive uncertainty in the population distribution. Given a well-defined polytope of plausible type masses, checking whether one candidate can overtake another is a linear optimization problem. But the paper does not supply that uncertainty model. Its national adjustment weights experimental participants by their self-reported official vote; participation and geography are central limitations, and the different evaluative scales were tested in different places. Keeping the paper’s weighting assumptions gives a sensitivity exercise around its estimates. Modeling nonresponse and sampling uncertainty more fully requires additional statistical assumptions and data. Either could be useful, but neither is a computational result of this paper.

One could also add costs for voters changing their ballot types and ask how much mass must move to reverse a ranking. That is a recognizable continuous robustness problem, but the costs and intervention are new modeling choices. They do not arise from the paper’s question of comparing observed voting-rule outcomes.

The negative case is strongest for the paper as written: its natural continuous population is real, while the proposed calculation is only tallying and its harder versions need a separately motivated uncertainty or intervention model. I cannot make the universal claim airtight; a well-designed robustness study could be worthwhile in its own right. The case against is that it would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a computational contribution this paper makes.
