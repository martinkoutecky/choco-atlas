The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 5.4

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 5.4 — survived: The named round bound is computational, and the homogeneous population limit gives a recognizable continuous version of its robustness threshold.

The continuous mirror question

Given odd \(k\ge 3\), consider a homogeneous continuum population with red mass \(x_t\), initially \(x_0=1\). Each agent samples \(k\) peers uniformly with replacement, and each sampled red opinion is independently corrupted to blue with probability \(p\); agents adopt the sample majority. With \(F_{p,k}(x)=\sum_{j=(k+1)/2}^{k}\binom{k}{j}((1-p)x)^j(1-(1-p)x)^{k-j}\) and \(x_{t+1}=F_{p,k}(x_t)\), compute the largest \(p\) for which the trajectory from \(x_0=1\) retains a red majority indefinitely, giving an exact algebraic representation and isolating intervals for the threshold and its positive fixed point.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over two types, red and blue, with masses \(x_t\) and \(1-x_t\). The decision parameter is the edge-corruption rate \(p\); the objective is the largest rate for which the deterministic mass recurrence retains a red majority.

The objection that survived

The paper already derives the scalar recurrence and characterizes its fold threshold, leaving exact root isolation as a narrow parameter-computation task; this limits novelty but does not remove the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the unanimous-initialization edge-corruption threshold in Theorem 5.4; leaves non-unanimous initializations, node corruption, and the voter and large-\(k\) limit results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(k\), can the critical bias and its positive fixed point be isolated exactly in time polynomial in \(k\) and the output bit length, using \(F_{p,k}(x)=x\) and \(\partial_xF_{p,k}(x)=1\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the continuous \(k\)-majority robustness threshold for a homogeneous two-opinion population. Take agents with the same communication and update rules, each sampling \(k\) peers uniformly with replacement. A type records an agent’s current opinion, red or blue, so the society is a distribution over those two types. If red has mass \(x\), edge corruption turns each sampled red opinion blue with probability \(p\), and the next red mass is

\[ F_{p,k}(x)=\sum_{j=(k+1)/2}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\bigl((1-p)x\bigr)^j\bigl(1-(1-p)x\bigr)^{k-j}. \]

Thus \(x_{t+1}=F_{p,k}(x_t)\), with \(x_0=1\) for an initially unanimous red population. The decision variable is the bias level \(p\); the objective is to find the largest bias the population can withstand while keeping a red majority. The continuous problem is: given odd \(k\ge 3\), compute the exact critical value

\[ p_k^\star=\max\{p\in[0,1]: F_{p,k}(x)=x\text{ for some }x\in(0,1]\}. \]

A solution should give an exact algebraic representation of \(p_k^\star\), with isolating intervals for it and a corresponding positive fixed point, and certify that no larger \(p\) admits one. This is a genuine population mirror: individual agents still sample and update as in the paper, while the mass of each opinion type is fractional.

My anchor is Theorem 5.4, proved in this paper. In its \(q=1\) case, for every fixed odd \(k\ge3\) and every sequence of graphs with minimum degree \(\omega(\log n)\), the edge biased \(k\)-majority dynamics has critical value \(p_{k,1}^\star=p_k^\star\). If \(p<p_k^\star\), then for every constant \(\gamma>0\), after some constant \(T\), the red fraction stays at least \(\varphi^+_{p,k}-\gamma\) through every polynomial horizon \(n^K\), with high probability; consequently disruption does not occur by \(n^K\), with high probability. If \(p>p_k^\star\), disruption occurs within a constant number of rounds with high probability. The theorem leaves the equality case out of these two time bounds. In the continuous model, the threshold is the fold where the positive fixed point ceases to exist: below it the trajectory from \(1\) approaches a red-majority fixed point, and above it the trajectory eventually crosses the majority boundary.

I expect this threshold computation to be Class A. It reduces to finding the largest bias at a positive fixed point of a univariate polynomial family; at the critical value the fixed point is a tangency, described by \(F_{p,k}(x)=x\) and \(\partial_xF_{p,k}(x)=1\). For variable \(k\), exact real-root isolation or elimination gives a plausible polynomial-time route in \(k\) and the coefficient bit length. The theorem supplies the meaningful robustness threshold that this algorithm would compute. Further questions include computing the threshold for a non-unanimous initial red mass, comparing edge corruption with node corruption, and extending the computation to several network types with a rational type-mixing matrix.

The regime is a large, well-mixed electorate or opinion population whose members have the same sampling and update behavior: \(n\) is large while the number of types is two. A complete graph is a natural instance of the paper’s dense-graph regime. The mirror preserves the paper’s central robustness question, its sampling rule, its corruption mechanism, and its disruption criterion.

The weak point is that the paper proves round-complexity bounds for a stochastic distributed process; it gives no named NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity classification. The one-type continuous recurrence is also closely related to the tree-process analysis already in the paper. So this is a narrow case for a new algorithmic question about computing the robustness threshold, not a claim that the paper already studies computational social choice in the usual complexity-theoretic sense. If only classical decision-problem complexity theorems count as anchors, the paper has no eligible named anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.4 is not a computational complexity result in the ChoCo sense. It bounds the random time for a distributed process to lose a majority, given the graph, bias, and initialization. It does not ask for an algorithm that chooses an intervention or computes a solution to an instance. Calling the number of rounds “complexity” would conflate the dynamics’ behavior with the complexity of solving a computational problem.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is a homogeneous population on a complete graph, with red and blue as the two current-state types. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and its density follows the map \(F_{p,k}\). But the paper already analyzes that scalar recurrence on the tree, including the fixed points and the critical bias. Asking for an exact algebraic representation of \(p_k^\star\) turns that analysis into a parameter-extraction task; it does not create a complexity question about computation over a continuous society. The threshold is a model parameter, not an intervention the algorithm must choose.

A richer finite-type model with a rational mixing matrix is a plausible extension, but it changes the proposed anchor into bifurcation analysis for a higher-dimensional dynamical system. Alternatively, choosing costly mass transfers to disrupt the majority would create an optimization problem, but the paper’s adversary applies independent stochastic bias; it does not choose agents or allocate an intervention budget. Either direction may be worthwhile research, but neither mirrors Theorem 5.4’s computational content.

So the case against this anchor rests on the missing computational problem, not on the absence of multiplicity or on a prediction about what a continuous model would conclude. The negative case is not airtight: the complete-graph population is a genuine continuous limit, and exact threshold computation is technically a computational task. Its weakness is that this task appears to be an add-on to an analytic result, rather than a continuous computational-social-choice question the programme is designed to classify.
