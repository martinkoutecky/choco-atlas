What the mirror covers

The proposed aggregation extends Theorem 1's payoff-equivalence identity; the paper's other payoff and liability analyses remain outside it, and none states a computational guarantee or hardness result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: this paper has no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires as an anchor. Its only numbered theorem, Theorem 1 (proved here), says that a trader changing conditional-market prices has the same expected payoff as a forecaster making the same reports in the scoring-rule decision market if and only if the securities payout for action \(\alpha_j\) is \(v_j=1/\phi_j\). That is a payoff-equivalence result, not a complexity result. So there is no qualifying hardness or algorithmic anchor in the paper.

The closest population mirror I can defend is Mass-Weighted Payoff Calibration, with Theorem 1 as a clearly nonqualifying lead. Consider a large retailer using decision markets to forecast the effects of alternative campaigns. Thousands of store managers could fall into a much smaller number of store-role-information cohorts. A type records everything relevant to the market interaction: its conditional beliefs, current reports and positions, and feasible trades. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the forecaster population.

An instance consists of finite action and outcome sets, the conditional scoring rules and cost functions, a full-support decision rule \(\phi\), and a rational finite-support population \(\mu\). The question is to find an action-dependent payout rule \(v\) such that, for every feasible profile of type-specific trades, the population’s aggregate expected payoff in the securities market equals its aggregate expected payoff in the corresponding scoring-rule market. A solution is the payout rule. Applying Theorem 1 type by type gives \(v_j=1/\phi_j\); integrating the resulting equality over \(\mu\) preserves it. For rational, supplied values of \(\phi\), this calibration looks directly tractable.

This does make the population of forecasters continuous; the probability forecasts and outcomes remain those in the paper. In the proposed regime, \(N\) managers might number in the thousands while \(\tau\) standardized cohorts number in the tens or hundreds. The scenario is plausible where managers share information and trading conditions within store-role groups, though exact sameness of beliefs is a real modeling constraint.

The soft spot is also decisive for the programme’s purpose: the paper does not study a society of forecasters or optimize over their mass distribution. The theorem applies to a trader’s payoff, and the population result is just its weighted aggregation. The proposed problem is one the authors could recognize as an aggregate extension of their mechanism, but it does not reveal a new computational landscape or ask whether discrete hardness dissolves. Further questions—such as choosing \(\phi\) to approach a deterministic decision rule while bounding liabilities—are motivated by the paper’s discussion, but are not anchored by named computational results here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent already concedes: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes when securities trades match scoring-rule markets in expected payoff: \(v_j=1/\phi_j\). The other results are payoff and liability identities. None asks for, or gives, a complexity result or algorithm for a computational problem.

The proposed “Mass-Weighted Payoff Calibration” does not supply that missing problem. If type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\) and expected payoff \(G_t\), then the population payoff is \(\sum_t \mu_t G_t\). Theorem 1 applies to each type’s trade, so the aggregate equality follows by summing it. The masses do not affect the calibration: \(v_j=1/\phi_j\) either way. This is a legitimate population interpretation of the mechanism, but it adds no computational question for the continuous model to answer.

A stronger population-based candidate would be to choose the decision rule or insurance arrangement to control aggregate liabilities across cohorts of forecasters. Large organizations could plausibly have many forecasters sharing beliefs, budgets, and trading conditions, so the population itself need not be an implausible continuum. But the paper does not formulate that optimization problem. Its liability analysis takes trades and the decision rule as given; the unbounded-loss result comes from allowing arbitrarily large purchases. Adding cohort budgets, insurance prices, or an objective for selecting \(\phi\) could make a worthwhile computational project, but those additions create a new market-design problem rather than continuize a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as a ChoCo input: the one proposed anchor is a weighted restatement of a pointwise identity, and the more interesting population questions require new constraints and objectives. I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could ever be built around decision markets. A large forecaster population could support one. What the paper does not provide is a computational result whose complexity landscape such a mirror would chart.
