The continuous mirror question

Given a finite tree \(G=(V,E)\), a finite category set \(B\), and rational-mass types \(t=(v_t,\succ_t,e_t)\) with strict rankings over \(B\), let \(\mu_t\) be each type's mass and set the supply of category \(b\) to \(\sum_{t:e_t=b}\mu_t\). Start with \(x_{t,e_t}=\mu_t\) and all other \(x_{t,b}=0\). A legal step chooses types \(t,s\) at adjacent nodes, categories \(b,c\) with positive assigned mass, and real \(\delta\) satisfying \(0<\delta\le\min(x_{t,b},x_{s,c})\), \(c\succ_t b\), and \(b\succ_s c\); it decreases \(x_{t,b},x_{s,c}\) by \(\delta\) and increases \(x_{t,c},x_{s,b}\) by \(\delta\). Is there an allocation reachable by finitely many such steps that is Pareto-efficient among reachable allocations under type-preserving couplings and has voting mass \(\sum_t\sum_{b\succ_t e_t}x_{t,b}\ge q\), for a given rational threshold \(q\)?

The model it lives in

A tree of cohort locations \(G=(V,E)\) hosts types \(t=(v_t,\succ_t,e_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\). The decision variables \(x_{t,b}\) give the mass of type \(t\) assigned category \(b\); row sums preserve type masses and column sums preserve baseline category supplies. Adjacent cohorts may exchange any mutually preferred equal mass. The objective is to determine whether a reachable allocation is Pareto-efficient among reachable allocations and achieves at least threshold voting mass \(q\).

The objection that survived

Allowing any members of adjacent cohorts to exchange creates complete bipartite cross-cohort interactions and lets mass split among trades, changing the individual tree's reachability structure; Theorem 24's reduction may therefore fail to transfer.

The case FOR (proponent)

I would anchor the case on Theorem 24, proved in this paper. It states that finding a maximum-votes Pareto-efficient matching is NP-hard on tree networks when preferences are strict. In full, the input is a housing-market configuration on a tree, with each agent initially endowed with one object and a strict ranking of the objects. The task is to find a matching reachable by a sequence of mutually beneficial swaps along tree edges, Pareto-efficient among the reachable matchings, and maximizing the number of agents who strictly prefer their assigned object to their initial endowment. The paper proves this hardness by reduction from reachable-object on strict-preference trees; the NP-completeness of that source problem is cited from Gourvès et al. [13].

The continuous mirror I find plausible is a tree-cohort continuous MVPE threshold problem. Picture a large organization whose teams occupy the nodes of a tree: employees can trade positions only with employees in adjacent teams. Many employees share the same team, strict preference ranking over position categories, and initial position category. Those three features form a complete type \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\); the support size \(\tau\) counts distinct types, not employees. Position supplies scale with the workforce, so a category can have positive mass. Each individual still receives one indivisible position; a mass such as \(x_{t,b}\) records how much of type \(t\) is assigned category \(b\).

Here is a precise version a prover could take up. The input gives a finite tree \(G=(V,E)\), a finite set \(B\) of position categories, supported types \(t=(v(t),\succ_t,e(t))\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and a rational threshold \(q\). Category \(b\) has supply \(\sum_{t:e(t)=b}\mu_t\), and initially every member of type \(t\) holds \(e(t)\). A current allocation is a nonnegative table \(x_{t,b}\) whose row sums are \(\mu_t\) and whose column sums are the category supplies.

A mass-swap step chooses types \(t,s\) at adjacent tree nodes, categories \(b,c\) with positive assigned mass, and a rational \(\delta>0\) no greater than \(\min(x_{t,b},x_{s,c})\). It is allowed when \(c\succ_t b\) and \(b\succ_s c\); it moves mass \(\delta\) from \(t\) at \(b\) and \(s\) at \(c\) to \(t\) at \(c\) and \(s\) at \(b\). An allocation is reachable if a finite sequence of these steps produces it. Its voting mass is \(\sum_t\sum_{b\succ_t e(t)}x_{t,b}\). For Pareto efficiency, compare two reachable allocations by coupling the members of each type between their old and new assigned categories: a dominator must leave everyone weakly better off under that coupling and make a positive mass strictly better off.

The decision question is: does there exist a reachable allocation that is Pareto-efficient among reachable allocations and has voting mass at least \(q\)? Its yes/no answer is the solution; a yes instance also has a finite mass-swap sequence witnessing reachability. Varying \(q\) gives the corresponding maximum-votes problem. This threshold form avoids assuming that the supremum over reachable continuous allocations is always attained.

This keeps the paper’s central question intact: which locally allowed swaps can reach an allocation that is both Pareto-efficient and maximizes improvement over the initial endowment? The continuity is in the population and per-capita supplies. It does not make an individual’s position divisible. In the proposed regime, millions of employees may fall into a much smaller set of team, preference, and endowment types.

My provisional expectation is tractability for this mirror: splitting type mass may let a prover characterize reachability with flow or LP constraints, even though the discrete version is hard. Whether the reachable, Pareto-efficient region admits such a characterization is open. The competing possibility is that the tree and preference gadgets in Theorem 24 still enforce all-or-nothing choices at the type level, so hardness transfers. A useful next question is whether the reachable allocations have a finite linear description, and whether the discrete reduction survives when each gadget role has positive mass.

The weakest point is the network interpretation. Several agents at one tree node with swaps allowed across adjacent cohorts form a tree of cohorts, whose expanded individual-agent graph is not literally a tree. The paper’s authors might regard this as a meaningful high-multiplicity analogue of their tree-network problem, but they could insist on a literal tree on individual agents. This is a real change to the network model, made to give repeated agents the same role and neighborhood in a population limit. The mirror remains worth asking because it preserves the swap rule, strict preferences, baseline votes, and reachable-set Pareto criterion; the result should make clear whether those ingredients yield a tractable mass problem or retain the paper’s hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 24 is a genuine computational result, so the negative case cannot rest on a lack of anything to mirror. Its hardness concerns finding a reachable, reachable-set Pareto-efficient matching that improves as many agents as possible. The difficulty is that the paper’s agents are tied to distinct objects and specific positions in a network. In the strict-preference model, each agent’s initial object is unique, and the baseline-vote comparison depends on that object. Legal swaps also depend on which individual vertices are adjacent. A complete type therefore has to retain the endowment and network role; in the paper’s instances, that leaves essentially one type per agent. The direct high-multiplicity version has support size \(\tau\) growing with the population, so it offers little of the compression that motivates the programme.

The cohort model in the case for is a sensible problem, but it changes more than the population scale. It replaces individual edges with permission for mass from any two adjacent cohorts to trade. In an expanded individual-agent graph, that usually creates many cross-cohort edges and cycles, rather than the tree in Theorem 24. Its fractional swaps also let a cohort split its mass across trades without tracking which particular agents and objects can meet along a sequence of bilateral exchanges. That may be exactly the right model for teams exchanging position categories, but the result would characterize a new cohort-exchange process. The paper’s authors would need to accept that process as their problem before it counts as a mirror.

A stronger attempt at fidelity would keep an actual tree and use repeated, symmetric branches, with cloned position categories and repeated preference roles. That avoids the simple objection that only one agent can occupy each node. But the aggregation still has to preserve which branch agents are connected and how their exchanges interact. In star-like constructions, for example, one central agent can mediate many swaps while accounting for a vanishing fraction of the population. A pure distribution over positive-mass types does not automatically represent that agent’s persistent role; retaining it requires a hybrid model with distinguished individuals and a continuum of cohorts. Such a model may be worth studying, but it takes substantial new modeling before the paper’s reachability and Pareto-efficiency question has a continuous counterpart.

This is the strongest negative case I can make, and it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The tree-of-cohorts interpretation is plausible, and a carefully designed symmetric-tree or hybrid model might preserve enough of the original question to interest the authors. The case against is strongest on fidelity: the obvious continuous version changes the network and exchange process, while the faithful version appears to retain much of the individual structure that continuization was meant to compress.
