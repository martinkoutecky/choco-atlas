The continuous mirror question

For each population size \(N\), let a finite-support measure \(\mu\) on a Ptolemaic metric space have masses in multiples of \(1/N\) and support size at most \(\tau\). Among lottery rules mapping \(\mu\) to pairs of facility locations and remaining strategyproof when one individual moves mass \(1/N\) from their true location to any reported location, what is the infimum worst-case ratio of expected per-capita social cost to \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\) as \(N\) grows with \(\tau\) fixed?

The model it lives in

Locations are types, with mass \(\mu_x\) at type \(x\); each individual has mass \(1/N\). A rule outputs a lottery over facility pairs, and per-capita cost is \(\mathrm{SC}(F,\mu)=\int \mathbb{E}[\min\{d(x,\ell_1),d(x,\ell_2)\}]\,d\mu(x)\). The mass-weighted Proportional rule draws anchor \(x\) with probability \(\mu_x\), then partner \(y\) proportional to \(\mu_y d(x,y)\); Global Pair weights each pair \(x,y\) proportional to \(\mu_x\mu_y d(x,y)\). Mix these with probabilities \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), respectively.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Proportional–Global Pair mixture and its \(11/3\) guarantee in Theorem 3.5, with the profile-wise bounds as supporting results; it does not establish the optimal strategyproof approximation ratio or turn Theorem 4.2’s performance lower bound into computational hardness.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed support bound \(\tau\), determine the limiting optimal worst-case approximation ratio over rules strategyproof under \(1/N\)-mass deviations as \(N\to\infty\), including whether the mass-weighted mixture’s \(11/3\) guarantee can be improved.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: this paper studies a population-weighted version of its two-facility mechanism-design problem. Its two useful anchors are Theorem 4.2, a lower bound on mechanism performance, and Theorem 3.5, a constructive upper bound. Neither is an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result, so this is not a direct anchor for ChoCo’s usual complexity landscape.

Take location \(x\in\mathbb R\) as the type: people at the same location have identical costs for every facility pair. A society is a finite-support measure \(\mu=\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{x_t}\), where \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), \(N\) is the population size, and the number of occupied locations \(\tau\) can be much smaller than \(N\). Mass is the fraction of residents at a location. A mechanism \(F_N\) maps each such measure to a lottery over facility pairs. Its expected per-capita social cost is
\[ \mathrm{SC}(F_N,\mu)=\int \mathbb E_{\ell\sim F_N(\mu)} \big[\min\{|x-\ell_1|,|x-\ell_2|\}\big]\,d\mu(x), \]
and the benchmark is the minimum of the same integral over facility pairs.

My lead question, Granular Mass Two-Facility Approximation, is: among rules \(F_N\) that are strategyproof when one resident changes their report—equivalently, when \(N^{-1}\delta_x\) is moved from \(x\) to \(y\)—what worst-case approximation ratio is possible as \(N\) grows while \(\tau\) stays small? This preserves the paper’s individual incentive constraint while expressing a high-multiplicity society by its type masses.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.2 (proved here): every strategyproof mechanism for the two-facility game has approximation ratio at least \((1+\sqrt2)/2\). Its proof uses the two profiles \(A_{m,k}=\langle 0^m,(1/2)^k,1^m\rangle\) and \(B_{m,k}=\langle -m,0^m,(1/2)^{k-1},1^m\rangle\), which differ by one midpoint agent’s report. With \(k/m\to 2-\sqrt2\), the theorem’s bound \(R(m,k)\) approaches \((1+\sqrt2)/2\). In the mass formulation these witnesses have at most four types, despite \(N\) growing. The expected direction is that this approximation lower-bound obstruction transfers to the high-multiplicity representation. That is a performance lower bound, though, not computational hardness in ChoCo’s Class B sense.

A constructive companion is Theorem 3.5 (proved here): the mechanism that runs Proportional with probability \(2/3\) and Global Pair with probability \(1/3\) is strategyproof on every Ptolemaic metric space and has approximation ratio exactly \(11/3\). The corresponding mass-sampling question is to find a strategyproof rule for \(\mu\) with that guarantee. The natural candidate samples the Proportional anchor from \(\mu\), chooses its partner with probability proportional to distance times mass, and mixes this with Global Pair, whose pair weight is proportional to \(d(x,y)\mu(dx)\mu(dy)\). For rational masses on the \(1/N\) grid, this is precisely the paper’s mechanism on a compressed profile; with distances available, its support-level sampling can be implemented using pairwise calculations in \(\tau\). The expected direction is constructive tractability for this candidate, not a claim that the optimal mechanism is computable or that \(11/3\) is optimal. The question left open is whether the asymptotically best ratio at fixed support size can be determined or improved.

The regime is plausible for large-area facility planning when many residents share a small set of locations or neighborhood types: \(N\) is large and \(\tau\ll N\). This covers the paper’s two-facility strategyproof approximation results, not its broader cited literature or facility-location questions generally. It keeps the facility outcomes as pairs and continuizes the population description.

The soft spot is also the model’s hinge. To retain individual strategyproofness, the proposed mirror keeps a report granularity of \(1/N\). In a strictly atomless model, one person’s report does not change \(\mu\), so strategyproofness becomes vacuous; choosing an optimal facility pair then gives ratio \(1\). And the lower-bound witness has a vanishing-mass outlier at \(-m\), so it does not by itself establish the same bound for a fixed limiting distribution. The authors would likely recognize the granularity-preserving version as their problem written in type-mass coordinates. Whether they would accept the strict atomless limit as a mirror is much less convincing.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorems 3.5 and 4.2 give an approximation guarantee and an approximation lower bound for strategyproof mechanisms; Theorem 3.1 establishes truthfulness. None classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity or gives an algorithm for a computational task. That matters here: changing the population description can produce a new performance question, but it does not turn these results into the complexity landscape ChoCo is charting.

Theorem 4.2 has a credible high-multiplicity reading. Its witnesses use only three or four location types, and replacing agent counts by their normalized masses preserves the finite-\(N\) profiles and the one-person deviation exactly. That is a sensible regime for clustered populations, and existing high-multiplicity work supports it. The difficulty is that the theorem’s asymptotic witness does not yield a nontrivial incentive constraint in a genuine population limit. With \(k/m\to\alpha=2-\sqrt2\), the two empirical measures converge weakly to the same distribution,
\[ \mu_\infty=\frac{\delta_0+\delta_1}{2+\alpha} +\frac{\alpha\,\delta_{1/2}}{2+\alpha}. \]
The distinguishing outlier has mass \(1/n\) but sits at \(-m\). It disappears in the weak limit; retaining its effect requires finite-agent granularity and a location escaping to infinity. A stronger topology that tracks its distance does not give convergence of these profiles. So the exact mass-grid version is a faithful high-multiplicity restatement, while the strict continuous limit loses the move that drives the lower bound. Either way, the theorem supplies no computational hardness or algorithmic boundary.

Theorem 3.5 is the strongest proposed mirror. For an empirical measure, its mechanisms can be written using mass-weighted sampling: draw the Proportional anchor from \(\mu\), choose its partner with probability proportional to distance and mass, and choose Global Pair proportional to \(d(x,y)\mu(dx)\mu(dy)\). On rational mass grids, this is precisely the paper’s lottery after compressing co-located agents. A finite-support version can be evaluated or sampled by summing over pairs of support points. That makes the formulation clean, but leaves no new computational obstacle for the theorem to expose: its contribution is the strategyproofness and approximation guarantee, not the cost of computing the mechanism or the complexity of finding one. In a strictly atomless model, a single person’s report does not change the measure, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous and an optimal facility pair gives ratio \(1\). Requiring positive-mass coalitions to report together could restore substance, but that changes the incentive notion and is a new problem.

One could pose the broader question of finding the best approximation ratio over all strategyproof mechanisms on population measures. That is a coherent mechanism-design frontier, but the paper gives neither a computational formulation of that search nor a complexity result for it. My negative case is strongest on ChoCo’s scope, not on the plausibility of clustered facility planning: the population regime is sensible, and the weighted formulas are natural. What fails is the proposed bridge from these results to a computational continuization problem.
