The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its findings in Section 5 are empirical results from user studies: for example, participants using restricted interfaces earned more on average and spent less time deliberating. Those are not eligible anchors under your criteria, so I can’t give this paper an anchor-backed continuous mirror.

The strongest plausible positive case is a prospective population version of interface design, rather than a mirror of a named result. Consider a large delivery fleet whose vehicles charge through the same market. A type records the driver’s vehicle and battery state, the distribution of possible delivery tasks and their values, and a behavioral rule for choosing bids through an interface. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the fleet with type \(t\). A plausible regime would have thousands of drivers but only dozens of recurring vehicle, route, and behavior types.

Here is a precise candidate question. The input is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), a finite set \(Q\) of candidate bid reports, a menu-size limit \(K\), and a fleet-wide deliberation-time budget \(D\). For each type \(t\) and report \(q\), the input gives a score \(a_t(q)\) predicting which report that type selects when offered a menu, expected driver profit \(u_t(q)\), and expected deliberation time \(d_t(q)\). Ties are broken by a fixed rule. Choose a menu \(B\subseteq Q\), with \(|B|\le K\), that maximizes
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,u_t(q_t(B)) \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,d_t(q_t(B))\le D, \qquad q_t(B)=\arg\max_{q\in B}a_t(q). \]
Mass means share of the fleet; the decision is one shared interface menu; the objective is fleet-wide expected profit subject to an average deliberation constraint.

This is recognizable as an extension of the paper’s interface-design question: the authors compare restricted and expressive bidding interfaces, and identify optimized interfaces as future work. For this candidate problem, I would expect any hardness of choosing menus to persist when moving from integer fleet counts to rational population masses; the report-menu combinatorics are still there. But the paper establishes no such hardness, and its experiments do not show that optimizing a menu is computationally hard or tractable.

The main weakness is that the behavioral response rule \(a_t\) is doing substantial work. The paper studies how people actually respond to interfaces; supplying a response model as input risks assuming away the central empirical challenge. Also, comparing the paper’s three fixed interfaces alone would just be a small evaluation task, not a computational result. So this is a credible scenario for a future continuous-population interface-design problem, but it cannot rescue the missing anchor or support a claim about the paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no eligible result to mirror. Its claims are experimental: participants using restricted interfaces earned more on average and deliberated for less time. It proves no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous population could support a new study of those effects, but that would not be a continuous mirror of a named result from this paper.

The proposed menu-selection problem is the best rescue, because the paper compares interfaces and leaves optimized interfaces as future work. But it is a prospective algorithmic project, not a mirror of one of the paper’s results. Its inputs also include each type’s predicted response to each menu. That captures the authors’ central empirical difficulty—the way real people understand and use an interface—as supplied data. With those responses given, the problem optimizes over a finite menu and aggregates type-level scores and times by their masses. The paper neither formulates nor evaluates that optimization problem.

The fleet scenario is plausible, and grouping recurring driver and behavior profiles into types is consistent with the programme’s high-multiplicity model. So I would not reject it on the ground that drivers have individual differences. But that plausibility establishes a possible setting for future work; it does not supply the missing computational result in this paper. The negative case is therefore strong about the absence of a paper-anchored mirror, and weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population project could grow out of the paper’s future-work direction.
