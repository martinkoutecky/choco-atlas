The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\) goods, \(\tau\) buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), a horizon \(T\), and an unknown stationary distribution \(P\) over a finite set of positive valuation matrices \(V=(v_{\theta j})\), each shared within its type, find an online policy for mass bids \(z_{\theta j,t}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j z_{\theta j,t}=\mu_\theta\). Prices are \(p_{j,t}=\sum_\theta z_{\theta j,t}\), and per-person allocations are \(x_{\theta j,t}=z_{\theta j,t}/(\mu_\theta p_{j,t})\). Against \(z^\star\in\arg\min_z\Phi_\mu(z)\), where \(\Phi_\mu(z)=\mathbb E_{V\sim P}[-\sum_{\theta,j}z_{\theta j}\log v_{\theta j}+\sum_j p_j(z)\log p_j(z)]\), does the type-level proportional response initialized at \(z_{\theta j,1}=\mu_\theta/M\) achieve expected cumulative fairness regret \(\sum_{t=1}^T(\mathbb E[\phi_\mu(z_t;V_t)]-\Phi_\mu(z^\star))\le\log(M\tau)\) while clearing every good each round?

The model it lives in

An online Fisher market whose population is the finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete buyer types. Each type submits aggregate mass bids \(z_{\theta j}\), has per-capita budget \(1\), and receives per-capita allocation \(x_{\theta j}=z_{\theta j}/(\mu_\theta p_j)\); thus \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{\theta j}=1\). The objective is the mass-weighted Shmyrev objective \(\phi_\mu\), and the policy updates \(z_{\theta j,t}=\mu_\theta v_{\theta j,t}x_{\theta j,t-1}/\sum_k v_{\theta k,t}x_{\theta k,t-1}\).

The objection that survived

The mirror applies to cohorts sharing realized valuation vectors, so it does not cover the paper’s more general buyer-specific signals; this bounds coverage but leaves the stated common-shock question intact.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s stationary-input fairness-regret guarantee and uses Lemma 3.2’s update. It leaves the individual buyer regret bound, the nonstationary guarantees of Theorems 5.1–5.3, and the convergence results outside the stated mirror; population-weighted utility regret and extensions to those input models remain follow-up questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the stationary fairness-regret guarantee be established for this mass-bid formulation with bound \(\log(M\tau)\)?
  • What utility-regret guarantee is meaningful for a mass-weighted population, and do the nonstationary-input bounds extend to this model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is an online Fisher market whose population is a distribution over buyer types. The paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar. Its strongest relevant named result is an algorithmic performance theorem, so this is a positive case for an online algorithm mirror, not a claim about dissolving discrete hardness.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in the paper’s Appendix C. Under Assumptions 3.1 and 4.1, with stationary i.i.d. input, buyers using online proportional response, and initial bids \(b_{ij,1}=1/(MN)\), it proves fairness regret \(\mathcal F\le \log(MN)\) and individual buyer regret \(\mathcal R_i\le (v/p)\sqrt{2\log(MN)}\sqrt T\). The assumptions include positive bounded values, utilities and equilibrium prices, and conditionally unbiased noisy gradients with finite variance. This is a regret guarantee, not a running-time classification. Lemma 3.2, also proved here, supplies the closed-form bid update that makes the algorithmic mirror concrete.

A plausible regime is a large ad exchange with, say, a million small advertisers represented by a few dozen campaign types. Members of a type have the same per-period budget, targeting profile, valuation process, and realized type-level value vector for the current ad inventory. Their value shocks can be shared within a type, as when audience or market conditions move together. Here \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of advertisers in type \(\theta\), with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\); \(N\) is very large while the support size \(\tau=|\Theta|\) is moderate. A type is complete for this market: it includes everything that affects its budget, values, and bid updates.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance gives \(M\) goods, a finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\), horizon \(T\), and a stationary distribution \(P\) over a finite set of positive rational valuation matrices \(V=(v_{\theta j})\). \(P\) defines the performance benchmark but, as in the paper, the policy need not know it. Normalize the per-person budget to one, so total population budget is one. The decision variable is a mass bid \(z_{\theta j,t}\): total bids from type \(\theta\) on good \(j\) in round \(t\). Feasibility requires \(z_{\theta j,t}\ge0\) and \(\sum_j z_{\theta j,t}=\mu_\theta\). Prices and per-person allocations are

\[ p_{j,t}=\sum_\theta z_{\theta j,t}, \qquad x_{\theta j,t}=\frac{z_{\theta j,t}}{\mu_\theta p_{j,t}}. \]

Thus \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{\theta j,t}=1\): each unit of supply clears. Define the stochastic Shmyrev objective and its offline comparator by

\[ \phi_\mu(z;V) =-\sum_{\theta,j}z_{\theta j}\log v_{\theta j} +\sum_j p_j(z)\log p_j(z), \qquad z^\star\in\arg\min_{\substack{z\ge0\\\sum_j z_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta}} \mathbb E_{V\sim P}[\phi_\mu(z;V)]. \]

The problem is to give an online policy, using each type’s current values and its previous allocation and prices, that respects each type’s budget, clears the market every round, and achieves expected fairness regret \(O(\log(M\tau))\) against \(z^\star\), without knowing \(P\). A solution is the policy together with a proof of those guarantees. The direct candidate is the population version of Lemma 3.2:

\[ z_{\theta j,t} =\mu_\theta \frac{v_{\theta j,t}x_{\theta j,t-1}} {\sum_k v_{\theta k,t}x_{\theta k,t-1}}, \qquad z_{\theta j,1}=\frac{\mu_\theta}{M}. \]

Each round takes \(O(\tau M)\) arithmetic operations. The manuscript’s mirror-descent potential argument gives a concrete route to the regret proof: treat each type as a representative buyer with budget \(\mu_\theta\), and apply its initial-divergence bound, which yields \(\log M+\log\tau\). The source theorem’s separate individual-regret guarantee does not transfer literally to a nonatomic population; deriving a meaningful mass-weighted per-person utility-regret bound is a further question.

I expect this mirror to be Class A under the stated finite-support regime. The exact update is cheap, the benchmark is a convex optimization problem over \(\tau M\) bids, and the paper’s potential-function proof already has the right structure. The question is recognizably theirs: it keeps online valuations, per-period budgets, trading-post prices, allocations, and the Shmyrev fairness objective, replacing the finite buyer sum with mass over types. The goods were already divisible in the paper; the continuization here is the population.

The mirror also suggests follow-ups: can the fairness bound be sharpened to \(\log M\) under mass weighting; what utility-regret guarantee holds for a type-weighted population; and do the corruption, ergodic, and periodic-input bounds in Theorems 5.1–5.3 carry over with dependence on \(\tau\) rather than \(N\)?

The soft spot is the shared realized valuation within each type. The paper allows richer buyer-specific random values, and identical value *laws* alone do not reduce those agents to a single type when their realized signals differ. The proposed regime is still plausible—common audience conditions can move a cohort together—and the paper’s stochastic model permits correlated inputs. But it is a particular high-multiplicity regime, not a general continuum limit for all the paper’s markets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against a worthwhile mirror is that the proposed type model is already an exact weighted version of the paper’s finite market. Give type \(\theta\) budget \(\mu_\theta\) and let its representative buyer submit the cohort’s total bids \(z_{\theta j}\). Then prices are \(\sum_\theta z_{\theta j}\), and the representative buyer’s allocation is \(z_{\theta j}/p_j\), equal to the cohort’s aggregate allocation. The Shmyrev objective and proportional response update are unchanged. The paper already allows arbitrary budgets in its market model; its displayed regret bound uses equal initial bids, but the same potential calculation with initial bids \(\mu_\theta/M\) gives initial divergence at most \(\log M\). Thus Theorem 4.1’s proposed \(O(\tau M)\) computation is the paper’s update on \(\tau\) weighted buyers. The mass notation changes the interpretation, while the feasible actions and calculations remain the same.

That defeats the strongest anchor as a distinct continuous question. Theorem 4.1 is a regret guarantee, not a complexity classification, and the natural mass version adds no population scale that the paper’s weighted-buyer model cannot already express. Lemma 3.2 is the same update under aggregation. Theorems 5.1–5.3 vary the input process in the same potential argument, so common shocks shared within each type carry over by the same substitution. Corollary 4.1’s price convergence likewise does not create a separate mirror. These results are one algorithmic story, not several independent anchors.

A richer alternative would give advertisers in one campaign type independent, idiosyncratic value signals. That avoids the representative-buyer reduction: agents with different signals or histories may bid differently, and a continuum model would track a distribution of states and bids over time. But the paper supplies no compact representation of that evolving distribution or complexity question for computing with it. With finite states and histories, one can encode the resulting cohorts as weighted buyers, potentially with a state space growing with the horizon. With genuinely continuous signals, one needs a new input and algorithm model. That could be a research direction, but it is not yet a mirror anchored in the paper’s stated results.

The negative case has a real limit: the shared-shock advertiser scenario is plausible, and the authors could recognize the weighted type model as their market. If the programme treats a continuous population interpretation as sufficient even when the paper’s own weighted-buyer formulation already subsumes it, Theorem 4.1 survives. I would call the case against weak rather than claim that no useful scenario exists.
