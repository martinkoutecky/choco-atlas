The case FOR (proponent)

The strict answer is that this paper contains no eligible named computational anchor. Its named results are axiomatic or structural:

  • Theorem 1 (Savaglio and Vannucci 2021, cited rather than proved here) characterizes strategy-proof aggregation on median join-semilattices via monotone \(M_{\mathcal X}\)-independence and order filters.
  • Proposition 3, proved here using that characterization, asserts existence of aggregation rules satisfying \(AMP_P\), anonymity, idempotence, weak neutrality, Basic Pareto, and strategy-proofness on the stated single-peaked domain.
  • Theorems 2–4, Proposition 4, and Corollary 1 are Arrow/Wilson/Hansson-style impossibility results.

None asserts membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, parameterized tractability, or a comparable computational classification. The only computational result is an unnumbered concluding remark, citing Hudry (2012) and Wakabayashi (1998): computing median linear orders or median total preorders is NP-complete in the relevant profile regimes, and computing medians of arbitrary binary relations is NP-hard. It is not numbered and is not proved in this paper. Under the stated gate, that is not a valid anchor.

The strongest positive case nevertheless comes from treating that cited computational claim as a provisional lead. The continuous mirror would be:

\[ \textsc{Kemeny-Median}_{\infty} \]

An instance consists of a finite alternative set \(A\), the voter-type space \(T=\mathcal L_A\) of linear orders, and a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_R)_{R\in T}\), where \(\mu_R\) is the fraction of society with ranking \(R\). The decision variable is a social ranking \(L\in\mathcal L_A\). With \(d_K\) denoting Kendall distance, the objective is

\[ \operatorname{cost}_\mu(L) =\sum_{R\in T}\mu_R d_K(L,R). \]

The task is to output an \(L^\star\) minimizing this quantity, or decide whether some \(L\) has \(\operatorname{cost}_\mu(L)\leq B\). A total-preorder version replaces \(\mathcal L_A\) by \(\mathcal R_A\) and uses the corresponding median-total-preorder distance.

This is a genuine population continuization. A type is a complete ranking, mass is the fraction of agents holding that ranking, and the social output is still a preference relation over the original discrete alternatives. The natural regime is a large electorate with relatively few recurring preference types: for example, many citizens, employees, or institutional stakeholders whose complete rankings fall into a modest number of ideological or role-based profiles. The paper itself explicitly embraces full-profile elicitation, including redundant elicitation, and explicitly identifies the Condorcet–Kemeny median among its quota aggregation rules in Remark 5.

The named results support plausibility even though they are not computational anchors. Theorem 1 says that on the total-preorder semilattice, strategy-proof aggregation rules are exactly those represented through order filters over the meet-irreducible two-block preorders. Proposition 3 then says that suitable positive weakly-neutral quota rules exist which are anonymous, idempotent, weakly neutral, Basic-Pareto, \(AMP_P\), and strategy-proof on the relevant single-peaked meta-preference domain. The Kemeny/median rule is therefore not an arbitrary tractable simplification: it belongs to the paper’s own family of aggregation rules.

The expected complexity is Class B. Given a discrete profile \(R_1,\ldots,R_n\), define

\[ \mu_R=\frac{|\{i:R_i=R\}|}{n}. \]

Then

\[ \operatorname{cost}_\mu(L) =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n d_K(L,R_i), \]

so the discrete and continuous optimization problems have exactly the same minimizers. Any NP-hardness of median-order computation therefore transfers directly to the rational-distribution version. The hardness lives in the number and structure of alternatives, not in population multiplicity. Further questions would concern approximation, parameterization by \(|A|\) or support size, and whether high-multiplicity input enables faster algorithms when the agenda is small.

My weakest point is decisive: the paper’s actual contribution is axiomatic, not computational, and the computational statement is only an unnumbered citation. A referee could reasonably say that \(\textsc{Kemeny-Median}_{\infty}\) is a natural extension of a problem mentioned by the authors, but not a mirror anchored in one of their numbered computational results. So this is a credible positive mirror, but not a fully compliant ChoCo case under the anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is decisive at the programme’s eligibility gate: this paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is a structural characterization of strategy-proof aggregation rules, not a complexity or algorithmic theorem. Proposition 3 is an existence result. Theorems 2–4, Proposition 4, and Corollary 1 are axiomatic impossibility results. None specifies a computational problem, input encoding, complexity class, or algorithm.

The only computational statement appears in an unnumbered concluding remark, where the paper cites Hudry and Wakabayashi for NP-completeness or NP-hardness of computing medians of orders and relations. That is prior literature reported by the author, not a numbered computational result of this paper. Under the stated gate, it cannot anchor a mirror. Nor can the paper’s remark that some aggregation rules have a “polynomial description” be promoted into an algorithmic theorem: no input model, evaluation task, or complexity bound is given, and the family of relevant two-block preorders is itself exponential in \(|A|\).

The proposed \(\textsc{Kemeny-Median}_{\infty}\) is nevertheless a legitimate high-multiplicity question. A distribution over ranking types is sensible for a large electorate, and

\[ \sum_{R\in\mathcal L_A}\mu_R d_K(L,R) \]

is exactly the weighted version of the finite Kemeny objective. The proponent is right that one should not reject it because the answer coincides with the discrete problem, or because hardness transfers: those would be valid programme results, not objections.

But that concession also exposes the proposal’s status. It is not a continuous mirror of a computational theorem in this paper. It takes the phrase “Condorcet–Kemeny median rule” from Remark 5, imports an optimization formulation from the external literature, and turns the paper’s cited computational aside into a new research problem. Even the stronger versions—weak orders, rational distributions, approximation, or parameterized algorithms—remain worthwhile only as newly formulated questions, not as continuations of one of the paper’s named results.

One could instead continuize the paper’s axioms directly by replacing finite coalitions with measurable populations and cardinality quotas with mass thresholds. That would be a recognizable model, especially for the anonymous quota rules in Proposition 3. But AMP, IIA, BP, MDR, anonymity, and idempotence are properties of aggregation functions, not computational tasks. The paper supplies no optimization objective or decision problem whose high-multiplicity version is being sought. Constructing one would again be a new programme question rather than a mirror anchored in the paper.

Thus the proponent has not defeated the negative case under the formal assessment rule: there is no eligible computational anchor at all. The negative case is much weaker if that rule is relaxed. If the unnumbered Hudry/Wakabayashi remark is accepted as an anchor, then the Kemeny distribution model is a sound and probably worthwhile mirror, and I would not honestly claim that no continuous scenario exists.
