The continuous mirror question

Given chore categories \(G\), worker types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), costs \(c_{tg}\), and integer per-worker limits \(h_g\), does there exist a rational mass table \(x_{t,b}\geq 0\) over integral legal bundles \(b\in\prod_{g\in G}\{0,\ldots,h_g\}\) such that \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=q_g\), and every pair of positive-mass cohorts satisfies the exact chore-PMMS inequalities under their respective type costs?

The model it lives in

A workforce of mass \(1\) has finitely many complete cost types. Each worker receives an integral bundle of recurring chore categories subject to per-worker limits; rational cohort masses \(x_{t,b}\) use the per-capita supplies \(q_g\). For every pair of supported cohorts with bundles \(b,b'\), type \(t\)'s cost \(c_t(b)\) must be at most \(\kappa_t(b+b')=\min_{0\leq z\leq b+b',\,z\in\mathbb{Z}^{G}}\max\{c_t(z),c_t(b+b'-z)\}\), with the corresponding condition for type \(t'\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact PMMS existence for chores and, separately, goods, corresponding to Theorems 4 and 5. It leaves the nonexistence examples, quantitative lower bounds, and the \(4/3\)-PMMS guarantee without continuous analogues.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact PMMS feasibility change when worker mass can split among integral bundle cohorts at fixed type proportions and per-capita supplies?
  • Is the continuous feasibility problem hard because of support selection, or can continuous-optimization methods solve it efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the chores result, because its continuous version keeps the paper’s central question intact: can every agent receive a bundle satisfying the exact pairwise maximin-share condition?

A plausible regime is a large workforce receiving recurring tasks. Workers fall into a small number \(\tau\) of complete cost types—same task costs and eligibility rules—while the workforce size \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\). Task categories recur across the population, with \(q_g\) units of category \(g\) available per worker on average. A worker still receives an integral bundle of chores; only the mass of workers assigned each bundle is continuous. Per-worker task limits \(h_g\) make the set of legal bundles finite and model ordinary workload constraints.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: given an additive-chore instance, deciding whether it has an exact PMMS allocation is NP-hard. The paper proves this by reducing from mixed goods-and-chores EFX, using its Lemma 7 to identify EFX and PMMS under lexicographical valuations and relying on the cited NP-completeness result of Hosseini et al. The theorem establishes NP-hardness, not NP-completeness; the paper notes that verifying PMMS is not known to be polynomial-time solvable.

Here is a precise continuous counterpart. An instance gives a finite set \(G\) of chore categories, a finite set \(T\) of worker types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational costs \(c_{tg}\), per-capita chore supplies \(q_g\), and integer limits \(h_g\). A legal bundle is \(b\in\prod_{g\in G}\{0,\ldots,h_g\}\). The question, Continuous Chore-PMMS Existence, is whether there are rational masses \(x_{t,b}\geq0\) such that \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=q_g\) for each chore category. Those equations assign every worker a whole bundle and use the available chores.

For \(u\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{G}\), define the chore benchmark \(\kappa_t(u)=\min_{z\in\mathbb Z^G,\ 0\leq z\leq u}\max\{c_t(z),c_t(u-z)\}\), where \(c_t(z)=\sum_g c_{tg}z_g\). For every pair of supported cohorts \((t,b)\), \((t',b')\)—meaning \(x_{t,b}>0\) and \(x_{t',b'}>0\)—require both \(c_t(b)\leq\kappa_t(b+b')\) and \(c_{t'}(b')\leq\kappa_{t'}(b+b')\). A solution is the mass table \(x\) satisfying these constraints. The union benchmark is evaluated on the combined integral bundles, just as in the paper; mass appears only in how many people receive each bundle.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity bridge for the stated model. Clearing denominators in \(\mu\), \(q\), and a rational solution \(x\) gives a finite replicated workforce and an integer chore inventory with a PMMS allocation. Conversely, a PMMS allocation of such a replicated instance gives a feasible mass table. The question is therefore whether exact PMMS feasibility changes when the number of workers is allowed to grow while the type proportions and per-capita supplies are fixed.

The second anchor is Theorem 5, also proved in the paper: deciding exact PMMS existence is NP-hard for additive goods. It follows from Theorem 4 and the chores-to-goods Karp reduction in Lemma 1, so it is related evidence rather than an independent hardness result. The corresponding Continuous Goods-PMMS Existence problem uses the same type masses, per-capita supplies, bundle limits, and mass variables, with values \(v_{tg}\). Replace the chore benchmark by \(\lambda_t(u)=\max_{0\leq z\leq u,\ z\in\mathbb Z^G}\min\{v_t(z),v_t(u-z)\}\), and require \(v_t(b)\geq\lambda_t(b+b')\) and \(v_{t'}(b')\geq\lambda_{t'}(b+b')\) for every supported pair. The solution is again a rational mass table. A large population of households with a few complete value profiles, dividing recurring categories of indivisible resources, is one plausible setting.

For both mirrors my tentative expectation is Class C, continuum-specific hardness. Once the supported bundle cohorts are fixed, the mass equations are linear; the difficult part appears to be choosing a mutually compatible support of bundles. Fractional cohort sizes remove population-count constraints but do not remove that support-selection problem. This is a hypothesis for a prover, not a consequence of Theorems 4 or 5. A useful next question is whether the support search is hard even for small \(\tau\), or whether a pricing or configuration-LP method makes it tractable.

These mirrors cover the paper’s exact-existence decision results, Theorems 4 and 5. They do not claim a continuous analogue of the nonexistence examples or the \(4/3\)-PMMS guarantee.

My weakest point is the finite per-worker bundle limit: the paper allows any bundle of its finite item set, while this scenario puts explicit workload limits on each worker. The PMMS condition itself is unchanged, and such limits are natural in task allocation, but a referee could regard this as a constrained variant rather than the unrestricted problem. More fundamentally, the paper’s reductions do not show that hardness survives when a type’s population mass can split across bundles. The continuous problem may be tractable—or even always feasible—so the discrete anchors motivate the question without settling its answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not rely on “there is no computational result to mirror”: Theorems 4 and 5 are explicit NP-hardness results for exact PMMS existence. Theorem 5 adds little independent weight because it follows from the chores result through the padding reduction, but Theorem 4 is a clear anchor.

The strongest skeptical point is that the paper’s hardness does not establish hardness, or even nontriviality, in a high-multiplicity regime. Its reductions do not show that a no-instance stays infeasible when each type’s mass can split among bundles and the inventory scales with the population. Theorem 5 inherits that gap. That undermines any claim that these results predict what the continuous answer will be.

But it does not undermine the question. A faithful mirror can assign mass to cohorts receiving *integral* bundles, preserve each type’s PMMS comparisons against every bundle in the positive-mass support, and balance per-capita supplies. Removing the proposed per-worker bundle cap would make that model closer to the paper still. Repeated worker cost profiles and recurring task categories give it a plausible high-multiplicity setting. The fact that the paper’s reductions may not survive this change is exactly a reason to study the mirror.

The remaining skeptical argument is computational: once bundles are fixed, the mass equations are linear, but the chosen support must be mutually PMMS-compatible. That can leave a difficult combinatorial search over bundle patterns; fractional masses do not automatically produce a tractable LP or pricing oracle. Yet this points toward a possible continuum-specific hardness result. The programme explicitly treats identifying such a boundary as worthwhile, so it cannot support the universal negative.

The goods anchor is weaker as a separate case: its hardness is transferred from chores, and the padding gadget’s equal-cardinality argument would need to be checked in any mass model. Still, that is a technical bridge question, not a fundamental objection to a goods mirror.

So the best case against is narrow: do not infer tractability—or inherited hardness—from Theorems 4 and 5, and do not oversell a capped-bundle formulation as the unrestricted mirror. I cannot honestly defeat the improved chores mirror. The negative case is weak because the paper has a named computational problem, and a plausible repeated-task regime makes its high-multiplicity version sensible.
