The continuous mirror question

Given a finite binary type set \(T=\{H,L\}\), a finite list of rational population states \(\mu\), and the fair-rule bundles \(x_H^\epsilon(\mu)\) and \(x_L^\epsilon(\mu)\), decide whether per-capita taxes \(p_H(\mu),p_L(\mu)\) exist such that each type prefers its truthful bundle and tax to the other report’s bundle and tax, and each type’s truthful net utility is nonnegative. Equivalently, determine whether, at every listed state, \(v_H(x_H^\epsilon(\mu))+v_L(x_L^\epsilon(\mu))-v_L(x_H^\epsilon(\mu))-v_H(x_L^\epsilon(\mu))\geq 0\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic network population with complete demand types and rational masses \(\mu_t\); the fair rule maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t g_\epsilon(v_t(x_t))\) under per-capita capacity, and each type receives its report-specific bundle and pays a per-capita tax.

The objection that survived

A zero-mass agent’s report cannot change the aggregate allocation, so the atomless incentive problem differs from the finite-agent one; the proponent flags this but does not establish that the authors would accept the resulting menu problem as theirs.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The direct mirror covers Theorem III.1’s binary DSIC-IR implementability condition. The finite-state BIC-SBB tax-feasibility question related to Theorem IV.1 is a broader extension; full Bayesian uniqueness and the numerical fairness and tax gains are not computational guarantees covered by this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the implementability characterization extend from two types to general finite type sets while retaining weighted strong budget balance?
  • What input representation permits efficient tax-feasibility algorithms when the set of population states or the fair-allocation rule is implicit?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible, though narrower than the programme’s usual target, positive case here. The paper does not prove a named polynomial-time or hardness classification. Its numbered results concern whether fair allocations can be implemented with suitable taxes. Those are still useful anchors for a continuous computational question: given a type distribution and a fair allocation rule, can we find taxes that make it incentive compatible? I would not present the paper as already establishing a complexity landscape.

A plausible regime is a large network with millions of subscribers sharing a small menu of demand curves, budgets, and outside options. Those attributes together define a type: two subscribers of the same type are indistinguishable to the allocation and mechanism. The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of subscribers of type \(t\). A per-subscriber capacity constraint is a natural co-modification: if \(x_t\) is the bundle given to each member of type \(t\), require \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t\leq B\), where \(B\) is per-capita supply. The fair allocation is then

\[ \hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\mu)\in \arg\max_{x\in X}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t g_{\epsilon}(v_t(x_t)). \]

This is the population version of the paper’s centralized objective. The continuous object is the society’s composition; the fractional \(x_t\) and weighted resource constraint follow from expressing the original allocation per capita. Taxes are also per subscriber, so strong budget balance becomes \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=0\).

My lead is Theorem IV.1, proved in this paper. It says that for a finite type space with priors over type profiles, if Condition (AB) and Condition (B) hold, then for every \(0\leq\epsilon<\epsilon_{\max}\) there are taxes \(t_i(\phi)\) that make truthful reporting a Bayesian Nash equilibrium and satisfy strong budget balance at every report profile.

Condition (AB) requires \(H(\epsilon)>0\), where

\[ H(\epsilon)= \min_i\min_{(\theta_i,\phi_i)\in F_i} \mathbb{E}_{p_i(\cdot\mid\theta_i)} [h_i(\theta_i,\phi_i,\theta_{-i},\epsilon)] \]

and

\[ h_i(\theta_i,\phi_i,\theta_{-i},\epsilon) = \sum_j \left[ v(\hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\theta_i,\theta_{-i});\theta_j) - v(\hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\phi_i,\theta_{-i});\theta_j) \right]. \]

Condition (B) is a nondegeneracy condition on the priors: for every nonzero vector \(R\) indexed by profiles, there must be no nonnegative vector \(\lambda_i(\theta_i,\phi_i)\), indexed by agents and ordered pairs of distinct types, such that for every agent \(i\) and profile \(\psi\),

\[ p_i(\psi_{-i}\mid\psi_i) \sum_{\phi_i\ne\psi_i}\lambda_i(\phi_i,\psi_i) - \sum_{\theta_i\ne\psi_i} p_i(\psi_{-i}\mid\theta_i)\lambda_i(\theta_i,\psi_i) = R(\psi). \]

The theorem’s strength for this case is that it treats general finite type sets and couples incentive compatibility to budget balance—the type-level population and weighted balance both have a direct interpretation in the proposed regime.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Continuous Fair-Allocation BIC-SBB Tax Feasibility. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), a finite explicit set of possible population states \(\Omega\), rational distributions \(\mu^\omega\) with positive mass on every type, conditional probabilities \(\pi(\omega\mid t)\), utilities \(v_t\), a fair-allocation rule \(\hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\mu^\omega)\), and binary-encoded rational input data. A mechanism observes the aggregate report distribution \(\nu\); an individual reporting \(r\) receives \(x_r^\epsilon(\nu)\) and pays \(p_r(\nu)\). A single agent has zero mass, so a unilateral report does not change \(\nu\).

The question is whether there is a tax schedule that satisfies, for every true type \(t\) and report \(r\),

\[ \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\pi(\omega\mid t) \left[v_t(x_t^\epsilon(\mu^\omega))-p_t(\mu^\omega)\right] \geq \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\pi(\omega\mid t) \left[v_t(x_r^\epsilon(\mu^\omega))-p_r(\mu^\omega)\right], \]

and, in each state,

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t^\omega p_t(\mu^\omega)=0. \]

A solution is the tax table at the listed population states, together with a budget-balanced extension off those states; no regularity of the tax function is imposed, as none is required in the paper. For explicit rational target allocations, these conditions form a finite linear-feasibility problem. The expected answer is therefore tractable in the number of types, states, and input bits; if computing the fair allocation is included, its cost must be added. Theorem IV.1 motivates this question, but it does not itself give this polynomial-time result for the continuum model. Further questions include whether the paper’s prior condition has a useful mass-distribution analogue, and what happens when the uncertainty over population states has continuous or implicit support.

A second, more exact binary anchor is Theorem III.1, also proved here. With two types per agent, it says that for any \(\epsilon\geq0\), taxes satisfying dominant-strategy incentive compatibility and ex-post individual rationality exist if and only if, for every agent \(i\) and every fixed profile of other agents’ types \(\theta_{-i}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} &v(\hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\theta_i^H,\theta_{-i});\theta_i^H) +v(\hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\theta_i^L,\theta_{-i});\theta_i^L)\\ &\quad -v(\hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\theta_i^H,\theta_{-i});\theta_i^L) -v(\hat{x}^{\epsilon}(\theta_i^L,\theta_{-i});\theta_i^H) \geq 0. \end{aligned} \]

Its mirror is Continuous Fair-Allocation Binary DSIC-IR Feasibility. For each possible aggregate state \(\nu\), take the two type-specific bundles \(x_H^\epsilon(\nu)\) and \(x_L^\epsilon(\nu)\) selected by the fair allocation rule. Decide whether there are per-subscriber taxes \(p_H(\nu),p_L(\nu)\) such that each type prefers its truthful bundle and tax to the other report’s bundle and tax, and each type’s truthful net utility is nonnegative. The solution is the two taxes for each state. The mirror’s version of the theorem’s cross-difference condition is

\[ v_H(x_H^\epsilon(\nu))+v_L(x_L^\epsilon(\nu)) -v_L(x_H^\epsilon(\nu))-v_H(x_L^\epsilon(\nu))\geq0. \]

For a finite listed set of states and rational bundle values, the question is tractable by checking this condition and constructing taxes. This is a crisp implementability mirror, though narrower than the lead because it has only two types and does not impose budget balance. Further questions include how the characterization extends to many types and whether the same tax schedule can also be balanced.

The binary theorem is the cleaner exact characterization; Theorem IV.1 is my strongest anchor because it covers general finite types and balanced taxes. Together they support a case for continuizing the population in this paper’s fairness-and-implementation problem. I would not stretch the case to cover every result: in particular, Theorem V.3’s extra continuous message concerns the mechanism’s message space, not the population, and is outside this mirror.

The weakest point is that the finite-agent theorems let one person’s report affect the allocation rule, while a person of zero mass cannot change the aggregate distribution. In the proposed mirror, the person still chooses which type-specific bundle and tax they receive, so truthfulness remains a real constraint, but the strategic externality is different. Conditions (AB) and (B), and the paper’s finite-population existence claims, do not automatically carry over. That is the main question for authors and referees: whether this atomless tax-implementation problem is a useful continuation of their problem, rather than merely a related nonatomic mechanism-design model. The large-subscriber scenario and the exact type-mass objective make that case plausible; the altered effect of an individual report keeps it from being a strong claim of direct theorem transfer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that the paper proves implementability, not computational complexity. Theorem III.1 characterizes when taxes exist for a two-type finite-agent mechanism; Theorem IV.1 gives sufficient conditions for their existence with balanced taxes. Neither states a running time, a hardness result, or a complexity classification for an encoded computational problem. Appendix C writes tax design as linear feasibility, but the paper does not analyze the cost of that computation in the relevant input parameters. So the proposed mirrors supply a new computational framing; they do not carry over a computational result from the paper.

For Theorem IV.1, the mismatch is especially clear in the assumptions doing the work. Conditions (AB) and (B) concern beliefs over finite agents’ type profiles and deviations that can change the reported profile. With a deterministic atomless society, one agent’s report leaves the aggregate distribution unchanged. BIC then becomes a type’s choice among bundles and taxes at that fixed distribution; the paper’s prior condition no longer describes the problem. The proponent restores uncertainty by adding a finite set of aggregate states and beliefs over them. That is a coherent model, but it is a substantive extension. With listed states and allocations, the tax table is a finite feasibility problem; a more ambitious version over all population distributions would need an input representation and conditions on the tax schedule that this paper does not supply. The latter may be worth studying, but it is not a computational mirror of Theorem IV.1.

Theorem III.1 fares somewhat better as a mirror, but its continuous version is a pointwise menu-implementation question. At each population state, two types compare two bundles; the cross-difference is the familiar two-type incentive condition. A stronger version could require budget balance as well, or characterize the states where the allocation rule satisfies the condition. Those are legitimate questions, but the theorem gives no complexity landscape for them. If allocations are supplied, the checks are finite; if allocations must be computed from the fair objective, the computational problem depends on how utilities and constraints are represented. Either way, the nontrivial algorithmic question would have to be formulated afresh.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large network with many subscribers sharing a small number of complete demand types. Scaling capacity per subscriber also avoids a vanishing per-agent resource level. Those facts make an atomless fair-allocation mechanism a sensible research object, and I would not argue that mass loses relevant identity or that the fair objective degenerates. They do not, however, turn this paper’s existence theorems into computational results.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo’s computational programme, not as a claim that no worthwhile continuous model exists. The proposed tax-feasibility questions may be useful new problems, especially the broader balanced-tax version, but the paper itself does not establish a computational target for them. That makes the universal negative hard to sustain: a large-network scenario and a well-posed algorithmic extension remain plausible, even though neither anchor is a direct mirror of a named complexity result.
