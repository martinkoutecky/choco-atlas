The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a natural population-level extension, but this paper has no named computational result to anchor it to. Theorem 2, proved here, says that for \(k\ge 3\) there is no continuous \(f:(S^n)^k\to S^n\) satisfying the Twin Condition. Theorem 10, also proved here, says there is no family of continuous maps \(f^{(k)}:(S^n)^k\to S^n\) satisfying the Participation Condition. Both are topological impossibility theorems. Neither asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm. The paper’s references to results such as Brandt et al.’s SAT work do not change that: they are cited background, not computational results established by this paper.

The strongest candidate for a population mirror is a measure-level Twin Condition. Let a type be a preference \(x\in S^n\), and let \(\mu\in\mathcal P(S^n)\) describe the population’s preference distribution. Moving mass \(\varepsilon\) from type \(y\) to type \(x\) gives
\[ \mu'=\mu+\varepsilon(\delta_x-\delta_y), \qquad x\ne y,\quad 0<\varepsilon\le\mu(\{y\}). \]
One could ask whether there is a weakly continuous aggregation rule \(F:\mathcal P(S^n)\to S^n\) such that
\[ d(F(\mu'),x)\le d(F(\mu),x), \]
with strict inequality whenever \(F(\mu)\ne x\). This has a plausible large-population story: many participants express preferences on the sphere, and a positive fraction changes from \(y\) to \(x\). Mass is their share of the population. In the equal-mass case, moving \(\varepsilon=1/k\) corresponds to the paper’s twin operation.

That is a coherent axiomatic extension, but it is not an eligible anchor or a computational mirror under the programme’s criteria. It replaces the paper’s finite profile by a distribution, yet asks another global existence question about an aggregation rule; it supplies no finite computational input or algorithmic task. Moreover, the proposed measure-based rule forgets agent identities and therefore imposes anonymity, while Theorem 2 does not assume anonymity. Theorem 2 would also rule out the candidate’s equal-mass finite-profile restriction, so the proposed extension is not an independent complexity result.

The paper’s continuity is already present in the map from preference profiles to social preferences. It does not study a society represented by masses over voter types. Participation is an especially awkward candidate for continuization: adding one person has vanishing effect on a normalized population distribution, so a nontrivial analogue would need to specify a positive added mass, changing the paper’s condition.

So the honest positive case is limited: the Twin Paradox suggests a natural mass-transfer question that topological social choice authors could recognize as an extension of their subject. But there are no named computational anchors, and that extension remains axiomatic rather than computational. I would not submit it as a continuous mirror for this programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that this paper proves no computational result. Theorem 2 rules out a continuous aggregation map on \((S^n)^k\) satisfying the Twin Condition; Theorem 10 rules out a family of such maps satisfying Participation. Both are existence theorems about axioms. The paper gives no algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or optimization task to continuize. The cited SAT result is background about other authors’ work, not an anchor in this paper.

The strongest Twin mirror is still easy to state. Let \(\mu\) be a finite-support distribution of preferences on \(S^n\), and move mass \(\varepsilon\) from \(y\) to \(x\), giving \(\mu'=\mu+\varepsilon(\delta_x-\delta_y)\). Ask whether a continuous rule \(F\) on such distributions can satisfy the corresponding Twin Condition. One can broaden this to general probability measures and finite mass changes. That is a coherent axiomatic sequel, and a population of people sharing preference types is a sensible regime. But its question is still whether a rule exists under a global condition. The paper supplies no representation of \(F\), no finite input for the rule itself, and no computational objective. Restricting \(F\) to a circuit or a parametric family could create an algorithmic problem, but that would add the computational object the paper lacks.

Participation admits an equally strong mass version: add a positive share \(\varepsilon\) of agents with preference \(x\), compare the outcome before and after, and ask whether their participation improves the result for them. Using positive mass handles the vanishing-effect concern; it does not make the question computational. It remains an existence question over an entire family of rules and distributions, with no finite encoding or algorithmic task specified.

This is not a collision with an existing continuous treatment: the paper’s agents remain finite in number, even though preferences and outcomes lie on a continuous sphere. Nor is the objection that high multiplicity is implausible or that the answer might be unchanged. The mass-level questions could be worthwhile for topological social choice. The negative case is strongest—and quite strong—for this programme: neither named theorem anchors a computational mirror, and turning either into one requires inventing a new representation and computational task. A broader claim that no such task could ever be worthwhile would go beyond what this paper supports.
