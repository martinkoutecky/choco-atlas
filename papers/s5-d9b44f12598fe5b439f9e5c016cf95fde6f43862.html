The continuous mirror question

Given \(s+1\) populations, each of total mass \(1\), with finite supports of rational-mass preference types, where side \(0\)'s types are partitioned into the \(m\) classes of an input Latin rectangle and type-level preferences satisfy the paper's three compatibility conditions, find a joint mass assignment \(x_q \ge 0\) over feasible type-tuples \(q\). Require each type's marginal to equal its input mass, and require that no type-tuple \(a\) has positive mass \(b_j(a;x)=\sum_{q:q_j=a_j,\ a\succ_{a_j}q}x_q\) of agents on every side who prefer \(a\) to their assigned tuple.

The model it lives in

A feasibility problem for many-sided team assignment: each side has a unit-mass society of role and preference types; the variables \(x_q\) are masses assigned to team type-profiles, with prescribed marginals and no positive-mass blocking tuple. There is no optimization objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Latin-rectangle-compatible matching problem.

A qualification about the anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its closest named computational result is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that for \(s+1\) equally sized sets \(S_0,\ldots,S_s\), a stable matching exists whenever their preferences are compatible with a Latin rectangle \(L(m,s)\), where \(m\le s\). Compatibility means that \(S_0\) splits into \(m\) nonempty classes, each with a shared lexicographic priority order over the other sides given by its row of \(L\); every agent on another side ranks agents from \(S_0\) first; and those agents’ preferences across the \(S_0\) classes follow the order in which their side appears in the columns of \(L\). Stability means that no tuple, one agent from each side, is preferred by every member to their assigned tuple. The proof gives a staged deferred-acceptance algorithm; the paper states its running time as \(O(sn^2)\). That runtime is in the text, not in a numbered complexity theorem. The introduction also mentions NP-completeness results from [6] and [14] for variants with incomplete lists or stronger stability, but those are cited results, not numbered results proved here.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. Call it Latin-Rectangle Continuous Stable Matching. There are \(s+1\) populations, one for each side, each of total mass \(1\). A type is a complete description of an agent’s role and preferences over feasible team types. For each side \(j\), the instance gives a finite support of types \(\mathcal T_j\) and rational masses \(\mu_{j,t}\) summing to \(1\). The type preferences satisfy the type-level versions of the paper’s three Latin-rectangle conditions, with \(L\) and the partition of side \(0\)’s support included in the input. Preference orders over the finite set of type-tuples are explicit.

A solution is a nonnegative rational mass \(x_q\) for each feasible tuple \(q=(t_0,\ldots,t_s)\). Its marginals must reproduce the input populations:
\[ \sum_{q:q_j=t}x_q=\mu_{j,t} \quad\text{for every side }j\text{ and type }t\in\mathcal T_j. \]
Interpret \(x_q\) as the mass of people assigned to teams with that type profile. Every person still gets a whole team; mass simply splits a large cohort into subcohorts.

Define
\[ b_j(a;x)=\sum_{\substack{q:q_j=a_j\\a\succ_{a_j}q}}x_q \]
for a proposed tuple of types \(a=(a_0,\ldots,a_s)\). The question is to find a feasible \(x\) such that there is no \(a\) with \(b_j(a;x)>0\) for every \(j\). In other words, no positive mass of each proposed type prefers that team profile to its current assignment. For integer populations with common type preferences, this agrees with the paper’s blocking-tuple test: positive mass corresponds to at least one agent on each side who wants to block.

A plausible regime is a large company or regional consortium that repeatedly assigns project teams, with one person from each of several role pools. Each pool might have thousands or tens of thousands of people, while preference types are relatively few: for example, workers evaluate team compositions through role, skill, and manager categories rather than individual names. Side \(0\) has a small number \(m\) of manager preference classes, and the priority orders and specialist preferences satisfy the Latin structure. This is a real high-multiplicity regime: the population grows while the number of complete preference types stays moderate. It also resembles an application the authors themselves give—forming project teams from managers and specialist roles—so I expect them to recognize the underlying problem.

I would tentatively expect Class A for fixed \(s\), via a mass-valued version of the paper’s staged deferred acceptance algorithm. A useful bridge is exact: for rational masses, scale by a common denominator \(N\), create \(N\) copies per side, and apply Theorem 1. Compressing the resulting matching gives a stable mass assignment. But this only gives the paper’s \(O(sN^2)\) bound in the denominator \(N\), which can be exponential in the masses’ bit length \(L\). The substantive algorithmic question is whether one can process type-level mass transfers directly, in time polynomial in the explicit preference input, the support size \(\tau=\sum_j|\mathcal T_j|\), and \(L\). A fixed number of sides makes that target credible; succinct preferences and variable \(s\) raise further questions.

The weakest point is that the paper ranks tuples of named agents. In many real settings, people have idiosyncratic preferences over particular colleagues, so the number of complete preference types may grow with the population and the mirror loses its high-multiplicity advantage. The mirror is also limited to the sufficient Latin-rectangle conditions, not arbitrary many-sided matching. I think the case survives for team-allocation settings where preferences genuinely depend on a small set of role and team categories: there, the theorem’s staged matching logic has a direct mass analogue, and the continuous society is the object being assigned.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is a structural existence theorem for a narrowly specified preference system, not a complexity result about stable matching. The paper proves existence by staged deferred acceptance and gives an \(O(sn^2)\) running time, but its central question is which rankings guarantee a stable matching. A continuous version that asks for a stable mass assignment under type-level Latin-rectangle conditions would therefore need to establish that this is still the authors’ problem, rather than merely a useful analogue.

The identity issue makes that case strongest for the paper as written. Preferences rank tuples of named people, and a blocking tuple is made of particular people who all prefer to be together. A complete type preserving those rankings can encode a person’s identity-specific comparisons; as the population grows, the number of such types may grow with it. The proposed mirror avoids that by making preferences depend on type profiles and by calling a block only when positive mass on every side wants the profile. That is coherent, but it changes both the preference domain and the blocking test. It can also miss a discrete block involving only a few people, whose share vanishes as the population grows.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A large team-allocation setting with repeated role and skill types, shared rankings over team profiles, and positive-mass blocking is plausible, and the paper itself discusses project teams. Under that interpretation, mass is meaningful, the Latin conditions still make sense, and the staged matching construction has a natural continuous counterpart. I cannot honestly argue that no such scenario exists. The negative case is therefore weak: the paper’s named result is not itself a complexity classification, and an identity-faithful limit is problematic, but the improved type-profile mirror remains a credible question the authors might recognize.
