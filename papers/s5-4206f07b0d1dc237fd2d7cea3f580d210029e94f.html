The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), an integer budget \(K\), a finite support \(T\) of nonempty approval types \(A_t\subseteq C\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational fractional committee \(x\in[0,1]^C\) with \(\|x\|_1\le K\), decide whether there is an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|\le K\) such that \(|W\cap A_t|\ge\left\lfloor\sum_{c\in A_t}x_c\right\rfloor\) for every \(t\in T\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over approval types; every member of a type has the same approval set and receives the same utility from the shared committee. The decision variable is one integral committee \(W\), constrained by the supplied fractional benchmark’s utility floor for every supported type.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 23’s floor-preserving rounding problem and its connection to the rounding route in Lemma 7. It does not establish anything about discrete-core nonemptiness, Nash-core computation, or the paper’s heuristics.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity in support size \(\tau\) and the number of fractional candidates?
  • Can the paper’s exact-cover hardness be sharpened under restrictions on approval-type patterns while retaining a high-multiplicity society?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s floor-preserving rounding problem. Proposition 23, “NP-completeness of floor-preserving rounding,” is proved in this paper. It says that, given an approval election \((N,C,(A_i)_{i\in N},K)\) and a rational fractional committee \(x\in[0,1]^C\) with \(\|x\|_1\le K\), it is NP-complete to decide whether there is an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|\le K\) such that
\[ |W\cap A_i|\ \ge\ \left\lfloor\sum_{c\in A_i}x_c\right\rfloor \qquad\text{for every voter }i. \]
The paper proves this by reducing from Restricted Exact Cover by 3-Sets. In its construction, candidates represent triples, voters represent elements and private constraints, and \(x_c=1/3\) for every candidate. The authors also show that this particular \(x\) is a fractional Nash-core allocation.

Here is the continuous-population problem I would hand to a prover: Type Floor-Rounding. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), an integer budget \(K\), a finite support \(T\) of nonempty approval sets \(A_t\subseteq C\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational fractional committee \(x\in[0,1]^C\) with \(\|x\|_1\le K\). The society is the distribution \(\mu\): mass \(\mu_t\) consists of agents with approval type \(A_t\). Decide whether there is an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|\le K\), satisfying
\[ |W\cap A_t|\ \ge\ \left\lfloor\sum_{c\in A_t}x_c\right\rfloor \qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
A solution is the committee \(W\). This is a feasibility question, so it has no separate optimization objective. It is NP-complete: the discrete instances in Proposition 23 map to distributions by setting \(\mu_t\) to each type’s fraction of the electorate. Merging identical ballots loses no constraint. The reduction’s hard instances even have a fractional Nash-core benchmark.

A plausible regime is a large public election with many thousands of voters but a much smaller, recurring repertoire of approval ballots: \(n\gg\tau\), where \(\tau=|T|\). Each type has a positive population mass, and every member of that type receives the same utility from the shared committee. The decision asks whether one actual committee can preserve the integer-part representation promised by a supplied fractional benchmark for every ballot type. This is a faithful mirror of the paper’s rounding question: only the population representation changes, while the shared committee and each type’s per-person guarantee remain intact. Repeating every voter \(R\) times leaves \(\mu\) and the answer unchanged while making every type high-multiplicity; it also preserves the reduction’s Nash-core benchmark, since replication scales the Nash objective without changing its maximizers.

I expect Class B: hardness transfers. The reduction’s combinatorics lie in the candidate and approval-incidence structure used to encode exact cover. Replacing voter identities with approval types and rational masses retains that structure. This gives useful follow-up questions about fixed-parameter algorithms in \(\tau\) or the number of fractional candidates, and about what additive loss from the floor guarantees permits efficient rounding.

The paper’s authors should recognize this as their own underlying problem in a high-multiplicity regime, especially because their hardness construction already supplies a Nash-core benchmark. My weakest point is that the masses do not affect Type Floor-Rounding’s yes/no answer once the support and \(x\) are fixed; they identify which types must be protected. So this is a clean continuous-population mirror, but not a mass-sensitive complexity phenomenon. Its scope is Proposition 23 and the paper’s floor-rounding route. It says nothing by itself about discrete-core nonemptiness or the hardness of finding a discrete-core committee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 23 gives this mirror a real foothold: it is a named complexity result, and the proposed type formulation is well defined. Collapsing identical ballots preserves every constraint. The reduction also survives a high-multiplicity interpretation: replicate each voter many times, leaving the type masses unchanged. Its \(x_c=1/3\) benchmark remains Nash-optimal when the voter objective is written using those masses. So I cannot rule it out on the grounds that the paper lacks a computational result, that multiplicity is impossible, or that the hardness fails to transfer.

The strongest case against spending ChoCo effort on it is that the transferred problem does not use the population masses. A type with arbitrarily small positive mass imposes exactly the same floor constraint as a type with a large mass. The continuous input therefore contributes only the support; computationally, this is a set-cover-style rounding problem over approval patterns. If there are \(\tau\) types, candidates can be grouped into at most \(2^\tau\) approval patterns, yielding a fixed-dimensional integer program when \(\tau\) is fixed. The high-multiplicity regime may thus be easy precisely when the support is small, while the NP-hardness requires the number of types to grow.

Making the question depend on mass—for example, allowing a small fraction of society to miss its floor guarantee—would make it more distinctively population-based, but it would no longer be Proposition 23’s problem. The paper’s floor-rounding lemma needs the guarantee for every voter; relaxing it changes the certificate and its connection to core stability. Letting different portions of a type receive different committees would change the shared-committee model as well.

That is a limitation on the mirror’s distinctiveness, not a decisive objection to its worth. A type-compressed formulation and its parameterized complexity could still be useful, and the programme explicitly treats hardness that transfers as a legitimate outcome. The honest negative case is weak here: the anchor survives.
