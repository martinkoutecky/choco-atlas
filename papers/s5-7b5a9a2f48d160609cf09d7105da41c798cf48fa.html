The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(G\) with \(|G|=m\), a finite set \(T\) of agent types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), a matroid \(\mathcal M_t\) on \(G\) and rational entitlement \(w_t>0\) for each type, and per-capita capacities \(q_g\ge 0\), choose masses \(x_{t,I}\ge 0\) assigned to independent bundles \(I\in\mathcal I(\mathcal M_t)\). Require \(\sum_{I\in\mathcal I(\mathcal M_t)}x_{t,I}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\) and \(\sum_{t,I:g\in I}x_{t,I}\le q_g\) for each \(g\). Let \(D=\{r/w_t:t\in T,\ 0\le r\le m\}\), ordered increasingly, and let \(H_x(d)=\sum_{t,I:\,|I|/w_t=d}x_{t,I}\). Find a feasible allocation whose vector \((H_x(d))_{d\in D}\) is lexicographically smallest.

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents has type distribution \(\mu\); each type fixes both its matroid rank valuation and entitlement. Decision variables \(x_{t,I}\) assign mass to integral independent bundles, subject to per-capita course capacities. The objective lexicographically minimizes the mass at each possible normalized utility level \(|I|/w_t\), from lowest to highest.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.3, weighted leximin allocation for matroid rank valuations; it leaves the general framework, strategyproofness, weighted Nash welfare, other fairness objectives, and envy results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this lexicographic configuration problem be solved in polynomial time in \(m\), \(|T|\), input bit length, and matroid-oracle costs, using sequential linear programs and cardinality-constrained matroid pricing?
  • What precise encoding and finite-instance scaling assumptions give a formal high-multiplicity correspondence between the population objective and weighted leximin?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper offers no named hardness result to carry into the continuum. Its strongest positive anchors are the weighted leximin and weighted Nash welfare results. I think both have credible population mirrors, with weighted leximin the cleaner lead.

Consider a large course allocation system with many students but relatively few complete preference-and-constraint types. A type \(t\) specifies both a matroid rank valuation \(v_t(S)=r_{\mathcal M_t}(S)\) over the \(m\) course types and an entitlement \(w_t>0\). Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students of that type. Course seats scale with the population: \(q_g\) is the number of seats of course \(g\) per student. For example, a system might have tens of thousands of students but only hundreds of combinations of matroid and entitlement. That is plausible for recurring cohorts with shared eligibility and schedule constraints, though it would fail if those complete descriptions made nearly every student a distinct type.

A continuous allocation is a population partition, not a lottery. For each type \(t\) and independent bundle \(I\) of \(\mathcal M_t\), let \(x_{t,I}\) be the fraction of the whole population of type \(t\) assigned that integral bundle. Require \(\sum_I x_{t,I}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,I:g\in I}x_{t,I}\le q_g\) for each course \(g\). Every unit of mass receives an integral bundle; splitting a type’s mass among bundles is possible because the population is nonatomic. Restricting to independent bundles loses nothing for these objectives: redundant goods can be removed without lowering anyone’s rank utility, freeing seats.

The seat scaling is a permitted co-modification: with \(N\) students, the corresponding discrete instance has \(N\mu_t\) students of type \(t\) and \(Nq_g\) seats of course \(g\), when those counts are integral. The continuum model relaxes the mass assignments while keeping each person’s bundle integral.

Lead mirror — weighted leximin. Theorem 6.3, proved in this paper, says that for matroid rank valuations and positive weights \(w_i\), General Yankee Swap with gain function \(\phi(X,i)=(-v_i(X_i)/w_i,-w_i)\) computes a weighted leximin allocation: it lexicographically maximizes the utilities divided by the corresponding weights, sorted in ascending order.

Here is a continuous version of that problem. Let \(D=\{r/w_t:t\in T,\ 0\le r\le m\}\) be the possible normalized utility levels, sorted as \(d_1<\cdots<d_s\). For an allocation \(x\), define \(H_x(d)=\sum_{t,I:\,|I|/w_t=d}x_{t,I}\), the mass receiving normalized utility \(d\). Find a feasible allocation whose vector \((H_x(d_1),\ldots,H_x(d_s))\) is lexicographically smallest. This is the continuum form of sorted leximin: at the first utility level where two populations differ, the one with less mass stuck at that level has the better sorted utility profile. When masses are multiples of \(1/N\), this ordering agrees with leximin on the corresponding finite population.

I expect this problem to be Class A. It is a sequence of linear programs over variables indexed by type and independent bundle. There may be exponentially many bundle variables, but the number of utility levels is at most \(\tau(m+1)\). Pricing a bundle reduces to considering each possible bundle size and finding a minimum-cost independent set of that size in the type’s matroid, which matroid optimization can solve efficiently. This gives a concrete route to a polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\), with matroid-oracle costs accounted for. The continuous question is the same fairness objective over the same agents and valuations; it does not replace individual fairness with fairness of type averages.

Second mirror — max weighted Nash welfare. Theorem 6.6, also proved here, says that for matroid rank valuations and positive weights \(w_i\), General Yankee Swap with \(\phi(X,i)=(1+1/v_i(X_i))^{w_i}\) when \(v_i(X_i)>0\), and a sufficiently large \(M\) when \(v_i(X_i)=0\), computes a max weighted Nash welfare allocation. The objective first minimizes the number of zero-utility agents, then maximizes \(\prod_{i\in P_X}v_i(X_i)^{w_i}\), where \(P_X\) is the set of agents with positive utility.

For the same continuous instance, first maximize the positive-utility mass \(\sum_{t,I\ne\varnothing}x_{t,I}\); among those allocations, maximize \(\exp(\sum_{t,I\ne\varnothing}w_t x_{t,I}\log |I|)\). This is the population-scaled analogue of the paper’s objective: maximize the share of people served before maximizing the weighted geometric welfare of their integral bundles. I also expect Class A. Both stages are linear objectives over the configuration allocation polytope, with pricing reducible to minimum-cost independent sets by bundle size. A formal bit-complexity result would need to handle exact comparisons involving \(\log r\), or specify a precision guarantee; that is an additional detail to settle.

These mirrors cover the paper’s weighted leximin and weighted Nash results, not its whole framework, strategyproofness theorem, or envy questions. The paper’s stated finite-instance runtime analysis makes the anchors constructive; it does not itself establish either continuous algorithm.

The weakest point is the type regime. A student’s full matroid and entitlement must match those of many others, and course seat supplies must grow with population size. If real systems have highly individualized constraints, the support \(\tau\) may approach the population size and the intended computational advantage disappears. Also, the continuous LPs and their pricing algorithms are new results to prove; they do not follow automatically from General Yankee Swap. Those are substantial qualifications, but they leave a precise high-multiplicity question whose solutions preserve integral bundles and the paper’s own fairness objectives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest skeptical case is about how much of the paper a mirror would capture. Its contribution is a mechanism built around path augmentations, with utilitarian optimality and strategyproofness alongside its fairness guarantees. The proposed continuous questions retain the fairness objectives but discard the mechanism and its incentive guarantee. So they would mirror the paper’s output criteria, not its full algorithmic contribution. The paper does give clear computational anchors—weighted leximin in Theorem 6.3 and max weighted Nash welfare in Theorem 6.6—so the “no named computational result” objection cannot carry the case against them. ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07311))

The type regime also looks narrow. A course-allocation type must fix a student’s whole matroid and entitlement. Eligibility, completed courses, and schedule conflicts can make those descriptions vary widely, pushing the support size toward the population size. Scaling course capacities with the population is a coherent model, but it describes a growing or replicated course system. These requirements make the application case fragile.

That still does not defeat either anchor. In a low-support regime, the leximin histogram is a natural population version of the finite sorted utility vector: when masses are multiples of \(1/N\), minimizing mass at successive low utility levels reproduces the finite ordering. The Nash objective also has a direct population scaling: positive-utility mass corresponds to the finite count of served agents, and weighted log welfare corresponds to the normalized log of the finite product. The logarithmic coefficients raise a bit-complexity question, but that is a technical issue to resolve, not a reason to reject the model.

I cannot make the negative case decisive without relying on objections the brief rules out. The lost strategyproofness and narrow type regime are real costs, but neither defeats a well-posed allocation mirror. Both proposed anchors survive; the skeptical case is weaker.
