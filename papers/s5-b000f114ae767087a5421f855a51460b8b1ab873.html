The continuous mirror question

Given two atomless populations partitioned into finitely many types, with rational type masses, integer quotas, and strict rankings over opposite-side types, compute a stable matching. Each individual must receive an integral bundle of distinct partners within quota; the aggregate description may assign mass to different integral bundles. Stability requires that no pair of types has positive mass on both sides who would prefer to match with one another over a current partner or vacancy. Can such a matching always be computed in time polynomial in the number of types \(\tau\), maximum quota \(Q\), and input bit length \(L\), with a polynomial-size aggregate description?

The model it lives in

Two atomless populations with rational masses over finitely many complete types. Each type has an integer quota and a strict ranking of opposite-side types; a matching is represented by masses assigned to integral partner-count vectors, with feasible marginals and no positive-mass blocking type pair. The objective is to find any stable matching.

The objection that survived

Proposition 5 gives termination and correctness but no running-time bound, so the proposed polynomial-time question extends beyond the computational guarantee proved in the paper.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 5’s stable-matching construction for two-sided quotas. It leaves Proposition 6’s proposer-optimality, the classical marriage and college-admission results, and the application’s filtering, manual co-advisor assignment, and reruns unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every such market admit a polynomial-size exact aggregate representation of a stable matching, and can one be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(Q\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is Mass-Stable Many-to-Many Matching. The anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper: PolyGS terminates and returns a valid, stable matching in the authors’ two-sided quota model. “Valid” means each person stays within quota and no pair is matched more than once; “stable” means there is no unmatched pair who each prefer one another to a current partner or an empty slot. The paper proves this for its generalized Gale–Shapley algorithm. It does not give a named theorem placing the problem in P or proving a complexity lower bound, so this is an algorithmic correctness anchor, not a complexity-class result.

Here is a continuous version that keeps the matching question intact. There are two atomless populations, each partitioned into finitely many types. The instance gives each type a rational mass, an integer quota, and a strict ranking of the types on the other side, with remaining capacity treated as being matched to oneself. All members of a type share that quota and ranking; members of the same opposite-side type are interchangeable. Preferences over bundles are responsive: an agent prefers replacing a current partner or vacant slot with a more-preferred acceptable type. A solution is a measurable matching in which each agent receives an integral bundle of distinct people, within quota. Its finite aggregate description gives, for each type, the mass receiving each possible vector of partner-type counts. Those vectors are integral; their mass-weighted averages can be fractional. The description must be realizable by a matching and stable: for any pair of types, there cannot be positive mass on both sides whose members would prefer one another to some current partner or vacancy. The task is to compute such a stable matching from the rational masses, quotas, and rankings.

This fits a plausible high-multiplicity regime for large assignment systems—such as assigning many applicants to several evaluators each—when preferences are generated by a relatively small set of shared profiles. The population might be very large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct complete types is much smaller. “Complete” matters here: people are grouped only when they share the preferences and quotas the model uses. The paper’s actual ELLIS cohort of roughly 500 students is not itself compelling evidence of a continuum regime; the stronger case is the broader many-to-many model the authors introduce and apply across selection phases.

I would expect this problem to be tractable (Class A), through a mass version of deferred acceptance. The finite number of types and quotas offers a route to aggregate proposals and acceptances while preserving integral bundles for individuals. The concrete computational question is whether one can produce a stable plan in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the maximum quota \(Q\), the input bit length \(L\), and the output description size—and whether a polynomial-size description always suffices. Proposition 5 motivates that question but does not establish its answer for continuous masses.

The scope is the stable many-to-many matching engine underlying the paper’s algorithmic phases. It does not claim to mirror the manual co-advisor assignment or the application-specific removals and reruns. Nor does it assert a continuous analogue of Proposition 6’s proposer-optimality result: that result assumes strict preferences over individuals, while the type model deliberately makes same-type partners interchangeable.

The weak point is that the paper’s markets rank named people, whereas this mirror groups people by shared rankings and quotas. That is a substantive instance regime, not just a change of notation. I think the authors could recognize it as their matching problem in a high-multiplicity setting because the core objects—two sides, quotas, acceptable partners, blocking pairs, and stable outcomes—remain in place, and the paper itself permits tied preferences. But the continuous mass algorithm and its complexity are open questions raised by the mirror, not results of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Proposition 5 is a weak anchor for a computational-complexity programme. It proves that PolyGS terminates and returns a valid stable matching; it does not classify the complexity of a problem or give a complexity bound for the two-sided-quota algorithm. The paper’s explicit \(O(|M||W|)\) bound is for the classical marriage-market algorithm in the appendix. So the proposed mirror asks a real computational question, but it is not a direct mirror of a complexity result in this paper.

The ELLIS application also gives little evidence for a high-multiplicity regime. Students submit preferences involving named advisors; advisor rankings use applicant-specific scores; capacities and eligibility change across phases. If those details define a complete type, the number of types may grow with the cohort. Advisors are scarce named people, too, which makes turning both sides into atomless populations a stretch for this application. A continuum of applicants facing a finite set of advisors is more plausible, but that is the familiar admissions setting: continuum models already characterize stable outcomes through market-clearing conditions, and later work gives a deferred-acceptance analogue. [Azevedo and Leshno’s model](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/), [Arnosti’s continuum model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881).

The proponent’s stronger version—two atomless populations, finitely many shared types, quotas, and aggregate descriptions that preserve integral bundles—is coherent. It also changes the paper’s preference representation from rankings over named people to rankings over interchangeable types. That change is reasonable, but it means the case for studying the mass model comes from the broader matching problem, rather than from a distinctive computational obstacle identified by PolyGS. Continuum work has already studied many-to-many markets with a continuum of each type and established stable-outcome existence under conditions such as substitutability; that supports the model’s legitimacy, though it does not settle its exact finite-input complexity. [Azevedo and Hatfield](https://eduardomazevedo.github.io/papers/Azevedo%20Hatfield%20-%20Existence%20of%20Stable%20Matchings%20with%20Complements.pdf).

That last gap is where the negative case fails. Existing continuous results do not answer the proposed complexity question, and the atomless, finite-type many-to-many version could be a sensible object. I can argue that Proposition 5 does not itself motivate a strong complexity mirror, and that the ELLIS use case is poorly suited to one. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario.
