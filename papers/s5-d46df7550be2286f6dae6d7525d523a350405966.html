The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(V\), complete bidder types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), monotone supermodular quadratic utilities \(f_t\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\in[0,1]\), compute a welfare-maximizing mass assignment \(y_{t,S}\ge 0\) to integral bundles \(S\subseteq V\). Maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq V}y_{t,S}f_t(S)\), subject to \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni v}y_{t,S}=q_v\) for every \(v\); determine the exact complexity in \(n=|V|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A configuration LP over complete bidder types \(t\in T\), population masses \(\mu_t\), and integral bundles \(S\subseteq V\), with assignment masses \(y_{t,S}\), welfare objective \(\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}f_t(S)\), type-mass constraints, and per-capita item-supply constraints \(q_v\). Its pricing problem for type \(t\) maximizes \(f_t(S)-\sum_{v\in S}p_v\), equivalent to submodular minimization for supermodular quadratic \(f_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.3’s supermodular quadratic allocation result; it leaves the submodular results, gross-substitutes algorithm, two-bidder case, and approximation results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the continuous welfare optimum be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(n\), \(\tau\), and \(L\) using submodular pricing and separation?
  • What additive approximation or rounding guarantees relate this optimum to finite replicated auctions with bidder counts and item supplies scaled by population?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the supermodular case. Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper, says that optimal allocation with supermodular quadratic utilities is NP-hard when there are more than two bidders, even when there are exactly three. The underlying problem is: given items \(V\) and bidders with monotone quadratic utilities
\[ f_i(S)=\sum_{\{u,v\}\subseteq S}a_i(u,v)+\sum_{v\in S}b_i(v), \]
choose a partition of \(V\) among the bidders maximizing total utility, where supermodularity means \(a_i(u,v)\ge 0\). The paper proves the hardness by reducing from multiway uncut.

Here is a continuous version I think the authors could recognize as their welfare maximization problem. Consider a large population of bidders, divided into a finite support \(T\) of complete valuation types. A type specifies all the coefficients \(a_t(u,v)\) and \(b_t(v)\); \(\mu_t\) is its population fraction, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). There are \(n\) standardized item categories, each supplied at a rational per-capita rate \(q_v\in[0,1]\). An individual still receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq V\); the continuum lets a type’s mass be distributed across different bundles.

Lead problem — Continuous Supermodular Quadratic Allocation. Given \(V,T,\mu,q\), and monotone supermodular quadratic utilities \(f_t\), choose nonnegative masses \(y_{t,S}\), where \(y_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\), to maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq V} y_{t,S}f_t(S) \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq V}y_{t,S}=\mu_t\quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni v}y_{t,S}=q_v\quad(v\in V). \]
A solution is an optimal mass assignment to integral bundles. This directly mirrors the paper’s objective and bundle utilities; the per-capita supply is the co-modification needed to keep the market meaningful as the population grows.

A plausible regime is a procurement platform serving many similar branches or production sites, with a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of standard facility types. Sites of a given type have identical preferences over bundles of standardized inputs, including the same complementarities. The number of sites can be very large while \(\tau\) remains moderate. The item catalog and each site’s bundle choices remain discrete; only the population and its aggregate assignment are continuous.

For this mirror, I expect tractability: the exponential-variable configuration LP has a pricing problem for each type. Given item prices \(p_v\), pricing asks for a bundle maximizing \(f_t(S)-\sum_{v\in S}p_v\). Its negative is the submodular function
\[ \sum_{v\in S}\bigl(p_v-b_t(v)\bigr)-\sum_{\{u,v\}\subseteq S}a_t(u,v), \]
so pricing is polynomial-time submodular minimization. Separation and the ellipsoid method then give a polynomial-time algorithm in \(n,\tau\), and the rational input bit length. The discrete NP-hardness in Theorem 4.3 would thus dissolve in this continuous population model: the theorem’s finite partition choices become distributions of population mass across whole bundles.

That is a meaningful question to put to the authors because it retains the auctioneer’s welfare objective, the bidders’ quadratic complementarities, and individual bundle integrality. The further questions are whether finite high-multiplicity instances approximate this LP with useful rounding bounds, and how the picture changes when the number of valuation types grows with the population.

My weakest point is the inventory scaling. The paper’s hardness result is about allocating a finite set of items among three named bidders; this mirror instead considers repeated bidder types and supply that scales with population. Theorem 4.3 does not itself establish a discrete-to-continuous identity, and its reduction does not automatically prove hardness of this LP. The case rests on that population-and-supply regime being a sensible version of their allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to Theorem 4.3 is that its hard instance is a one-off auction: three named bidders divide a finite set of items, and the reduction encodes multiway uncut in those particular bundle choices. The proposed mirror replaces that setting with a replicated market. It needs many sites with identical valuation types, plus a supply of each item category that grows with the population. That is a substantial change in the economic setting, especially when the motivating goods are unique licenses or parcels of land.

But that objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. Many sites can plausibly buy standardized inputs from a small catalog, with a modest number of complete valuation types. Scaling the stock of each input with the number of sites is the natural way to keep per-site supply meaningful. The mass assignment still gives each site an integral bundle, and the objective still sums the same quadratic utilities. This fits the programme’s allowance for supply to scale with population, and the paper’s finite-bidder setup does not rule out that regime.

Nor does the mirror lose the computational question merely because its allocation is a configuration LP. Given item prices, its pricing problem is polynomial-time submodular minimization for these supermodular quadratic utilities. That may make the mirror tractable, but tractability is a possible result of continuization, not a reason the question is unworthy. The resulting model also supplies a plausible high-multiplicity interpretation of the theorem’s welfare objective.

So my negative case is weak. The paper does not establish that this replicated-site regime matters to its authors, and the proponent’s version is narrower than the original class of auctions. But I cannot rule out a convincing scenario, or show that the needed supply scaling makes the model nonsensical. Theorem 4.3 remains a viable mirror anchor.
