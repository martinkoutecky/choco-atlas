For Theorem 1.1, the strongest candidate is to repeat the two near-equal-budget agents across a large population and ask for a competitive equilibrium. If each type’s population receives the original per-capita supply, repeating the two agents’ allocation repeats the original market; the continuous formulation adds no new computational question. With fixed supply, the share of goods per person vanishes as the population grows, so the paper’s allocation and fairness question loses its population-level meaning. Adding more agent types could produce a substantial market-computation problem, but the theorem no longer anchors it: the paper’s two-agent characterization does not extend, and it explicitly identifies the multi-agent case as qualitatively harder.

Theorem 1.4 has the same boundary. Clones of its two preference-and-budget types reproduce the original market when supplies scale per capita. A richer distribution of budgets or preferences creates a different market class; the theorem only establishes generic existence for two agents with identical preferences.

Proposition 3.2 is the strongest candidate for a high-multiplicity regime: it holds for any number of agents, and grouping agents by complete type is natural. But it is a fairness implication for an equilibrium that already exists, proved by a price bound and a pigeonhole argument. The population continuum does not create a computational task in that result. Computing the maximin share would introduce a separate partition problem; computing an equilibrium would likewise be a new algorithmic question, rather than a mirror of the proposition.

A computational study of equilibria in high-multiplicity markets could still be worthwhile, and the paper’s computational appendix makes that possibility concrete. The negative case is weaker if “mirror” includes any new algorithmic question suggested by the paper.
