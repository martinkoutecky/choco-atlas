The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, proposition, or other named computational result asserting a complexity or algorithmic classification. Its numbered Definitions 1–3 define sincere ballots; its computational work enumerates ballots for six candidates and compares the resulting predictions with survey data. The reported behavioral and aggregate findings are empirical results, not complexity results. So there is no honest way to meet the requested “one continuous problem per anchor” requirement for this paper.

The strongest follow-on mirror I can see would take a voter’s type to be the payoff vector \(u\in\{0,\ldots,10\}^{6}\). In a large electorate, let \(\mu_u\) be the fraction with each vector, and let \(x_{u,v}\) be the mass of type \(u\) assigned ballot \(v\). For a specified voting rule and the paper’s fixed poll-based tie probabilities \(p_{ij}\), require each type’s ballots to maximize the paper’s instrumental utility \(G_p(v,u)\). Then ask which candidates can win under the resulting aggregate scores \(\sum_{u,v}x_{u,v}v_i\), allowing the mass of a type to split across its optimal ballots. This preserves the paper’s strategic-behavior model and its aggregate-outcome question; it does not make outcomes or beliefs the continuous object.

That is a plausible research question, but it is not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The regime is also only prospective: the study found 445 distinct payoff types among 455 valid respondents, so its sample itself gives little evidence of high multiplicity. The paper does not establish how the proposed problem scales or whether its Borda case is tractable or hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its numbered items are definitions of sincere ballots; its results compare observed behavior with behavioral benchmarks in a survey. It formulates no algorithmic problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic classification. No choice of population model can supply the missing anchor.

The proponent’s \(\mu_u\) model is a plausible research lead. The payoff vectors form a finite type space, so a larger electorate could have genuine high multiplicity; the fact that this sample had 445 distinct types among 455 respondents does not rule that out. But asking which winners are possible under typewise optimal ballots changes the paper’s question. The paper compares individual submitted ballots and aggregate scores with two particular benchmarks. It does not ask for the set of outcomes compatible with every mixture of optimal ballots.

There is also a modeling fork in the instrumental benchmark. Its pivotality weights \(p_{ij}\) come from poll scores and represent finite-election beliefs. Holding them fixed leaves a population-mass calculation conditional on external weights. Deriving incentives from an atomless society instead makes a single voter’s ballot have zero mass; restoring the finite pivotality effect would require additional sampling or noise assumptions, or coordinated type blocs. Those may support worthwhile new work, but they do not recover a computational result from this paper.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo mirror, while treating it as a possible new research lead. The broader claim that no useful continuous voting model could be built in any scenario would be too strong: the finite payoff-type space gives a credible high-multiplicity setting. The case against is strongest on the narrower, decisive point that this paper offers no computational result for such a mirror to preserve.
