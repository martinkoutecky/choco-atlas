The continuous mirror question

Given \(\mu\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-capita weights \(a_t>0\), a target type \(t^\star\), and threshold \(\theta\), form \(n_t=N\mu_t\) interchangeable voters of each type for denominator-compatible \(N\), apply the paper's weighted-median rule and Definition 6.2, aggregate \(P_{t^\star}^{(N)}=\sum_{i\in t^\star}\operatorname{SSI}_i\), and decide whether \(\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{t^\star}^{(N)}\ge\theta\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity weighted-median society with type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita weights \(a_t\), and \(N\mu_t\) exchangeable copies per type; the objective is the aggregate Shapley–Shubik power carried by a type.

The objection that survived

The \(\tau\)-player type-block formula changes the random order and can disagree with the aggregate of \(N\) individual powers, as with \(91\) voters of type \(A\) and \(10\) of type \(B\), so that displayed formula is not itself the high-multiplicity mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified. The paper does not contain a named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or approximation result. Its named results are structural lemmas, axiomatic properties, and conjectures. Thus it has no fully compliant ChoCo computational anchor. The best available anchor is a computational identity.

My lead anchor is Lemma 6.10, stated in the paper without proof. It says that, for a weighted-median aggregation rule with voter weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\), assuming no subset of the weights sums to \(\|w\|_1/2\), the continuous Shapley–Shubik index defined in Definition 6.2 is exactly the ordinary Shapley–Shubik index of the weighted binary game \([\|w\|_1/2;w_1,\ldots,w_n]\). The paper proves no general algorithmic complexity consequence from this lemma; its appendix only works out examples.

A plausible population mirror is the following.

Consider a large electorate divided into finitely many homogeneous types \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). Type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and every member of the type has the same per-capita voting weight \(a_t\), report protocol, and tie-breaking convention. Its total voting weight is \(W_t=\mu_t a_t\), with \(W=\sum_tW_t\). The intended regime is \(N\) very large and \(\tau\) small: for example, a national tax or spending electorate with millions of voters but a few dozen recurring socioeconomic or ideological types.

A type-level policy profile is \(x=(x_1,\ldots,x_\tau)\in[0,1]^\tau\). The collective policy is the weighted median of the type masses: define \(L_W(x)\) as the smallest \(z\) with \(\sum_{t:x_t\le z}W_t\ge W/2\), and \(U_W(x)\) as the largest \(z\) with \(\sum_{t:x_t\ge z}W_t\ge W/2\). Set \(M_W(x)=L_W(x)\) when these coincide and \(M_W(x)=(L_W(x)+U_W(x))/2\) otherwise. This is exactly the paper’s weighted-median rule, with named voters replaced by population mass.

For a permutation \(\pi\) of the types, reveal the types in that order. Before a type is revealed, complete the unrevealed types once at policy \(1\) and once at policy \(0\). If type \(t\) is revealed at position \(r\), let \(x^{\pi,+,r}\) and \(x^{\pi,-,r}\) denote those two completions. Its output-interval contribution is

\(\delta^\pi_t(x)=M_W(x^{\pi,+,r-1})-M_W(x^{\pi,+,r})+M_W(x^{\pi,-,r})-M_W(x^{\pi,-,r-1})\).

Define the type’s population Shapley–Shubik power by

\(\Phi_t(\mu,a)=\frac{1}{\tau!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal S_\tau}\int_{[0,1]^\tau}\delta^\pi_t(x)\,dx\).

The decision problem is:

Given rational \(\mu\), rational positive weights \(a\), a target type \(t^\star\), and a rational threshold \(\theta\), decide whether \(\Phi_{t^\star}(\mu,a)\ge\theta\); in the search version, output the complete vector \(\Phi(\mu,a)\).

This is not merely “the outcome is continuous.” The policy interval \([0,1]\) is inherited from the paper, but the main continuization is the electorate: the input is a distribution \(\mu\) over interchangeable voter types. The action is a type’s policy report, the decision is the weighted-median policy, and the objective is the aggregate power of a positive-mass type.

The authors should recognize this as their problem rather than an easier substitute. It retains their weighted-median aggregation rule, their output-interval interpretation of pivotality, their random-order construction, and their continuous Shapley–Shubik definition. A rational vector \(\mu\) is also the normalized multiplicity vector of a finite electorate: \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). The natural algorithmic question is whether the computation depends on the enormous population size \(N\), or only on the number of distinct types \(\tau\) and the encoding length of the masses.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable in the intended fixed-type regime: the weighted-median map is piecewise linear, and the integral can in principle be decomposed over finitely many coordinate-order regions. At minimum, enumeration gives an algorithm parameterized by \(\tau\), independent of \(N\). With \(\tau\) variable, the weighted-game identity in Lemma 6.10 suggests a harder boundary: exact power computation may retain combinatorial difficulty in the type weights. Thus my expected classification is Class A for bounded \(\tau\), with the variable-\(\tau\) complexity left as a genuine follow-up question.

This covers only Lemma 6.10 and the continuous SSI construction around Definition 6.2. I would not stretch the case to cover the Banzhaf and nucleolus sections: Lemmas 6.7 and 6.15 establish axiomatic properties, while Conjectures 6.8, 6.9, 6.16, and 6.21 are explicitly conjectural rather than computational results.

The weakest point is serious. The paper studies the power of named individuals, whereas the mirror studies the power of a homogeneous type-block. Literal individual power becomes degenerate in an atomless population, so aggregation or rescaling is necessary; the paper does not provide that construction. Moreover, after contraction to \(\tau\) type-blocks, Lemma 6.10 reduces the question to a weighted game, which an opponent could call a compressed finite reformulation rather than a new continuous-computation problem.

That objection limits the strength of the case, but does not destroy its substantive plausibility. The paper genuinely supplies the weighted-median power concept, and its finite-\(n\) formulation leaves population multiplicity entirely discrete. The proposed mirror asks whether the same power question remains meaningful and algorithmically manageable when millions of interchangeable voters are represented by rational masses. It is therefore a credible continuous-population research question, but not a strong formal ChoCo case under the programme’s strict named-complexity-anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s entry gate: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Definition 6.2 is a definition, and Lemma 6.10 is a structural identity. Neither states a complexity bound, an algorithm, a hardness result, an approximation guarantee, or even a computational decision problem. The remaining named results are axiomatic lemmas, structural properties, and conjectures. Thus the proposed mirror is not a continuization of a computational result from the paper.

Even granting Lemma 6.10 as an anchor, the proponent’s construction does not actually preserve the paper’s object. In the paper, \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) are the policy positions of \(n\) named voters, and both the integral and the random permutation range over those voters. Lemma 6.10 says that, for weighted-median aggregation, this index happens to equal the ordinary Shapley–Shubik index of the finite weighted game.

The proposed formula instead replaces every type block by one player with weight \(W_t=\mu_ta_t\). Under the lemma, it is simply

\[ \Phi_t(\mu,a) = \operatorname{SSI}_t\!\left([W/2;W_1,\ldots,W_\tau]\right), \]

assuming the stated nondegeneracy condition. The population masses appear only as weights in a \(\tau\)-player game. The random order is over types, not over the many voters represented by those types. That is a block-power index newly invented by the mirror, not the paper’s individual Shapley–Shubik index.

The distinction is substantive. Take \(91\) voters of type \(A\) and \(10\) of type \(B\), all with unit per-capita weight. The proposed two-player type game has normalized weights \(91/101\) and \(10/101\), so \(A\) is a dictator and the type powers are \((1,0)\). The genuine high-multiplicity expansion has \(101\) equal-weight voters under majority. Every individual has Shapley–Shubik power \(1/101\), so the aggregate type powers are \(91/101\) and \(10/101\). The proposed construction therefore changes the target quantity, rather than representing the high-multiplicity version of it.

There are possible repairs, but each abandons the anchor in a different way. Preserving individual power requires expanding to all \(n_t\) copies and retaining a permutation over all voters; in an atomless limit, individual power vanishes and there is no canonical analogue of the finite uniform permutation. Aggregating the copies gives a new group-power problem. Ordering types directly gives the proponent’s block game, which is again a new finite weighted-game model. Allowing heterogeneous policy positions within a type requires additional conditional distributions over \([0,1]\), absent from \(\mu\), and a new definition of type influence.

This is not the mistaken objection that the continuous answer might equal the discrete one. The problem is prior: the proposed mirror either does not continuize the population, or it no longer measures the paper’s power index. A genuine high-multiplicity study of aggregate Shapley–Shubik power could be sensible, but it would be a new computational-social-choice problem motivated by the paper, not a mirror of one of its named computational results. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, the proponent’s sole anchor therefore does not survive.

The limitation of this negative case is that “no worthwhile related HM problem could ever be formulated” is not provable. If the programme accepts newly introduced block-power problems as sufficient anchors, the mirror becomes plausible. Under the stricter standard given here, however, the paper should receive no continuous mirror.
