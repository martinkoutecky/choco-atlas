The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest positive case is that the paper studies exactly the kind of aggregate matching market a later computational analysis could work on. But it supplies no named computational result to anchor that mirror.

The closest numbered results, all proved in the paper, are analytical. Proposition 1 says that when sellers’ premuneration values do not depend on buyers’ attributes, efficient uniform-price equilibria exist and every uniform-price equilibrium is constrained efficient. Proposition 2 says that if an efficient outcome can be supported by uniform prices, sellers’ premuneration values must be independent of buyer attributes along the matched pairs. Proposition 3 gives conditions under which a complete uniform-price equilibrium exists, including conditions for positive investment by some or all agents. None asserts a complexity classification or an algorithm.

There is also a mismatch with ChoCo’s high-multiplicity model: this paper already assumes continua of buyers and sellers, but indexes them by continuously varying types \(\beta,\sigma\in[0,1]\), with type-dependent investment costs. Replacing those agents with finitely many classes, each containing a mass of otherwise identical agents, would be a plausible computational follow-on. It would also change the paper’s “totally heterogeneous” environment and its continuous sorting structure. That is a possible new problem, not a mirror anchored by one of the paper’s computational results.

So I would not claim a qualifying continuous mirror here. The paper’s contribution is already an analytic study of a continuous population; it does not provide the named computational anchor this task requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the missing computational result. Proposition 1 gives an efficiency characterization under a condition on sellers’ premuneration values; Proposition 2 gives a related necessity condition; Proposition 3 proves equilibrium existence under stated assumptions. None defines a computational problem or claims an algorithm or complexity bound. Computing an equilibrium, checking efficiency, or finding when the existence conditions hold could each become a research question, but each would add a problem the paper does not formulate.

The three propositions do not supply separate computational anchors. For Proposition 1, checking whether \(h_S\) is independent of \(b\) is a property of the model’s primitives, not an optimization task. Proposition 2’s condition is evaluated along equilibrium matches, so turning it into an algorithmic question would first require specifying how the equilibrium is represented and computed. Proposition 3 establishes existence through a fixed point argument; it does not give a procedure for finding an equilibrium or bound the resources such a procedure would use.

There is also no population limit left to take in the paper’s model: buyers and sellers already form continua. Their indices \(\beta\) and \(\sigma\) range over \([0,1]\), and the strictly single-crossing costs make agents with different indices choose different positive investments. A finite type model with masses of otherwise identical agents could describe, for example, cohorts of workers and firms with shared costs. That is a plausible economic setting, but it changes the paper’s continuous sorting environment. Studying its computational complexity would be a new finite type equilibrium problem, rather than continuizing one of the paper’s results.

The best positive reply is that computational questions about equilibrium in continuous matching markets might still be worthwhile. I cannot rule that out universally: a carefully encoded version could be an interesting project. But the proponent has not supplied such an encoding or a computational task tied to any named result. On the anchors actually offered, the case against a ChoCo mirror is strong; the broader claim that no related computational project could ever be worthwhile is weaker.
