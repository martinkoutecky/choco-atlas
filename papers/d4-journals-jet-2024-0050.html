The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets your anchor rule: the paper has no named result about computational complexity, such as membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, or an algorithm for a computational problem.

The closest candidate is Proposition 6, proved in the paper. It characterizes the welfare-maximizing *static* information and trading mechanism: the buyer learns whether \(v_B\) is below a threshold \(x\), and the seller whether \(v_S\) is above a threshold \(y\), where
\[ \mathbb{E}[v_S\mid v_S\le y]=x,\qquad \mathbb{E}[v_B\mid v_B\ge x]=y. \]
Any price in \([x,y]\) implements trade when both parties accept. That is an optimization characterization, but not a complexity result of the required kind. Proposition 2 similarly proves existence of a dynamic mechanism that implements ex-post efficient trade; it does not classify the computational complexity of finding one.

A possible population story would be a mediation service handling many similar disputes, with dispute-context types and mass equal to their share of cases. But aggregating cases changes the paper’s object: its strategic agents are one buyer and one seller, while its continuous distributions describe uncertainty about their values, not a continuum of people. A mass-based optimization problem for many disputes would therefore be a new problem, without a named computational result here to anchor it. The main weakness is also the decisive one: I cannot state a continuous problem per anchor without treating a non-computational economic result as a computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent leaves no surviving anchor. Proposition 6 is the closest: it characterizes an optimal static information policy through thresholds \(x,y\). But it gives an economic characterization, not a computational result. The paper specifies no finite input encoding for the prior distributions, running time, approximation guarantee, or complexity classification. Turning “compute \(x,y\) from a distribution” into an algorithmic question would require a new input model and a new computational claim.

Proposition 2 has the same gap: it proves that suitable discovery paths exist and implement ex post efficiency. The paths are functions over continuous time, with no representation or algorithm for finding them. Proposition 4’s finite round version establishes existence of sufficiently fine intervals, but still gives no complexity result for computing them. These may be worthwhile questions in mechanism design; they are not computational results of this paper to continuize.

The proposed cohort of similar disputes does not supply the missing population model. Each case still has one buyer and one seller with private values. Grouping cases by legal context leaves the strategic parties distinct; if each case uses its own mechanism, aggregate welfare is just the weighted sum of separate cases. If instead the mediator must use one policy across cases, or coordinate a shared settlement budget, that coupling creates a new mechanism design problem. The paper gives no result about its computational complexity.

The strongest alternative is a continuum of claimants, as in the Roundup example. That could be a sensible population model if claimants share complete types and the mediator faces common settlement constraints. But the paper’s results concern bilateral trade. A many-claimant version needs new allocation and incentive constraints, and may need to track claim-specific identities and payoffs. It could be good research; it does not rescue Proposition 2 or Proposition 6 as computational anchors.

The honest weakness in this negative case is that the paper itself says its approach could extend beyond two parties, and a many-claimant model may be a natural sequel. The case against a *mirror of this paper* remains strong: its continuous value distributions describe uncertainty about two agents, while no named result studies computation over a continuous society. Treating those prior distributions as population masses would conflate probability over possible values with realized population.
