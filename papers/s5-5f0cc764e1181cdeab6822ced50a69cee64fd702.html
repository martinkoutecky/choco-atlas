What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its closest results are Theorem 2 and Theorem 3, both proved here. Theorem 2 says that, under its boundedness, connectedness, and constraint-qualification assumptions, if an anonymous, unanimous, continuous social choice function exists, then the final constraint value must be a global maximum or minimum of \(g_m\) on \(Y\). Theorem 3 says that for an open dense set of smooth \(g_m\), choosing that value to be a maximum or minimum ensures such a function exists. Theorem 3 is the strongest nearby positive result, but neither theorem gives an algorithm or a complexity classification. With arbitrary smooth functions and no input encoding or running-time bound, calling this “in P” would overstate what the paper proves.

The strongest plausible population mirror is a continuum-agent aggregation existence problem. Let \(X\) be a compact set of alternatives defined by equality constraints. A type is an agent’s chosen ideal alternative \(p\in X\); a society is a probability measure \(\mu\) over \(X\), with \(\mu\)’s mass recording the fraction of agents of each type. Ask whether there exists a weakly continuous aggregation map \(A:\mathcal P(X)\to X\) satisfying \(A(\delta_p)=p\) for every \(p\in X\). The map is the decision rule; the question is feasibility, with no objective to minimize. A plausible regime is a large body of residents or stakeholders whose ideal bundles fall into a moderate number of shared types.

That is a recognizable continuum-agent extension of the paper’s finite-agent model: an ordered tuple of choices becomes a distribution of choices, and unanimity becomes the condition on point masses. But it adds a rule coherent across all population sizes and distributions; Theorems 2 and 3 do not establish results about that rule. More fundamentally, it remains a topological existence question, not the computational question ChoCo is meant to study. I would not present it as a valid mirror of either theorem or assign it a tractability, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness prediction. The paper’s continuous object is already the alternative space \(X\); its named results do not continuize the population or analyze computation over populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the missing anchor. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 prove existence claims about a social choice function on \(X\). Neither gives an algorithm or complexity bound for a computational problem. The constrained optimization problem \((P)\) is part of the explanation of “optimal design”; the paper does not specify an input encoding or analyze its running time. In particular, Theorem 3’s open dense set of functions in \(C^\infty(\mathbb R^n,\mathbb R)\) is a genericity result, not an algorithmic classification.

The proponent’s resident or stakeholder scenario is plausible: many people could share one of a modest number of ideal bundles, giving a meaningful distribution \(\mu\). The difficulty is that \(\mu\) has no operative role in the paper’s results. They depend on the geometry of \(X\), defined by the constraints, and ask whether a rule exists on \(X\). The paper explicitly leaves the constraints’ economic interpretation open; its designer’s optimization is not tied to the agents’ choices. Theorem 2’s condition on \(c_m\) therefore cannot be turned into a population computation without adding a relationship between the society and the constraint design.

The proposed \(A:\mathcal P(X)\to X\) changes the question. The paper gives a separate rule \(F:X^k\to X\) for a finite number \(k\) of agents. A single rule on distributions requires coherence across population sizes and makes populations with the same proportions equivalent under replication. Anonymity in the paper only makes \(F\) invariant to permutations; it does not supply that cross-size condition. More fundamentally, asking whether such a global \(A\) exists is a rule-existence question about its whole domain. A particular \(\mu\) is not an instance that the question needs to inspect. Asking for an outcome for that \(\mu\) leaves no criterion for choosing among outcomes beyond unanimity at point masses.

One can add a loss function and ask for an outcome minimizing aggregate disagreement, or make the designer choose constraints based on the population. Either could lead to a computational problem with a high-multiplicity input. But the paper supplies neither the loss function nor that population-to-design link. They would define a new model, rather than expose a computational question already present in these results. Even an encoded version of the paper’s geometry would place the complexity in the description of \(X\), not in the distribution of a society.

So I would not treat this as a worthwhile ChoCo mirror: a sensible high-multiplicity setting can be imagined, but making it computationally operative requires importing the central problem. The limit of this negative case is that it cannot rule out a separately motivated population-aggregation project; it explains why this paper does not furnish one.
