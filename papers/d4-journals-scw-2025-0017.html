The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3>0\) summing to \(1\) and the five options \(X_1,X_2,X_3,Y,Z\), with type \(t\) valuing \(X_t\) at \(1\), the other favorite options at \(0\), \(Z\) at \(v_Z<1\), and \(Y\) at \(v_Y<1\) for types \(1,2\) and \(0\) for type \(3\), determine whether weighted MaxParC has a strong Nash equilibrium with \(p_Z=1\), under Proposition 2’s parameter assumptions. Each faction controls a measurable rating assignment over its mass, and the weighted rule replaces voter counts by approval masses and rating sums by integrals.

The model it lives in

A society with three utility types carrying masses \(\mu_t\); each faction is a strategic player controlling measurable ratings over its members. Define approvals by the paper’s threshold rule using mass, score options by \(s_x=a_x+\int r_x\,d\mu\), and assign the winning lottery from the mass of voters whose top-scoring approved options are \(x\).

The objection that survived

The paper lets each faction choose a ballot profile across its members, whereas the proposed mirror gives a type one rating vector; with measurable ballot assignments, arbitrarily small mass deviations may preserve approval where a discrete deviation of at least \(1/N\) would collapse it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the strong-equilibrium existence claims in Propositions 2 and 3. It leaves Proposition 1’s rule properties, the conjectures, simulations, and behavioral experiment outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under the mass-weighted rule and the paper’s factional strategy space, does any strong Nash equilibrium with \(p_Z=1\) exist throughout Proposition 2’s parameter regime, or do arbitrarily small mass deviations rule it out?
  • Can strong-equilibrium existence or search be characterized when the society has arbitrary rational type support \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a limit to the positive case: this paper has no named result classifying a computational problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The strongest anchors available are Propositions 2 and 3, proved in the paper, which construct strong equilibria in its three-faction game. They are equilibrium results, not complexity theorems. If the anchor requirement means only complexity classifications, this paper has no eligible anchor. If a constructive equilibrium result can anchor a search problem, these two support a narrow, credible mirror.

The lead is Proposition 2. In the paper’s game, three factions \(F_1,F_2,F_3\), of population shares \(n_1,n_2,n_3\), have distinct favourite options \(X_1,X_2,X_3\). Each faction gets utility \(1\) from its favourite and \(0\) from the other factions’ favourites. All factions value a full-consensus option \(Z\) at \(v_Z<1\); factions \(F_1,F_2\) value a partial-consensus option \(Y\) at \(v_Y<1\), while \(F_3\) values \(Y\) at \(0\). The surrounding assumptions include \(v_Z>\max_t n_t\) and \(v_Z>(n_1+n_2)v_Y\). Proposition 2 says that under MaxParC, if \((n_1+n_2)v_Y\le v_Z\), the factional game has a strong Nash equilibrium selecting \(Z\) with probability \(1\). The authors prove it here.

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large membership organization making recurring, relatively low-stakes decisions, with members in three stable caucuses that share utility schedules and coordinate their ballots. The population might number thousands or millions while the support has just \(\tau=3\) types. That closely follows the paper’s own strategic abstraction, where each faction chooses its members’ ballots. A type here is its full utility vector and caucus role; its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the electorate.

The precise mirror is MaxParC\(_\infty\) Full-Consensus Equilibrium Search. Its instance is the five options \(X_1,X_2,X_3,Y,Z\), rational masses \(\mu_1+\mu_2+\mu_3=1\), and rational utilities satisfying Proposition 2’s assumptions. A type chooses a rating vector \(r_t\in[0,1]^5\), and all members of that type use it. For each option \(x\), let \(A_x\) be the largest set of types for which every \(t\in A_x\) satisfies \(\mu(A_x)+r_{tx}>1\). Rank options lexicographically by approval mass \(\mu(A_x)\), then by average rating \(\sum_t\mu_t r_{tx}\). Each type’s share is assigned among its highest-scoring approved options; normalize those shares over non-abstaining mass to obtain the winning lottery. This makes the approval threshold and rating tie-breaker meaningful for fractional populations. A solution is a rating profile inducing a strong Nash equilibrium with \(p_Z=1\): no coalition of types can change its ratings so that every coalition member is at least as well off and one is strictly better off.

The paper’s proof gives a direct continuous witness: each type rates its favourite \(1\), gives \(Z\) a small positive rating \(\epsilon<\min_t\mu_t\), and rates the other options \(0\). Everyone approves \(Z\), which has the highest approval mass. The mass inequalities in the proof rule out profitable faction coalitions. For this promised three-type family, I expect the search problem to be tractable: the profile is explicit and has polynomial encoding length. This is a continuous-population question because the instance is given by rational masses and the threshold rule acts on those masses. The lottery over options is already part of the paper’s method; it is not the continuization.

Proposition 3 gives a useful second anchor, also proved here. In the same three-faction utility setting, remove \(Z\). If \(v_Y>\max\{n_1,n_2\}/(n_1+n_2)\), the factional game has a strong Nash equilibrium giving \(Y\) probability \(n_1+n_2\) and \(X_3\) probability \(n_3\). Its continuous search problem takes rational masses and utilities satisfying that condition, and asks for a strong-equilibrium rating profile inducing exactly \(p_Y=\mu_1+\mu_2\) and \(p_{X_3}=\mu_3\). The paper’s proof supplies the analogous profile: types \(1\) and \(2\) rate \(Y\) just above \(\mu_3\), as well as rating their own favourites \(1\); type \(3\) rates only \(X_3\) positively. Again, I expect this restricted search problem to be tractable by a direct construction. Proposition 2 is the stronger lead; Proposition 3 shows the mirror can retain the paper’s partial-consensus result too.

The scope is deliberately limited to those two MaxParC equilibrium claims. The broader question they suggest is whether strong equilibria implementing full or partial consensus can be found for arbitrary utility types and support size \(\tau\). That is a real computational question, but the paper’s propositions do not settle its complexity. I would treat it as open rather than infer that the general problem is tractable or hard.

The weakest point is the faction-level strategy assumption. The mirror keeps the paper’s caucuses as the strategic players, so a caucus controls the ballots of its whole type. If the authors or a referee require coalition deviations by arbitrary subsets of otherwise identical individuals, this is a different equilibrium model; the small positive threshold rating used in the construction need not protect against arbitrarily small deviating masses. The other limitation is that the anchors establish equilibrium existence and provide witnesses, not a complexity landscape. Even so, this is an unusually recognizable mirror: it continuizes the population in the paper’s own factional strategic model, while preserving the underlying question of whether consensus-supporting proportional outcomes can be sustained strategically.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central weakness is that neither anchor is a computational result. Propositions 2 and 3 prove equilibrium existence in a specially structured three-faction game. They do not classify an algorithmic problem or leave an equilibrium-search task for that promised family: the paper gives a witness and proves its stability.

For those exact claims, replacing faction sizes by rational masses adds little. The propositions are stated in terms of \(n_t=N_t/N\), and their proof inequalities use faction shares and utilities. Any rational mass vector can be represented by integer counts after scaling by a common denominator; the continuous formulation follows the same share-based argument. Proposition 3 has the same feature as Proposition 2: its conclusion is an explicit lottery with probabilities given by faction shares. These are near-collisions for the proposed mirrors—the paper’s results already do the relevant mass-based reasoning.

The proponent’s particular witnesses also need repair. Under MaxParC, \(s_x=|A_x|+\sum_i\beta_{ix}/N\). In the proposed Proposition 2 profile, rating a faction’s favourite \(1\) gives it score \(2n_t\), while \(Z\) has score \(1+\epsilon\). If a faction has \(n_t>(1+\epsilon)/2\), its members prefer their favourite to \(Z\), so that profile does not yield \(p_Z=1\). For Proposition 3, take \(n_1=.49,\ n_2=.01,\ n_3=.5\): with the proposed ratings, \(Y\)’s score is about \(.75\), while \(X_1\)’s is \(.98\). A repaired witness can lower favourite ratings while keeping those options approved. That makes the equilibrium claim more plausible, but also underscores that the promised cases already admit direct constructions; it does not create an unsolved computational mirror.

A more serious issue appears if the mirror treats voters as a nonatomic population rather than treating each faction as one strategic player. A type is a shared utility description; it does not imply that every member coordinates on one ballot. If a positive submass of a type may deviate, the proposed full-consensus profile is vulnerable: a small mass can switch to its favourite while the remaining voters still approve \(Z\), provided the deviating mass is below the threshold slack. Those deviators shift probability from \(Z\), worth \(v_Z<1\), to their favourite, worth \(1\). Preserving the paper’s faction-level coalition model avoids that problem, but then the mirror retains the very three strategic players whose equilibrium result is already explicit.

One could instead ask for equilibrium computation with arbitrary utility types and support size \(\tau\), or for weighted MaxParC tallying. Those are coherent computational questions. They extend beyond the two anchored results, though, and the broader equilibrium question requires specifying whether identical voters coordinate and which coalitions may deviate. I can make a strong case that Propositions 2 and 3 do not motivate a worthwhile new continuous result. I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no scenario or broader algorithmic question would be worthwhile; that is where the negative case is weakest.
