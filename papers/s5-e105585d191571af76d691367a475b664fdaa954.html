The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: the paper has no named computational result. Its Propositions 4.1, 4.2, 5.1, and 5.2 characterize voting behavior or equilibria; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So there are no eligible anchors, and no continuous problem to state as a mirror of one.

The closest candidate is Proposition 5.1, proved in the paper. In the costly-voting model, for all sufficiently large finite electorates, there is a symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium with a cost cutoff: voters whose costs are at most \(c_n^*\) vote for the morally preferred but less self-interested alternative \(A\), and higher-cost voters abstain. The probability that \(A\) wins approaches \(1\) as the electorate grows. That is an equilibrium result, not a computational one.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would describe a large electorate by masses of voter types, where a type includes preferences and voting cost; an action assigns each type to vote for \(A\), vote for \(B\), or abstain. One could ask for an equilibrium under plurality. But the continuum changes the key mechanism: an individual has zero effect on the aggregate outcome. With positive voting costs, abstention then saves the cost without changing the outcome; with costless voting, individual actions do not select a unique equilibrium. The paper’s expressive effect relies on a small but positive pivotal probability in every finite electorate. Thus this is a recognizable extension of the paper’s topic, but a weak mirror of its result—and it does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Propositions 4.1 and 4.2 characterize equilibria in a voting model; Proposition 5.1 proves existence of a symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium for large electorates; Proposition 5.2 rules out a particular behavior under the EIA model. None formulates an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity bound. Turning one of them into “compute an equilibrium” would add a computational task the paper does not pose.

Proposition 5.1 is the closest candidate, and its population setting is natural: voters can be grouped by preferences and voting costs. But its key force comes from each voter retaining a small positive chance of being pivotal. In a nonatomic population, one voter has zero effect on the outcome. With positive voting costs, voting then loses to abstaining; with zero costs, voters are indifferent among actions, leaving the mass of votes and the winner unselected by the model. Proposition 4.2 faces the same loss of individual influence in the costless voting model. A continuum-limit question could describe this change, but it would be an analytic equilibrium question of the kind the programme excludes. Keeping a positive pivotal chance requires retaining finite population size or giving a whole bloc strategic agency, which changes the object being modeled.

The best attempt to repair the mirror might use the costly-voting model’s distribution of costs, or the direct expressive payoff discussed in Section 5.4, and ask for equilibrium vote masses across finitely many voter types. That would be a defined computational question only after adding an input representation for the probability-weighting function, costs, and any heterogeneity in preferences and rules. The paper supplies no such computational problem: its results concern behavior under a specified model and derive equilibrium conditions. Choosing an encoding and asking for an algorithm would start a new project. The direct expressive-payoff variant could preserve a type-level participation threshold, but that still does not turn any result in this paper into a complexity result.

So the proponent is right that Proposition 5.1 is a weak mirror. Its weakness is not that the underlying electorate lacks a high-multiplicity regime: the paper plainly studies large electorates, including identical voters. It is that the finite pivotal mechanism central to its result degenerates in a nonatomic model, while the surviving equilibrium question is outside ChoCo’s computational scope. The universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile computational follow-up is hard to establish; this paper simply offers no strong anchor for one.
