The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and position type sets \(I,J\), rational applicant masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), rational per-applicant position supplies \(q_j\), and acceptable pairs \(E\) with ranks \(r_{ij}\), define feasible flows by \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le q_j\). Lexicographically maximize the rank totals \(z_\ell(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E:\,r_{ij}=\ell}x_{ij}\). Output every pair \((i,j)\in E\) for which some lexicographically optimal flow has \(x_{ij}>0\).

The model it lives in

Applicant types are complete preference rankings with mass \(\mu_i\); position types have per-capita supplies \(q_j\). A feasible allocation is a nonnegative flow \(x_{ij}\) respecting type masses and supplies. Rank-maximality lexicographically maximizes total allocated mass at each rank, and type-pair support consists of pairs receiving positive mass in at least one optimum. Sequential rational linear programs can optimize the rank totals and then maximize each \(x_{ij}\) over the optimal face.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s rank-maximal pair support result. It leaves the switching-graph characterization, counting hardness and approximation, and the popular rank-maximal matching algorithm without continuous mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a flow analogue of the switching graph compute type-pair support more directly than solving a sequence of linear programs?
  • What additional structure does the support problem have when the number of applicant and position types is small?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a rank-maximal mass-allocation problem. Consider a large centralized allocation of graduates to training positions. Applicant type \(i\) is a complete ranking over interchangeable position types, and \(\mu_i\) is that type’s fraction of applicants. Position type \(j\) has supply \(q_j\), measured per applicant, so its capacity scales with the population. The regime of interest has many applicants but comparatively few distinct applicant and position types. A flow \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) assigned to type \(j\): every applicant still gets one position or remains unmatched, while a cohort can be split across positions.

Precisely, an instance gives finite type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), rational supplies \(q_j\), and acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\) with ranks \(r_{ij}\). Let
\[ \mathcal F=\left\{x\ge0: \sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i,\quad \sum_i x_{ij}\le q_j \right\}, \qquad z_\ell(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E:\,r_{ij}=\ell}x_{ij}. \]
Let \(\mathcal F^\star\) be the feasible flows whose signature \((z_1,\ldots,z_r)\) is lexicographically maximum. This is the same rank-maximal objective as in the paper, applied to mass.

My lead is Rank-Maximal Extreme-Allocation Counting: given this instance, output \(\lvert\operatorname{vert}(\mathcal F^\star)\rvert\), the number of vertices of the rank-maximal flow polytope. This gives the paper’s counting question a finite, canonical object to count; counting all optimal mass allocations would usually mean counting uncountably many flows.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: counting rank-maximal matchings is #P-complete for both strict and tied preference lists. The authors prove this by reduction from counting matchings in 3-regular bipartite graphs; in the strict-list construction, perfect matchings correspond one-to-one with rank-maximal matchings. The mirror preserves that correspondence. For an instance with \(n\) applicants and \(n\) posts, give each applicant a singleton type of mass \(1/n\), each post a singleton type of supply \(1/n\), and preserve the original ranks and acceptable pairs. The resulting scaled bipartite matching polytope is integral: its vertices are exactly the matchings, and the vertices of \(\mathcal F^\star\) are exactly the rank-maximal ones. Thus the mirror’s count equals the paper’s count on those instances, and the hardness transfers. I expect this problem to be hard by Class B; the reduction’s combinatorics live in the preference graph. It does not, by itself, prove hardness when many applicants are compressed into very few types. Whether exact counting remains hard in that regime, and whether the discrete FPRAS extends to rational type masses and supplies, are natural follow-up questions.

A second, cleaner mirror is Rank-Maximal Type-Pair Support: output every pair \((i,j)\) for which some \(x\in\mathcal F^\star\) has \(x_{ij}>0\). The anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: the paper shows that the set of rank-maximal applicant–post pairs can be computed in \(O(\min(n+r,r\sqrt n)m)\) time, using its switching-graph characterization and the rank-maximal matching algorithm. For the mass version, optimize the signature rank by rank, then, for each acceptable pair, maximize \(x_{ij}\) over \(\mathcal F^\star\); include the pair if that maximum is positive. This uses polynomially many rational LPs in \(|E|,\tau,\kappa,r,L\), so I expect it to be tractable by Class A. Further questions include whether a flow analogue of the switching graph yields a faster support algorithm, and whether it gives useful structure beyond support.

The scope is deliberately narrow: these are mirrors of the paper’s counting and rank-maximal-pair results. I am not claiming a continuous version of the whole switching-graph theory or the popularity result.

My weakest point is the counting mirror’s choice to count vertices. The paper counts matchings, whereas the continuous flow model has infinitely many feasible allocations; counting extreme points is a deliberate polyhedral extension. It is nevertheless grounded in the exact singleton-type correspondence, and Theorem 2’s support problem shows that the mass-flow model directly preserves another named question from the paper. The hardness claim is strongest for the unrestricted type-count model; the low-type, high-multiplicity regime remains an open test of how much continuization changes the complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The count mirror is the weak anchor. Counting vertices of \(\mathcal F^\star\) is a coherent polyhedral problem, but it is not the continuous version of counting matchings in the regime that motivates ChoCo. The singleton-type construction preserves the paper’s count precisely by retaining every applicant’s identity, with \(\tau=n\). Once applicants are genuinely grouped, the natural aggregate object is a flow; it has infinitely many feasible points, and choosing its vertices gives a new count rather than a direct continuation of the paper’s question. Counting integer flow tables is a closer analogue, but it depends on a chosen population scale and is therefore discrete again. This makes the proposed counting mirror interesting at most as a separate question, not a strong reason to pursue this paper.

I can’t make the same case against Theorem 2. “Which applicant type and position type can carry positive mass in some rank-maximal allocation?” is a sensible aggregate version of asking which pairs can occur. A large graduate cohort with a small set of preference types and position categories is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. For that model, sequentially optimizing the rank coordinates and then each pair’s flow gives a direct exact algorithm using linear programming. The answer may be routine, and it loses the paper’s finer question of whether one particular applicant can receive one particular post. But those are not fundamental objections: aggregate support is meaningful, and a straightforward Class A result still fits the programme.

So the strongest negative case is that the counting result does not survive as a compelling mirror, while the pair-support result offers limited algorithmic novelty. The universal negative claim does not hold up honestly: the support mirror is well-posed in a plausible population regime, and the paper is a strong candidate for the programme.
