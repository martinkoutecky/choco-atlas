What the mirror covers

The proposed model covers only the risk-neutral contract-optimization discussion in Contracts Under Uncertainty; it leaves repeated encounters, hidden-type mechanisms, and shared-outcome group incentives aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, so it supplies no eligible anchor under your rule. Equations (1)–(3) are a numbered optimization problem, not a named result. The paper does say that, with risk-neutral agents, the contracting problem can be solved by linear programming; that is its strongest near-anchor, but it is an informal claim, not a numbered theorem. It is not a hardness result.

A plausible continuous mirror of that claim would be Risk-Neutral Population Contract Design. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of contractor types with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), where each type completely specifies its effort costs, reservation utility, and outcome probabilities. Each agent performs an independent copy of a standardized task. For each type \(t\), the principal chooses an intended effort \(e_t\) and an outcome-contingent wage schedule \(w_t(q)\). The schedule must make \(e_t\) at least as attractive as every other effort and give the agent at least its reservation utility. The objective is to maximize per-capita expected principal payoff,
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{q\in Q}p_t(q\mid e_t)\bigl(V_t(q)-w_t(q)\bigr). \]
A solution is an optimal set of schedules and intended efforts satisfying those incentive and participation constraints. This preserves the paper’s central question—how a selfish agent can be induced to perform the desired effort without supervision—while treating a large pool of similar contractors as type masses.

The regime is plausible for a large fleet of robots or a crowdsourcing platform handling many standardized jobs: the number of agents is large, while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct productivity, cost, and outcome profiles is comparatively small. The paper’s strongest support is its Contracts Under Uncertainty discussion: it formulates the principal’s optimization over outcome-contingent wages, effort incentives, and acceptance, then says the risk-neutral case is solvable by linear programming. That claim is made in this paper, but it is not a numbered result, so this candidate cannot honestly count as a required anchor. For the finite, risk-neutral version above, I would expect tractability by LP methods; extending it to richer preferences or hidden types raises separate questions about the size and structure of the resulting optimization problem.

The weakest point is that the paper does not establish a complexity result, and the population model assumes independent task copies. Its section on contracting with a group instead discusses shared output and strategic interaction, whose incentives may behave differently in a continuum. The mirror is a reasonable research direction the authors might recognize, but without an eligible named anchor, this paper cannot support the requested anchored positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. It has no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity of a computational problem. Equations (1)–(3) define an optimization problem; they are not a result. The closest candidate is the informal claim that risk-neutral agents yield a linear program. That gives no stated complexity bound or input-size analysis, and the paper supplies no numbered result for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

The proposed population model also adds assumptions that the paper does not analyze. It assigns each type its own contract and intended effort. If types are observable, this removes the hidden-type problem; if they are private, the model needs a common menu and constraints ensuring each type prefers its designated contract. For independent standardized tasks, the resulting objective is a mass-weighted sum of type-level payoffs. To make population masses shape the problem itself, one could add a shared resource limit, a common menu, or group production with a shared outcome. Those are plausible contract-design questions, but each requires a new model and computational result; none is a continuous version of a named result in this paper.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: a platform or robot fleet handling many similar tasks with contractors drawn from a small set of complete types. That makes the proposed scenario sensible. It does not supply the missing anchor. The paper’s type probabilities describe uncertainty about an individual counterpart, while population masses would describe the actual composition of a workforce. Moving from one to the other may motivate a project, but it is a different question.

So I would reject this paper as support for an anchored continuization case. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is harder to defend: a population model of contract design could be worth studying. My negative case is strongest on the paper’s failure to state a computational result, and weaker on the possibility of a worthwhile new problem inspired by its subject.
