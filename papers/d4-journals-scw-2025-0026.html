What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 3 and Corollaries 1–2 only as axiomatic statements; it leaves the paper’s other propositions and axiomatic comparisons without a computational question.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case: the paper contains no named computational result. Its closest anchor, Theorem 3 (proved in this paper), says that when the agenda family contains the full set of alternatives, no collective choice correspondence on either the strict or weak universal preference domain can satisfy full range, weak decisiveness, weak pairwise justifiability, and non-dictatorship simultaneously. Corollaries 1 and 2 derive the corresponding impossibilities for social choice functions and social welfare functions. These are axiomatic impossibility results; none asserts that a computational problem is hard, tractable, or otherwise classifiable.

The nearest possible mirror would take a ranking as a type, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of society with ranking \(t\), and ask whether a rule \(C(\mu,B)\) can satisfy continuous-population versions of those axioms. Large electorates with relatively few ballot types make that population regime plausible. But this would continuize the paper’s axiomatic question, not formulate its computational counterpart. It also leaves a substantive modeling gap: the paper’s notion of dictatorship concerns an individual, which a distribution over types does not naturally preserve. I would not present this as a continuous-computational mirror or claim Theorem 3 as a qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not left a computational anchor standing. Theorem 3 and Corollaries 1–2 are named results, but they are axiomatic nonexistence theorems: none classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm. Proposition 2 connects weak pairwise justifiability to strategyproofness; it still does not pose a task such as finding a manipulation, deciding whether one exists, or computing its cost. There is no result here to translate into a high-multiplicity complexity landscape.

The most natural population model is easy to see: use rankings as types and represent a large electorate by rational masses \(\mu_t\). That makes a continuous *axiomatic* question plausible. One could ask whether mass-based versions of full range, weak decisiveness, and pairwise justifiability imply dictatorship. But even if the authors accepted those versions—and even if the answer were interesting—that would continue the paper’s axiomatic programme, not formulate its computational counterpart.

The dictator and individual-change clauses do need care in that model. An atomless population has no positive-mass individual, so a literal single-agent change may vanish; a mass-level version would instead concern changes to a positive fraction of society. That is a real modeling choice, but it is not my decisive objection: one could repair the formulation, allow atoms, or otherwise preserve the intended axiom. The stronger point is that none of those repairs supplies a computational input-output problem.

A computational extension could invent one. For example, given a succinctly represented rule and a rational distribution over rankings, one could seek a minimum-mass perturbation that changes the selected alternative. But this paper specifies no perturbation costs, budget, control action, or fixed rule whose outcome is to be computed. Complexity would depend on choices the extension adds—especially how the rule is represented—not on a computational problem established by the paper. Such a project might be worthwhile on its own; it is not a continuous mirror of Theorem 3.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo anchor, though it does not rule out a later computational project growing from its framework. The plausible high-multiplicity setting can support a continuous axiomatic analogue. It cannot turn the paper’s existing results into computational results.
