The continuous mirror question

Fix rational λ>0 and a rational threshold η. Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(μ_t\) summing to one, and a connected loopless graph with nonnegative rational edge weights \(w_{tu}\), let \(V=∑_{\{t,u\}∈ E} w_{tu}>0\). Decide whether there is a nonnegative allocation vector \(x\) with \(∑_t x_t=V\) such that \(∑_t r_t x_t≥∑_{\{t,u\}∈ E}w_{tu}r_tr_u\) for every \(r∈[0,1]^T\), and \(D_λ(Q\|P)≥η\), where \(Q_t=x_t/V\), \(P_t=μ_t\), and \(D_1\) is the usual Kullback–Leibler divergence. Here each type is a cohort, \(x_t\) is its total allocation, and each member receives the same per-capita rate.

The model it lives in

A society consists of finitely many cohorts \(t∈T\) with rational masses \(μ_t\). An edge weight \(w_{tu}\) is the total value created when the corresponding cohorts cooperate. A type-anonymous allocation gives cohort \(t\) total value \(x_t\), or per-capita rate \(a_t=x_t/μ_t\); it must satisfy the core inequalities for every vector of participating mass fractions \(r∈[0,1]^T\). Measure fairness by the divergence between the allocation shares \(Q_t=x_t/V\) and population masses \(P_t=μ_t\).

The objection that survived

The model excludes unequal core payoffs within a type, and the paper does not show that an unrestricted fairness witness can be symmetrized without changing its divergence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's egalitarian threshold problem for induced-subgraph games. It leaves the arbitrary-convex-game approximation results, fairness against the Shapley benchmark, and the paper's comparison of approximation guarantees outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • For arbitrary rational cohort masses, what is the complexity of the threshold problem, and does an orientation-based reduction establish hardness beyond the equal-mass case?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a block-structured, high-multiplicity version of the induced-subgraph game, focused on the paper’s strictly egalitarian worst-case fairness.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For every fixed \(\lambda>0\), it says that the following decision problem is NP-complete: given an induced-subgraph game \(\Gamma=(G,w)\) with nonnegative edge weights and a constant threshold \(\eta>0\), decide whether there is an integer core cover \(x\) with \(D_\lambda(x,U)\ge\eta\), equivalently whether \(Fair_\lambda(\Gamma,U)\ge\eta\). The proof connects the problem to minimum-entropy orientations: it cites the known hardness at \(\lambda=1\) and adapts the argument to all \(\lambda>0\).

Here is a continuous-population version of that question. Let \(T\) be a finite set of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(G=(T,E)\) be a loopless weighted graph with nonnegative rational weights \(w_{tu}\). Think of each type as a large cohort of interchangeable participants. The weight \(w_{tu}\) is the total value created when the two whole cohorts cooperate; at the individual level, pairwise interaction weights are scaled so their total across the cohorts is \(w_{tu}\).

A coalition takes a fraction \(r_t\in[0,1]\) of each cohort, and its value is
\[ v(r)=\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E} w_{tu}r_tr_u. \]
Write \(V=v(\mathbf{1})\). A type-anonymous allocation assigns each type a per-capita rate \(a_t\ge0\), or equivalently a total allocation \(x_t=\mu_ta_t\). It is in the core when
\[ \sum_t x_t=V \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t r_tx_t\ge \sum_{\{t,u\}\in E}w_{tu}r_tr_u \quad\text{for every }r\in[0,1]^T. \]
The uniform population has type masses \(P_t=\mu_t\); the normalized allocation has masses \(Q_t=x_t/V\). The proposed problem, Continuous IS Worst-Case Fairness, asks: given \((T,\mu,G,w,\lambda,\eta)\), is there a type-anonymous core allocation whose \(D_\lambda(Q\|P)\) is at least \(\eta\)? For \(\lambda\ne1\),
\[ D_\lambda(Q\|P)=\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\log_2\!\left(\sum_t (x_t/V)^\lambda\mu_t^{\,1-\lambda}\right), \]
with the usual KL-divergence limit at \(\lambda=1\). A solution is the allocation vector \(a\), or \(x\).

This is a high-multiplicity regime with \(N\gg\tau=|T|\): many agents share each type, and the interaction graph records complementarities among types. The mirror preserves coalition rationality for partial cohorts as well as whole cohorts. In fact, the displayed core constraints are equivalent to checking whole-type coalitions: if the inequalities hold for every subset \(S\subseteq T\), taking a random subset with independent inclusion probabilities \(r_t\) and averaging its core inequality gives the constraint for every fractional \(r\). With equal masses \(\mu_t=1/\tau\), the divergence is exactly the paper’s divergence for the vector of type totals \(x\) against the uniform distribution on types.

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). The graph combinatorics remain in the support size \(\tau\), even as the population per type grows. There is also unusually direct evidence in the paper: its Lemma 4 states that an optimal *fractional* cover of an induced-subgraph game corresponds to an optimal minimum-Rényi-entropy orientation, and conversely. Together with the orientation hardness used in the proof of Theorem 2, that suggests the change from integer allocations to continuous per-capita rates does not remove the hardness in this model. A formal result would need to state the reduction for the population encoding, but the paper supplies a strong route to it.

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s strictly egalitarian threshold problem for induced-subgraph games. It does not claim to cover fairness against the Shapley-value benchmark, or the paper’s results for arbitrary convex games. Natural follow-up questions are whether the hardness persists under simple restrictions on the type-interaction graph, what approximation guarantees are possible, and how the answer changes for a marginalist benchmark.

The weak point is the type-anonymity of the allocation: every member of a type receives the same rate. The paper’s fairness measure ranges over all core allocations, including ones that distinguish otherwise identical players. If the authors insist that such within-type differences must remain part of the problem, this is a restricted mirror. I still find it credible because the paper explicitly studies fractional covers, and its own fractional-cover/orientation correspondence gives a reason that type-level continuous allocations preserve the central combinatorics.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is real: Theorem 2 gives an NP-completeness result for worst-case fairness in induced-subgraph games. The negative case cannot rest on a lack of computational content or on what the continuous problem might turn out to answer. It has to rest on whether this problem has a natural high-multiplicity regime.

In the paper, a player is a vertex in an arbitrary weighted interaction graph. A player’s incident edges specify how they contribute to coalitions, so those interactions are part of their type. In the instances behind Theorem 2, there is no reason for many vertices to share the same interaction profile. If we preserve those profiles when making a population model, most vertices remain distinct types; the paper gives no basis for treating its hard instances as large cohorts of interchangeable agents.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s block-interaction model: many participants per role, with each pair of roles having a shared interaction weight, and equal allocation within each role. That is coherent, but it changes two features at once. It replaces arbitrary individual interactions with a block graph, and it restricts the fairness search to type-anonymous allocations. The paper’s fairness measure ranges over all integer core covers, including allocations that distinguish players. A cohort-level divergence \(D_\lambda(Q\|P)\) cannot see those within-cohort differences.

Retaining those differences would require allocations that vary across individuals within each type, with fairness measured over that full payoff distribution and core constraints imposed on arbitrary subgroups. That is a conceivable continuum problem, but the paper’s reduction to minimum-entropy orientations concerns aggregate covers; it does not establish that this richer problem is the paper’s fairness question in a continuous population. The mass-transfer version therefore has a genuine fit problem: the faithful version lacks the proposed type compression, while the compressed version narrows the objective.

This is still a weak negative case. A paper about cooperative settings with many interchangeable participants and type-pair complementarities could reasonably recognize the block model as its problem, especially if its allocation rules require equal treatment within a role. I can argue that the mirror is not well supported by this paper’s general interaction-graph model; I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every plausible scenario.
