There is no eligible anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are Definitions 1–3, defining scoring rules, welfare measures, and a strategic vote. Its contribution is a set of numerical experiments and qualitative summaries; it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity or an algorithm. The manipulation-complexity results mentioned in related work belong to cited papers, not this one. So I cannot honestly attach a continuous complexity question to a named result here.

The nearest plausible mirror, explicitly unanchored, would preserve the paper’s single-manipulator problem. Let \(C\) be the candidates, \(T\subseteq L(C)\) the supported true rankings, and \(\mu_t\) their rational population shares. A distinguished manipulator has true ranking \(t^\star\) and mass \(\varepsilon\le\mu_{t^\star}\); in a population of \(N\) voters, \(\varepsilon=1/N\) represents one voter. Given a scoring vector \(s\), the manipulator chooses a reported ranking \(r\). The reported ballot mass becomes
\[ \nu^r_b=\mu_b-\varepsilon\mathbf{1}[b=t^\star]+\varepsilon\mathbf{1}[b=r], \]
and the winner maximizes \(\sum_b\nu^r_b s(\operatorname{pos}_b(c))\), with the paper’s lexicographic tie-breaking. The solution would be an optimal report \(r\), the resulting winner, and the change in welfare measured on the true population \(\mu\). For example, with \(u_t(c)=m-\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\), the paper’s Borda, Rawls, and Nash measures extend by replacing voter counts with masses: \(100\sum_t\mu_tu_t(c)/(m-1)\), \(100\min_{\mu_t>0}u_t(c)/(m-1)\), and \(100\exp(\sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(c))/(m-1)\), respectively, taking Nash welfare as zero if a positive-mass type ranks \(c\) last.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large election with few recurring ballot types and a participant whose information lets them choose a strategic report; then \(N\) can greatly exceed the support size \(\tau\). The model preserves the paper’s single strategic voter and evaluates the same scoring outcome by the same welfare notions. But the paper itself motivates that voter through *small* expert panels, and finding a convincing large-population setting with one informed, influential voter is difficult. Replacing the individual with a positive-mass strategic bloc would make influence more credible, but would change the problem.

This candidate also offers little of the programme’s intended complexity contrast: the single-manipulator report problem appears tractable directly by considering desired winners and sorting rivals by their sincere scores. That is not a result of this paper, and there is no hardness anchor here whose fate under continuization can be tested. The proposed mirror would cover only the underlying single-manipulator welfare calculation, not the paper’s experimental comparisons across cultures and scoring rules. Its weakest point is therefore decisive: it is a plausible reformulation of a definition, but not a continuous mirror of any named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to mirror. Definitions 1–3 define scoring rules, welfare measures, and a strategic vote; the contribution is numerical experiments and qualitative summaries. The complexity results in the related work belong to other papers. By the programme’s stated standard, that is the decisive problem for a paper-specific mirror.

The closest candidate is the single voter’s optimal report. Giving that voter mass \(\varepsilon=1/N\) preserves their influence in a finite high-multiplicity instance. But as \(N\) grows, the individual’s mass vanishes; in an atomless society, changing one person’s report changes neither the score distribution nor the winner, except at score ties. Keeping finite-\(N\) effects or modelling fluctuations around the population limit could make a useful study, but it retains the finite-population question rather than producing a computational result from this paper.

The strongest repair is a strategically acting bloc of positive mass. That avoids the vanishing-influence problem and could support a worthwhile study of welfare under manipulation across scoring rules. It also changes the paper’s unilateral deviation into coordinated group action—or, with heterogeneous strategic voters, a game over responses. The paper itself treats group manipulation and incomplete information as different models from the small-panel experiment it conducts. So this is a plausible extension, but not a continuization of a named computational result here.

The negative case is strongest on that boundary, and weaker as a universal claim about every adjacent project. A positive-mass bloc model could be worth pursuing, and the paper’s discussion makes it plausible that the authors would recognize it as relevant. I cannot honestly rule out that broader project; I can say that the proponent’s single-voter mirror does not survive the continuum limit, and the bloc version does not supply the missing paper-specific computational anchor.
