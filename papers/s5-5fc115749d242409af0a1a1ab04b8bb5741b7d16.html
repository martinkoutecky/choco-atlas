Why no mirror

Proposition 3 and Theorem 2 are computational results, but their questions are about the security, network structure, and aggregate trade. The proposed trader cohorts can be deleted from both instances without changing the decision or update, so they do not yield a continuous-population question the authors would recognize as a result of this paper.

What the mirror covers

The proposal nominally targets Proposition 3 and Theorem 2; it leaves the paper's other theorems and its approximation discussion outside the claim.

The case FOR (proponent)

A narrow but credible mirror is the market’s trader population. Consider a large prediction market for recurring sports or election events: many participants may fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of cohorts with the same forecasting model, relevant trading policy, budget class, and security choice. For the results below, focus on a cohort trading one public security \(F\). Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of active traders of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). If type \(t\) contributes normalized net volume \(r_t\), the population’s aggregate order is \(\Delta=\sum_t\mu_t r_t\), measured in units of the LMSR liquidity parameter \(b\). A finite market with \(N\) traders represents the same cohort society by counts \(N_t\) and per-trader orders \(r_t/N\), so \(\mu_t=N_t/N\) gives the same total order. The many-agent, few-type regime is plausible here; mass is a fraction of traders, not probability mass over outcomes.

My lead is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It states that checking whether a CNF security \(F\) is compatible with a decomposable Bayesian-network DAG \(G\) is coNP-complete. The paper proves hardness even when the outcome variables are binary and \(G\) has no edges, by a reduction from the complement of SAT; membership follows because a violation of compatibility has a polynomially checkable witness. In market terms, “compatible” is precisely the property that makes trading \(F\) preserve the network structure, as Theorem 2 establishes.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Cohort Preservation Recognition\(_\infty\). An instance gives finite outcome variables and their domains, a decomposable DAG \(G\), a CNF security \(F\), and a trader society \(\mu\) over finitely many trader types authorized to trade \(F\), with a nonzero aggregate order profile. Decide whether, for every strictly positive \(G\)-compatible starting market distribution and every scale of the cohort’s aggregate order, the updated market distribution remains \(G\)-compatible. The solution is a yes/no answer. The universal scale is important: it asks the same “preserves the structure after any shares” question as the paper, rather than whether one particular starting price happens to survive one particular trade. By Theorem 2, this decision is equivalent to checking compatibility of \(F\) with \(G\); Proposition 3 therefore makes it coNP-complete.

I expect this hardness to persist in the continuous mirror. Its combinatorics live in the security formula and outcome variables, not in the number of traders: the paper’s hardness already holds with binary variables and an edgeless network, and a finite trader population can realize any rational cohort distribution. In the programme’s terms, this is a Class B style boundary: going to high multiplicity does not dissolve this hardness. It is not evidence that adding more traders creates the difficulty.

A second anchor, and the strongest algorithmic complement, is Theorem 2, also proved here. For decomposable \(G\), it says that \(F\) is structure preserving if and only if \(F\) is compatible with \(G\). When that condition holds, after any number of shares of \(F\) are traded, the new market distribution can be computed in polynomial time; only the CPTs of variables pivotal in \(F\) and their ancestors need updating. The paper presents an update algorithm drawing on Pennock and Wellman’s earlier LogOP aggregation method.

That result gives Cohort LMSR Update\(_\infty\). An instance gives the same kind of trader society and security \(F\), a decomposable \(G\), a \(G\)-compatible initial price distribution represented by its CPTs, and the rational per-type order rates \(r_t\), with \(F\) promised compatible with \(G\). Return the exact CPT representation of the resulting distribution after aggregate order \(\Delta=\sum_t\mu_t r_t\). In the market, the update is \(P'(\mathbf v)\propto P(\mathbf v)e^{\Delta F(\mathbf v)}\). The expected answer is tractability, conditional on the compatibility promise, by Theorem 2. The paper states polynomial time; because the formulas involve exponentials, a prover should make the arithmetic and output representation explicit. A Turing bit-complexity bound or a precision guarantee would be a further result, not something I would infer from the theorem.

Together, these anchors cover a coherent slice of the paper: recognizing which commonly traded securities preserve a compact market representation, and updating prices efficiently once a qualifying security is traded by a large cohort. They do not cover strategic choice of orders, arbitrary security mixtures, or the paper’s approximation methods for non-structure-preserving securities. Natural follow-on questions include how complexity changes when the \(\tau\) trader types choose different securities, whether their mass-weighted trades preserve \(G\) under a useful condition, and what bit-complexity or approximation guarantees the update algorithm admits.

The weakest point is also the reason to keep the claim narrow: the paper’s price update ultimately depends on aggregate share volume \(\Delta\), so the trader population enters through a small sufficient statistic. Its results do not analyze population multiplicity as the source of computational difficulty. Still, the paper is about aggregating traders’ information into collective prices, and replacing a list of many cohort members’ orders with their type-mass aggregate is a plausible high-multiplicity reading of the authors’ price-update problem. The market’s outcome distribution is not the society being continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has named computational results, so the case cannot rest on their absence. The problem is that its computational object is the market maker’s aggregate state, not a trader population. The state is the quantity vector \(\vec q\); trading \(\Delta b\) shares of \(F\) changes it by \(\Delta b\,\vec 1_F\). Trader identities, counts, and types do not enter the results.

That makes the proposed mirror of Proposition 3 redundant. Its yes/no condition depends on \(F\) and \(G\), while the cohort masses and order rates can be deleted from the instance without changing the answer. A single trader and a continuum of traders making the same aggregate order give the same compatibility question. The coNP-completeness result is real, but the proposed trader society does no computational work in it.

A stronger version could give traders different securities and ask whether their combined trade preserves \(G\). That may be a worthwhile extension of the paper’s questions about sets of securities. But the market update then depends on the aggregate potential \(H(\mathbf v)=\sum_t \mu_t r_t F_t(\mathbf v)\), or equivalently on the resulting aggregate quantity vector. The computational input is the weighted portfolio of trades and its security structure. Replacing repeated traders with masses just compresses that input; the paper already accepts aggregate quantities. Any new difficulty would come from the portfolio of securities, not from population multiplicity.

Theorem 2 has the same issue. For a cohort trading one security, the proposed update first calculates \(\Delta=\sum_t\mu_t r_t\), then applies the paper’s update for arbitrary \(\Delta\). The theorem already handles any number of shares. Continuous trader mass adds a weighted sum before a computation whose input is already that sum. Scaling the liquidity parameter with population size still leaves the relevant quantity as the trade-to-liquidity ratio. If traders choose orders strategically, deriving those orders requires a behavioral model absent from this paper; that would be a different computational problem.

Many traders can plausibly share a small set of forecasting and trading profiles. That makes a crowd scenario credible, but it does not make the crowd the object of these results. The market’s outcome distribution is central to the paper, yet continuizing that distribution would be outside this programme’s scope. The strongest negative case is therefore a scope mismatch: a population can be added as the provenance of orders, but the named results already discard that provenance before computation begins. A richer continuous model of strategic trading could be interesting, though its value would come from the added behavioral problem rather than from mirroring Proposition 3 or Theorem 2.
