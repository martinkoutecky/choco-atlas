The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bipartite graph of worker and firm types with rational masses, rational type-pair capacities and worker quotas, and exact rational piecewise-linear type-level choice maps satisfying the continuous analogues of the paper’s axioms and gapless condition, construct a finite weighted poset and a stable base allocation that represent every stable mass allocation uniquely as \(x=x_{\min}+\sum_{L\in R}\lambda_L d_L\), with \(0\leq\lambda_L\leq\theta_L\) and \(L\prec L'\), \(\lambda_{L'}>0\) implying \(\lambda_L=\theta_L\). Give a running-time bound polynomial in the number of supported types, the rational input bit length, and the choice-map description size or oracle cost.

The model it lives in

A finite two-sided graph of complete worker and firm types, each with rational mass. Contract variables are masses on type-pair edges; capacities and quotas scale with cohort mass, and type-level choice maps act on incident contract masses. Stability means every type receives an acceptable mass vector and no edge admits a positive increase selected by both endpoint maps. The target is an efficiently constructible finite weighted-poset representation of the entire stable set.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large labor market represented by a small number of complete worker and firm types. A worker type fixes the full ranking of compatible contracts, quota, and all other relevant terms; a firm type fixes its entire choice rule and contract terms. Suppose millions of workers and hiring units fall into a few dozen or few hundred such types. The mass of a type is its fraction of the relevant population, and \(x_e\) is the mass of contracts on a compatible type pair \(e\). Capacities and quotas are measured in mass units and scale with the cohorts. Individual bundles can remain integral; the type-level average \(x\) can be fractional.

That is a population mirror, rather than just a continuous allocation space: the paper’s graph has individual workers and firms as vertices, while this model has cohorts with identical complete attributes. The paper’s use of real-valued allocations in the broader Alkan–Gale framework does not itself make its society continuous; its vertices are still individual agents. Here the computational question is about mass stability and its representation.

For precision, take a finite bipartite graph of worker and firm types, rational type masses, rational type-pair capacities, and rational worker quotas. Each worker type has its order-based aggregate choice map; each firm type has a rational piecewise-linear choice map, given explicitly or by an exact efficient oracle. The maps obey the continuous analogues of the paper’s acceptability, consistency, substitutability, and size-monotonicity conditions. A type-pair edge is interesting to an endpoint if a small increase in its contract mass causes that endpoint’s choice map to select more mass on the edge. An assignment is stable if it is acceptable to every type and no edge is interesting to both endpoints.

The lead anchor is Theorem 6.7, proved in this paper. It states that, under the paper’s gapless condition (C), the poset of rotations \((R,\tau,\prec)\) can be constructed in weakly polynomial time: \(\log b_{\max}\) times a polynomial in \(|E|\), including choice-function oracle calls. The paper’s oracle calls are assumed efficient. The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

Continuous Stable Allocation Poset. Given the type graph, masses, capacities, quotas, and choice maps above, with a continuous gapless promise, construct a finite weighted poset of cycle directions \((R,\theta,\prec)\) and a stable base allocation \(x_{\min}\) such that every stable mass allocation is represented uniquely as
\[ > x=x_{\min}+\sum_{L\in R}\lambda_L d_L, > \]
where \(d_L\) is the signed type-edge direction for rotation \(L\), \(0\leq\lambda_L\leq\theta_L\), and \(L\prec L'\) with \(\lambda_{L'}>0\) implies \(\lambda_L=\theta_L\). Construct this representation in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau\), the rational input bit length \(L\), and the choice-map description or oracle cost.

Here, continuous gaplessness is an explicit promise, not something the paper proves for mass choices: if \(z_1\prec z_2\prec z_3\) are acceptable firm-type bundles and a small increase on the same edge \(a\) is locally offset by a decrease on edges \(c_1,c_2,c_3\), respectively, then \(c_1=c_3\) must imply \(c_1=c_2\). The requested output is a representation of the whole stable set, not an objective-minimizing allocation.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). The paper’s rotation method gives a concrete reason to ask for a finite representation, and the type graph—not the number of people—would control the structural size. A successful result would need to establish that the finite rotation structure survives fractional mass shifts and that its poset can be found with bit complexity polynomial in the rational data.

A narrower second anchor is Proposition 6.4, proved in this paper. Under condition (C), its Stage I algorithm finds a stable g-allocation in weakly polynomial time, polynomial in \(|E|\) and linear in \(\log b_{\max}\). Its continuous mirror is:

Continuous Stable Mass Allocation. On the same type-level input and gapless promise, find any stable mass vector \(x\), with \(x\) acceptable to every type and no blocking type-pair edge, in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and the choice-map description or oracle cost.

I also expect this to be tractable (Class A). It asks for one stable point rather than a full encoding, so it is the more modest question. Neither anchor is a hardness result, and this paper contains no comparable named hardness result to prefer instead. The scope here is just the paper’s construction of stable allocations and their rotation-poset representation; I would not stretch the mirror to every result or motivation in the paper.

The weakest point is the passage from integer rotations to continuous mass. The paper defines tandems using unit changes and proves its bounds on an integer domain. Its gapless condition therefore does not automatically transfer to an atomless population, and the paper does not show that a finite real-weighted poset represents all stable mass allocations. My continuous gapless promise makes that open question precise; it is a substantive extra condition, not a consequence of Theorem 6.7. The case still holds because the mirror preserves the paper’s underlying object—stability under type-level choice rules—and asks whether its algorithmic structure extends when the society is a population of identical cohorts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s main computational object is a finite integer lattice, and the proponent’s mass model does not yet explain how that object arises from a population of identical agents.

Theorem 6.7 builds its poset from unit tandems, immediate lattice successors, and rotations shifted by integer weights. In an atomless cohort, a feasible shift of size \(\lambda\) may also be feasible at every smaller positive size. Then intermediate stable allocations fill the interval, so there are no immediate successors to enumerate. A finite set of cycle directions with real weights could still represent the stable set, but that would be a different structural theorem; the paper’s construction gives no reason to expect such a representation or its polynomial-time construction.

There is also a problem in aggregating the firms’ choice rules. A type must specify an individual firm’s full choice function. For a cohort of identical firms, the choice from aggregate offers generally comes from distributing those offers among the individual firms and summing their choices. The paper’s axioms for an integer-valued single-firm function do not guarantee that this aggregate is a unique, well-behaved continuous choice function. The proponent can repair this by directly specifying rational piecewise-linear mass choice maps and promising continuous gaplessness. That makes a clear new problem, but the promise and the maps are additional modelling structure; the paper does not show that they describe aggregates of its agents. Restricting firms to choice rules for which aggregation behaves cleanly may produce a sensible model, though it narrows the generality that motivates SGAM.

Proposition 6.4 has the same obstacle. Its algorithm makes discrete progress through integer allocations until it reaches stability. A continuous stable-allocation algorithm may exist for a carefully represented class of mass choice maps, but it would need its own input model and complexity measure. An “exact efficient oracle” for arbitrary real-valued maps does not by itself supply a finite bit-length input. Giving the maps as rational piecewise-linear functions would fix that issue, while again defining a new computational setting.

These objections do not establish that the questions are unworthy. A market with many workers and firms in a small number of genuinely complete types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the existing real-valued Alkan–Gale theory supplies background, not a computational collision. Nor does the paper’s use of integer allocations mean a mass mirror is pointless. The negative case is that neither named result currently transfers its central algorithmic object to that regime without a substantial new aggregation model and a new theorem.

That leaves the case against weaker than the case for. With rational polyhedral type-level choice maps, constructing a stable mass allocation or representing all stable mass allocations is well-posed and plausibly worthwhile. The paper itself gives little basis for saying no scenario could support such a mirror; it mainly shows that the proposed anchors are research directions extending the paper, rather than direct continuizations of its algorithms.
