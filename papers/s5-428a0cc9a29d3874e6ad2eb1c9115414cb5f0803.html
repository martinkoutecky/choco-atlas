The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a plausible population-level mirror here, but the paper supplies no named computational result in the required sense: it has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition establishing that a problem is tractable or hard. Table 2 reports different winning options under different voting methods, and the conclusion summarizes that finding, but these are empirical results from an experiment with 120 participants—not complexity results. They can motivate a mirror; they cannot honestly serve as the required anchors.

The strongest candidate I see is a Population Legitimacy–Outcome Selection problem. Imagine a large online consultation with a fixed set of options and voting methods. A type \(t\) is a complete response profile: its scores for every option under each method, plus its legitimacy rating \(\ell_{t,v,c}\in\{0,\ldots,4\}\) for method \(v\) in context \(c\). Given a distribution \(\mu\) over these types, ask, for a specified context, which method maximizes population-average legitimacy,
\[ L_{v,c}=\sum_t\mu_t\ell_{t,v,c}, \]
and what winner that method produces,
\[ w_v\in\arg\max_o\sum_t\mu_t s^v_{t,o}. \]
A solution is the maximizing method, its winner or winner set, and the resulting aggregates. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the share of participants with that full profile.

A plausible regime is recurring municipal or national consultations run through a digital platform: millions of participants, with many sharing the same response and rating profiles, so the support size \(\tau\) is much smaller than the population. The mirror covers the paper’s comparison of input methods, legitimacy ratings, and outcome variation. Given the type distribution, it should be tractable by weighted summation and comparison across the four methods. The article’s reported winner changes and context-dependent legitimacy ratings make those quantities natural to aggregate, though neither finding is a formal algorithmic anchor.

The weakest point is that this turns an experimental study of how people perceive methods into a population-level selection problem for a policymaker. The authors might accept it as a useful computational extension of their question, but the paper itself does not pose or solve that optimization problem. So this is a plausible mirror, not an anchored positive case of the kind the prompt requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this is not a computational paper. It states no complexity result and poses no algorithmic problem. Its findings are empirical: the authors compare legitimacy ratings and voting outcomes from a preregistered experiment with 120 participants. Those findings may motivate a computational question, but they do not supply one to mirror.

The proposed Population Legitimacy–Outcome Selection problem is coherent, and a large consultation could plausibly have many people with the same complete response profile. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; treating repeated profiles as types is not the problem. The problem is that, given the listed types and their masses, the proposed task is just weighted summation. Compute each method’s average legitimacy, tally its scores for each option, and compare the four methods. With \(\tau\) listed types, this takes \(O(\tau)\) work up to the fixed number of methods and options. The same applies to the paper’s other findings: context comparisons, consistent-voter subgroups, and AUC summaries can all be calculated type by type and averaged. This is a population formulation, but it offers no evident complexity landscape for the programme to chart.

The strongest improvement would be to ask how much the population must change before a winner or the legitimacy ordering changes. That would connect naturally to the paper’s finding that voting methods can produce different winners. But the paper supplies no model of population changes, costs of changing responses, or intervention objective. Adding those could produce a useful robustness or manipulation problem; the resulting computational question would come from the added model, not from a result this paper establishes. Likewise, optimizing a choice among the four methods remains a comparison of four aggregate values. Making the method family or intervention structure richer could create harder algorithms, but would import the central computational content from elsewhere.

So the affirmative case has a plausible population setting, but its proposed task is elementary aggregation. The negative case is strongest when framed narrowly: this paper offers no anchored computational mirror, and making one nontrivial requires adding a new problem. That does not prove no adjacent high-multiplicity study could be worthwhile—winner robustness is a credible lead—but the paper itself gives no basis for treating that extension as the computational counterpart of its results.
