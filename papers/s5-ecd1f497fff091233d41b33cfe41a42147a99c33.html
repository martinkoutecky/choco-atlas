The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–5 characterize the quasi-Owen value axiomatically; Theorem 6 characterizes the core; Theorem 7 gives a sufficient condition for core membership; and Theorem 8 gives conditions for a PMAS. None states a complexity bound or studies an algorithm. Equation (7) defines an allocation, but the paper does not analyze its computation. There is therefore no named computational claim here whose continuous version could complete, simplify, or delimit the programme’s complexity landscape.

The best rescue would be to ask how hard it is to compute the quasi-Owen allocation, or test core membership or the PMAS conditions, from a high-multiplicity input. The paper does not specify a computational input model for those tasks. Its game includes both an augmenting system over player subsets and a coalition-value function \(v\) over feasible coalitions. A distribution of player types alone does not determine either one: it does not say which combinations can cooperate or what their joint surplus is. A continuous version would need additional mass-dependent feasibility and value functions. Complexity results would then depend on those choices and their encodings, making them a new computational agenda rather than mirrors of results in this paper.

The food-supply-chain example does not supply the missing anchor. It has five named companies, each occupying a distinct role, with specific dependencies and competition. One could imagine many interchangeable firms within each role, but then a continuum model would need to say how fractional participation interacts with feasible coalitions, chain order, and surplus. Even if that model were made persuasive, it would create a new setting for computational questions; the paper’s stated results remain axiomatic allocation claims.

Theorem 8 is the proponent’s closest candidate, but its claim is that specified chain-count conditions and quasi-chain coalitional convexity imply a PMAS. A continuous analogue could ask whether a mass-based allocation remains population monotonic. That is a plausible axiomatic question, and the brief places axiomatic continuization outside ChoCo’s scope. Turning the conditions into a decision or computation problem would require a representation of the continuum feasibility structure and value function that the paper does not provide.

I would not claim that no repeated-player model could ever be worthwhile. A carefully specified anonymous supply-chain game might support one. The honest negative case is narrower: this paper offers no computational result as an anchor, and its plausible population extension would require substantial new model choices before any computational problem is well-posed.
