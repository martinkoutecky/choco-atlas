The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and at most \(K\) rounds, each adaptive arrival reveals a good type and a rational supply \(q_r>0\) copies per capita. Goods must be allocated immediately, and each individual receives an integer number of copies. What is the supremal ratio \(\rho^*(\tau,K,L)\) that a deterministic online policy can guarantee, at every possible stopping round and for almost every individual, under \(B_a+U_a\geq \rho^* V_a\), and can a policy attaining that ratio be computed efficiently?

The model it lives in

A population of mass \(1\) with finite support over complete additive valuation types. At round \(r\), an adaptive adversary reveals rational per-capita supply \(q_r\) of a good and its value \(v_t(g_r)\) to each type; the policy distributes the copies among individuals so each receives an integer quantity, represented through the masses of type-history states. For each individual \(a\), \(V_a=\sum_r q_r v_t(g_r)\), \(B_a=\sum_r k_r(a)v_t(g_r)\), and \(U_a\) is the value of their best unreceived copy. The objective is the largest \(\rho\) guaranteeing \(B_a+U_a\geq \rho V_a\) almost everywhere at every stopping round.

The objection that survived

An efficiency result needs a finite encoding for policies over measurable individual allocations, such as a representation by masses of type-history states.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 3.6 in the no-information setting; it leaves Theorems 3.4, 4.5, 4.6, and 5.1 unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the optimal guarantee \(\rho^*(\tau,K,L)\) against an adaptive adversary?
  • Can type-history mass states and online policies be represented finitely so that an optimal or near-optimal policy can be computed efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s no-information, adaptive PROP1 result. Call it Online Mass-PROP1. Imagine an aid agency serving a very large population whose members fall into a small number \(\tau\) of complete valuation types. Donations arrive in bulk lots; lot sizes scale with the population. The number of residents \(N\) is large while \(\tau\) stays small. A type specifies everything relevant to an individual’s additive values, so two people of the same type are interchangeable for this problem.

Here is the question I would hand to a prover. Normalize population mass to \(1\), with rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). At each round, an adaptive adversary reveals a batch of \(q_r>0\) copies per capita of one good, with value \(v_t(g_r)\geq 0\) for each type \(t\). The policy sees the batch, allocates it immediately, and does not know when arrivals will stop. Its action is a measurable integer-valued function \(k_r(a)\): each individual \(a\) receives an integer number of copies, while \(\int k_r(a)\,da=q_r\). Thus a fractional quantity is permitted at the population level, but no individual receives a fraction of a good.

For an individual \(a\) of type \(t\), let \(B_a=\sum_r k_r(a)v_t(g_r)\) be their allocated value and \(V_a=\sum_r q_rv_t(g_r)\) the per-capita value of the full supply. In this scaling, \(V_a\) is the limit of the paper’s proportional share \(v_i(M)/n\). Let \(U_a=\max_r v_t(g_r)\), the value of the best unreceived good. The policy succeeds at ratio \(\alpha\) if, whenever the adversary stops, \(B_a+U_a\geq\alpha V_a\) for almost every individual. Given support size \(\tau\) and a maximum of \(K\) arrival rounds for analysis, the problem is to determine the best ratio \(\rho^*(\tau,K)\), and whether a policy attaining it can be computed efficiently. The policy itself is not told \(K\).

The anchor is Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(n\geq2\), every sufficiently large \(m\), and every deterministic online algorithm with no future information, there is an adaptive \(m\)-good instance on which the algorithm’s PROP1 ratio is at most \(4n\log\log m/\log m\). For fixed \(n\), this tends to zero, ruling out any positive guarantee depending only on \(n\). The construction uses two agents with nonzero valuations and extends to larger \(n\) by adding zero-valued dummy agents. This is an online impossibility bound, not an NP-hardness theorem, and it is proved here rather than cited.

This mirror preserves the authors’ central question: can an online allocator maintain individual proportional fairness against adaptive arrivals when future goods are unknown? It keeps additive values, irrevocable decisions, the one-good PROP1 correction, and an ex-post guarantee for individuals. What changes is the population object and the supply scale: batches have per-capita quantities so a growing population still faces a meaningful allocation problem. That is a direct consequence of making the population continuous. Fractional type averages do not replace integral individual bundles.

My lead conjecture is Class A for fixed \(\tau\): the atomless cohorts may let a policy spread each bulk lot across interchangeable members and balance proportionality debt without the discrete bottleneck of repeatedly forcing one named agent to receive or miss a whole good. That suggests asking first whether a positive constant ratio independent of \(K\) is possible, then determining its optimal dependence on \(K\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. I would not predict exact PROP1 without a construction.

The weak point is substantial. Theorem 3.6’s construction acts on two particular agents, so its lower bound does not automatically survive when those roles become large cohorts whose members can share the supply. Also, batches change the adversary’s decision granularity compared with choosing after each individual good. The theorem therefore motivates this question; it does not establish its answer. A surviving lower bound would need a new argument showing how an adaptive adversary defeats mass splitting.

I would limit the mirror to Theorem 3.6’s no-information setting. The paper’s MIV results and its positive online algorithm are separate anchors that need their own continuous questions; they are not needed for this case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is the strongest anchor, but its impossibility construction does not carry over to a continuous population. The key mismatch is how supply scales.

If we duplicate the paper’s \(m\) individual goods across a population of \(N\) agents, the one-good correction makes the problem trivial once \(N\ge m\). For any agent, let \(H\) be the value of their most valuable good. They either received it, so \(B+U\ge H\), or missed it, so \(U=H\). Since \(V\le mH\), we get \(B+U\ge V/m\ge V/N\): every allocation is PROP1. Theorem 3.6’s meaningful lower bound is for fixed \(n\) and a growing stream; it does not give a useful bound when the population grows with the stream.

The proponent’s stronger version avoids that degeneracy by scaling supply with population. But now each arrival is a batch that can be split across a cohort. In Theorem 3.6, the adversary repeatedly forces one indivisible good to one of two particular agents. A mass policy can distribute a batch among subsets of each type, so those forced choices and the theorem’s individual score dynamics no longer apply. The theorem motivates the question; it does not support the proposed conjecture that the continuum removes the bottleneck.

There is a sensible way to state that stronger question: track the mass of each type across its possible allocation histories, and ask for the best online guarantee against an adversary that observes those mass allocations. That would preserve integer bundles for individuals while allowing the population to split across histories. It is a legitimate continuous-population problem, but its state and adversary are new. Its computational representation would need to be specified, and the answer could be interesting either way.

So the best negative case defeats Theorem 3.6 as an anchor for a transferred impossibility result: with fixed supply, PROP1 trivializes; with supply scaled to population, the construction’s indivisible-agent mechanism disappears. I cannot honestly extend that into a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The mass-over-histories version survives as a plausible research question, and that is where the negative case is weakest.
