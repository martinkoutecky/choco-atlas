The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_θ>0\) summing to \(1\), rational value vectors \(u_{θ j}\), per-capita budgets \(B_θ>0\), and rational separable stepwise-increasing seller costs \(C_j\) on normalized quantities, compute rational prices \(p_j>0\) and per-agent bundles \(x_{θ j}\ge 0\). Each type's bundle must maximize its quasi-linear utility subject to \(\sum_j p_j x_{θ j}\le B_θ\), and each aggregate quantity \(y_j=\sum_{θ\in T}\mu_θ x_{θ j}\) must maximize \(p_j z-C_j(z)\) over feasible \(z\).

The model it lives in

Represent each type θ by one synthetic buyer with value vector \(u_θ\) and budget \(\mu_θ B_θ\); its allocation is the type's aggregate bundle \(\mu_θ x_θ\). Apply Theorem 2 to the τ support types with seller costs measured per unit of normalized population, then divide each type allocation by \(\mu_θ\). The resulting algorithm is polynomial in the number of goods, τ, the total cost-segment count, and the input bit length.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's polynomial-time competitive-equilibrium computation, parameterized by the number of goods, support size, cost segments, and input bit length; it does not separately mirror Theorem 1's rational-value argument or the paper's application claims.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact finite-buyer to continuous-type equilibrium correspondence be stated bidirectionally, with bit-length bounds under per-capita normalization?
  • Can the polynomial-time bound be strengthened to a strongly polynomial one for stepwise seller costs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Continuous Arctic Equilibrium Computation. Let \(G\) be the goods, and let the finite support \(T\) consist of buyer types \(\theta=(u_\theta,B_\theta)\): a type’s complete description is its value vector for the goods and its budget. The input gives each type a rational mass \(\mu_\theta>0\), with \(\sum_{\theta\in T}\mu_\theta=1\), and separable stepwise-increasing seller cost curves per unit of population. A solution is a price vector \(p\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^{G}\) and a per-agent bundle \(x_\theta\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{G}\) for each type. Each \(x_\theta\) must maximize that type’s quasi-linear utility subject to its budget at prices \(p\); the aggregate quantity \(y_j=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{\theta j}\) of each good must maximize the seller’s profit \(p_jz_j-c_j(z_j)\). There is no separate welfare objective: the task is to find an outcome satisfying buyer demand and seller profit maximization simultaneously.

A natural regime is a restructuring auction with many creditors submitting from a small menu of standardized bid templates. Creditors who share the same values for the offered instruments and the same budget are one type; their mass is their fraction of the creditor population. The model is most persuasive when there are many more creditors than distinct templates. As the population grows, supply breakpoints and costs should be expressed per capita, so that duplicating the population scales aggregate supplies and costs with it. This is a population continuation; the fact that the paper already allows divisible allocations does not do that work.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that for a rational Arctic auction with \(n\) goods, \(m\) buyers, \(K=\sum_jK_j\) marginal-cost segments, and maximum input-rational bit length \(L\), the paper’s algorithm computes a competitive equilibrium in at most
\[ O\!\left((m+K)(m+n)^5(L+\log(m+n))\right) \]
outer-loop iterations and
\[ O\!\left((m+K)mn(n+\log(m+n))(m+n)^5(L+\log(m+n))\right) \]
max-flow computations. The paper proves this result for individual buyers, not for a continuous population; the mirror follows by aggregating identical buyers. For type \(\theta\), the compressed buyer has budget \(\mu_\theta B_\theta\) and the same value vector \(u_\theta\). Its aggregate demand is exactly the mass-scaled demand of identical individuals, so the algorithm applies to the \(\tau=|T|\) type rows. Dividing each returned type allocation by \(\mu_\theta\) gives the stated per-agent bundle.

Thus the continuous question is: given rational type masses, type values and budgets, and rational per-capita stepwise seller costs, compute a competitive equilibrium. The expected class is tractable: substituting \(m=\tau\) in Theorem 2 gives a polynomial-time algorithm in the number of goods \(n\), support size \(\tau\), total number of cost segments \(K\), and input bit length \(\Lambda\) after aggregation. The resulting bounds are those above with \(m=\tau\) and \(L=\Lambda\). This is a genuine high-multiplicity compression: if the discrete population has counts \(n_\theta\) among these types, its normalized instance has \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\); arbitrary rational masses extend that representation to the continuous model.

The authors should recognize this as their equilibrium-computation problem: the buyer types retain the paper’s full preference and budget data, and seller costs remain part of the equilibrium conditions. The mirror covers the paper’s polynomial-time equilibrium-computation result, not every application or its extensions. Further questions include whether the compressed algorithm can be made strongly polynomial, as the discussion asks for the costless case, and whether tractability survives nonseparable seller constraints.

The weak point is the scenario, not the aggregation: real creditors may have sufficiently distinct bids or budgets that \(\tau\) grows almost as fast as the population. Standardized bid templates make a large-\(N\), small-\(\tau\) regime plausible, but the paper itself does not establish that empirical premise. The continuous result is therefore a strong mirror for that regime, not a claim that every sovereign-debt market has low type support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject this as a worthwhile mirror on one ground: the proposed continuous instance is an exact re-encoding of the market already handled by Theorem 2. The concern is not that the algorithm happens to remain polynomial, or that divisible allocations make the population continuous. It is that, for these buyers, grouping a mass of identical agents creates no new demand problem.

For a type \(t=(u_t,B_t)\) with mass \(\mu_t\), replace the type by one buyer with value vector \(u_t\) and budget \(\widehat m_t=\mu_t B_t\). At any prices \(p\), that buyer’s demand set is exactly \(\mu_t\) times the type’s per-agent demand set: \(D(u_t,\mu_t B_t,p)=\mu_t D(u_t,B_t,p)\). Thus an aggregate allocation \(Y_t\) to the synthetic buyer corresponds to the per-agent bundle \(x_t=Y_t/\mu_t\). Summing those allocations gives the same total supply, so the seller faces the same profit-maximization conditions. With rational masses and budgets, this is a polynomial-bit input transformation.

Consequently, the continuous equilibrium question in the proposed mirror is already an instance of the paper’s problem with \(m=\tau\). Theorem 2 applies directly, including when types are interpreted as large creditor cohorts; the per-capita cost schedule can be expressed on the normalized population. The mirror introduces no distinct objective, constraint system, or computational object for the paper to analyze. A version with an uncountable range of valuations might escape this reduction, but it would require a density or oracle input model beyond the paper and the programme’s finite-support encoding, so it would be a different problem.

This is a narrow objection: the standardized-creditor scenario is plausible, and the type-level model is sensible. Its force depends on treating this exact demand-preserving aggregation as a model collision rather than as a worthwhile dictionary result. If ChoCo counts even this direct support-compressed corollary as a meaningful deliverable, I cannot honestly rule out the mirror; the negative case is weak beyond the collision argument.
