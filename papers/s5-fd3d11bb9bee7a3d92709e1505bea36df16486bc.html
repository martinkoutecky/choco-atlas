What the mirror covers

The proposed role-based extension would concern aggregate pivotality; it does not specify a mirror for the paper's general multi-alternative influence measure or its axiomatic results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-backed positive case under the stated rules: the paper has no numbered result claiming a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another computational class.

The nearest result is Theorem 17. It says that, for a finite simple game and a vote-profile distribution whose probability depends only on the number of “yes” votes, a player’s probability of being pivotal in a random roll-call equals that player’s Shapley–Shubik index. Kurz proves this combinatorially here; the paper notes an earlier equivalent result, [10, Proposition 4]. That is a mathematical identity, not a complexity or algorithmic result. Theorem 27 gives a formula for the influence shares of a separable continuous game, but it keeps \(n\) players fixed and makes their input and output spaces continuous. That is continuity of the alternatives, not of the population, so it is outside ChoCo’s scope.

A plausible *unanchored* lead would be a large assembly with repeated voting roles: types encode complete roles such as voting weight, mass is the fraction of members in each role, and the output is each type’s aggregate pivotality under a random roll-call. But this replaces individual power with type-level aggregate power, and the paper states no computational result about that limit. With one symmetric type, the aggregate Shapley–Shubik power is simply \(1\); richer roles would need a new model and new results. I therefore can’t honestly turn this into a prover-ready mirror for any named anchor in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is also the cleanest: the paper contains no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent is right that Theorem 17 is the nearest anchor, but it proves a combinatorial identity for a finite simple game. It does not analyze an algorithm or a complexity class.

A better mirror of Theorem 17 is possible: take larger and larger assemblies with repeated voting roles, then study each role’s *aggregate* Shapley–Shubik power. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and aggregation can avoid the problem that any one person has zero mass in a continuum. But the paper does not define this limit or supply a computational representation for it. A direct continuum model would also need to specify how the random roll-call and the role-level influence measure work. Those choices may lead to worthwhile research, but they introduce a new computational question; they do not mirror a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 27 gives even less of an anchor for ChoCo: it fixes \(n\) players and makes each player’s input and the collective output continuous. The number of players remains finite. A version with a continuum population would need a new model of type-level influence, with its own algorithmic input and complexity measure.

The repeated-role assembly is therefore the best case for a continuous extension, and I would not dismiss it as an implausible high-multiplicity scenario. Its weakness is that it establishes a possible new project, rather than a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s named computational results. The negative case is strong on that point, while the universal claim that no such extension could be worthwhile is weaker: type-level aggregate power could be worth studying in its own right.
