The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find an eligible computational anchor in this paper. Proposition 1 says that a welfare-maximizing policy lies at \((\alpha,1-\alpha)\), on \(\beta=1\), or on \(\gamma=1\). Proposition 2 says that if \(g\) is concave, \((\beta,\gamma)=(1,1)\) is optimal. Both are analytical results about welfare and policy structure; neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm with a stated complexity. So there are no anchors here for a continuous mirror under the requested criteria.

The strongest plausible model is already the paper’s own: two types, majority and minority, have masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\); a policy chooses \((\beta,\gamma)\); and the objective is to maximize the utilitarian welfare \(W(\beta,\gamma)\) in equation (4). A city or institution with many people in each of two homogeneous groups makes the high-multiplicity regime plausible. But the paper already expresses its society in proportions and its policy in continuous shares. Turning those same quantities into a “mirror” would restate the model, not continuize a discrete problem. The continuity of \(\beta,\gamma\) is also continuity of the policy space, not evidence of a new continuous-population question.

A computational study of how to find the welfare-maximizing policy could be a sensible follow-up, but it would add a problem and an input representation for \(g\) that this paper does not specify. It cannot supply the missing anchor. My positive case is therefore limited: the model is naturally expressed in population masses, but the paper is already there, and it contains no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest reason against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes where welfare maximization can occur; Proposition 2 gives a condition under which the fully mixed policy is optimal. The examples show that other policies can be optimal for particular utility functions. None states a complexity result or analyzes an algorithm for a computational problem.

A city or institution with large majority and minority groups is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But the paper already models those groups by their population shares, \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\), and represents policy choices by continuous shares \((\beta,\gamma)\). A finite version could restrict the group shares—or the policy choices—to increments of \(1/n\), but that would add a grid to an already continuous welfare model. It would not produce the discrete computational problem whose high-multiplicity version the programme is meant to study. The same applies to the paper’s core propositions: they already reason over continuous shares, rather than deriving a continuous model from individually listed people.

The strongest proposed computational follow-up would be to maximize \(W(\beta,\gamma)\) given \(g\). But the paper does not specify how \(g\) is represented as input. For a fixed, explicit family such as the polynomial example, Proposition 1 reduces the search to the boundary cases and ordinary low-dimensional optimization. For an unrestricted \(g\), there is no finite input from which to compute an exact optimum. One could build a different problem by supplying a rich representation of \(g\), or by allowing many utility-function types, but any resulting complexity could come from that added encoding and heterogeneity. It would be a new optimization model, not a mirror of the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strongest for a faithful mirror: this paper’s population is already expressed in masses, while its results are analytical rather than computational. A broader follow-up might be worth studying, but the paper does not provide a grounded computational anchor for it. The universal claim that no imaginable extension could be worthwhile is not provable from the paper; the narrower conclusion is that no convincing continuous mirror of its results is apparent.
