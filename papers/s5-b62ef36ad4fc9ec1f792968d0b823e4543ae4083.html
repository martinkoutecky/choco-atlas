The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(T_A,T_B\) with positive rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) summing to \(1\), strict preferences over opposite-side types, and a candidate flow \(\pi_{ij}\ge 0\) with row sums \(\alpha_i\) and column sums \(\beta_j\), consider offline certification of stability and \(B\)-optimality. The \(A\)-type preferences are known to the verifier; the full \(B\)-type preferences are known to the query-set optimizer but hidden from the verifier. A query asks whether \(B\)-type \(j\) prefers \(A\)-type \(i\) to \(A\)-type \(i'\), and its answer applies to that whole type. Define \(B\)-optimality by requiring that for every stable flow \(\pi'\), each \(B\)-type receives at least as much mass in every prefix of its preference order under \(\pi\) as under \(\pi'\). Given a true stable, \(B\)-optimal flow \(\pi\) and an integer \(k\), is there a set of at most \(k\) comparisons whose true answers certify that every completion of the hidden preferences consistent with them leaves \(\pi\) stable and \(B\)-optimal?

The model it lives in

A large standardized placement market with interchangeable cohorts: each type has a rational population mass and a shared ranking over partner types; a feasible flow \(\pi\) records the mass assigned between types. The offline optimizer knows the full rankings and chooses a minimum set of type-level comparisons whose answers certify stability and \(B\)-optimality against every consistent completion.

The objection that survived

The paper does not establish that improvements among fractional type-flows are characterized by type-level rotations, or that query certificates correspond exactly to feedback arc sets in that model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the offline comparison-query hardness and approximation results, Theorems 11 and 12. It leaves the online competitive-ratio results, interview and set queries, and two-sided uncertainty aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can type-level rotations characterize improvements among stable fractional flows, and does a surviving directed cycle always support a positive-mass transfer?
  • Under what conditions does the minimum type-level certificate correspond to a feedback arc set, yielding the proposed hardness and approximation results?
  • How does the type-mass problem relate to finite replicated markets when the masses are rational?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the offline comparison-query problem for certifying a \(B\)-optimal stable matching. In the expanded version matching the supplied text, Theorem 11 proves that, given full information about the hidden \(B\)-side preferences, finding a minimum set of comparison queries sufficient to find or verify the \(B\)-optimal stable matching is NP-hard. The authors prove this here by reducing from Minimum Feedback Arc Set: graph vertices become matched pairs, graph arcs become potential rotation edges, and queries that rule out those edges correspond to deleting arcs until no directed cycle remains. The conference version numbers the corresponding hardness result Theorem 10. [Expanded version](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/34707/34707.pdf), [conference version](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol317-approx-random2024/LIPIcs.APPROX-RANDOM.2024.17/LIPIcs.APPROX-RANDOM.2024.17.pdf)

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. Call it Type-Mass Offline \(B\)-Optimality Certification. The input has finite \(A\)-type and \(B\)-type sets \(T_A,T_B\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict preference orders over opposite-side types. Every member of a type shares its type’s preferences. The \(A\)-type orders are known to the verifier; the \(B\)-type orders are hidden from the verifier but known to the offline query-set optimizer. A matching is a mass flow \(\pi_{ij}\ge0\) with row sums \(\alpha_i\) and column sums \(\beta_j\).

For a proposed flow \(\pi\), type pair \((i,j)\) can block if both types have positive mass currently assigned to less-preferred partners: \(w_A(i,j)=\sum_{j':\,b_j\succ_i b_{j'}}\pi_{ij'}>0\) and \(w_B(i,j)=\sum_{i':\,a_i\succ_j a_{i'}}\pi_{i'j}>0\). Stability means no such pair exists. Define \(B\)-optimality by stochastic dominance: for every stable flow \(\pi'\), each \(B\)-type receives at least as much mass in every prefix of its preference order under \(\pi\) as under \(\pi'\). Thus no stable flow improves any \(B\)-type’s partner distribution.

The decision question is: given the full preference profile, a true stable and \(B\)-optimal flow \(\pi\), and an integer \(k\), is there a set \(Q\) of at most \(k\) type-level comparison queries that certifies \(\pi\)’s stability and \(B\)-optimality? A query asks whether \(B\)-type \(j\) prefers \(A\)-type \(i\) to \(A\)-type \(i'\). It costs one query because its answer applies to every member of type \(j\). “Certifies” means that every completion of the hidden preferences consistent with the query answers still makes \(\pi\) stable and \(B\)-optimal. The solution is the query set \(Q\); the objective is to minimize \(|Q|\). The relevant input parameters are \(|T_A|,|T_B|\), the rational input bit length \(L\), and \(k\), rather than the total population size.

The regime is a large standardized placement market: many applicants share a small set of credential and preference templates, and many positions share a small set of role templates. Within a type, agents share all preferences relevant to the matching. With \(N\) participants on each side and \(\tau=|T_A|+|T_B|\), the intended regime has \(N\gg\tau\). The population is the continuous object: type masses replace headcounts, and \(\pi\) records how much of each cohort is matched to each other cohort. Fractional entries in \(\pi\) describe averages; each actual member still receives one partner. This is the allowed co-modification that follows from making the population continuous.

The FAS reduction appears to survive in this model, which is why I expect the exact problem to be Class B, hardness transferred. For a directed graph \(G\), use one type of each side per vertex, give each type mass \(1/|V|\), and propose the diagonal flow. Let \(A\)-type \(v\) rank its out-neighbour \(B\)-types below its own matched type and all non-neighbours above it; let each \(B\)-type rank its own \(A\)-type first. The diagonal flow is stable and \(B\)-optimal. Each graph arc \(v\to u\) is a potential rotation edge, and a comparison by \(B\)-type \(u\) between its incumbent \(A\)-type \(u\) and \(A\)-type \(v\) can rule it out. A surviving directed cycle permits a positive mass rotation of size \(\epsilon\) around that cycle under a preference completion consistent with the unanswered comparisons. Hitting every such cycle requires a feedback arc set. The combinatorics remain in the support graph, even when every type contains many agents.

My supporting anchor is Theorem 12, also proved in the expanded paper. It says the offline comparison-query optimization problem for finding the \(B\)-optimal stable matching admits an \(O(\log n\log\log n)\)-approximation, using an approximation for weighted Feedback Arc Set. For the type-mass version, the corresponding target is an \(O(\log\tau\log\log\tau)\)-approximation. The same cycle-certificate structure gives a plausible route to that guarantee with the type graph’s size replacing the number of named agents. [Expanded version, Theorems 11–12](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/34707/34707.pdf)

The authors should recognize this as their problem: the objective is still to minimize preference comparisons that let a verifier establish stability and \(B\)-optimality, and rotations still supply the obstacle. Type-level queries merely reuse information among agents with identical preferences—the high-multiplicity premise. It is not a claim that fractional outcomes alone make the paper continuous; the society itself is represented by mass over complete preference types.

The weakest point is that the paper’s proof uses one-to-one matchings of named agents, strict individual rankings, and the classical rotation structure. A fractional type-flow needs a sound stability and \(B\)-optimality theory, and the proposed stochastic-dominance definition must line up with what the authors mean by “\(B\)-optimal.” Theorem 11 therefore does not, by itself, prove NP-hardness of this mirror. What makes the case survive that objection is that the reduction’s cycle argument has a direct mass interpretation: every directed cycle supports an \(\epsilon\)-mass rotation, while deleting a feedback arc set removes all such cycles.

This covers the paper’s offline comparison-query results only. It does not claim a mirror for the online competitive-ratio results, the interview or set-query models, or the paper as a whole. The next questions are whether the type-mass rotation theorem and the FAS certificate equivalence can be proved formally, how the optimum relates to query complexity in a finite replicated market, and which parameters of the type graph yield fixed-parameter algorithms or stronger approximation guarantees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the paper’s query problem is built around named people. A comparison query is asked of a particular \(b\) about two particular agents \(a\); its rotation certificates are cycles of named pairs. To preserve that model, a type-level flow would have to retain how each \(B\)-agent ranks the individual members of each \(A\)-type. A strict ranking distinguishes those members. If the mirror instead ranks types and shares one answer across every member, it has changed the preference and query model, not just replaced headcounts with masses.

That is a real problem for both anchors. Theorem 11’s feedback-arc-set reduction concerns queries that rule out edges between named agents. Theorem 12 approximates that same query-set objective through a graph of those edges. Neither result transfers merely by replacing \(n\) with \(\tau\); the type-level model needs its own account of stability, \(B\)-optimality, and what information a query certifies. And the proposed reduction has a concrete error as written: it puts graph-neighbour types below their matched type in the \(A\)-side ranking. Then those types do not want the proposed rotation, so a cycle does not give the claimed mass transfer. Reversing that ranking would fix this particular flaw.

But I cannot honestly turn these objections into a case that no worthwhile mirror exists. In a market where agents really do rank partner *types* and members of a type are interchangeable, a type-mass matching problem is natural. With the ranking direction repaired, a directed cycle can support a positive mass transfer; unanswered comparisons can plausibly allow a completion that improves the \(B\)-types around that cycle. The feedback-arc-set idea could therefore survive in a well-defined type-level certification problem. That is a plausible mirror of both results, not an objection settled by the paper’s named-agent formalism.

So the negative case is weak at its hardest point. I would reject the proponent’s reduction as written and require a formal type-level certificate theorem before accepting the claimed hardness or approximation. I cannot responsibly claim that this defeats the stronger, repaired mirror; Theorems 11 and 12 remain promising anchors for it.
