What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the paper's structural constancy results for strategy-proofness, strong non-bossiness, and secure implementation over a high-multiplicity queueing society, but it covers none of the computational results because the paper contains none.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a continuous-population mirror of the queueing model, although this paper is not a standard ChoCo complexity anchor: it contains no named theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), FPT, or similar. Its named results are structural mechanism-design results. That limitation should be explicit.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: if the domain satisfies weak indifference—equivalently, convexity in this model—then a social choice function satisfies strategy-proofness and strong non-bossiness if and only if it is constant. Corollary 1, also established here using the secure-implementation characterization of Saijo, Sjöström, and Yamato, states that on a weak-indifference domain a social choice function is securely implementable if and only if it is constant.

A natural mirror is a large appointment or event queue. There are many customers, but only a small number of complete types: for example, urgency class, waiting-cost parameter, eligibility, and transfer constraints. A society is a mass distribution \( \mu \) over those types, with only \( \tau \) materially distinct types among millions of customers. The queue position becomes a normalized rank \(q\in[0,1]\), and money is transferred per customer, subject to a per-capita budget. For type \(t\), with waiting cost \(c(t)>0\), utility is

\[ u_t(q,p)=-c(t)q+p. \]

An allocation is a family of bundle distributions \(K_{\mu,t}\) over queue ranks and transfers such that

\[ \sum_t \mu_t K_{\mu,t}^{q}=\lambda_{[0,1]}, \qquad \sum_t\mu_t\int p\,dK_{\mu,t}\le 0. \]

Thus every unit of population receives one queue position and one transfer, while the type-level description is fractional. This is not a cosmetic relaxation: the original queueing problem itself concerns scarce positions, waiting costs, transfers, and incentive properties. Only the population and the queue scale have been continuized. Remark 2 of the paper, which says the theorem does not depend on finiteness of the position set, makes the passage from discrete slots to normalized queue rank especially plausible.

The precise problem I would propose is Continuum Secure Queueing. Its instance consists of an allowed waiting-cost domain \(C\subseteq\mathbb R_{++}\), a finite-support population distribution \(\mu\) over complete customer types, and a measurable anonymous rule \(F\) assigning the kernels \(K_{\mu,t}\). A positive mass \(\varepsilon\) of type \(t\) may report \(t'\), producing

\[ \mu'=\mu+\varepsilon(e_{t'}-e_t). \]

The rule is continuum strategy-proof if the deviating mass never gains according to its true cost, and strongly non-bossy if equality of its utility implies that the aggregate allocation to the rest of society is unchanged. The secure version asks whether there exists a direct mechanism \(M\) whose truthful outcomes are dominant-strategy outcomes and whose Nash-equilibrium outcomes are exactly \(F(\mu)\). A solution is either a nonconstant rule and implementing mechanism, or a proof that every such rule is constant.

I would expect the answer to be a constant-only theorem under an appropriate continuum definition, making this structurally tractable rather than hardness-transferring. But that would be a new result: the paper proves the finite-agent statement, not its nonatomic version. It is therefore not legitimate to claim that Theorem 1 already gives a polynomial-time continuum algorithm.

The mirror is plausible because it preserves the authors’ actual question. It does not replace queue allocation by a generic voting or welfare problem, and it does not make only the outcome fractional. The paper’s own applications—large experimental facilities and event sites—provide a credible many-agent regime. The continuous question would ask whether mass-level queue rules can be both responsive and securely implementable, exactly the issue addressed by Theorem 1 and Corollary 1.

The weakest point is strategic: with a truly nonatomic population, one individual’s report has zero mass, so naïve individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. My formulation therefore uses positive-mass cohort deviations. That is a defensible high-multiplicity analogue, but it is a co-modification of the strategic model, and the authors might regard it as stronger than their original notion. A second weakness is that the paper has no computational anchor, so this is evidence for a meaningful continuous mirror—not yet a Class A, B, or C result in the ChoCo sense.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strong on the programme’s actual eligibility criterion, although not as an absolute claim that no queueing model could ever be continuized. This paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization of strategy-proofness and strong non-bossiness; Corollary 1 is not an independent computational result, but an immediate consequence of Theorem 1 combined with an external characterization of secure implementation. Neither states a complexity bound, optimization algorithm, approximation guarantee, or computational hardness result.

The proposed event-queue scenario is a sensible high-multiplicity story. That is not the weakness. The weakness is that the proposed “Continuum Secure Queueing” problem is not actually a computational problem of the kind ChoCo studies. Its input includes an arbitrary domain \(C\) and an arbitrary measurable rule \(F\), while its output is either a new mechanism or a proof that all such mechanisms are constant. There is no finite encoded optimization instance, no objective, and no meaningful resource parameter. It is a new nonatomic mechanism-design theorem, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed continuum queue \(q\in[0,1]\) adds little. It is an outcome-space change, and Remark 2 explicitly says that Theorem 1 does not depend on finiteness of the set of queue positions. The genuinely new part would therefore be making the population atomless—but that creates a strategic problem rather than a computational opportunity.

For a rule represented only by the mass distribution \(\mu\) and type-level kernels, one individual’s report changes \(\mu\) on a null set and hence does not change the aggregate outcome. Strategy-proofness is then vacuous at the population level, while strong non-bossiness of the aggregate allocation is automatic. The kernels also do not specify which particular individual receives which position or transfer, so they cannot by themselves evaluate the deviator’s own utility. An identity-level “lifting” can restore that information, but then the crucial strategic object is no longer the continuous society; it is additional individual assignment data that the mass model discarded.

The proponent’s repair—positive-mass cohort deviations—is defensible as a new model, but it changes the strategic game from individual dominant-strategy/Nash implementation to implementation against coordinated coalitions of positive measure. That may be interesting, but it is not the paper’s secure-implementation problem in a high-multiplicity limit. It requires a new definition of equilibrium, deviation, and mechanism, and the paper supplies no computational result to anchor it.

One could add welfare, fairness, payment, or optimal-ordering objectives to obtain an optimization problem. But those objectives are absent from the paper. Adding them would produce a new continuous queue-allocation or transport problem, not a mirror of Theorem 1 or Corollary 1. Restricting attention to the paper’s convex domains leaves only the constant-rule characterization; moving to nonconvex domains may be richer, but is likewise new territory unsupported by this paper.

Thus the strongest fair verdict is: the paper admits a plausible continuous-population mechanism-design reinterpretation, but no worthwhile ChoCo mirror of its contribution. The proposed mirror survives the basic high-multiplicity plausibility test, yet fails the more important test: there is no named computational question to continuize, and the nonatomic limit either erases the individual strategic content or requires replacing it with a new coalitional model.
