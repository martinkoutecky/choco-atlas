The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\subseteq\mathbb R\), finite candidate set \(M\subseteq\mathbb R\), society \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), and reported distribution \(\widehat\mu\), does there exist a deterministic mechanism \(F(M,\widehat\mu)\in M^2\) that is positive-mass group-strategyproof and guarantees, for every \(\mu\), a maximum-cost ratio strictly below \(3\), where \(\operatorname{MC}_{\mu}(Y)=\max_{t:\mu_t>0}\min_{y\in Y}|t-y|\)?

The model it lives in

Types are residential locations \(t\), masses are \(\mu_t\), candidate facilities remain indivisible sites in finite \(M\), and the mechanism chooses \(Y\in M^2\). Its objective is worst-case maximum distance over occupied types, with positive-mass group strategyproofness as the incentive constraint.

The objection that survived

The strongest objection is that positive-mass group strategyproofness discards \(o(n)\) coalitions and is not literally the finite-agent incentive notion, so the high-multiplicity correspondence requires explicit justification.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the two-facility line maximum-cost results in Theorems 4.5 and 4.6, including the deterministic branch; it leaves the social-cost result in Theorem 4.3, the single-facility results, and the randomized upper-bound question open.

Open questions for a prover

  • Formalize the finite-to-continuous correspondence for positive-mass group strategyproofness and prove the deterministic \(3\)-barrier.
  • Determine whether a randomized mass-level strategyproof mechanism achieves ratio \(2\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s two-facility, maximum-cost mechanism-design problem on a line. It is a genuine population continuization: facilities remain restricted to the finite candidate set, while the electorate of agents is replaced by a distribution over location types.

Let \(S=\mathbb R\) with its usual metric, let \(T\subseteq S\) be a finite set of location types, and let \(M\subseteq S\) be the finite candidate-location set. A society is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents at location type \(t\). Here a type is simply a complete location description, since location is the only private characteristic used by the paper’s utility function. A finite profile with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\) gives \(\mu_t=n_t/n\).

The authority chooses an ordered pair \(Y=(y_1,y_2)\in M^2\), allowing the same candidate twice if the original model does. The cost of type \(t\) is \(d(t,Y)=\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}|t-y_j|\), and the continuous maximum-cost objective is

\[ \operatorname{MC}_\mu(Y) = \max_{t:\mu_t>0} d(t,Y). \]

This is exactly the finite maximum cost whenever \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); multiplicities disappear from the objective, but not from the strategic reporting problem.

A mechanism \(F\) maps a reported distribution \(\widehat\mu\) and candidate set \(M\) to a pair \(F(M,\widehat\mu)\in M^2\). To retain a meaningful incentive notion in the continuum, I would use positive-mass group strategyproofness: no measurable coalition of positive population mass may change its reports so that every member strictly lowers their true-location cost. This is the natural mass-level analogue of the paper’s GSP notion. Randomized mechanisms use expected costs.

The named lead anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved in this paper. It says that, for two facilities on a line under the maximum-cost objective, Mechanism 4.1 is GSP, anonymous, and a \(3\)-approximation. Mechanism 4.1 chooses the candidate closest to the leftmost reported agent and the candidate closest to the rightmost reported agent, with the paper’s specified tie-breaking rules.

Its continuous mirror is:

\[ \textsc{Two-Facility-MC-GSP}_{\infty} \]

Given \(T\), \(M\), and a reported distribution \(\widehat\mu\), output the two candidates

\[ y_L\in\arg\min_{y\in M}|y-\min\operatorname{supp}(\widehat\mu)|, \]

breaking ties toward the right, and

\[ y_R\in\arg\min_{y\in M}|y-\max\operatorname{supp}(\widehat\mu)|, \]

breaking ties toward the left. The question is whether this rule is positive-mass GSP and whether, for every \(\mu\),

\[ \operatorname{MC}_\mu(\{y_L,y_R\}) \leq 3\min_{Y\in M^2}\operatorname{MC}_\mu(Y). \]

The answer should be yes. The paper’s approximation proof transfers almost verbatim. Take an optimal pair \(Y^\star\), partition the support types according to their nearer optimal facility, and apply the single-facility \(3\)-approximation argument to the left and right parts. The global leftmost type belongs to the left part and the global rightmost type belongs to the right part, so the two extreme-based choices each incur at most three times the relevant optimum radius. The same extremal argument establishes positive-mass GSP.

Operationally, this is tractable: find the minimum and maximum occupied types and scan \(M\) twice, in time polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\), \(m=|M|\), and the encoding length. This is therefore a Class-A-style mirror, although it is not an NP-versus-P result.

The second anchor is Theorem 4.6, also proved here. It states that, for the two-facility problem on a line under maximum cost, no deterministic strategyproof mechanism has approximation ratio better than \(3\), and no randomized strategyproof mechanism has approximation ratio better than \(2\).

Its continuous deterministic mirror is the threshold problem

\[ \textsc{Two-Facility-MC-SP-Boundary}_{\infty}: \]

Does there exist an anonymous deterministic positive-mass GSP mechanism whose worst-case ratio is strictly below \(3\)?

The deterministic answer should again be no. The paper’s lower-bound construction survives with positive masses. Put positive mass at \(1-\varepsilon\), \(1+\varepsilon\), and a very distant point \(L\), with candidates \(M=\{0,2,L\}\). The mechanism must select \(L\) for any ratio below \(3\). If its other facility is \(0\), let the positive-mass group at \(1+\varepsilon\) report \(3\). The resulting reported distribution forces the mechanism to select \(2\) rather than \(0\), because otherwise its maximum cost is \(3\), whereas the optimum is \(1+\varepsilon\). Every member of that group strictly improves from cost \(1+\varepsilon\) to \(1-\varepsilon\), contradicting positive-mass GSP. If the original other facility is \(2\), use the symmetric deviation by the group at \(1-\varepsilon\).

Thus, under this natural mass-level incentive definition, Theorems 4.5 and 4.6 together suggest an exact continuous answer: the optimal deterministic worst-case approximation ratio is \(3\). The randomized branch asks whether the paper’s lower bound \(2\) is tight in the continuous model; the paper itself leaves the matching upper bound open, so that is a genuine follow-up question rather than a claimed result.

A plausible regime is a large population distributed across a small number of residential clusters along a transportation corridor, with two public hospitals, shelters, or service centres restricted to a finite set of zoned candidate sites. Millions of residents may occupy only a few dozen relevant location types, so \(n\gg\tau\). Residents in the same cluster have the same distances to every candidate and are indistinguishable for the problem. Their reports can still be strategically relevant because the mechanism reacts to reported extremes.

The original authors should recognise this as their problem. The line metric, finite candidate locations, nearest-facility cost, two-facility decision, maximum-cost objective, anonymity, and strategyproofness are all preserved. The paper’s continuous line is not itself a collision: its agent set is still finite. What changes is only that the population profile is represented by masses. Facility locations are not fractional, and the objective is not replaced by an expectation or a lottery.

The main weakness is that maximum cost depends only on the support of \(\mu\), not on the numerical masses. Consequently, this mirror does not showcase the full computational payoff of fractional population mass as strongly as a social-cost or bribery problem would. Also, literal unilateral strategyproofness becomes vacuous in an atomless continuum, so the positive-mass coalition formulation is essential and must be defended explicitly. I regard that as a real modelling obligation, but not a fatal objection: high multiplicity naturally treats a cluster of identical agents as a manipulable mass, and the paper itself studies group strategyproofness. This is therefore a modest but credible continuous mirror, covering the paper’s two-facility maximum-cost results without pretending that the paper contains a discrete hardness result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both proposed anchors are formally definable, but neither is a genuine continuous-population computational problem in the sense of ChoCo.

First, neither Theorem 4.5 nor Theorem 4.6 is a complexity result. Theorem 4.5 proves that a particular mechanism is group-strategyproof and \(3\)-approximate; Theorem 4.6 proves impossibility bounds for strategyproof mechanisms. There is no numbered theorem about the complexity of computing an optimum, deciding manipulability, or solving an algorithmic optimization problem. A continuous analogue would therefore be a new mechanism-design question, not a continuization of the paper’s computational content.

The proposed Theorem 4.5 mirror also loses the population almost entirely. Under maximum cost,

\[ \operatorname{MC}_{\mu}(Y)=\max_{t:\mu_t>0} d(t,Y). \]

Thus the numerical values of the masses never matter. Any two societies with the same support but radically different population proportions are identical for the objective, the optimum, and Mechanism 4.1, which itself only reads the leftmost and rightmost occupied locations. The proposed “continuous society” is therefore just a finite set of occupied locations with multiplicities erased. Its algorithm is a scan for two order statistics followed by two nearest-candidate queries; no continuous optimization, high-multiplicity structure, or fractional population enters.

This is not repaired by changing the type space. With finitely many location types, maximum cost still depends only on which types have positive mass. With an atomless population, the paper’s individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous: one agent has measure zero and cannot change the aggregate report. The proposed positive-mass group strategyproofness is a new incentive notion, not the limit of the paper’s unilateral or finite-coalition notion. It deliberately discards deviations by \(o(n)\) agents, including the individual deviations that strategyproofness is designed to prohibit. Retaining those deviations requires extra finite-agent information outside the distribution, which defeats a population-only model.

The same problem undermines the proposed Theorem 4.6 mirror. The distant agent at \(L\) forces selection of \(L\) regardless of how much mass is placed there. An arbitrarily small atom has exactly the same effect as a large population because the objective is a maximum. Likewise, the deviating group at \(1+\varepsilon\) is introduced only to make the altered profile a positive-mass coalition. The lower bound is therefore still a three-point support construction; it says nothing about population multiplicity. In an atomless model the individual witness disappears, while in a finite-atom model the result is simply the original finite-support argument with weights attached.

The best escape would be to use the paper’s social-cost result, where masses genuinely matter:

\[ \operatorname{SC}_{\mu}(Y)=\sum_t \mu_t d(t,Y). \]

But that exposes why the paper’s guarantee does not have a stable continuum counterpart. Take masses \(\delta,1-2\delta,\delta\) at \(1,4/3,2\), with candidates

\[ M=\{2/3+\varepsilon,4/3,2\}. \]

Mechanism 4.1 chooses \(2/3+\varepsilon\) and \(2\), while the optimum chooses \(4/3\) and \(2\). The approximation ratio is

\[ \frac{\delta(1/3-\varepsilon)+(1-2\delta)(2/3-\varepsilon)} {\delta/3}, \]

which tends to infinity as \(\delta\to0\). This is exactly the paper’s \(2n-3\) phenomenon with the endpoint agents assigned mass \(1/n\). In the continuous regime, the guarantee is controlled by the smallest atom and collapses as that atom becomes negligible. Keeping a positive lower bound on all type masses merely reintroduces a finite-population parameter.

One could certainly study a new problem: optimal anonymous strategyproof mechanisms for weighted or atomless location distributions under social cost. That is a plausible research direction, especially for clustered residents and public facilities. But it is not a continuous mirror of the paper’s named results. The paper’s mechanism and proofs are driven by individual extremes and by the finite count \(n\); the maximum-cost results erase masses altogether, while the social-cost guarantee degenerates when masses become genuinely continuous.

So the negative case is strong against the two anchors actually proposed: they are support-based mechanism restatements, not computational continuizations. It is not airtight against every conceivable reformulation, because the social-cost model with clustered populations is a sensible new mechanism-design problem. I would therefore reject the proposed mirrors as insufficient for ChoCo, but I would not honestly claim that this paper admits no worthwhile continuous population question whatsoever.
