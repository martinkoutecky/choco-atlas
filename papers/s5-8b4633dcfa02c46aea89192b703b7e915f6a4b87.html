The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are about equilibrium existence, characterization, and comparative statics; none asserts a complexity bound, algorithm, or hardness result.

The closest results are Proposition 2 and Proposition 3, both proved in the paper. Proposition 2 says an equilibrium exists; every equilibrium has a unique vector of voting distortions \(d\), with each policy maker voting for \(R\) exactly when \(\theta_n+d_n\ge 0\). The static model’s pivots remain pivotal. Under a quasi-dictatorial institution, the unique equilibrium is sincere voting; otherwise \(d_1<\cdots<d_N\), with the left pivot’s distortion negative and the right pivot’s positive. Proposition 3 characterizes equilibrium distortions by the fixed-point equations
\[ d_n=\delta\int_{G(\Omega,d)}(\theta_n+d_n)\,dP(\theta), \]
and gives a formula for their average. These are analytic characterizations, not claims that an underlying problem is in \(P\), hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The fixed-point equations may invite computation, but the paper gives no computational result to anchor that question.

A possible new, unanchored problem would be to find the least-polarized stationary equilibrium of a weighted legislature. Its input could be a finite ordered set of ideological types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational ideological positions, a finite rational-support shock distribution, a rational discount factor, and status-dependent passage quotas. Its output would be the equilibrium voting thresholds for each type and the induced policy transition probabilities. One plausible setting is a large, party-disciplined assembly whose ideological caucuses vote as blocks.

That last assumption is the weakness: the paper’s agents vote individually, while this proposal makes each type a coordinated bloc. If instead the members are genuinely atomless individual voters, any one person’s vote has zero mass and cannot affect the outcome. The pivotal-voter incentives that generate Proposition 2’s polarization then disappear or become indeterminate. Treating a bloc as the strategic actor restores pivotality by changing who makes decisions.

So there is a possible computational extension involving a continuous mass of weighted caucuses, but I would not present it as a continuous mirror of one of this paper’s named computational results. The stochastic shocks and infinite horizon do not make the population continuous; the paper keeps a finite set of policy makers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the main reason against a mirror: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Propositions 2 and 3 characterize stationary equilibria and their voting distortions; Propositions 4–10 give comparative statics and welfare claims. None gives an algorithm, runtime bound, or hardness result. In particular, the fixed-point characterization of \(d\) is an analytic result, not a complexity classification. And the paper’s distribution \(P\) is over shocks to a fixed finite legislature, not a continuous population of voter types. Making \(P\) finite-support would discretize uncertainty, not continuize society.

The proposed equilibrium-computation problem is the strongest rescue, but it changes the strategic object at its core. In an anonymous continuum of legislators, an individual’s vote has zero mass and cannot change the policy now or the status quo later. The paper’s pivotal-voter incentive—the source of its equilibrium distortions—therefore does not carry over. If instead each ideological cohort or caucus casts a binding bloc vote, the blocs become the strategic players. That may be a sensible model of party organization, but it adds collective action or party discipline; it is not high multiplicity of the paper’s individual policy makers. Making institutional role part of each type preserves distinct roles, but then those roles do not repeat in the way a continuous population requires.

The same tension appears in the turnover extension: candidates and positions matter because they determine who can be pivotal. A distribution over candidates or shocks might support a new model, but it would not by itself give the paper’s individual pivotality a continuous-population mirror. Preserving a few pivotal officeholders as atoms in a hybrid model is possible, but then the finite strategic core remains; the continuous mass supplies a new environment around it.

So the best negative case is that the proposed problem is unanchored twice over: the paper supplies no computational result, and the natural population limit removes or replaces the mechanism behind its main equilibrium results. That makes this a poor ChoCo mirror of this paper. The universal claim is less secure: one could build a worthwhile model around disciplined blocs or a hybrid atom-and-continuum legislature. But those would require new assumptions and a new computational question, rather than continuizing a result this paper establishes.
