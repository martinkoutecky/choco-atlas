The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly supply a qualifying anchor. The paper has no numbered result establishing that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The sentence in Section 4 that exhaustive search over partitions is NP-hard is not a named result, and the Bell-number count alone does not establish NP-hardness.

The closest results are all unnumbered in the supplied text: “The Nash-stable set can be non-empty” is proved here as a feasibility reformulation; “Additively separable and symmetric preferences always admit a Nash-stable partition” is cited from elsewhere; and “Any additively separable and symmetric gain results in a potential game” is proved here, with the stated potential function. They concern existence and structure, not a complexity classification. So the set of anchors required by your instructions is empty.

There is still a plausible continuous problem in the paper’s central model. Imagine a very large deployment of identically configured edge clients, with a small number \(\mathcal A\) of complete client types: clients of a type share the same model contribution, data-size class, link reliability, reservation price, and payoff-relevant costs. Let \(\mu_a\) be each type’s population fraction. Write \(\delta(q)\) for the half-surplus available to allocate to pairwise gains in a coalition with type-mass vector \(q\), after communication costs and reservation prices. A continuous version of the paper’s symmetric allocation LP would choose a symmetric interaction matrix \(R\) to maximize \(\tfrac12\mu^\top R\mu\), subject to \(\tfrac12q^\top Rq\leq\delta(q)\) for every \(q\in[0,\mu]\). This is the mass-scaled counterpart of its pairwise gain variables and budget-balance constraints.

To capture stable clustering too, one could ask for a partition into at most \(K\) positive-mass coalitions \(q^1,\ldots,q^K\), with any remaining mass in singleton coalitions, such that the masses sum to \(\mu\) and no type prefers another coalition. In this nonatomic model, that means \((Rq^k)_a\geq(Rq^\ell)_a\) for every type \(a\) present in coalition \(k\) and every destination \(\ell\), and singleton types must weakly prefer their zero-gain singleton to every coalition. With rational \(\mu\), an explicitly represented rational polynomial \(\delta\), and \(K\) as input, this is a precise optimization question whose solution is \(R\) and the coalition mass vectors. Its generic form looks difficult because budget balance must hold over a continuum of possible coalition compositions; exploitable structure in \(\delta\) might make separation tractable.

The high-multiplicity regime is plausible for repeated, standardized client cohorts, but it is narrower than federated learning generally. The main weakness is more fundamental: the paper’s finite-player potential argument does not automatically survive the limit. An infinitesimal client changes coalition composition by zero, so a potential maximizer need not be stable under that client’s deviation. The continuous question therefore needs its own stability analysis, and the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor the hoped-for complexity comparison.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. The “Nash-stable set can be non-empty” theorem gives no computational result: its proposed feasibility condition already includes the existence of a stable partition, so it restates the question rather than supplying an algorithm. The additive symmetric result is an existence theorem cited from prior work. The potential-game theorem gives structure, but no complexity bound. And the claim that exhaustive partition search is NP-hard is not established by the Bell-number count. On the programme’s stated terms, there is no result here whose continuous complexity classification a mirror would complete.

The proposed \(R\)-matrix model is the strongest nearby idea, but it is a new continuous hedonic-game problem, not a mirror of one of those results. The paper does not specify the continuous input \(\delta(q)\) or a finite representation for it. In the stated federated-learning model, coalition gains depend on model parameters, data sizes, random transmission outcomes, and an expected loss over \(2^n\) reception patterns. Choosing an explicit polynomial \(\delta\) would make a crisp problem, but its complexity would then depend on that added payoff model; it would not classify a computational result in this paper. A careful limit would also need to define how pairwise gains and budgets scale with population, and how deviations to zero-mass or newly formed coalitions work. Those choices are possible, but they are new modeling and computational commitments.

A large deployment with repeated, standardized client types is plausible, so I would not claim that federated learning has no sensible high-multiplicity regime. Nor does the nonatomic limit make stability impossible: a suitable mass-based equilibrium notion could be studied. That is where the negative case is weak. The more defensible conclusion is narrower: this paper may inspire a worthwhile continuous hedonic-game project, but the case for a *mirror of this paper’s computational results* has no anchor, and the proposed formulation needs substantial additional choices before it becomes a specific complexity question.
