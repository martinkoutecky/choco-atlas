The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is for a computational follow-up to the opinion-pooling part of the paper, not a mirror of one of its computational results. The paper contains no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named results are aggregation and representation theorems. So it has no valid computational anchor under the criterion in the prompt.

The closest result is Theorem 24, proved here. It says that for a Boolean algebra \(X\), extended events \(\bar X\), and preorders whose restrictions to \(X\) are comparative-likelihood relations, if the extended preorders satisfy strong independence and the family satisfies P1–P4, then each restricted preorder has a representing vector measure \(f_i\), and there is a strictly positive linear map \(L:V_I\to V_0\), with each component \(L_i\) an order embedding, such that \(f_0=L f_I\). This establishes a linear-pooling representation. It is a structural existence result, not an algorithm or complexity result.

A plausible ChoCo follow-up would be Finite-Type Opinion-Pool Evaluation\(_\infty\). The instance is a finite state set \(\Omega\), a support of \(\tau\) complete belief types \(p^t\in\Delta(\Omega)\) with rational probabilities, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and two events \(A,B\subseteq\Omega\). The task is to compute the population pool
\[ q(E)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t p^t(E) \]
and decide whether \(q(A)\ge q(B)\). The output is the rational distribution \(q\) and the comparison. Exact arithmetic gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\), \(|\Omega|\), and the input bit length.

The regime is a large forecasting panel in which many forecasters share the same complete belief profile over a fixed set of states: \(N\) can be very large while \(\tau\ll N\). A type is the full probability vector \(p^t\); its mass is the fraction of the panel with that belief profile. The authors would likely recognize this as a population-scale version of linear opinion pooling, which the paper explicitly studies.

That is a modest mirror. Its main weakness is that the paper does not impose mass-weighted pooling: Theorem 24 permits a positive linear map \(L\), and does not require equal influence for individuals with the same belief type. Fixing influence to be proportional to mass adds an anonymity and normalization assumption. Also, the paper’s use of extended events supplies convex structure on the event side; it does not itself represent society as a distribution over types. The proposed problem therefore covers only the opinion-pooling application, and the paper gives no computational result to anchor it. The tractability claim belongs to this simple finite-input follow-up, not to a complexity result proved by the authors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 24 is an axiomatic representation theorem: under strong independence and P1–P4, the restricted comparative-likelihood relations admit vector-measure representations related by a strictly positive linear map. It says nothing about the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The proposed evaluation task therefore cannot be a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The finite-type pool \(q(E)=\sum_t\mu_t p^t(E)\) is a coherent population model, but it adds the key rule the theorem does not provide: influence proportional to population mass. Theorem 24 allows an arbitrary positive linear map \(L\); it does not require identical influence for people with identical beliefs, or even reduce the indexed population to a distribution over belief types. Its infinite-population result is about maps on products of individual representation spaces, not integration over a population measure. The proposed task also narrows the paper to complete, scalar-valued beliefs on a finite state space.

A stronger attempt to stay close to the theorem would ask whether a finite collection of fully specified individual and social preorders admits such a map \(L\), or ask for a witness map. But that requires a new finite input language and a choice of how the map is represented; the paper’s abstract theorem supplies neither, and population masses do not determine the map. Including influence in each type and imposing a mass-weighted pool would make the model more complete, but it would add anonymity and aggregation assumptions. An atomless version would also need to restate P3 and P4: an individual has zero mass, so pointwise strict-improvement and incomparability conditions cannot simply be inherited by an integral model.

The proposed exact pooling calculation may still be useful as a separate computational exercise; its tractability is not my objection. The problem is that it computes a chosen weighted pool, while the paper proves when a social relation has a representation of a much broader form. More ambitious tasks such as finding a pool under constraints or measuring its robustness would add a new computational question absent from the paper. So the negative case is strong on eligibility as a ChoCo mirror, though weaker as the universal claim that no related continuous research could be worthwhile: high-multiplicity forecasting panels are plausible. A project on them would need its own computational question and assumptions, rather than inheriting one from this paper.
