What the mirror covers

The proposed mixture uses the deterministic choice class in Corollary 1; it leaves Proposition 1's characterization of one person's random comparisons and Proposition 2's shortlisting characterization untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is an aggregate version of deterministic list rationalizability. The important limitation is that the paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or in another complexity class. Its closest computationally useful anchor is Corollary 1, proved here. It says that a deterministic choice function \(c\) is list-rational exactly when its revealed-to-follow relation \(F_c\) is acyclic, and that the consistent lists are precisely the completions of the transitive closure of \(F_c\). For distinct \(x,y\), \(x F_c y\) when some menu \(S\) witnesses either \(x=c(S\cup\{y\})\) and \([y=c(\{x,y\})\text{ or }x\ne c(S)]\), or \(x\ne c(S\cup\{y\})\) and \([x=c(\{x,y\})\text{ and }x=c(S)]\). The paper does not state a running-time bound, but with the full choice table explicit, this characterization gives a polynomial-time recognition and list-recovery procedure in the table size. That algorithmic consequence is an inference from the corollary, not a stated complexity theorem.

A continuous population mirror of that anchor is Population List-Rationality Recognition. An instance consists of a finite alternative set \(X\) and rational choice shares \(p_x(S)\) for every nonempty menu \(S\) and \(x\in S\), with the shares on each menu summing to one. A type is a complete deterministic list-rational choice function \(c_t\) on \(X\)—equivalently, a list and an asymmetric pairwise comparison relation inducing choices by the paper’s recursive rule. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with behavior \(c_t\). The question is whether there is such a distribution with
\[ p_x(S)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[c_t(S)=x] \]
for every menu and alternative. A solution is a finite set of types and nonnegative masses summing to one that reproduce the table exactly. If \(Q=\sum_{S\ne\emptyset}|S|=m2^{m-1}\) is the number of table entries, a feasible instance has a representation using at most \(Q+1\) types.

One plausible regime is a large panel making choices from menus drawn from the same short catalog, with many respondents following a small number of stable decision routines. For example, thousands of respondents might share only a few exact patterns of how they order and compare products. Type identity here means identical choices on every menu, so the multiplicity assumption is explicit and testable in principle. Mass is the population fraction following each routine; the task is to determine whether observed aggregate shares can be explained by such a population.

This is a genuine population question: the mass distribution is over stable individual behaviors, not over fractional outcomes assigned to one individual. It is also a modest extension authors could recognize as an aggregate version of their deterministic rationalizability question. But it must not be conflated with their stochastic model. Proposition 1 concerns random comparisons by one decision maker with a fixed list; it does not establish that a population mixture of deterministic list-rational people has the same characterization. I would keep the mirror to Corollary 1 and leave that distinction open.

My tentative expectation is continuum-specific hardness for the unrestricted aggregate problem: Corollary 1 supplies an efficient test for a single deterministic type, while the population problem asks for a decomposition into an unknown mixture of those types. That mixture question may introduce new combinatorics, though tractability through a suitable separation or pricing method is also plausible. This is a research hypothesis, not a result of the paper. Natural follow-ups include the complexity of the mixture problem, whether bounded support changes it, and how population mixtures relate to the paper’s within-person stochastic comparisons.

The weakest point is also the central one: Corollary 1 is an algorithmically useful characterization, not a named complexity result, and the aggregate decomposition problem is new. So this gives a plausible continuous mirror and a tractable type-level anchor, but no honest claim that the paper itself has already established a ChoCo-style complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor, Corollary 1, is a characterization of one person’s choice function, not a computational result. With the full choice table supplied, one can compute \(F_c\) by scanning the table and test acyclicity with a graph algorithm; that is a useful algorithmic consequence, but the paper neither poses nor analyzes a complexity problem. The result identifies which lists fit observed behavior. It does not study computation over a population.

Population List-Rationality Recognition is the strongest proposed mirror, and its consumer-panel scenario is plausible: many people could share a small number of stable choice routines. But the proposed question changes the object. It asks whether aggregate choice shares lie in the convex hull of deterministic list-rational choice functions. Corollary 1 tests an individual function; it says nothing about that convex hull or the cost of finding a decomposition. This is a potentially interesting mixture problem, but its computational content comes from adding population aggregation to the paper, not from continuizing a computational question the paper asked.

The distinction from the paper’s stochastic model matters here. Proposition 1 concerns one decision maker with a fixed list and random comparisons. A population mixture of deterministic routines instead permits heterogeneity in lists and correlations across each person’s choices on different menus. The resulting aggregate shares need not satisfy the paper’s stochastic characterization. The two models may be related, but the proposed recognition problem is not simply the paper’s stochastic result with population mass substituted for probability.

The best way to make the subject matter more clearly social choice would be to treat the routines as voter types and study control or bribery. On a fixed ballot menu, though, list rationality places no restriction on which candidate a voter selects: any candidate can be made the sequential winner by its pairwise comparisons. A one-round election then loses the paper’s distinctive structure. If the agenda changes and voters’ routines determine their choices on each new menu, that structure returns—but the intervention problem and aggregation rule have been imported. That could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new behavioral voting model, not a mirror of Corollary 1.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo’s programme: this paper offers no named computational problem to continuize, and the most plausible population extension becomes a mixture-identification problem in individual choice theory. It is weaker as the universal claim the assignment asks for. A large panel sharing stable routines is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the mixture problem could be valuable in its own right. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile population-based question; I can argue that this paper does not anchor one in the programme’s computational social choice sense.
