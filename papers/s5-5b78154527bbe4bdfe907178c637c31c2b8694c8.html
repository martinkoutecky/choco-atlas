The most plausible *unanchored* mirror would be continuous weighted Schulze winner determination. Let \(C\) be the alternatives and let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over strict rankings of \(C\). Interpret \(\mu_t\) as the share of total criterion weight assigned to criteria with ranking \(t\); this matches the paper’s treatment of criteria rankings as ballots and its support for criterion weights. For each pair, compute \(d_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t\), then return the alternative or alternatives that win under the Schulze strongest-path rule on those pairwise weights. The instance is \((C,\mu)\); the solution is the winner set. This is a winner-determination question, not a claim that the rule optimizes an external welfare objective. It should be tractable: the pairwise weights and strongest paths can be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length.

A plausible regime would be a decision process with many standardized evaluation criteria or subcriteria, many sharing the same ranking of alternatives, so the number of criteria is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings. The mirror would cover the paper’s Condorcet winner outputs, not its SMART comparison or its conversion of ratings to rankings. Its main weakness is that criteria are not literally a population: treating criterion weight as mass is a meaningful extension of the paper’s weighted-input model, but it is less clearly the continuous-population model ChoCo studies. And because the paper supplies no named computational anchor, this candidate cannot serve as the required positive case tied to a result.

Criteria such as cost, compliance, and long-term effectiveness have distinct meanings; treating many criteria or subcriteria as interchangeable population units would need its own justification.
