The continuous mirror question

Given a finite resource catalogue \(R\), rationally weighted support \(T\) of valuation types, rational valuations \(v_t:2^R\to\mathbb{Q}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_r\), decide whether every feasible aggregate allocation with no welfare-increasing one-resource transfer between positive-mass cohorts maximizes total welfare.

The model it lives in

Model a nonatomic population by type masses \(\mu_t\) and aggregate allocations \(\pi_{t,S}\), where each agent holds an integral bundle \(S\subseteq R\) and only the mass assigned to bundles is fractional. Resource categories have scalable per-capita supplies \(\rho_r\); welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}\pi_{t,S}v_t(S)\), and a one-item deal transfers positive mass between cohorts holding bundles that differ on one resource.

The objection that survived

The finite-state termination argument and the paper’s reduction do not establish what fractional splitting does to suboptimal stable allocations; the proponent identifies this gap but does not resolve it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 4’s \(\textsc{1-Conv}\) complexity question; it leaves aside the modularity sufficiency and maximality results, the results without side payments, and the other convergence claims.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether a suboptimal one-deal-stable aggregate allocation exists for the stated valuation languages?
  • Can a reduction preserve a suboptimal stable allocation when each type’s mass may split across bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 4. It is a result proved in this paper, extending an earlier hardness result for straight-line-program valuations. In the paper’s problem \(\textsc{1-Conv}(\mathrm{REP})\), the input is a finite negotiation problem whose agents’ valuation functions are encoded in \(\mathrm{REP}\). The question is whether, from every initial allocation, every sequence of individually rational deals that move one resource at a time eventually reaches an allocation of maximum social welfare. Theorem 4 proves this problem NP-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions, even with two agents, provided \(\mathrm{REP}\) is polynomial-time computable, has the stated \(r\)-composition relationship to a language whose valuation-improvement problem is NP-hard, and can represent the zero valuation. The theorem’s reduction encodes the search for a better bundle into the convergence question.

Here is a continuous-population version I would put to a prover. Call it \(\textsc{Cont-1-Conv}(\mathrm{REP})\). The input is a finite resource catalogue \(R\); a listed support \(T\) of complete preference types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\); a \(\mathrm{REP}\)-encoded rational valuation \(v_t:2^R\to\mathbb{Q}\) for each type; and a rational per-capita supply \(\rho_r\in[0,1]\) for each resource category \(r\). A type is a full valuation function: agents of that type have identical preferences over every bundle. The supply \(\rho_r\) represents a growing stock of individually indivisible copies of category \(r\), scaling with population.

An aggregate allocation is a distribution \(\pi_{t,S}\) of type-\(t\) mass over integral bundles \(S\subseteq R\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq R}\pi_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni r}\pi_{t,S}=\rho_r \quad\text{for each }r\in R. \]
Its welfare per capita is \(W(\pi)=\sum_{t,S}\pi_{t,S}v_t(S)\). A continuous 1-deal selects positive mass \(\varepsilon\) of type-\(t\) agents holding \(S\) and type-\(u\) agents holding \(Q\), with \(r\in S\setminus Q\), and transfers one copy of \(r\) from the first group to the second. Each participating agent still holds an integral bundle; only the aggregate mass moved may be fractional. With side payments, this deal is individually rational when
\[ v_t(S\setminus\{r\})+v_u(Q\cup\{r\})>v_t(S)+v_u(Q). \]
The question is whether every feasible allocation with no such profitable deal has maximum welfare among all feasible aggregate allocations. A “yes” instance has no suboptimal 1-deal-stable allocation; a “no” instance has one. This terminal-state formulation is the direct continuous analogue of the paper’s convergence guarantee: in its finite model, strict welfare increases and finitely many allocations make every sequence terminate.

The regime could be a large network of repeated sites exchanging indivisible equipment, licenses, or capacity tokens. Sites share one of a modest number of valuation schedules over bundles, while both population and resource stocks grow; thus \(N\) can be very large relative to \(\tau=|T|\). This makes the type masses natural quantities, while each site’s bundle and each transferred item remain discrete. The underlying question should be recognizable to the authors: do myopic agents who make mutually beneficial, one-item deals necessarily reach maximum utilitarian welfare? The mirror keeps their local-deal rule, side payments, indivisible bundles, and welfare objective. It aggregates the population and adds per-capita resource supplies.

I would predict hardness transfers (Class B). Theorem 4’s reduction is driven by the combinatorics of improving a bundle under the agents’ valuations, and it already uses only two agents; the hard part is not a large headcount. But the paper does not establish this continuous hardness result. A proof must preserve a suboptimal stable allocation even when each type’s population can split across bundles, and that fractional mixing may eliminate a discrete deadlock. This is also the main follow-up question: does \(\textsc{Cont-1-Conv}(\mathrm{REP})\) remain NP-hard under these conditions, or can the aggregate formulation be decided efficiently, perhaps through its welfare optimization structure? A further useful result would be a two-way dictionary relating finite high-multiplicity instances to these aggregate states.

My weakest point is that the paper’s convergence theorem concerns named agents and a finite state space, whereas this mirror admits fractional aggregate allocations and infinitely many possible deal sizes. The stable-state question handles that difference explicitly, but it is a genuine modeling choice; hardness does not follow just from restating Theorem 4. A reduction must show that the extra aggregate states do not wash away the obstruction. The paper itself does not already study a continuous population: its allocation remains finite and discrete. High-multiplicity work would support the plausibility of this regime, while the proposed question remains about computational convergence in the population limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real computational anchor, but it does not by itself establish the proposed continuous result. Its finite model has finitely many allocations, so strict welfare increases guarantee termination. In the continuum, deal sizes can shrink indefinitely. Replacing that process question with “every stable aggregate allocation is optimal” gives a coherent problem, but it changes the object being studied. The reduction in Theorem 4 does not show that fractional type populations preserve its yes-instances: new aggregate allocations could create suboptimal stable states even when no such state exists in the finite instance.

That is the strongest objection to this particular mirror, but it does not defeat the better version. One could study the aggregate stable-state problem directly, or define convergence in terms of limit points and welfare. Both are recognizable computational questions about myopic, one-resource negotiation. The theorem’s two-agent hardness also means the computational difficulty is not inherently tied to a large headcount; that limits the case that continuization will make it easier, but a negative answer would still be useful to the programme.

The resource scaling is a genuine co-modification: the paper allocates a fixed set of unique items, whereas a growing population needs repeated copies of standardized resource categories. But that fits plausible settings such as many sites allocating interchangeable equipment under a small number of valuation schedules. Each agent can still receive an integral bundle, while only the population distribution is fractional. I do not see a sound basis for ruling out that regime.

So the negative case is weak. It can demand a careful definition and a new reduction; it cannot show that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 4 survives as an anchor: its continuous counterpart is not already proved by the paper, and the stable-state or limit-convergence question remains worth asking.
