The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) over finite \(T\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) for item classes \(j\in J\), and each type's personalized bivalued additive goods values \(v_{tj}\), compute finite-support masses \(x_{t,z}\ge 0\) on integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\) such that \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j=q_j\), and \(x_{t,z}>0\) implies \(\sum_jv_{tj}z_j\ge\beta_t^G(q)\), where \(\beta_t^G(q)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi(q)}\min_{z\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi)}\sum_jv_{tj}z_j\) and \(\Pi(q)\) is the set of finite-support distributions over integral bundles with expected bundle vector \(q\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of complete bivalued valuation types, with item-class supplies scaling per capita and every individual receiving an integral bundle; the decision variables are type-by-bundle masses, and each assigned bundle must meet its type's distributional MMS.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 for goods, with Theorem 2 yielding the parallel chores question; it does not mirror any other result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact benchmark and a finite-support allocation be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|J|\), and input bit length \(L\), without expanding to a common replication denominator?
  • Does the type-compressed problem admit polynomial-size exact witnesses, or does the compressed representation introduce continuum-specific hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered hardness result to anchor on. Its two numbered computational results are the goods and chores algorithms, and both are worth mirroring: they address different exact-fairness problems, with the inequality reversed for chores. My lead is Theorem 1 and goods.

A credible regime is a large municipality or university allocating goods from a repeated annual supply, or assigning recurring chores, to a very large cohort. People share a small set of complete valuation types: each type has its own pair of values and specifies which item classes receive the higher value. Items belong to classes with the same valuation pattern across all population types. The number of people is large, while the number of distinct agent types and item classes is comparatively small. For instance, \(\tau\) might be dozens or hundreds while the population is in the millions.

Here is the continuous model. Let \(T\) be the finite set of agent types, with population masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Let \(J\) be the finite set of item classes. A bundle is a vector \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{J}\): each person still receives an integral bundle of indivisible items. The per-capita supply of class \(j\) is \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\). For type \(t\), each item class has one of that type’s two values, so utility or chore cost is additive in \(z\). Fractional quantities occur in population masses, per-capita supplies, and type-level averages; they do not make an individual bundle divisible.

Define the continuous MMS benchmark from the same per-capita stock. Let \(\Pi(q)\) be the finite-support probability distributions over integral bundles whose expected bundle vector is \(q\). For goods, set
\[ \beta_t^{G}(q)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi(q)}\ \min_{z\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi)} u_t(z). \]
For chores, set
\[ \beta_t^{C}(q)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(q)}\ \max_{z\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi)} c_t(z). \]
This is the high-multiplicity analogue of partitioning the whole stock into one bundle per agent: an empirical distribution of bundles records such a partition, and its average bundle is the supply per person.

Goods-Cont-MMS, the mirror of Theorem 1, asks for a finite-support list of masses \(x_{t,z}\geq 0\) such that
\[ \sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j=q_j\quad(j\in J), \]
and every bundle assigned positive mass to type \(t\) satisfies \(u_t(z)\geq\beta_t^{G}(q)\). The requested solution is this mass assignment of whole bundles to each type. I expect this to be tractable, likely through a type-compressed configuration LP and pricing over bivalued bundles.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: every instance of indivisible goods with additive personalized bivalued valuations has an exact MMS allocation, and one can compute it in polynomial time. The value pair may differ for each agent; the result does not require a common pair or the earlier integer-ratio restriction. Goods-Cont-MMS asks for that same guarantee and construction when agents of identical type are represented by mass and supply is represented per capita.

Chores-Cont-MMS, the mirror of Theorem 2, has the same input and mass constraints, but each positive-mass assignment must satisfy \(c_t(z)\leq\beta_t^{C}(q)\). Its solution is again a type-level mass assignment over integral bundles. I also expect this problem to be tractable, though its capacity-style constraints and upper-bound benchmark deserve their own analysis.

Its anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: every instance of indivisible chores with additive personalized bivalued costs admits an exact MMS allocation, computable in polynomial time, with no common value pair required across agents. This is a genuine second anchor: chores reverse the benchmark and the allocation inequality, so the result is not merely a change of terminology.

The replication bridge makes these questions recognizably about the authors’ problems. For rational \(\mu\) and \(q\), scale by \(N\) so that \(N\mu_t\) agents of each type and \(Nq_j\) items of each class are integers. A finite partition into \(N\) bundles gives an empirical distribution in \(\Pi(q)\). Conversely, a rational finite-support witness for the continuous benchmark can be realized as a partition after choosing a suitable common denominator for \(N\). Thus the continuous benchmark agrees with the finite MMS on suitable replications. The paper’s theorems then guarantee exact allocations in those expanded instances. The mirror asks whether the result can be computed from the compressed population and supply description, rather than by expanding it.

That keeps population continuization at the center. Each unit of mass stands for agents indistinguishable in every relevant respect; each class supply scales with the population; and the fractional entries describe how many people of a type receive each *integral* bundle. The paper’s quotas, envelopes, and rounding techniques are relevant machinery, but this is not a claim that the paper already studies a continuum society. Nor is it continuity of outcomes, lotteries, time, or an axiomatic profile simplex. The scope is specifically the exact MMS results in Theorems 1 and 2 for personalized bivalued goods and chores.

My weakest point is the computational bridge. The paper’s polynomial running time is in the explicit finite instance size \(n,m\); the common denominator \(N\) needed to realize a rational continuous instance may be exponentially large in the compressed input length. Its \(n\times n\) construction therefore does not itself give a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\), the number \(k=|J|\) of item classes, and the binary encoding length \(L\). A polynomial-size continuous solution and efficient pricing for the type-compressed problem are open obligations of this mirror, not consequences of Theorems 1 and 2. That makes the tractability prediction a research question rather than an inherited theorem. It is still a plausible one: bivalued valuations constrain each type’s bundle value to two item categories, and the mirror preserves indivisible individual bundles while compressing repeated agents. The main further question is whether this structure supports exact type-compressed algorithms for both goods and chores, or whether either problem is hard for continuum-specific reasons.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that these theorems are about allocating a particular finite stock of indivisible items among named agents. To get a genuinely compressed population model, one must assume that both the stock and the number of agents scale together, that many agents have exactly the same valuation over every item class, and that those item classes capture all relevant differences. If preferences over labeled items are mostly unique, then the complete types are mostly unique too, and the proposed compression disappears. That makes this a narrow regime for a one-shot allocation problem.

That does not defeat Theorem 1’s mirror. The repeated-cohort scenario is coherent: a finite set of valuation types can share a growing supply of repeated goods, while people still receive integral bundles. The proposed distributional MMS benchmark can be related to finite replications. Scaling rational masses and supplies realizes the mass assignment as an allocation in a sufficiently large instance. But that expansion may be exponentially large in the compressed input’s bit length. The paper’s \(n \times n\) construction and polynomial running time in explicit \(n,m\) therefore do not establish a polynomial algorithm in the number of types, item classes, and encoding length. That is a serious limit on what the theorem gives the mirror, but it leaves a distinct compressed-input complexity question.

Theorem 2 has the same type-compression issue and the same gap between polynomial time on an expanded instance and polynomial time on a succinct one. Its minimax benchmark gives the chores version a coherent formulation, though: types receive integral chore bundles in population proportions, and supply is measured per person. The reversed inequality and capacity argument do not make this a mere restatement of the goods case, so I cannot dismiss it as a duplicate anchor.

Nor can I use high-multiplicity work as a collision: the programme explicitly treats it as support for the regime. In fact, related work on few agent and item types includes MMS among its fairness criteria, which strengthens the plausibility of the setting rather than settling this continuous computational question ([“Fair and efficient allocation with few agent types, few item types, or small value levels”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370222001606)).

So the best negative case is about reach, not validity: these are worthwhile mirrors only for repeated-stock, repeated-type settings, and the paper’s algorithms do not directly solve their succinct versions. I cannot honestly make that case defeat both anchors. The proposed cohort model survives, and the main computational obstacle is itself a legitimate question for the programme.
