The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\) on two sides, positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) each summing to \(1\), weak preference orders over opposite-side types, and one incompatible pair \((i_0,j_0)\), does there exist a rational mass-flow matrix \(x\) with \(x_{i_0j_0}=0\), row sums \(\mu_i\), and column sums \(\nu_j\), such that for every compatible pair \((i,j)\), either \(\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}=0\) or \(\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}=0\)? Determine the complexity in \(|I|, |J|\), and the binary bit length \(L\) of the masses.

The model it lives in

A two-sided nonatomic matching market in which each type groups agents with identical preferences and eligibility. Rational type masses on both sides are matched through a mass-flow matrix; a compatible type pair blocks when positive mass on each side is assigned to types it strictly likes less. One entire type pair is incompatible.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's perfect weakly stable matching question with one forbidden pair; it leaves Theorem 1 and the strong- and super-stability results, including the free-edge results, aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether a weakly stable full-mass flow exists, in \(|I|, |J|\), and \(L\)?
  • Can a reduction preserve hardness when each type's mass may split across partners?
  • When can rational stable flows be rounded into finite cloned markets?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is Perfect Weakly Stable Cohort Matching. It keeps the paper’s central question—whether a perfect weakly stable matching exists—while treating a large cohort, rather than each named person, as the unit described by a type.

A plausible setting is a large internship or residency clearinghouse. Each side contains many people sharing a complete preference profile over categories on the other side: applicants rank position types, and position types rank applicant types. A type includes the preferences and eligibility information used by the model. Each type has a rational mass, and many individuals may belong to each type, so the number of people \(N\) can greatly exceed the support size \(\tau\). One pair of categories may be incompatible because of a licensing or eligibility rule. This is a cohort-level version of the paper’s dense market with one missing edge.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that max-smti-dense is NP-complete: given a bipartite graph \(K_{n,n}\) missing exactly one edge and preference lists with ties, deciding whether there is a *perfect weakly stable matching* is NP-complete, even when every tie has length two, ties occur on only one side, and they appear at the beginning of those preference lists. The authors prove this result here by deleting the forbidden edge from instances of their Theorem 1.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover:

Perfect Weakly Stable Cohort Matching (lead). An instance consists of finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\) on the two sides, positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and weak preference orders over the opposite side’s types. Every type pair is compatible except one pair \((i_0,j_0)\). Determine whether there is a nonnegative rational mass-flow matrix \(x=(x_{ij})\), with \(x_{i_0j_0}=0\), such that
\[ > \sum_{j\in J}x_{ij}=\mu_i\quad(i\in I),\qquad > \sum_{i\in I}x_{ij}=\nu_j\quad(j\in J), > \]
and the resulting matching is weakly stable.

For each compatible pair \((i,j)\), let

\[ > A_{ij}(x)=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'},\qquad > B_{ji}(x)=\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}. > \]

These are the masses on each side assigned to types they strictly like less than the other type in the pair. The flow is weakly stable exactly when, for every compatible \((i,j)\), at least one of \(A_{ij}(x)\) and \(B_{ji}(x)\) is zero. If both are positive, some positive mass on each side could be paired and would strictly prefer that deviation. The output is a full-mass flow matrix; this is an existence problem, so there is no separate objective.

I would preserve the theorem’s distinctive tie pattern at the type level: pair ties on only one side, at the top of its preference lists. The question’s input parameters are the support sizes \(|I|,|J|\) and the binary bit length \(L\) of the masses, as well as the preference-list description.

I expect this problem to be Class C, but that is a research bet, not a consequence of Theorem 2. The theorem’s NP-hardness does not directly transfer: fractional flows can split a type’s mass among several partners, so a discrete matching reduction may lose its Boolean choices. On the other hand, the continuum stability conditions still impose a choice for each type pair about which side has zero mass assigned below it. Those complementarity choices could retain computational hardness even after individual mass is divisible. A proof of that would need a reduction for this cohort-flow problem itself.

The weakest point is the co-modification of the missing edge. Theorem 2 removes one edge between two individuals; this mirror makes an entire pair of cohorts incompatible. That fits markets where eligibility depends on cohort attributes, and the resulting problem remains recognizably stable matching with ties and a restricted edge. But it is not the literal high-multiplicity relaxation of every instance of Theorem 2. The mirror covers Theorem 2 only, not the paper’s other stability definitions or its free-edge results. Its further questions include whether this cohort problem is polynomial-time solvable, whether it is NP-hard for reasons specific to the flow stability conditions, and when rational stable flows can be rounded into finite cloned markets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational result, so the “nothing here to mirror” objection is unavailable. Nor does stable matching inherently track named individuals: with homogeneous preferences and eligibility, a mass-flow matrix retains the information needed to ask whether a matching is stable.

The strongest objection is the theorem’s single missing edge. If it means one particular pair of people, preserving that restriction in a large population makes it negligible in the continuum. The paper notes that a complete bipartite instance always has a complete weakly stable matching. Splitting those two people into separate types to retain the restriction does not preserve the original problem’s one-to-one choices: each positive-mass type can now split its mass among several partners. The reduction’s discrete perfect-matching choices may disappear.

The proponent’s stronger version avoids that problem by making an entire pair of cohorts incompatible. That is coherent: cohort-wide licensing or eligibility rules are plausible, and stability can be stated precisely for the resulting mass flows. But it changes the exceptional person-pair restriction at the heart of Theorem 2 into a block-level market rule. That may be a worthwhile problem in its own right; the theorem’s hardness result alone does not establish that it is a compelling mirror.

This is a weak negative case. The cohort version survives, and neither its fit with the paper’s subject nor its computational interest can honestly be ruled out from the text. The most I can say against this anchor is that the theorem’s distinctive hard feature does not carry over cleanly: preserve it as an individual exception and it fades with the population; scale it up to preserve its effect and the mirror asks a materially different question.
