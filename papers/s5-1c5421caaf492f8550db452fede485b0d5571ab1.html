What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 5's equilibrium-existence result for \(2\)-binary games on grids; it leaves the other existence results and the price-of-anarchy and price-of-stability bounds without a mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that every \(2\)-binary \(\lambda\)-type tolerance Schelling game on a grid has an equilibrium, assuming the game is balanced and has at least two agents of each type. Here “\(2\)-binary” means \(t_0=t_1=1\) and \(t_d=0\) for \(d\ge2\). The proof gives an explicit construction in Algorithms 1 and 2, so the theorem supports a polynomial-time equilibrium search on that restricted class. The paper does not give a named NP-hardness or standard complexity-classification result; this constructive theorem is its best computational anchor.

I would mirror it with the Balanced Atomless Tolerance-Grid Equilibrium problem. A type is one of the \(\lambda\) ordered household categories; members of a type have the same tolerance toward every other type. The population has mass \(1\), with mass \(1/\lambda\) of each type. The instance includes an \(m\times M\) grid and a rational capacity \(\kappa_v\) for each grid cell, with \(\sum_v\kappa_v>1\). These are neighborhood-scale cells whose housing capacity grows with the finite population used to approximate them. A feasible allocation is a rational matrix \(x_{\ell v}\ge0\), where \(\sum_v x_{\ell v}=1/\lambda\) and \(\sum_\ell x_{\ell v}\le\kappa_v\).

Residents choose a cell with spare capacity to maximize their utility. For type \(\ell\) at cell \(v\), define \(U_\ell(v;x)\) as the tolerance-weighted mass of types in cells adjacent to \(v\), divided by the total adjacent mass; define it as \(0\) if that mass is zero. A solution is an allocation \(x\) such that every type with positive mass at \(v\) gets utility at least \(U_\ell(w;x)\) for every cell \(w\) with spare capacity. In other words, no infinitesimal resident can improve by jumping. The computational question is whether every valid instance has such a rational equilibrium and whether one can be found in polynomial time. Residents seek individual utility; aggregate social welfare can be measured at the resulting equilibrium, but is not the objective of this search problem.

The motivating regime is a large housing market with millions of households and a small fixed number of ordered household categories—for example, the life-stage types discussed in the paper. In a finite approximation with \(n\) households, there are \(n/\lambda\) households of each type and each cell’s capacity is proportional to \(n\). Thus the neighborhood contains many households while the number of types stays small. Continuity represents shares of these types across neighborhoods. It preserves the paper’s tolerance utilities, grid adjacency, relocation choice, and equilibrium question, while scaling housing supply with population. I think the authors could recognize this as a natural neighborhood-scale version of their model.

My expectation is Class A for this restricted tolerance rule and grid topology: the constructive tiling in Theorem 5 is a credible starting point for a mass-allocation algorithm. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of the theorem. It also does not make a hardness-dissolution claim: this paper supplies no comparable hardness anchor, and its finite-grid result is already constructive. The natural follow-up questions are whether the guarantee extends to larger tolerance radii or unequal type masses, and whether welfare bounds survive in the atomless model.

The weakest point is the change in scale. The paper’s grid vertices are single-occupancy locations, so a resident can materially change a neighbor’s local composition by moving. In my mirror, cells represent districts with population-scaled capacity, and one infinitesimal resident has no such effect. Keeping vertices as individual homes would preserve that finite-player externality, but would make the continuum-population regime much less convincing on a bounded-degree grid. The positive case rests on the authors accepting neighborhood-sized cells and scaled capacity as a sensible population-continuous version of their housing model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a real computational anchor: it gives a constructive equilibrium for balanced \(2\)-binary games on grids. The strongest objection is about what survives when the population becomes continuous. In the paper, each grid vertex holds one agent, and an agent’s utility depends on a bounded set of neighboring vertices. Increasing the number of residents while preserving that interaction structure also increases the number of vertices. The location-by-type assignment then grows with the population; type proportions alone do not describe the local arrangements that determine utility.

The proposed district model avoids that problem by giving each cell population-scaled capacity. But this changes the interaction structure: a resident now responds to the aggregate composition of neighboring cells, effectively interacting with many residents where the paper’s agent sees at most a few neighbors. A move by an infinitesimal resident leaves those aggregates unchanged. The result is a coherent nonatomic location game, but its equilibrium is a Wardrop-style condition over aggregate neighborhood masses. Theorem 5’s tiling argument, which arranges individual agents around empty vertices, does not carry over as a population limit.

One could preserve local structure by refining the grid as the population grows and tracking the neighborhood configurations. That makes the state grow with the number of locations; compressing it back to a finite mass description would discard the correlations the utilities need. Or one could let each neighborhood grow with the population, as in the district model. Either choice may support a research question, but neither gives a clear bridge from this theorem to the proposed continuous problem.

That is the best case against the anchor: there is no obvious scaling that keeps both the paper’s sparse local interactions and a fixed-dimensional mass model. I would not use “the result is already high-multiplicity” or “the answer may be unchanged” as objections; neither applies. Nor can I honestly claim the negative case is airtight. The district model is plausible for housing, and the authors might recognize it as a natural coarse-scale Schelling game. The objection is that it looks like a new mean-field model rather than a continuization of Theorem 5. If that is enough to exclude it from ChoCo’s scope, the case against is strong; if authors accept the change in interaction scale, this anchor survives.
