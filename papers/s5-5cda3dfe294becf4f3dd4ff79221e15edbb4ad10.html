The continuous mirror question

Given chore classes \(E\), rational per-capita supplies \(s_e\), and an adversarial measurable arrival stream \(\sigma:[0,1]\to\{0,1\}^{E}\) of binary-additive cost types, with \(\mu_t=\lambda(\{u:\sigma(u)=t\})\), what is the least worst-case \(\alpha\) achievable by a deterministic causal policy? The policy sees each type only as its mass arrives and assigns each mass point \(u\) an integral bundle \(B(u)\), depending only on the stream prefix through \(u\); it must satisfy \(\int_0^1 a_e(B(u))\,du=s_e\) for every \(e\) and \(c_{\sigma(u)}(B(u))\le\alpha\,\mathrm{MMS}^{\infty}_{\sigma(u)}(s)\) almost everywhere, where \(\mathrm{MMS}^{\infty}_t(s)=\min_{\pi:\,\mathbb{E}_{B\sim\pi}[a_e(B)]=s_e\ \forall e}\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\pi}c_t(B)\), and \(\pi\) ranges over distributions on integral bundles.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic online population arrives as a measurable stream of complete binary-additive types with induced type masses \(\mu\). A causal policy assigns integral chore bundles to the arriving mass, matching rational per-capita supplies in aggregate while bounding almost every unit's cost against its continuous MMS benchmark.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.4's adversary tracks the exact residual set of whole chores, while a continuous policy can split homogeneous cohort mass and use fractional per-capita supplies, so the type and item lower bounds need a new proof.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the optimal deterministic competitive factor as a function of chore classes \(m\), realized type support \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), and do the type- and item-dependent lower bounds survive when cohorts can be split across integral bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

A convincing regime is a large seasonal workforce assigning task packets—municipal maintenance crews or disaster-response volunteers, for example. Each worker’s type is their complete cost profile over the task catalog; workers of a type share that profile, and each type may account for a substantial cohort. The task supply scales with workforce size, so it has a meaningful per-worker level. Each unit of worker mass still receives an integral bundle of chores; only the aggregate allocation across a cohort is fractional.

Lead: Continuous Universal MMS Partition. This is the strongest mirror because the paper states and proves a lower bound for this precise subproblem. Let \(E\) be a finite set of chore classes, with per-capita supply \(s_e\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\), and let \(T\) be a known set of binary additive cost types. A bundle \(B\) is an integral feasible bundle, and \(a_e(B)\) is its use of chore \(e\). A continuous partition is a distribution \(\pi\) over integral bundles such that \(\sum_B\pi_B=1\) and \(\sum_B\pi_B a_e(B)=s_e\) for every \(e\). Its mass \(\pi_B\) is the fraction of the workforce receiving \(B\). Define \(\mathrm{MMS}^{\infty}_t(s)\) as the least possible maximum \(c_t(B)\) among such distributions for type \(t\). The problem is to find a feasible \(\pi\) minimizing
\[ \max_{B:\pi_B>0}\ \max_{t\in T}\frac{c_t(B)}{\mathrm{MMS}^{\infty}_t(s)}. \]
Thus every bundle used in the precomputed partition must be fair for every known type.

The anchor is Theorem 4.8, proved in this paper. For every integer \(q\ge2\), it constructs an instance with \(n=(2q)^{2q}\) agents, \(m=qn\) chores, and \(k=(2q)^{q+3}\) binary additive types. Each type approves \(n\) chores and has MMS \(1\); every \(n\)-partition has some bundle costing at least \(q\) to some type. Therefore every universal partition has approximation ratio at least \(q=\Omega(\log k/\log\log k)\).

The same argument gives the continuous lower bound. Give each of the \(m\) chores per-capita supply \(1/n\), so total supply is \(q\). Each type has approved supply \(1\), and its continuous MMS is \(1\). Any distribution meeting the supplies has average bundle size \(q\), so some bundle in its support contains at least \(q\) chores. The covering property in the theorem’s construction gives a type that approves those \(q\) chores. This is a Class B mirror: the approximation obstruction is already present in the discrete construction and survives the mass representation. A natural next question is whether the \(\Omega(\log k/\log\log k)\) lower bound can be matched, or whether the paper’s \(O(\log k)\) upper bound is closer to optimal.

This is genuinely a population mirror: one can take all \(k\) types to have positive mass, for example \(\mu_t=1/k\), while the construction has \(n/k=(2q)^{q-3}\) agents per type for \(q\ge4\). The fractional object is the distribution of agents across bundles; bundles themselves remain integral. The authors should recognize it because a universal partition is an object they explicitly define and compute.

A broader, more ambitious mirror is the online problem behind Theorem 3.4. In that model, the input is per-capita chore supply \(s\), with binary additive types revealed as the workforce arrives. The final society is a distribution \(\mu\) over a support of \(\tau\) complete types, but the policy does not know future arrivals or their masses. At each time, it observes an arriving infinitesimal mass of type \(t\) and assigns that mass to integral bundles, irrevocably. By the end it must exhaust the supplies, and almost every arriving unit of type \(t\) must receive a bundle costing at most \(\alpha\,\mathrm{MMS}^{\infty}_t(s)\). The question is to determine the best worst-case competitive factor for deterministic causal policies, in terms of the chore catalog size \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and rational input length \(L\).

Theorem 3.4, also proved here, states that no deterministic online algorithm achieves \(o(\min\{n,k\log k,\log m\})\)-competitiveness, even for binary additive costs. Its proof establishes three bounds: no algorithm is \((n-\epsilon)\)-competitive for any constant \(\epsilon>0\); none is \((\tfrac14\log m)\)-competitive; and none is \(O(k\log k)\)-competitive. The construction uses nested approval sets, with each arriving type approving the currently unallocated chores. For the continuous question, I expect the type- and item-dependent obstruction to transfer—roughly, a barrier involving \(\tau\log\tau\) and \(\log m\)—but the finite-\(n\) term has no direct counterpart after normalizing population mass to one. The paper’s type-dependent construction groups agents of each type consecutively, so for fixed groups and growing \(n\), each type can have positive mass; this makes the mirror plausible beyond singleton-agent effects. It would be a competitive-ratio result, not by itself a claim of NP-hardness.

The main soft spot is the distinction between these two questions. Theorem 4.8 concerns universal partitions: every preassigned bundle must protect against every known type, including types that might never arrive. The paper explicitly notes that its lower bound does not lower-bound general online algorithms. A causal policy can use the revealed type, and the population version can split a type cohort across integral bundles. That may let it beat the universal-partition bound. So the strongest case is for the continuous universal-partition problem; the direct online mirror is a plausible extension whose lower bound needs its own proof. The paper supports a real, precisely stated continuous mirror, while leaving open how much of its online landscape survives that move.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.8 does have a real continuous extension as a statement about universal partitions. With supply \(1/n\) for each chore, total supply is \(q\); every type has continuous MMS \(1\), and the covering property forces some supported bundle to cost at least \(q\) for some type. That argument survives fractional bundle masses.

My objection is that this extends a universal *allocation template*, not the society. The partition variable \(\pi\) records how workforce mass is spread over bundles, but the type masses \(\mu_t\) never enter the input or objective: changing them while keeping the same types changes nothing. The model requires every bundle in the template to work for every possible type, including types with arbitrarily small mass or none at all. It is therefore a fractional relaxation of the paper’s auxiliary universal-partition problem. A population-level allocation would instead track a joint flow \(y_{t,B}\), assigning bundles to each type’s mass; fairness would apply to bundles actually given to that type. Theorem 4.8’s covering argument does not establish a lower bound for that model.

Theorem 3.4 offers a stronger route because it concerns online allocation itself, but the proposed fluid version changes what an arrival means. In the paper, each arrival triggers one irrevocable bundle decision, and the adversary’s next type is defined from the exact set of whole chores left. In a population flow, a cohort can be split across bundles and chore supplies can be depleted fractionally. To retain exact arrival positions, those positions must be part of an agent’s complete type, making repeated types disappear; to aggregate agents with the same cost profile, one must treat them as a cohort or batch and give the policy a different decision process. The theorem’s lower bound therefore does not establish the claimed continuous competitive barrier.

That is the best negative case I can make: Theorem 4.8’s extension is real but sits on the allocation side of the model, while a faithful continuous online population model needs a separate arrival-path formulation and new analysis. The case remains weak against the universal claim. A finite cohort-arrival model with rational masses is plausible and recognizable, and its online competitive question may be worthwhile even if the paper’s bounds do not transfer. I cannot honestly rule out that mirror; the negative case does not defeat the online anchor.
