Why no mirror

Theorems 4 and 5 have computationally relevant inference guarantees, so the case does not fail merely because the paper lacks a polynomial-time theorem. However, the proposed \(\mu\) is a distribution over complete encounter instances rather than mass over agent types affecting incentives or feasibility. An anonymously matched continuum of workers could be worthwhile, but it would be a new many-player mechanism-design model rather than a continuous analogue of the named results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest qualification is important: this paper has no named computational-complexity anchor. None of Theorems 1–5 proves membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or gives an LP/MILP/SAT formulation. Thus it cannot support a full ChoCo case under the strict anchor rule. The strongest positive case is nevertheless a natural population mirror of its player-inference results.

My lead anchor would be Theorem 4, proved in this paper, with the proof using Samet’s earlier result. It states that for a random variable \(t\) taking values in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), there is an output-agreement mechanism for eliciting the mean such that every sequence of best-response strategies beginning with a \(\Pi_i\)-specific strategy converges to a \(\Pi\)-specific, common-knowledge equilibrium.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large human-computation platform. Millions of worker-pair/task encounters are processed, but only finitely many complete interaction types occur: worker skill classes, task classes, information partitions, queries, report spaces, metrics, and payment rules. A type contains all of those features, so agents of the same type are genuinely interchangeable. If there are \(N\) encounters and only \(\tau\) recurring types, with \(N\gg\tau\), the type masses \(\mu_q\) are the natural population description.

The epistemic distribution in the paper and the population distribution are distinct. For each encounter type \(q\), \(P_q\) is the prior over states of one task. Separately, \(\mu_q\) is the fraction of the entire worker population assigned to encounter type \(q\). The proposed continuization is therefore of the population, not of the outcome space or of the uncertainty model.

I would define the following problem.

\(\mathrm{Continuous\ Mean\ Player\ Inference}\) takes a finite type set \(Q\), rational masses \(\mu_q\), finite state spaces \(\Omega_q\), rational priors \(P_q\), two signal partitions \(\Pi_{q,1},\Pi_{q,2}\), and value maps \(v_q:\Omega_q\to\mathbb{Q}^{d_q}\). The query is the posterior mean \(T_q(\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[v_q]\), and the mechanism pays according to squared Euclidean disagreement.

For each type, initialize the workers with truthful strategies \(s^{(0)}_{q,i}(\sigma)=\mathbb{E}[v_q\mid\Pi_{q,i}=\sigma]\), then repeatedly apply best responses. Under the squared-distance mechanism, a best response is the conditional expectation of the other worker’s report. Let \(\widehat{s}_q(\omega)=\mathbb{E}[v_q\mid\Pi_q(\omega)]\), where \(\Pi_q=\Pi_{q,1}\wedge\Pi_{q,2}\) is the common-knowledge partition. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), the task is to find the smallest number \(R\) of inference rounds for which the resulting strategies satisfy

\[ \sum_{q\in Q}\mu_q\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_q}P_q(\omega)\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left\|s^{(R)}_{q,i}(\Pi_{q,i}(\omega))-\widehat{s}_q(\omega)\right\|_2^2\le\varepsilon. \]

A solution consists of \(R\) and the corresponding strategy tables. This is recognizably the paper’s problem: same queries, same information structures, same output-agreement incentives, and exactly the same inference process. The new object is the aggregate population loss over many repeated interaction types.

The expected classification is Class A, although this is a conjectural complexity claim rather than a theorem of the paper. For fixed \(R\), every update is finite-dimensional conditional-expectation linear algebra, and the computation depends on \(\tau\), the state and partition descriptions, and the bit length of \(\mu\), not on the number of cloned workers. Theorem 4 implies convergence for every positive-mass type, hence convergence of the aggregate loss. The open computational questions are whether \(R\) can be bounded polynomially in the input size and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), and whether exact convergence or least-round computation has a clean complexity classification.

My secondary anchor would be Theorem 5, also proved in this paper. It states that when \(|\Omega|\ge3\), no output-agreement mechanism for eliciting the median or mode of a random variable in an arbitrary metric space can guarantee, for every information structure, that best-response inference beginning with either player’s \(\Pi_i\)-specific strategy converges to a \(\Pi\)-specific equilibrium.

The corresponding population problem is \(\mathrm{Median/Mode\ Mass\ Focality}\). The instance is a finite distribution \(\mu\) over repeated two-worker interaction types, each with its own finite state space, prior, partitions, metric report space, and median or mode query. For a chosen output-agreement mechanism, define the bad mass as the total \(\mu\)-mass of types for which some best-response sequence from a truthful initialization fails to converge to the common-knowledge-specific equilibrium. The decision version asks whether the bad mass is zero; the optimization version chooses an admissible mechanism minimizing bad mass.

Theorem 5 gives a sharp boundary: a single bad interaction type with mass \(1\) already defeats a universal guarantee, and replicating that type across a large population preserves the failure. This is not continuum-specific hardness; it is an impossibility inherited from the underlying two-player game. But it is a useful negative companion to the mean case: continuization creates a population-level objective while retaining the paper’s distinction between queries for which inference converges and queries for which it can cascade into an arbitrarily bad equilibrium.

The mirror covers only Theorems 4 and 5, with Theorem 1 supporting the construction: for every query and every strictly decreasing output-agreement reward, the paper proves the existence of a strict common-knowledge-specific equilibrium. I would not use Theorem 1 as a computational anchor because it is an existence theorem, not an algorithmic or complexity result.

The weakest point is that the paper studies a fixed two-player Bayesian game, whereas this mirror is a mass distribution over many independent copies of that game. A sceptical referee could call it a batch-processing extension rather than a genuine continuum-agent problem. The objection is not fatal—the worker-pair interpretation is natural, and \(\mu\) is genuinely population mass rather than the paper’s epistemic prior—but it means the mirror is weaker than a continuum voting or allocation problem. More seriously, because the paper supplies no named complexity result, this is a credible continuous research question rather than a compliant ChoCo complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts even earlier than the modelling details: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is an equilibrium-existence theorem; Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are impossibility and characterization results; Theorem 4 proves convergence; and Theorem 5 proves a non-convergence impossibility. None formulates a computational problem with an input encoding, resource bound, or complexity classification. The proposed \(R\)-round optimization problem is a new problem invented by the proponent, not a high-multiplicity version of a computational problem in the paper.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 4 also fails the population test. The proposed \(q\) is not an agent type. It contains an entire encounter: both players, the state space, prior, partitions, query, report space, metric, and payment rule. Thus \(\mu_q\) is a distribution over independent game instances, not a society whose mass affects feasibility, incentives, or an outcome.

For every \(q\), the best-response sequence is determined entirely by \(P_q\), \(\Pi_{q,1}\), \(\Pi_{q,2}\), and the mechanism. It is independent of \(\mu\). Replacing one encounter by a million identical encounters changes only the external average loss; it changes no strategy, equilibrium, or inference round. The claimed “continuum” is therefore just a weighted batch of separately solved two-player games. There is no high-multiplicity relaxation: the paper already fixes \(n=2\), and the number of cloned encounters never appears in the game.

This remains true under the best version of the proposed question. One may ask for the least \(R\) such that

\[ \sum_q \mu_q \ell_q(R)\le \varepsilon, \]

but this merely aggregates independently computed convergence curves. It is an ensemble or empirical-risk problem, not a continuous-agent problem. Taking an infinite type space and replacing the sum by an integral would add measure theory, not population structure. Theorem 4 supplies pointwise convergence for each component, but no population-level computational phenomenon.

The most credible rescue would be to place many workers in one anonymous random-matching market, so that \(\mu\) determines the distribution of opponents and therefore enters best responses. That would be a potentially interesting mean-field extension, but it is no longer the game analysed in the paper. Theorem 4’s conditional-expectation argument is for a fixed pair with two partitions; under population matching one must specify matching, cross-type report spaces, common priors, payoffs, and the resulting aggregate best-response operator. The common-knowledge partition in the theorem does not provide that model. This would be a new many-player mechanism-design problem, not a continuization of the theorem’s computational content.

Theorem 5 fares no better. The proposed bad mass is simply

\[ B(\mu)=\sum_q \mu_q\,\mathbf{1}\{\text{type }q\text{ admits a failing inference sequence}\}. \]

Theorem 5 already supplies the relevant counterexample at one two-player instance. Giving that instance mass \(1\), or cloning it arbitrarily many times, preserves exactly the same failure. Conversely, distributing mass over many instances only averages independent yes/no facts. The decision question \(B(\mu)=0\) is not a continuum-specific hardness question; for a finite explicit support it is just a collection of checks of the underlying paper’s condition.

The proposed optimization over mechanisms is also not defined at the computational level. The paper allows arbitrary report spaces, metrics, and strictly decreasing reward functions. Without a finite mechanism representation, “choose a mechanism minimizing bad mass” is an ill-specified design problem. Restricting mechanisms to a finite encoded family could make it computationally meaningful, but then that restriction—and the associated problem—would be new rather than supplied by Theorem 5.

Theorem 1 cannot rescue the case. Its strict common-knowledge equilibrium is established separately for each two-player game. Taking a distribution over such games merely integrates a pointwise existence statement. If instead one makes the population interact through a single aggregate mechanism, the theorem no longer applies without a new proof.

The honest residual possibility is a genuinely many-worker, anonymously matched output-agreement model. That might be worthwhile in mechanism design or mean-field game theory. But it would not be a continuous-population mirror of this paper’s named results, and it would not inherit their central theorems. Under ChoCo’s gate, the proposed construction is a distribution over independent Bayesian games, not a continuous society. Hence this paper supplies no worthwhile continuous mirror in the programme’s sense.
