What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named theorems establish probabilistic properties of majority dynamics; none classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard, or gives an algorithm for a computational task. The paper is closest to the programme’s analytic neighbours, which the brief explicitly says are not a substitute for computational results.

The strongest positive foothold is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that for a sequence of \((\varepsilon,W)\)-regular unimodular random networks evolving by majority dynamics, a random root almost surely eventually satisfies \(X_{t+2}(I)=X_t(I)\), and the expected number of times this fails is at most \(2W/\varepsilon\). A plausible population regime would be a large sparse society with repeated local network environments: a type records an agent’s rooted weighted network environment and opinion, mass is the population law over those types, and the action is the majority update from neighboring opinions. That is a natural population-level description, especially when many agents share relatively few environments.

But this gives an analytic convergence question, not a computational one. The theorem already studies a probability law over rooted networks, and it has no optimization objective or algorithmic problem to mirror. Turning it into a complexity question would require adding a new problem, rather than continuizing a named computational result of the paper.

Theorem 2, also proved here, is the closest result about a finite population: for \(G\sim G(n,p)\), with \(n\ge n_0\), \(p\ge c n^{-1/2}\), and independent uniform initial opinions, all vertices hold the initial majority opinion at time \(4\) with probability at least \(0.4\). A continuum graph model is conceivable, but the finite sampling fluctuations behind that probability are not preserved by a straightforward continuum population limit. This is therefore a weak foothold, not a sound anchor.

So the honest positive case is limited: the paper’s network-law model makes a population-level analogue feel natural, but the paper contains no named computational result on which to base the required continuous computational problem. I can’t supply a compliant problem per anchor without inventing one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the central obstacle: neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 4 proves an almost-sure convergence property and an expectation bound for a random root, using a potential argument and mass transport. Theorem 2 gives a probability guarantee for a random graph and random initial opinions. Neither states the complexity of a decision or optimization problem, or gives an algorithm for one. Under ChoCo’s remit, that leaves no named computational result to mirror.

Theorem 4 is the stronger-looking foothold because its hypothesis already involves a law over networks. But that is an analytic probability model, not a computational population problem. Recasting rooted network environments as types does not supply an algorithmic input and output. A more ambitious mirror might give a finite-support type law, an interaction kernel, and ask for the limiting fraction of agents that enter a two-cycle. That could be a sensible new question, but the kernel and the computational task would have to be added; they are not what Theorem 4 studies. The theorem’s convergence claim is not a complexity question waiting to be continuized.

Theorem 2 has a different problem. Its conclusion concerns finite-size fluctuations: the sign of the initial population mean determines which unanimous opinion appears, even though that mean is typically of order \(n^{-1/2}\). In a deterministic continuum with equal initial masses, the mean is exactly zero, so the stated target opinion disappears. One could preserve the fluctuation with a stochastic or finite-size correction, or specify a richer continuum graph model. Those may yield worthwhile probability or analysis questions, but they change the object of study; they still do not turn this theorem into a computational result. Asking for an algorithm to compute an exact unanimity probability would be a new task, not a mirror of the theorem’s probability bound.

So the negative case is strong under the programme’s stated criterion: neither proposed anchor supports a continuous *computational* mirror. It is weaker as a claim that no useful computational question could ever be built from this paper. Majority dynamics could motivate such questions, but the paper does not formulate them, and inventing them would answer a different question.
