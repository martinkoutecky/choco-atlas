Why no mirror

The proposed high-multiplicity allocation question is coherent, but it cannot supply the missing computational anchor.

What the mirror covers

The proposed model translates Theorem 1's \(d\)-multi-allocation value guarantee into type masses and per-capita item supplies; it does not provide a computational mirror of Corollary 6's asymptotic ρ_{\rm SA} bound.

Open questions for a prover

  • For succinctly represented valuations, what is the complexity of finding a feasible \(y_{t,B}\) that meets the guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest candidate is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For \(d\ge2\) a power of \(2\), it says that given a \(d\)-multi-allocation \(A\) and normalized, monotone subadditive valuations \(v_i\), there is a disjoint allocation with \(A'_i\subseteq A_i\) such that
\[ v_i(A'_i)\ge \frac{v_i(A_i)}{d}-\frac{d-1}{d}\delta_i, \qquad \delta_i=\max_{e\in A_i}v_i(\{e\}). \]
That is a natural allocation question, but it is an existence and value guarantee, not a result that places a computational problem in P, proves it hard, or gives another complexity classification. Corollary 6 is also an existence guarantee. So the paper has no anchor that meets the stated computational-result requirement; I can’t honestly give a Class A/B/C verdict for one.

There is a plausible *existential* population mirror of Theorem 1. Let \(t\) range over \(\tau\) complete agent types, with mass \(\mu_t\), valuation \(v_t\), and initial bundle \(A_t\). Let \(E\) be a finite set of exchangeable item categories, with per-capita supply \(q_e\). Require the initial claims to satisfy \(\sum_{t:e\in A_t}\mu_t\le d q_e\). A solution assigns mass \(y_{t,B}\) of each type to each integral bundle \(B\subseteq A_t\), with
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq A_t}y_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B:e\in B}y_{t,B}\le q_e, \]
and \(y_{t,B}>0\) only when
\[ v_t(B)\ge \frac{v_t(A_t)}{d}-\frac{d-1}{d}\delta_t. \]
Here mass means a fraction of agents, while each agent still receives an integral bundle. The intended regime is a large cohort with \(N\) agents and proportionally scaled supplies, but relatively few standardized agent and item types. This mirrors Theorem 1’s guarantee without making the outcome itself fractional.

The weakness is decisive for the programme’s purpose: clearing the rational denominators reduces this to a finite allocation instance, and Theorem 1 supplies existence after that scaling, but the paper gives no running-time bound for finding the allocation. The fixed-threshold question is therefore a plausible continuous analogue of the theorem’s *statement*, not an anchored computational-complexity question. My positive case stops there.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject Theorem 1 as a computational anchor. It proves that a qualifying allocation exists; it does not state a decision problem, give an algorithm, or classify the complexity of finding one. The same is true of the paper’s other main results: they are existence and approximation guarantees. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, that leaves no named computational result to mirror.

The proposed mass model is coherent. A large cohort of agents with the same valuation and initial bundle is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the variables \(y_{t,B}\) preserve integral bundles for each agent. The model’s search problem would be: given type valuations, initial bundles, and rational supplies, find a feasible assignment of mass to bundles meeting the threshold. But that is a new algorithmic problem added to the paper’s theorem. Clearing denominators shows why the existential guarantee carries over; it does not supply the missing complexity result.

The stronger version—ask for an efficient algorithm to construct such a \(y\)—still depends heavily on choices absent from the paper. With valuations given by full tables, the bundle choices can be enumerated and the feasibility question written as an LP, with size measured against an already exponential valuation input. With oracle or succinct valuations, finding useful bundles can dominate the computation. That could lead to worthwhile research, but its computational content would come from the chosen valuation representation and pricing task, not from a computational question this paper poses.

So the negative case is narrow: I cannot rule out a valuable high-multiplicity allocation project, and I would not argue that indivisible goods or type-level mass make the mirror nonsensical. The point is that this paper offers a strong existence theorem, not a computational result; turning it into a search problem would be a legitimate extension, but not a computational mirror of one of its results in ChoCo’s sense.
