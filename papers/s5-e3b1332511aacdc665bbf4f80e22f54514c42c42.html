The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 2, proved here, characterizes when the reward function induces Utilitarianism: Pareto (SWF), IIA, CUC-invariance, and Functional Anonymity hold exactly when the reward ranks alternatives as the sum of individual utilities does. Theorem 4, also proved here, says that under its stated assumptions, a policy is optimal exactly when it maximizes long-run discounted quasi-utilitarian reward. Theorem 3 is a cited Bellman-equation result, and Theorem 1 is a cited social-choice representation theorem. None asserts a complexity result, algorithm, or computational formulation that the paper uses to compute a solution. So there is no honest anchor here from which to state one continuous problem per anchor.

The strongest plausible positive direction is a population version of the Social Choice MDP: many people share utility types \(\theta\) over the finite alternatives \(X\); \(\mu_\theta\) is each type’s population fraction; actions are alternatives; and decisions induce transitions between distributions over types. Aggregate utility would be \(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta u_\theta(a)\), with a policy maximizing discounted long-run reward. A large public-service population with relatively few utility types is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and Theorems 2 and 4 provide a conceptual foothold for the reward and policy definitions.

But that is a proposed extension, not a computational question this paper studies. Its state is a finite set of profiles assigning utilities to named group members; it does not establish that aggregate type distributions form a sufficient Markov state. The weakest point is therefore decisive for this task: the population mirror may be sensible, but the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 is an axiomatic characterization of the reward functions that induce Utilitarianism. Theorem 4 characterizes optimal policies given a Bellman value function and those rewards. Theorem 3 is a standard MDP result, and Theorem 1 is a cited representation theorem. None asks for an algorithm, proves a complexity bound, or specifies a computational problem. The paper also gives no encoding for its real-valued utilities or transition probabilities from which a complexity question could be recovered.

The proponent’s population MDP is a plausible research direction, but it does not supply the missing anchor. Let \(\mu\) describe fractions of people with each utility type, and let actions change that distribution. To ask for an algorithm, one must specify how transitions are represented, how they depend on \(\mu\), and what form a policy takes. The paper does none of this: its transition kernel acts on whole profiles, and its reward axioms impose no structure on that kernel. A distribution over utility types alone need not be a sufficient Markov state. Adding exchangeability and a type-level transition model could make it one, but those choices would define a new computational problem; Theorems 2 and 4 would provide its welfare vocabulary, not its computational content.

A large public-service population with repeated utility types is credible, so I would not reject the idea on the ground that people must remain individually named. But a continuous restatement of Theorem 2 would be axiomatic, and a distributional version of Theorem 4 would still need a new transition model and computational formulation. The paper offers no result whose algorithmic complexity that formulation would mirror. The honest limit of this negative case is that such a model might be worthwhile as a separate project. What the paper cannot support is calling it a continuous computational mirror of its results.
