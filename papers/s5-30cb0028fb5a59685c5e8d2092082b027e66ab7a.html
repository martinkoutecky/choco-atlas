The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), integer voting weights \(q_t\), fixed types \(F\) with rankings \(\rho_t\), manipulable types \(M\), and a target \(p\), decide whether there are masses \(x_{t,r} \ge 0\) for reports \(r\) with \(\sum_r x_{t,r}=\mu_t\) for every \(t \in M\), such that \(p\) is a veto winner. For each \(c \in C\), its veto score is \(\sum_{t \in F} \mu_t q_t \mathbf{1}[c \ne \operatorname{last}(\rho_t)] + \sum_{t \in M} q_t \sum_r x_{t,r}\mathbf{1}[c \ne \operatorname{last}(r)]\); require this score to be at least every other candidate's.

The model it lives in

A veto election over a finite support of complete types, each carrying rational population mass and an integer voting weight. Fixed types retain their rankings; manipulable cohorts choose a distribution of reports through \(x_{t,r}\). The decision is whether those reports make the target a veto winner.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's constructive veto manipulation result for a society represented by type masses. It leaves the paper's atomic weighted instances and its Borda, STV, and other protocol results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a polynomial-time algorithm, with running time in \(m\), support size, and input bit length, for deciding continuous constructive veto manipulation.
  • Characterize how fractional reporting changes the discrete veto manipulation hardness, including when a fractional solution can be rounded to weighted votes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is weighted coalitional manipulation with fractional cohorts. It keeps the paper’s central question intact: can a coalition choose its reported rankings so that a specified candidate wins, or a hated candidate loses? It changes the population representation and lets a cohort of identical manipulators divide across reports.

Take a finite candidate set \(C\) and a finite support of types \(t\), each with a ranking \(\rho_t\), an integer voting weight \(q_t\), a rational population mass \(\mu_t\), and a flag saying whether the type is fixed or part of the manipulating coalition. Normalize so that \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For every manipulable type and report \(r\), choose \(x_{t,r}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_r x_{t,r}=\mu_t\). Fixed types cast \(\rho_t\); \(x_{t,r}\) is the mass of type \(t\) reporting \(r\). Mass counts agents, while \(q_t\) determines their voting power. The question is whether some such allocation achieves the target. A discrete profile embeds by taking \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); a successful discrete manipulation therefore gives a continuous one.

This fits a plausible regime in the paper’s own setting of software agents and weighted voters: a very large electronic consultation over a few proposals, where many agents share the same preferences and voting weight. The relevant \(\tau\) is the number of distinct preference, weight, and coalition-status types, not the number of agents. A type can split across reports because it represents a large cohort of independently voting agents. The paper’s stated interpretations of weights—as voting power or the size of a represented community—make this a recognizable version of its manipulation problem.

My lead is Continuous Borda Constructive Manipulation. The anchor is Theorem 1, which summarizes a result cited from Conitzer and Sandholm [4], rather than proved in this paper: constructive weighted coalitional manipulation under Borda is NP-complete for every number of candidates \(m\ge 3\). Given the fixed population, the manipulable mass, and target \(p\), the problem asks whether there is a report allocation \(x\) for which \(p\)’s Borda score is at least every other candidate’s. The score of \(c\) is \(\sum_{t\text{ fixed}}\mu_t q_t s(\rho_t,c)+\sum_{t\text{ manipulable}}q_t\sum_r x_{t,r}s(r,c)\). A solution is the allocation \(x\) satisfying those score inequalities.

I expect this continuous problem to be tractable, in Class A. Once reports are divisible, the manipulators’ types contribute only their total voting weight \(W=\sum_{t\text{ manipulable}}q_t\mu_t\); any cohort can use the same distribution over rankings. This leaves a linear feasibility problem over the convex hull of ranking score vectors. Although there are \(m!\) rankings, the pricing problem for a scoring rule can be solved by assigning score positions to candidates in sorted order, so column generation or separation avoids listing them all. The useful follow-up is to characterize how far a discrete weighted instance can be from this fractional answer: when can a continuous allocation be rounded, and what additive error does rounding incur?

A second, closely related anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: for veto, constructive weighted coalitional manipulation is NP-complete with three or more candidates. The paper proves this using a PARTITION reduction. The continuous question, Continuous Veto Constructive Manipulation, has the same input and solution form as the Borda problem, but asks whether the target can be made a veto winner. It also looks like Class A: veto is a scoring rule, so the same convex feasibility and pricing approach applies. The theorem’s reduction makes the fractional change particularly clear: its hardness depends on splitting indivisible weighted votes between candidates; a continuous cohort can split its mass. A useful next question is whether a rounding bound can be stated in terms of the largest cohort’s voting weight and the target’s score margin.

A more distinct, narrower mirror is Continuous Three-Candidate STV Destructive Manipulation. Its anchor is Theorem 7, proved here: destructive weighted coalitional manipulation under STV with three candidates is NP-complete. Given fixed and manipulable type masses and a hated candidate \(h\), ask whether some allocation of manipulator mass across rankings makes \(h\) lose under STV; the allocation \(x\) is the solution witness. I expect this version to be tractable. With three candidates there are only six rankings and a constant number of possible elimination sequences. For each sequence, the first-round elimination conditions and final-round transferred-vote conditions are linear inequalities in \(x\); feasibility is a union of a constant number of linear systems. A natural further question is whether tractability persists when \(m\) is part of the input, or whether the number of possible elimination orders becomes the new source of difficulty.

These mirrors cover only three manipulation results: the Borda constructive result in Theorem 1, the veto constructive result in Theorem 2, and the three-candidate STV destructive result in Theorem 7. They do not claim to mirror the paper’s full table.

The weakest point is the change from indivisible voters to divisible cohorts. The paper’s PARTITION reductions use lists of weights that can be distinct, so their hard instances need not have \(N\gg\tau\). If the intended agents are a small number of named blocs and every bloc must cast one indivisible weighted vote, splitting a bloc across reports would change the problem. The mirror is strongest for the large, repeated-type regime where mass over reports is a credible description of many independently acting voters. That limitation is also what makes the complexity question worthwhile: the discrete hardness may come from population-level indivisibility, and the continuous problem tests whether that hardness survives when the population is genuinely represented by type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that the paper’s central comparison concerns the indivisibility of weighted votes: how many candidates make a coalition’s choice of reports computationally hard? Its PARTITION reductions encode numbers as separate manipulator weights. In those instances, making each \((\text{preference},\text{weight},\text{coalition status})\) combination a type may leave essentially one voter per type. The continuous formulation then says little about those hard instances.

That objection does not defeat Theorem 1’s Borda constructive-manipulation result. A better mirror uses a genuinely repeated population: many equal-weight manipulators with the same complete type, whose members can report different rankings. Their mass can be distributed among reports, giving a well-posed high-multiplicity question. The reduction’s reliance on distinct weights limits what this mirror says about the paper’s hardness result; it does not make the mirror incoherent or uninteresting.

The same point applies to Theorem 2’s veto result. Its reduction also uses separate weighted votes, so the paper’s hard instances need not describe a repeated-type population. But veto manipulation has a natural mass version for large cohorts of equal-weight agents, and allowing a cohort to split across reports is consistent with the paper’s coalition model. The case against is narrower here: Borda and veto would likely be two instances of the same continuous scoring-rule question, rather than two independent contributions.

Theorem 7’s three-candidate STV result is also a valid anchor. With three candidates, there are finitely many reports and elimination cases; a mass version can express each case with linear constraints. Tie handling can be given a natural mass formulation. The fixed candidate count makes this a small mirror, but the discrete PARTITION hardness still gives the question a meaningful motivation: does indivisibility account for the difficulty in this repeated-type regime?

So the most defensible negative case is about scope and research priority. These mirrors study coordinated manipulation by a divisible, repeated coalition; they do not explain the paper’s candidate threshold for atomic weighted manipulators. And the three proposed anchors overlap substantially: Borda and veto are scoring-rule cases, while the STV mirror fixes the agenda at three candidates. That makes the package narrower than its three results suggest.

I cannot honestly turn that into a case that *no scenario* yields a worthwhile mirror. The paper names several computational results, manipulation is a population-level question, and the high-multiplicity version is sensible under the programme’s criteria. The objections limit the mirrors’ reach and novelty; they do not defeat the anchors.
