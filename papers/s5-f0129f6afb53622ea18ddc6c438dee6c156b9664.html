The continuous mirror question

Given chore classes \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and a value oracle for each type's cost function \(c_t\) on integral count bundles \(a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\), where \(c_t(0)=0\) and each class has marginal cost in \(\{0,1\}\), compute a rational finite-support assignment \(x_{t,a,b}\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|G|\), and input bit length \(L\). Require \(b\in\{0,1\}\), \(\sum_{a,b}x_{t,a,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,a,b}a_gx_{t,a,b}=q_g\), and \(\sum_{t,a,b}b x_{t,a,b}\le 1\). For every two positive-mass outcomes \((t,a,b)\) and \((s,a',b')\), require \(c_t(a)-b\le c_t(a')-b'\); also require \(c_t(a)\le c_t(a')\), or some \(g\) with \(a_g>0\) and \(c_t(a-e_g)\le c_t(a')\), for EF1 before subsidies.

The model it lives in

A continuum of workers with type masses \(\mu_t\), each type carrying a complete cost function over integral chore-class count bundles. The decision variable \(x_{t,a,b}\) assigns mass to each type, bundle, and unit subsidy; rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\) fix aggregate chore use, and envy-freeness and EF1 are checked across every outcome in the assignment's support.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5's envy-freeness, EF1, unit-subsidy, and computation guarantees. It leaves Proposition 1's Pareto-optimality incompatibility and the appendix's incremental-extension obstruction outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a feasible assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|G|\), and \(L\), rather than in the denominator-expanded population?
  • Is there a polynomial bound on the support size of a feasible assignment?
  • What approximation or rounding guarantees connect a computed mass assignment to finite populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that for every finite instance with negative dichotomous valuations, there is a complete chore allocation and a subsidy vector with each subsidy in \(\{0,1\}\), total subsidy at most \(n-1\), exact envy-freeness after subsidies, and EF1 before subsidies; the allocation and subsidies can be computed in polynomial time with value oracles. The paper contains no named NP-hardness result to anchor a hardness mirror. Proposition 1 gives a real incompatibility between Pareto optimality and unit subsidies, but it is not a computational-complexity result.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large workforce sharing a small number of complete burden profiles: for example, workers assigned recurring on-call, maintenance, teaching, or refereeing duties. A type records the worker’s entire cost function over bundles, so workers of one type are indistinguishable in every respect the model uses. The number of workers \(N\) can be much larger than the number of types \(\tau\). To keep chore allocation meaningful as the workforce grows, each chore class has a fixed per-capita supply. This co-scaling fits the paper’s roster examples; the chores themselves remain indivisible.

Here is the lead problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Unit-Subsidy EF1 Chore Assignment. The input is a finite set \(G\) of chore classes, positive type masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\geq 0\), and one cost oracle per type \(t\). A worker’s bundle is an integral count vector \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}^{G}\); each type’s cost function \(c_t\) satisfies \(c_t(0)=0\) and \(c_t(a+e_g)-c_t(a)\in\{0,1\}\). The task is to output a finite-support rational mass assignment \(x_{t,a,b}\), where \(b\in\{0,1\}\) is the individual subsidy, satisfying
\[ \sum_{a,b}x_{t,a,b}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,a,b}a_gx_{t,a,b}=q_g,\qquad \sum_{t,a,b}b\,x_{t,a,b}\leq 1. \]
Every worker thus receives an integral bundle and at most one unit of subsidy; only the mass of workers assigned each option is fractional.

The assignment must also satisfy the paper’s fairness conditions across its entire support. For every type \(t\), if \(x_{t,a,b}>0\) and some \(x_{s,a',b'}>0\), require \(c_t(a)-b\leq c_t(a')-b'\). For EF1 before subsidies, require for each such pair either \(c_t(a)\leq c_t(a')\), or some \(g\) with \(a_g>0\) satisfies \(c_t(a-e_g)\leq c_t(a')\). The search task is to find such an \(x\), not to optimize welfare or subsidy expenditure. The finite bound \(n-1\) becomes a per-capita bound of \(1\) in the continuum; I am not claiming that the one-agent slack survives normalization.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the theorem’s underlying problem: envy-freeness, the unit subsidy cap, and EF1 are all retained, while aggregate mass records how many otherwise identical workers receive each integral bundle. Clearing denominators gives a useful bridge. With a common denominator \(D\) for the masses and supplies, create \(D\) workers, \(D\mu_t\) of each type, and \(Dq_g\) chores of each class. Theorem 5 applies to that finite instance; aggregating its allocation yields a feasible mass assignment. So existence follows, but this route takes time polynomial in \(D\), which can be exponential in the input bit length \(L\).

I would expect the direct continuous search problem to be Class A, with an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|G|\), and \(L\), plus value-oracle calls. The denominator-clearing argument establishes feasibility and gives a pseudo-polynomial route; it does not provide the desired compressed algorithm. EF and EF1 depend on which outcomes have positive mass, so a plain LP over masses is not automatic. A direct result would need to control the support and fairness constraints without expanding the population. Useful follow-up questions are whether a polynomial support bound exists, and what approximation or rounding guarantees connect a computed mass assignment to finite populations.

The fragile part is the repeated-task scenario. The paper allows chores with arbitrary identities and agents with different valuations; this mirror assumes task classes can be replicated with per-capita supplies and that a type’s cost depends on the resulting count bundle. That is a narrower, but credible, regime for recurring duties—not a claim that every chore-allocation instance has a useful continuum. This mirror covers Theorem 5’s EF, EF1, subsidy, and computation guarantee. It leaves Proposition 1’s Pareto-optimality incompatibility outside scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 5’s population axis does not scale by itself. If the chore set stays fixed while the number of workers grows, chore supply per worker tends to zero and almost everyone gets nothing. Keeping supply meaningful requires replicating chores too. The proposed count-vector model does that, but it narrows the paper’s domain: the paper allows costs to depend on the identities and combinations of arbitrary chores, while the mirror treats chores in each class as interchangeable. Recurring duties make that a plausible regime, yet it is a new restriction on the problem, not a faithful high-multiplicity version of every instance the theorem covers.

The proponent’s construction is internally sound. For rational masses and per-capita supplies, clear denominators with a common denominator \(D\), apply Theorem 5 to the resulting finite instance, and aggregate workers with the same type, bundle, and subsidy. This proves feasibility, with average subsidy at most \(1-1/D\). But it takes time polynomial in the expanded population, which can be exponential in the binary input length \(L\). The only new question is whether one can construct a compressed, support-wide fair assignment in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|G|\), and \(L\). That is a genuine algorithmic question. It is also less directly served by the paper’s technique than the proposed “Class A” label suggests: EF and EF1 depend on which outcomes have positive mass, and arbitrary value oracles provide no evident compact LP or pricing problem.

Proposition 1 adds little to the case for this particular mirror. Its witness has two agents and three chores. Scaling it to a continuum would require choosing a per-capita chore supply and a notion of Pareto improvement for the resulting population; the paper’s finite counterexample does not settle that new question.

So I would be cautious about making this a lead ChoCo target: the natural continuous model is narrower than the paper, and its existence guarantee follows by denominator clearing. But the compressed-algorithm question could still be worthwhile, since denominator clearing gives no polynomial-in-\(L\) method. The honest case against is weak; I cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
