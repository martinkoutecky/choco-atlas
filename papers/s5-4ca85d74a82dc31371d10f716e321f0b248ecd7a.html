The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its numbered results are mathematical or probabilistic. For example, Theorem 1, proved here, says that under assumptions A1–A3, each relevant party’s aggregate seat share is \(q_i=\hat p_i+\hat p_i\hat n/(2m)-1/(2m)\). Theorem 2, also proved here, bounds how far the expected rounding residual \(\mathbb{E}(\{PU\})\) can be from \(1/2\). Neither classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard, nor gives a computational formulation that the paper evaluates.

The paper does include Algorithm 1, “Jump-and-step,” for finding a Jefferson–D’Hondt divisor. It is attributed to Pukelsheim, and this paper gives no complexity result for it. It therefore does not supply the required numbered computational anchor.

A plausible population model would treat a type as a district and party choice, with mass representing the share of voters of that type, and ask for the resulting district seat allocations. But the paper’s theorems do not establish a computational result about that problem. Its vote-share vectors and probability distributions are not, by themselves, a continuous population of voter types. Treating them as such would supply a new mirror without the required anchor, so I would not present it as this paper’s positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 gives a seat-share identity, \(q_i=\hat p_i+\hat p_i\hat n/(2m)-1/(2m)\), under assumptions about rounding residuals and district averages. Theorem 2 bounds an expected residual. Both are mathematical or probabilistic results: neither formulates a computational problem or establishes its complexity. Algorithm 1 does find a divisor, but it is presented as prior work, and this paper makes no complexity claim about it. None of these supplies a computational anchor.

A high-multiplicity setting is easy to imagine: group voters by district and party choice, with mass representing their share of the electorate. That may be a sensible description of voters, but it does not turn this paper’s results into computational results. The paper already works with aggregate vote shares and studies seat allocation and bias analytically. Asking for an algorithm to evaluate those quantities would add a computational question; asking for manipulation, robustness, or optimization over distributions would add a new problem. Those could be worthwhile research, but they are not mirrors of results established here.

The proposed type model therefore does not rescue an anchor: it makes the population interpretation plausible, while leaving the absence of a computational target unchanged. Treating districts themselves as the mass would shift the continuous object away from the population; making seats divisible would instead continuize outcomes. The negative case is strong against a ChoCo mirror *of this paper*. Its limit is that this cannot show no related computational question could ever be worthwhile—only that such a question would need a new target rather than a result in this paper to mirror.
