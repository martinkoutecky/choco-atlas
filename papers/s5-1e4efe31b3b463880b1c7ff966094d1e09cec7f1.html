The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is an author-recognizable extension: a large housing reallocation market with many households but relatively few complete preference types over ordered housing classes. Let \(\mu_t\) be the population share of type \(t\), and let \(q_o\) be the per-capita supply of class \(o\). A type-level allocation records the mass \(x_{to}\) assigned from each preference type to each class, with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(q_o\). Each household still receives one indivisible unit; the averages are fractional because they aggregate many households. This makes the population continuous and gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

The paper’s closest anchor is Theorem 3, proved here: for a fixed finite society and preference profile, choosing an endowment profile uniformly at random and applying the crawler yields the same probability distribution over allocations as choosing an agent order uniformly at random and applying the corresponding sequential priority rule. Theorem 1, also proved here, says the ascending and descending crawlers are the same rule. Theorem 2, proved here, says the crawler’s allocation is invariant to the choice of any object order with respect to which the profile is single-peaked. These are structural equivalences, not results asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The theorem establishing efficiency, individual rationality, and strategy-proofness is unnumbered and attributed to Bade (2019).

So there is no eligible named computational anchor in this paper, and no continuous problem can honestly be offered *per anchor* under the requested standard. Theorem 3 is the nearest lead, but it proves equality of two finite lottery rules; it gives no complexity result or running-time bound.

An unanchored follow-on question would be to compute the limiting type-to-class assignment flow induced by random priority as the numbers of households and housing units grow proportionally. That could be tractable through a finite fluid allocation process, but establishing the limit and its exact algorithm would be new work, not a continuation of a computational result in this paper. The weakest point in the positive case is therefore decisive: the proposed large-market setting is plausible, but the paper does not supply the required computational anchor, and grouping units into classes also extends its model of distinct, indivisible objects.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper states no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–3 prove identities between rules. The crawler is an algorithm, but the paper does not analyze the complexity of a computational problem or give an optimization problem whose complexity could change when the population becomes continuous. That leaves no paper result for a complexity landscape to classify.

Theorem 1’s best mirror would compare left-to-right and right-to-left crawler processes in a large housing market. But the rule operates by selecting individual agents and shifting ownership one position at a time. With a continuum of agents, those steps need a new mass-based process; comparing its two sweep directions would establish a mechanism property, not solve a computational problem from this paper. Theorem 2 has the same limitation: lifting order invariance from finite profiles to distributions over preference types either just aggregates the existing identity or requires a new model of the object space. Neither creates a complexity question.

Theorem 3 is the credible opening. A large market with finitely many preference types and housing classes could support a question about the limiting distribution of type-to-class assignments under random priority and random endowments. But the theorem already equates those lotteries for every finite market. Any aggregate distribution or convergent limit inherits that equality by projection; the theorem itself supplies no new computational task. To calculate a limiting assignment flow, one must instead define a continuum priority process—the finite random order has no first agent in an atomless population—and study that new process. The normalized allocation may even concentrate on a deterministic flow, leaving exact lottery behavior to a separate study of fluctuations. That could be worthwhile research, but it is a large-market random-assignment project, not a computational mirror of a result this paper establishes.

The negative case is strongest against Theorems 1 and 2, and strong against calling the Theorem 3 follow-on a mirror. Its weak point is the housing-market scenario: it is plausible, and a researcher could find the limiting assignment-flow problem valuable in its own right. So the honest conclusion is narrower than the requested universal claim: this paper offers no worthwhile *paper-anchored computational* mirror, but the material cannot rule out a worthwhile adjacent project.
