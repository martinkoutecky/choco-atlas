The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a real high-multiplicity reading of the paper’s market, but there is a binding limitation: the paper contains no numbered computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorem 1, proved here, says that with public buyer and seller volumes, the proposed mechanism is strategy-proof with respect to reservation prices, weakly budget-balanced, and individually rational. Theorem 2, also proved here, bounds the strong inefficiency ratio by \(O(1/k+1/\ell)\) when \(k\) buyers and \(\ell\) sellers trade, under the paper’s stated distributional and volume assumptions. Theorem 3 proves that expected inefficiency tends to zero as the market grows if the numbers of trading buyers and sellers both tend to infinity. These are mechanism and asymptotic-efficiency results, not claims that a problem is in P or hard.

The paper’s one explicit NP-completeness statement is in prose, attributed to Sandholm and Suri (2001): clearing a market with discriminatory bids is NP-complete. It is cited prior work, not a numbered result proved here, and this paper’s mechanism rules out discriminatory bidding. It cannot serve as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest mirror candidate is Mass-MDA Efficiency, anchored only in the broader, non-complexity sense to Theorems 2 and 3. Consider a standardized commodity market with many buyers and sellers who share a small number of reservation-price and public-volume profiles. A type is a side of the market, a reservation price, and a public per-agent volume \(q_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of that side’s population. The regime has \(N\gg\tau\), with \(\tau\) distinct profiles. For rational finite-support masses and a scale \(N\), ask for the type-level trade quantities, pairwise matches, prices, and market-maker surplus produced by the paper’s clearing and prorating rules. A solution is that allocation and payment table, together with its realized surplus \(W\) and the maximum feasible gains from trade \(W^*\). The main question is whether the mechanism’s strong inefficiency ratio \(1-W/W^*\) vanishes as the masses scale up, and what conditions and rate guarantee it. Aggregate type allocations may be fractional even though each agent receives integral units.

This is plausible for repeated procurement or wholesale markets with many participants sharing contract or capacity classes: the population is naturally described by type masses, and the paper already analyzes what happens as the market grows. Computing the finite-support clearing outcome looks tractable by sorting the types and applying the paper’s cutoff rules; a general maximum-surplus benchmark is an LP. But the theorem’s efficiency guarantee does not automatically transfer to fixed finite-support masses: its continuous reservation-price density assumptions matter, and price atoms may change cutoff behavior. The likely computational direction is Class A for clearing; whether the fixed-type mass model retains the asymptotic bound is an open part of this proposed mirror, not a result of the paper.

The mirror’s weakest point is that the paper studies a finite random market and proves asymptotic claims about it; it does not formulate a continuum-agent computation. Theorems 2 and 3 support a population-level question, but not the required computational anchor. Strategy-proofness is an even less secure extension: with infinitesimal agents, a unilateral report may have no effect on the aggregate market, making continuum incentive compatibility vacuous. So the honest positive case covers the paper’s efficiency analysis in Section 4, not its full mechanism-design contribution, and it should be graded as a promising mirror candidate rather than an anchor-compliant case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent has already conceded: none of this paper’s named results is a computational complexity result. Theorem 1 establishes incentive and participation properties; Theorems 2 and 3 establish asymptotic efficiency bounds. The paper’s NP-completeness claim concerns discriminatory-bid market clearing, is attributed to prior work, and applies to a market design this paper excludes.

That defeats the proposed Mass-MDA Efficiency anchor as stated. Its central question—whether the inefficiency ratio vanishes as type masses grow—is a convergence question. Replacing sampled agents with masses may give it a sensible continuous-population model, but does not turn it into a question about complexity or algorithms. The continuous reservation-price densities in Theorem 2 also do not directly describe the proposed finite-support society. Studying that variant could be useful economics, but it is a new asymptotic analysis, not a computational mirror of either theorem.

There is a credible high-multiplicity market here: many buyers and sellers could share a small number of reservation-price and per-agent-volume profiles. I would not reject it on realism grounds. But the computational task the proponent extracts from it is market clearing and surplus calculation. The paper already specifies the clearing rules, gives an LP for maximum gains from trade, and describes sorting reported prices to find the cutoff. A continuous version could ask for an algorithm on rational type masses, but that would be a separate implementation question; it is not what Theorems 2 or 3 establish.

Theorem 1 does not supply a stronger anchor. With a nonatomic population, one individual has zero mass, so their unilateral report cannot change the aggregate market. A positive-mass deviation or coalition model could avoid that degeneracy, but it changes the strategic question and still would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

So I would reject this as a case for the programme’s computational-continuization agenda: the positive case’s actual anchor is analytic, and its algorithmic add-on is not a result the paper establishes. The negative case is weaker if read as the literal universal claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The finite-profile market is plausible, and one could formulate algorithmic questions about it; the more defensible conclusion is that this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor for them.
