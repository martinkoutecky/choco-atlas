The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a case that meets the requested anchor rule. The paper has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification—for example, that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or W[1]-hard.

Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 give bounds on incentive compatibility under strong monotonicity and for Approval Voting. Proposition 2 gives lower bounds for PageRank. Theorem 2 constructs a non-imposing system with bounded manipulation, and Theorem 3 axiomatizes three-agent systems. These are substantial results, but they characterize ranking systems and their incentive properties; they do not classify the complexity of computing something.

One could define a continuous-population version of the incentive measure—for example, let types be outgoing-link sets, let \(\mu_t\) be the population fraction of each type, and ask for the maximum change in a type’s expected rank when some of its mass changes reports. But that would be a new optimization question, not a mirror anchored in a qualifying result of this paper. It also risks replacing the paper’s axiomatic questions with a different computational problem.

So the strongest honest positive case is limited: the paper’s measures could inspire a continuous-population research question, but this paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it, and its stated contribution is principally axiomatic.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is decisive under ChoCo’s gate: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Propositions 1–2 bound incentive measures; Theorem 2 constructs a ranking system with specified incentive properties; Theorem 3 gives an axiomatization for three agents. These are substantive results, but none gives a complexity classification or an algorithm for a computational problem. Even if computing \(\delta_F^G(v)\)\) is a natural optimization question, the paper bounds that quantity rather than studying its computational complexity. A continuous optimization inspired by those bounds would be a new question, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

The proposed type space of outgoing-link sets does not repair that gap. In the paper, an outgoing set names particular vertices, and those vertices are also the alternatives being ranked. A distribution over link sets alone does not say which named vertex has which set; that assignment can change the graph and its ranking. Preserving the identities avoids that loss, but then the model has not really replaced the finite society with an anonymous population of repeated types.

A stronger formulation could use a finite set of network roles, assign mass to each role, and specify links between roles with a kernel. That is coherent, but it changes the incentive question. In an atomless population, one agent’s link change has zero effect on the aggregate network and typically on population ranks. Allowing a positive mass of a type to change links can avoid that degeneration, but it studies coordinated, mass-based manipulation rather than the paper’s unilateral deviations. That could be a worthwhile project; the paper supplies no computational result anchoring it.

So the negative case is strong for the screening decision: this paper offers no qualifying ChoCo mirror. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous research could ever grow from it is weaker. A role-based network model might be interesting on its own, but presenting it as a continuous mirror of these results would overstate what the paper establishes.
