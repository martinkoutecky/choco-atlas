The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), type disutilities \(v_{t,j}<0\), budgets \(b_t<0\), and per-capita chore supplies \(s_j>0\), enumerate all competitive type-utility profiles \(u_t=v_t\cdot z_t\), together with one representative allocation \(z_t\) and price vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}^m\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t z_{t,j}=s_j\) and each type's budget-constrained utility maximization.

The model it lives in

Types \(t\in T\) carry masses \(\mu_t\); decision variables are per-capita allocations \(z_{t,j}\) and prices \(p_j\). Supplies satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_tz_{t,j}=s_j\), each type maximizes \(v_t\cdot x\) subject to \(p\cdot x\le b_t\), and the task is to enumerate all resulting \(u_t=v_t\cdot z_t\) and recover representative \(z,p\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 17's bounded-\(\tau\) or bounded-\(m\) enumeration of all competitive type-utility profiles and recovery of representative allocations and prices. It leaves Theorem 34's indivisible-chore fairness guarantees and the paper's other structural results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one compute a single competitive type equilibrium in time polynomial in \(\tau+m\) when both \(\tau\) and \(m\) vary?
  • Can the continuous type-level equilibria yield additive approximation or preprocessing guarantees for finite repeated populations and indivisible chores?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a Class A mirror of Theorem 17, which is proved in this paper. The paper does not itself prove a named NP-hardness result, so there is no hardness anchor whose difficulty might dissolve; the positive case must rest on its algorithmic theorem.

The anchor is Theorem 17: for a chore-division instance with \(n\) agents, \(m\) divisible chores, strictly negative additive-value matrix \(\vec v\), and strictly negative budget vector \(\vec b\), if either \(n\) or \(m\) is fixed, then one can compute, in strongly polynomial time, the complete set of competitive utility profiles and a competitive allocation-price pair for every profile. The running time is \(O(m^{n(n-1)/2+3})\) for fixed \(n\), or \(O(n^{m(m-1)/2+3})\) for fixed \(m\). This is proved here, not merely cited.

My continuous problem is High-Multiplicity Competitive Chore Equilibrium. There are \(m\) recurring chore classes and a finite set \(T\) of agent types, with \(|T|=\tau\). Type \(t\) has additive disutilities \(v_{t,j}<0\), budget \(b_t<0\), and mass \(\mu_t\ge 0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type is complete: agents of type \(t\) have identical disutilities and budgets. Chore class \(j\) has per-capita supply \(s_j>0\). Thus a finite population of size \(N\) contains \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Ns_j\) units of chore \(j\). The natural regime is \(N\gg \tau,m\), with \(\tau\) fixed or small.

The decision variables are a price vector \(p\in\mathbb R_{<0}^m\) and a type-average allocation \(z_t\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^m\). A solution must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t z_{t,j}=s_j\) for every chore class \(j\), and, for every positive-mass type \(t\), \(z_t\) must maximize \(v_t\cdot x\) over all \(x\ge0\) with \(p\cdot x\le b_t\). Its type utility is \(u_t=v_t\cdot z_t\). The problem asks for all competitive type-utility profiles \(u=(u_t)_{t\in T}\), together with one representative allocation and price vector for every profile.

This is a population continuization, not merely a fractional-outcome reformulation. The source problem already permits divisible chores; what changes is that the agent population becomes a distribution over finitely many complete types. The per-capita supply \(s_j\) is essential: without scaling recurring chores with population size, almost all agents would receive empty bundles and the market would degenerate.

The scenario is a large organization assigning recurring maintenance, care, or administrative duties. Workers share a small number of role, skill, and contract types; their disutilities and workload entitlements are type-level parameters. A national or organization-wide workforce can have millions of agents but only a modest number of such types. This is precisely the high-multiplicity regime, not an artificial claim that a seven-person committee is continuous.

The connection to Theorem 17 is exact. Let \(y_{t,j}=\mu_tz_{t,j}\), \(\widehat v_{t,j}=s_jv_{t,j}\), and \(\widehat b_t=\mu_tb_t\). After normalizing each chore supply to one, the continuous problem becomes the paper’s finite chore-division problem with \(\tau\) representative agents, values \(\widehat v\), and budgets \(\widehat b\). Aggregate utilities are \(\widehat u_t=\mu_tu_t\), and prices transform by \(\widehat p_j=s_jp_j\). Therefore Theorem 17 gives an exact algorithm for the continuous problem in \(O(m^{\tau(\tau-1)/2+3})\) operations when the number of types is fixed, and in \(O(\tau^{m(m-1)/2+3})\) operations when the number of chore classes is fixed.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is also two-way. For rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), clearing denominators gives \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\); rational supplies similarly give recurring chore copies. Conversely, any such replicated finite market compresses to the distribution \(\mu\). Hence this is a genuine high-multiplicity relaxation with an exact dictionary, not just a metaphor.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable, Class A. The paper’s consumption-graph enumeration, explicit utility formulas, and maximum-flow test all survive after replacing individual agents by representative types and scaling their budgets by mass. The result covers Theorem 17 itself, including both complete equilibrium-profile enumeration and recovery of representative allocations and prices. I would not stretch the case to the paper’s Theorem 34, because its indivisible-chore EF\(_1\)/Prop\(1\) guarantees would require a more delicate support-wise population formulation.

The main weakness is that the reduction makes the algorithm look almost immediate once one accepts representative types: it is essentially Theorem 17 applied to a weighted type market. That is a limitation of the novelty claim, not of the mirror’s legitimacy. Mass affects total budgets, clearing, and the equilibrium set, while the underlying competitive-chore question remains exactly recognizable to the authors. The mirror also preserves the boundary of the paper: when both \(\tau\) and \(m\) vary, listing every equilibrium may be output-exponential, and the existence of a polynomial-time algorithm for finding just one equilibrium remains an open further question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that it is almost a formal reparameterization of the paper’s own model. If \(z_t\) is a per-capita allocation, the correct normalization is \(x_{t,j}=\mu_tz_{t,j}/s_j\), with \(\widehat v_{t,j}=s_jv_{t,j}\), \(\widehat b_t=\mu_tb_t\), and \(\widehat p_j=s_jp_j\). Then the continuous equilibrium conditions become exactly the paper’s finite-agent conditions on \(\tau\) representative agents. Masses are absorbed into budgets, supplies into values and prices, and the consumption graphs and maximum-flow test are unchanged.

Thus the proposed mirror introduces no identity-sensitive objective, no new population-level optimization, and no genuinely new computational structure. The paper’s agents are already anonymous except for their rows in \(\vec v\) and entries of \(\vec b\), and its allocations are already fractional. The continuum merely replaces repeated identical rows by one weighted row. On a strict novelty criterion, this looks like a collision: Theorem 17 has already solved the resulting representative-type problem.

That is the best objection, but it does not defeat the anchor under the programme’s stated standards. The organization scenario is a credible high-multiplicity regime; per-capita chore supplies prevent degeneration; and the compression from \(N\) repeated workers to \(\tau\) types is computationally meaningful even if the resulting algorithm is an immediate corollary. The mirror has a valid two-way dictionary and can yield an algorithm whose complexity depends on \(\tau\), rather than on the enormous population size \(N\).

So I cannot honestly sustain the requested universal negative case. Theorem 17 survives: this is a legitimate Class A continuous-population mirror, albeit one with limited novelty because the paper’s structure makes the reduction essentially immediate.
