The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(k\ge2\), given a finite support \(T\) of explicitly listed types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a succinct polynomial-time value representation of a monotone, Lipschitz DR-submodular function \(\Phi:[0,1]^T\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\), determine the best polynomial-time approximation for maximizing \(\Phi(y)\) subject to \(0\le y_t\le1\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_t\le1/k\), with additive accuracy \(\eta\). In particular, does the discrete barrier \(1-(1-1/k)^k\) persist when type proportions are explicit?

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents with finitely many complete impact-profile types \(t\in T\), each carrying rational mass \(\mu_t\). Choose a fraction \(y_t\) of each type for the program, subject to the per-capita capacity \(\sum_t\mu_t y_t\le1/k\), and maximize an explicitly represented monotone DR-submodular aggregate benefit \(\Phi(y)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 4.5’s cardinality-constrained monotone submodular selection result. It leaves the welfare, matroid, and other approximation results, including Corollaries 4.6–4.9, aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed support size and a known Lipschitz bound, what is the sharp approximation guarantee for the type-mass optimization problem?
  • As \(\tau=|T|\) grows, can an inapproximability barrier matching \(1-(1-1/k)^k\) persist when types and their masses are explicit?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is Corollary 4.5: unless \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{RP}\), for every constant \(k\), there is no \(\bigl(1-(1-1/k)^k+\epsilon\bigr)\)-approximation for maximizing a succinctly represented monotone submodular function \(f\) subject to \(|S|\le n/k\), where \(n\) is the ground-set size. The paper derives this corollary from its Theorem 4.4; the constant-size symmetry-gap example it uses comes from Vondrák [13].

A plausible mirror is High-Multiplicity Submodular Participation Selection. Think of a large population eligible for a public outreach program with a limited number of places. A type is a complete profile of how an individual affects the program’s aggregate benefit. The instance lists a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a succinct, monotone, diminishing-returns benefit function \(\Phi:[0,1]^T\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\). The society has many members per type; \(y_t\) is the fraction of type \(t\) selected. Given a capacity \(1/k\), find a feasible \(y\) maximizing \(\Phi(y)\), subject to
\[ 0\le y_t\le1,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_t\le 1/k. \]
A solution is such a vector \(y\), or a specified additive-\(\eta\) approximation to the optimum. Each actual person is still selected or not; fractional values describe selected mass in the continuous population.

This is a natural instance regime when the eligible population is large but has relatively few impact profiles. It is also close to the paper’s construction: its symmetry-gap argument expands a small set of roles into many copies and evaluates the refined objective through proportions selected from each role. So the mirror keeps the underlying choice—select a subset under a cardinality limit—and makes the population proportions the decision variables.

The connection is especially clear in the symmetric example used for Corollary 4.5. It has \(k\) roles, each with mass \(1/k\), and the continuous benefit can be written as \(\Phi(y)=1-\prod_{i=1}^k(1-y_i)\), with \(\sum_i y_i\le1\). Selecting all of one type is feasible and gives value \(1\). The symmetric choice \(y_i=1/k\) gives \(1-(1-1/k)^k\), the approximation threshold in the corollary. In the discrete reduction, many copies of each role are present but their partition is hidden. In the societal mirror, the type support and masses are given, so the prover can optimize over the role proportions directly.

I expect this mirror to be Class A for fixed support size, with a regular, explicit \(\Phi\): it is a fixed-dimensional continuous optimization problem, and additive approximation can be obtained by discretizing the mass variables when the input supplies suitable smoothness bounds. The specific symmetry-gap example is exactly solvable. Whether a stronger multiplicative guarantee holds for broad function classes, or whether hardness returns when \(|T|\) grows, is a further question. The scope here is Corollary 4.5; I would not use it to claim a mirror for the paper’s welfare or matroid results.

The weak point is the change in what the input reveals. Corollary 4.5 applies to succinct functions whose reduction hides the role partition using Unique-SAT. The continuous society explicitly lists its types and masses, and its objective is represented at the type-proportion level. If the relevant impact profiles cannot be compressed into a small support without losing the problem’s substance, this mirror is less persuasive. But in a population where many people genuinely share a small set of complete profiles, that compression is the point of high multiplicity, and the authors should recognize the resulting optimization problem as their subset-selection problem in a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 4.5 is a real computational anchor, so the negative case cannot lead by saying the paper has nothing to mirror. The strongest objection is about the model’s input. In a general submodular instance, an element’s marginal contribution depends on the selected set around it. A complete type must capture that context-dependent behavior, which usually leaves no large groups of interchangeable elements. The corollary’s hard instances do have role groups, but their assignment to elements is hidden in the reduction. The proposed mirror lists the roles and their masses explicitly, while making the objective depend only on selected proportions by role. That is a sensible structured problem, but it drops the concealed element-to-role structure that makes this corollary a computational result.

There is a clear three-way tension. Give the model a small, explicit type support and a type-level objective, and it studies cohort selection under diminishing returns—a plausible population problem, but a restricted one. Keep the general succinct submodular objective, and elements’ distinct interaction patterns generally prevent a meaningful high-multiplicity population. Hide the type assignment to retain those interactions, and the input is no longer a society given by its support and masses; recovering the hidden structure reintroduces the paper’s computational core. The concern is that none of these options yields a population version of the same general input problem.

That objection does not defeat the proponent’s stronger scenario. Many people with genuinely shared, complete impact profiles, a known small set of types, and an aggregate diminishing-returns objective form a credible high-multiplicity regime. Optimizing participation by type is recognizable as cardinality-constrained submodular selection. The paper’s hardness result does not establish that this population regime is uninteresting, and making its answer easy would not by itself count against it. I can make a case that this is a narrower, newly structured problem rather than a compelling mirror of Corollary 4.5, but I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
