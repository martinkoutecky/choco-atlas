Why no mirror

Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are named probabilistic and axiomatic results, not qualifying computational results. The proposed cycle LP and continuous MPSR rule are sensible new problems over mass vectors, but they replace the paper's finite-sample likelihood questions rather than continuizing a computational result of the paper. The computational-anchor gate therefore fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed questions cover a deterministic population-margin LP and a deterministic MPSR implementation, but leave the paper's smoothed finite-profile rates, ANR theorem, main technical lemma, and other likelihood results without a qualifying continuous computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow but real: this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, although it does not contain a qualifying computational-complexity anchor. Its named results are probabilistic asymptotic theorems, not results saying NP-hard, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar. Indeed, Section 6 lists computational aspects as future work. So the mirror below is a plausible Class A research problem induced by the paper, not a computational theorem already proved in it.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, “Smoothed likelihood of Condorcet’s paradox,” proved in this paper from Lemma 1. It assumes a strictly positive, closed single-agent preference model \(\mathcal M=(\Theta,\mathcal L(A),\Pi)\). If every \(\pi\in\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\) has a UMG containing no weak Condorcet cycle, then, for every \(n\), the worst-case probability that a random \(n\)-profile has no Condorcet cycle is \(1-\exp(-\Omega(n))\). If some \(\pi\in\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\) has a weak Condorcet cycle, then for infinitely many \(n\) that probability is only \(1-\Omega(1)\). Equivalently, the probability of the paradox is exponentially small in the first case and bounded below by a constant along an infinite sequence in the second.

The continuous problem I would put beside it is Continuous Condorcet-Cycle Feasibility.

A type is a complete ranking \(R\in\mathcal L(A)\), and a society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal L(A))\). For alternatives \(a,b\), define the population margin

\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{R\in\mathcal L(A)}\mu_R \bigl(\mathbf 1[a\succ_R b]-\mathbf 1[b\succ_R a]\bigr). \]

The input is a rational polytope \(K=\operatorname{conv}\{q^1,\ldots,q^h\}\) of admissible population distributions, together with \(m\). The task is to maximize the weakest margin of a directed cycle:

\[ \Delta(K)= \max_{\substack{\mu\in K\\ a_1,\ldots,a_\ell\ \text{distinct}\\ 3\le \ell\le m}} \ \min_i M_\mu(a_i,a_{i+1}), \]

where \(a_{\ell+1}=a_1\). Return a maximizing society and cycle, or certify that no weak cycle exists. A weak cycle exists exactly when \(\Delta(K)\ge0\); a strict Condorcet cycle exists when the optimum is positive.

This is a genuine population mirror: \(R\) is the voter type, \(\mu_R\) is the fraction of society with that type, and the decision variable is the aggregate society \(\mu\). The objective is the robustness margin of the cycle. For every fixed candidate cycle, the optimization is a linear program; with \(m\) fixed, enumerating cycles remains finite and polynomial in the explicit type representation. Thus the expected classification is Class A in the high-multiplicity model.

The regime is plausible in a large repeated group-decision platform with a fixed menu of, say, five alternatives. Millions of users may be behaviorally indistinguishable for the decision because they share one of the \(5!=120\) learned complete-ranking templates. Then \(n\) is large while \(\tau=120\) is fixed, and the normalized histogram of the finite electorate is exactly the continuous society \(\mu\). The paper’s \(\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\) already identifies the relevant admissible aggregate distributions: for heterogeneous individual noise laws, the average expected histogram lies in that convex hull.

The bridge is precise. A finite profile with histogram \(h\) gives \(\mu=h/n\); all pairwise margins scale by \(n\). Conversely, a rational \(\mu\) can be cleared of denominators to produce a finite high-multiplicity profile. The paper does not itself make \(\mu\) the input society: its \(\pi\)'s are single-agent sampling distributions and its randomness remains over finite profiles. Therefore this is not a novelty collision, though it is deliberately close to the paper’s fractional-profile machinery.

A second, weaker but more algorithmically concrete anchor is Theorem 3, proved here. It assumes a strictly positive, closed model with the uniform ranking distribution in \(\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\). For any anonymous and neutral voting correspondence, the paper defines most-popular-singleton-ranking tie-breaking. It proves that the resulting rule violates anonymity or neutrality with probability \(O(n^{-m!/4})\), for every \(n\) and every vector of admissible individual distributions. It also proves that if the backup tie-breaker is anonymous, respectively neutral, the resulting rule remains anonymous, respectively neutral.

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous MPSR Tie-Breaking. Given a mass vector \(\mu\), define

\[ \operatorname{MPSR}_\infty(\mu) = \arg\max_R \{\mu_R:\nexists W\neq R\text{ with }\mu_W=\mu_R\}. \]

If this set is empty, use the backup tie-breaker; otherwise use the selected ranking to resolve the correspondence’s co-winners. The problem is: given rational \(\mu\), a scoring correspondence, and a backup rule, compute the resolute winner and either return the selected singleton ranking or certify that every ranking is tied in mass with another ranking. This is polynomial in the explicit number \(\tau=m!\) of types. The exceptional set is a union of linear strata defined by equations \(\mu_R=\mu_W\); when MPSR is defined, the same anonymity and neutrality argument applies exactly at the population level.

This second mirror is weaker because the finite probability rate \(O(n^{-m!/4})\) disappears after continuization: exact equality of masses becomes a geometric exceptional set rather than a sampling event. Still, it preserves the paper’s actual tie-breaking problem, and the continuous object is the population, not the winner space.

The weakest point of the positive case is therefore clear. The paper’s main questions concern the probability of rare finite-sample events, whereas the continuous mirror replaces sampling by an exact mass vector. That removes much of the paper’s asymptotic content. More importantly, because the paper proves no named hardness or algorithmic-complexity result, this cannot honestly be presented as evidence that discrete computational hardness dissolves in the continuum. The strongest defensible claim is narrower: Theorem 1 supplies a natural continuous feasibility-and-robustness problem over population masses, and Theorem 3 supplies a natural continuous canonical tie-breaking problem. Both are sensible high-multiplicity mirrors, but they are proposed computational continuations of the paper, not computational results established by it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with a decisive mismatch: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1, Lemma 1, and Theorem 3 are asymptotic probability statements; the paper proves no complexity, approximation, parameterized, or algorithmic theorem. “Easy-to-compute” MPSR is not a complexity result, and Section 6 explicitly postpones computational questions. The proposed mirrors therefore invent new computational problems rather than continuize computational problems from the paper.

For Theorem 1, the natural continuous population is indeed a mass vector \(\mu\) over rankings, with margins

\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_R \mu_R \bigl(\mathbf 1[a\succ_R b]-\mathbf 1[b\succ_R a]\bigr). \]

But this removes the paper’s central object. The theorem concerns the probability that a finite random histogram crosses systems of strict linear inequalities. Its exponential and polynomial regimes arise from finite-sample concentration and anti-concentration around the hyperplanes \(M_\mu(a,b)=0\). A continuum society has an exact margin vector and hence a deterministic UMG; there is no sampling fluctuation and no \(n\)-dependent likelihood left.

The proposed \(\Delta(K)\) makes that substitution explicit. It replaces

\[ \inf_{\vec\pi\in\Pi^n}\Pr(\text{finite random profile has a cycle}) \]

with an optimization over \(\mu\in K=\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\). That is a coherent new LP, but it is not the theorem’s problem. In the paper, \(\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\) is a set of possible mean single-agent distributions used to classify sampling probabilities; it is not a feasible region from which a planner chooses a society. The proposed problem retains the geometric condition while discarding the probabilistic result.

Changing the type space cannot repair this. If types are rankings, the continuum retains only the aggregate \(\mu\). If types include ground truths, noise parameters, or reporting kernels, the limit still produces the deterministic push-forward distribution

\[ \bar\mu=\int \pi_\lambda\,d\nu(\lambda). \]

To recover the paper’s rates one must retain a finite sample size and independent draws, or add a new random perturbation to \(\bar\mu\). The former is the original finite model; the latter is a noise or average-case model, not continuization of the population. Thus the best version of the proposed mirror is a sensible new robust-margin problem, but not a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 1.

Theorem 3 has the same obstruction more sharply. MPSR exploits exact coincidences among integer histogram counts. In the continuum, its analogue is a deterministic rule on \(\mu\): compare the coordinates \(\mu_R\), select the largest value occurring uniquely, and invoke a backup rule if every value is repeated. The bad event becomes a finite union of equality strata such as

\[ \mu_R=\mu_W. \]

There is no analogue of the paper’s \(O(n^{-m!/4})\) phenomenon, because there is no random histogram and no \(n\). Computing the continuous rule is merely a finite comparison problem. That simplicity is not by itself a defect; the fundamental issue is that the theorem’s substantive object—the anti-concentration rate—has disappeared.

The obvious repairs fail in opposite directions. A distribution over \(\mu\) or a random perturbation of masses restores probability only by adding a noise model. Optimizing a tie-breaker over all continuous societies becomes deterministic rule design or an ANR axiomatic question, not a computational mirror of MPSR’s theorem. The same diagnosis applies to the paper’s ANR result generally: continuum profiles yield stabilizer strata and deterministic symmetry obstructions, whereas the paper studies how often finite random histograms land on those strata.

Rankings are perfectly plausible high-multiplicity types; that is not the objection. The objection is that this paper studies finite-sample fluctuations of profiles and their probabilistic consequences, while ChoCo asks for computational questions on a continuous society. A generous programme could accept the proposed LPs as new Class A research directions. Under the stated gate, however, neither raised anchor survives: both replace the paper’s question rather than continuize it.
