The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is a population version of the paper’s group-consensus problem. Imagine a large stakeholder panel comparing a fixed set of products using a common, coarse ratio scale. A type is a stakeholder’s complete positive reciprocal comparison matrix \(A^t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the panel with that matrix. With many stakeholders and standardized responses, it is plausible that the number of distinct matrices \(\tau\) is much smaller than the panel size. This is a genuine population model: the group’s pairwise comparison matrix is the mass-weighted geometric mean
\[ \bar a_{ij}=\prod_t (a^t_{ij})^{\mu_t}, \]
and the decision is the weak ranking of the alternatives induced by the row geometric means of \(\bar A\). Rational masses match the paper’s observation that rational decision-maker weights can be represented by repeated judgments.

The nearest named anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper: the row geometric mean ranking is the unique ranking method satisfying anonymity, aggregation invariance, and responsiveness. Its population lift asks whether, for a distribution \(\mu\) over comparison-matrix types, those same requirements on the induced group ranking uniquely determine the row-geometric-mean ranking of \(\bar A\). The solution is precisely that ranking. The paper’s theorem and its note about rational weights make this a natural, recognizable mirror of the authors’ group decision problem.

But Theorem 6.1 is not a qualifying computational anchor under the programme’s rules. It characterizes a ranking by axioms; it does not establish that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Propositions 5.1, 5.2, and 6.1 likewise concern axiom satisfaction or independence. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. Computing the population ranking would be a possible new problem, but it would not be a problem whose computational result this paper anchors.

That is the weak point, and it is decisive for this programme’s scope: the paper is about an axiomatic characterization, not computational questions about a continuous society. Questions about extending the characterization to arbitrary real masses or the exact bit complexity of ranking from binary-encoded data could follow from the model, but they do not turn the paper’s results into computational anchors. So the honest positive case is that the population model fits the paper’s group-consensus content unusually well; it is not a ChoCo-compatible computational mirror of a named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The stakeholder-panel scenario is credible: many respondents can share the same pairwise judgments, and proportions are a natural way to describe the group. The problem is the anchor. Theorem 6.1 characterizes a ranking rule by axioms; Propositions 5.1, 5.2, and 6.1 also concern axiom satisfaction or independence. None states a computational problem or classifies its complexity. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, there is no computational result here to mirror.

The strongest population lift makes that gap clear. For types \(A^t\) with masses \(\mu_t\), the aggregate is \(\bar a_{ij}=\prod_t(a^t_{ij})^{\mu_t}\). Its row geometric mean ranking compares scores
\[ q_i=\sum_j\log\bar a_{ij} =\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j\log a^t_{ij}. \]
For rational masses, this is the paper’s finite group aggregation with decision makers repeated in the corresponding proportions; the paper explicitly notes that rational weights can be represented that way. So lifting Theorem 6.1 to rational population masses adds no computational classification, and its uniqueness claim largely restates the finite-group characterization. Extending it to arbitrary real masses would require a further axiomatic extension, still not a computational result.

A natural computational question could add an intervention model—for example, the least mass whose judgments must change to reverse a pairwise ranking. That would need costs and permitted changes that the paper does not specify, and no result in the paper anchors that optimization problem. It may be a worthwhile new project, but it would be an additional problem built around the paper’s subject.

The negative case is strong for a ChoCo mirror of this paper’s results: no named computational result supplies an anchor. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous computational question can arise from group pairwise judgments is weaker. The population regime is sensible, and new questions can be designed for it; they simply would not mirror a computational result in this paper.
