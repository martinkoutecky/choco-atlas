The continuous mirror question

Given a finite time-extended graph, explicit route menus \(R_t\) for \(\tau\) complete request types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), type-specific utilities and budgets, and per-capita edge supplies \(b_e\), find an allocation and nonnegative edge prices \(p_e\). The allocation may be represented by route masses \(q_{t,s}\) and dropout masses \(q_{t,\emptyset}\), with \(\sum_{s\in R_t}q_{t,s}+q_{t,\emptyset}=\mu_t\); its per-unit average bundle for each type must be optimal for that type’s fractional individual problem at prices \(p\). Require aggregate use \(\sum_t\sum_{s\in R_t}q_{t,s}a_{s,e}\le b_e\) and price complementarity \(p_e>0\Rightarrow\sum_t\sum_{s\in R_t}q_{t,s}a_{s,e}=b_e\). Determine the complexity of finding an exact or \(\epsilon\)-approximate equilibrium in the graph and menu sizes, \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), including whether the fixed-point route in Proposition 4.5 applies.

The model it lives in

A continuum of requests grouped into complete operational types, with mass \(\mu_t\) per type. Each unit of mass receives a whole route, while a type’s average bundle can be fractional; edge supplies scale to per-capita values \(b_e\). The decision variables are route and dropout masses plus edge prices, subject to type-level best responses, aggregate capacities, and price complementarity.

The objection that survived

The type-mass equilibrium and welfare formulation may be an exact aggregation of duplicate agent variables, so the mirror may add no complexity boundary or runtime beyond standard convex optimization.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the fractional-equilibrium and welfare-pricing theory, especially Propositions 4.3–4.5 and the convex program used in the distributed procedure. It leaves the integral-allocation step, the distributed algorithm’s convergence guarantees, and the empirical comparisons outside the formal mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an exact or \(\epsilon\)-approximate type-mass fractional competitive equilibrium be computed in time polynomial in the explicit graph, route menus, \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\)?
  • Does the type-mass fixed-point map \(\omega\mapsto\lambda^\dagger(\omega)\) always have a fixed point, and can one be found efficiently?
  • What changes when route menus are implicit and require a pricing or separation oracle?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a mirror of the paper’s fractional competitive equilibrium, especially its route from a convex welfare problem to market prices. The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result. Its usable anchors are results about convexity and equilibrium computation; neither proves a running-time bound.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large drone-delivery service using repeated mission templates. A type would specify the full operational profile the mechanism uses: origin and destination zones, request window, vehicle class, feasible time-trajectories, per-route valuations, air-credit budget, and outside-option utilities. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of requests with that complete profile. The relevant regime has \(N\) flights and \(\tau\) recurring mission types, with \(N\gg\tau\)—for example, many deliveries using standardized routes and service classes. Each infinitesimal flight still receives a whole trajectory; the mass variable records how many flights of a type take each route.

Capacities also need a per-capita interpretation: for a cohort of \(N\) requests, an edge capacity \(\ell_e\) becomes \(b_e=\ell_e/N\). In a scaled service regime, corridor and time-slot capacity scales with demand. That co-modification is part of this mirror. If the physical capacities stay fixed as demand grows, the limiting capacity ratios may instead collapse, and the useful regime needs more careful justification.

My supporting anchor is Lemma 4.4, proved in the paper: constraints (3b)–(3e) always have a feasible solution, and for every \(\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{|U|}_{\ge 0}\), optimization problem (3) is convex. The direct continuous question is Type-Mass Budget-Adjusted Welfare, with \(\omega\) given. Its instance is a finite time-extended graph, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-capita capacities \(b_e\), and each type’s explicit route menu, valuations, budget, and outside-option data. Let \(q_{t,s}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to route \(s\), with corresponding mass \(q_{t,\emptyset}\) dropping out and \(q_{t,o}\) consuming the outside option. Put \(x_t=q_t/\mu_t\), the average per-agent allocation. The constraints require each type’s route and dropout masses to sum to \(\mu_t\), its path flows to be valid, and total use of each edge to be at most \(b_e\). The objective is the type-aggregated version of (3): maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t(w_t+\omega_t)\log f_t(x_t)-p_o\sum_t q_{t,o}\). A solution is an optimal mass allocation and its resource-price and type-constraint dual multipliers, or an \(\epsilon\)-optimal primal-dual pair.

For explicitly represented types and route menus, I expect this problem to be Class A for approximate optimization: the support is finite, and the paper’s objective and constraints retain their convex structure. The open algorithmic questions include the dependence on \(\tau\), graph size, input bit length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), and whether the result survives when route menus are implicit and require a pricing or separation oracle.

The lead anchor is Proposition 4.5, also proved in the paper: if some \(\omega^*\in\mathbb{R}^{|U|}_{\ge 0}\) is a fixed point of \(\omega\mapsto\lambda^\dagger(\omega)\), then the optimizer of (3) and its optimal dual multiplier for the capacity constraints form a fractional competitive equilibrium. The continuous question is Type-Mass Fractional Competitive Equilibrium. Given the same type distribution, route data, budgets, and per-capita capacities, find route masses \(q_{t,s}\), dropout and outside-option masses, and nonnegative edge prices \(p_e\). For every type, \(q_t/\mu_t\) must be an optimal solution to that type’s fractional individual problem at prices \(p\), subject to its budget and path constraints. Aggregate edge use must obey \(\sum_t q_{t,e}\le b_e\), with \(p_e>0\) only when the edge is full. In the paper’s fixed-point route, the output also includes \(\omega\) and the normalized type multipliers \(\lambda_t^\dagger\), with \(\omega_t=\lambda_t^\dagger\). For an algorithmic version, an \(\epsilon\)-solution can bound typewise best-response gaps, capacity violations, complementarity errors, and the fixed-point residual.

I expect this equilibrium problem to be Class A for additive-\(\epsilon\) solutions when the number of types and their route descriptions are explicit. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Proposition 4.5: the proposition is conditional on a fixed point and gives no algorithm or convergence bound. Further questions are whether the continuum fixed point is guaranteed, whether an equilibrium can be computed without following that particular fixed-point map, and what communication and oracle complexity remains when values are private. A privacy-preserving version would keep utilities at local type agents and let the service provider see only aggregate route demands and market signals, following the paper’s distributed setup.

The paper’s own Proposition 4.3 separately proves existence of a fractional competitive equilibrium for its finite set of vehicles. That supports the plausibility of the equilibrium question, but it is not a complexity classification and does not settle its continuous algorithmic cost.

This mirror covers the paper’s fractional-equilibrium theory and its distributed pricing stage. It does not claim a hardness result, or mirror the integral-allocation step as a theorem about continuous populations. The weakest point is that the paper’s fractionality relaxes each vehicle’s allocation, while the proposed mirror gets fractionality from splitting mass across integral routes. Those are distinct models. The mirror remains recognizable because it keeps the same vehicles’ complete route menus, budgets, private valuations, and capacity-priced edge resources; it changes who receives each route only by aggregating identical requests. But if real requests have idiosyncratic route values or budgets, the complete type support may approach \(N\), and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that neither establishes a complexity result for the population limit to change. Lemma 4.4 proves feasibility and convexity of a finite market program. Proposition 4.5 gives a sufficient condition for its optimizer to be an equilibrium, conditional on a fixed point. Neither gives a running time, a complexity classification, or a guarantee that the fixed point exists. The paper’s iterative algorithm has no convergence or complexity theorem. That leaves little of the paper’s proved computational landscape for a continuous mirror to chart.

For the Lemma 4.4 anchor, the proposed type-mass welfare problem is an exact aggregation of (3) when agents in a type share their complete route data, valuations, budgets, and constraints. The per-agent feasible set is convex. Averaging allocations within a type preserves its flow constraints and aggregate resource use; concavity of the log objective makes a common average allocation at least as good as differing allocations among identical agents. The resulting type-mass program is therefore the same convex optimization problem with duplicate agent variables removed. With explicit route menus, its computational content is standard finite-dimensional convex optimization. With implicit menus, the hard part is generating or optimizing over routes, which remains the route problem in the original model.

Proposition 4.5 offers even less of a distinct anchor. Its fixed-point route is conditional, and the paper does not prove that its map has a fixed point. The direct type-mass equilibrium question inherits the existence argument of Proposition 4.3: replacing identical agents with mass preserves the market structure. A question about computing an approximate equilibrium could still be posed, but the proposition does not supply an algorithm or complexity boundary for it. The paper’s own iteration does not establish one either.

These points weaken the case for prioritizing this paper as a source of new complexity theory. They do not defeat the best mirror. A large delivery service with repeated mission templates, finite vehicle classes, and standardized budget and value profiles is plausible. Its type support could stay small as the number of requests grows. Fractional route use by a type can then mean that distinct vehicles of that type take different whole routes. This is a valid population continuization; high-multiplicity work supports its relevance. If capacities scale with demand, the aggregate model also avoids the vanishing-capacity limit.

So the negative case is weak on the user’s universal claim. The exact-type model may be a useful high-multiplicity compression of the paper’s market program, and further work on approximation or rounding could matter. The stronger objection is narrower: the cited results do not reveal a new complexity landscape, and the most direct mirror appears to inherit the paper’s convex market formulation rather than expose a new computational structure.
