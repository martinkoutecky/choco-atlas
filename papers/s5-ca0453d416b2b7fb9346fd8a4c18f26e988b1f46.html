The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(A\), a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a target \(a^\star\), and a rational budget \(\beta\), does there exist deleted mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\leq\beta\) such that \(a^\star\) is in the upward uncovered set of the remaining society? For each ranking \(t\), let \(\sigma_t(a,b)=1\) if \(t\) ranks \(a\) above \(b\), and \(-1\) otherwise, and let \(D_x(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}(\mu_t-x_t)\sigma_t(a,b)\). The required condition is that for every \(b\ne a^\star\), either \(D_x(b,a^\star)\leq0\), or there is a \(c\in A\) with \(D_x(c,b)>0\) and \(D_x(c,a^\star)\leq0\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types; each unit of mass has the same deletion cost. The decision variables \(x_t\) are deleted masses, the budget is \(\sum_t x_t\leq\beta\), and pairwise dominance is determined by the remaining mass margins \(D_x\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the bounded control question underlying Theorem 5.1, with deletion measured as mass over ranking types. It leaves aside the relation-algebraic implementation, its experiments, and the paper’s other winning conditions.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the unrestricted complexity of this mass-deletion problem as a function of the number \(m\) of candidates, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • How large can the gap be between the minimum discrete deletion fraction and the minimum mass deletion?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would anchor the case on Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper. It says that constructive control by deleting voters is NP-hard for Condorcet voting when the winners are the uncovered alternatives. In the underlying decision problem, the input is a profile of strict rankings, a target alternative \(a^\star\), and a deletion bound \(k\); the question is whether at most \(k\) voters can be removed so that \(a^\star\) belongs to the resulting election’s upward (Gilles) uncovered set. The paper proves this by reduction from X4C. This is my strongest anchor; I would not add the cited Condorcet-winner hardness as a second anchor, since it is not stated as a numbered result in this paper.

The direct mirror keeps the candidates, complete rankings, pairwise majority rule, uncovered-set criterion, and deletion objective. Let \(T\) be the supported rankings, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A decision instance gives \(a^\star\) and a rational deletion budget \(\beta\). Choose deleted masses \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\). For a ranking \(t\), let \(\sigma_t(a,b)=1\) when \(t\) ranks \(a\) above \(b\), and \(-1\) otherwise. The remaining pairwise margin is
\[ D_x(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}(\mu_t-x_t)\sigma_t(a,b). \]
Define \(a\) to beat \(b\) when \(D_x(a,b)>0\), as in the paper; a tie creates no dominance edge. The question is whether there exists such an \(x\) for which, for every \(b\ne a^\star\),
\[ \neg\left(D_x(b,a^\star)>0\ \wedge\ \forall c\,[D_x(c,b)>0\Rightarrow D_x(c,a^\star)>0]\right). \]
That is exactly the paper’s uncovered condition: no alternative covers \(a^\star\). A solution is the vector of deleted masses. This is a decision version of minimizing total deleted mass, matching the paper’s bounded-deletion problem. For a discrete profile with \(N\) voters, the corresponding threshold is \(\beta=k/N\).

The regime is credible for large membership elections or platform elections whose participants fall into recurring, complete ballot types, with many people sharing each ranking and the same unit deletion cost. The controller has the same voter-removal power assumed by the paper’s model; the continuous action deletes a share of each type. In fact, the paper’s own reduction already has high multiplicity: for an X4C universe of size \(q\), it uses \(q/4-2\) copies of each of \(2q\) ranking types, plus \(3q/4\) set-associated rankings and one more ranking. Thus the number of voters is \(\Theta(q^2)\), while the support is \(O(q)\), choosing fixed internal orders where the construction leaves them unspecified. The theorem’s hard instances already have many agents relative to distinct types.

There is a striking limit to what that anchor tells us about the mirror. In the reduction, each element occurs in three sets. Delete one third of the mass of every set-associated ranking. There are \(3q/4\) such rankings, so this removes \(q/4\) voter-equivalents, exactly the reduction’s budget. For each element, this changes its \(s_i\)-versus-\(b_i\) margin by a net \(q/4-2\): the non-containing set rankings help \(s_i\) when removed, while the three containing sets hurt it. That is precisely enough to reverse the original \(b_i\) lead of \(q/4-3\). The other relevant margins, initially at least \(q/4+1\), survive a total deletion of \(q/4\). So this fractional deletion makes \(a^\star\) uncovered on every X4C instance, whether or not an exact cover exists.

That failure of hardness transfer is part of why this is a worthwhile mirror. It exposes exactly what the discrete proof depends on: choosing whole set-voters. The continuous question remains the authors’ control problem with the same preferences and winner definition, now asking what happens when deletion is measured as population mass. The relaxation changes the answer on these instances, which is the kind of effect the programme is meant to investigate.

For unrestricted \(m\), my tentative expectation is Class C: uncoveredness creates a disjunction for each possible coverer, and the X4C proof no longer supplies the needed hardness. This is a conjectural direction, not a result of Theorem 5.1. There is also a useful parameterized route: for each \(b\ne a^\star\), choose either the linear condition that \(b\) does not beat \(a^\star\), or a witness \(c\) that beats \(b\) but does not beat \(a^\star\). Each collection of choices gives linear constraints on \(x\), checkable by LP; enumerating them gives an \(m^{O(m)}\) algorithm, polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length for each choice. Further questions are whether the unrestricted problem is NP-hard for continuum-specific reasons, whether stronger bounds in \(m\) or \(\tau\) are possible, and how large the discrete-to-continuous gap can be.

The mirror covers Theorem 5.1’s underlying control problem; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s relation-algebraic encoding or its computation over individual-voter subsets. Its weakest point is also clear: fractional deletion removes the integrality that drives the theorem’s reduction, and type-targeted deletion is most persuasive as a formal control or robustness model, not as a description of every real election. Those limits weaken any complexity claim carried over from the theorem. They do not make the mass-deletion question a different social-choice problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 5.1: constructive control by deleting voters is NP-hard when winners are the uncovered alternatives. It is a numbered computational result, and its underlying decision problem has a direct continuous mirror. So the objection cannot be that the paper lacks a result to mirror.

The strongest case against it is that the theorem’s proof depends on indivisible choices among set-associated voters. In its reduction, the X4C instance has \(n\) elements, the election has \(M=2nt+3n/4+1\) voters, and the deletion budget is \(n/4\). Delete one third of the mass of each of the \(3n/4\) set-associated ranking types. This uses exactly \(n/(4M)\) of the population—the normalized budget \(k/M\). For each element \(i\), the resulting gain for \(s_i\) against \(b_i\) is \(n/4-2\), enough to reverse the original \(b_i\) lead of \(n/4-3\). The other relevant margins, initially at least \(n/4+1\), survive deleting total mass \(n/4\) in voter-equivalent units. Thus \(a^\star\) becomes uncovered even when the X4C instance has no exact cover.

That does not refute the continuous question, but it shows that the paper’s only new hardness result supplies no hardness evidence for it: fractional deletion erases the reduction’s exact-cover choice. One could add type-specific deletion prices or eligibility rules to seek different combinatorics, but those would introduce structure absent from the theorem’s unit-cost problem.

Still, the best mirror keeps the original unit deletion cost, represents society as rational mass over rankings, and asks for the least mass to remove to put \(a^\star\) in the uncovered set. Its constraints are linear pairwise-margin tests combined by the uncovered-set condition; enumerating witnesses gives a finite family of LP feasibility questions. That is a legitimate computational problem in \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length. Large membership elections with recurring rankings and uniform removal costs are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the problem depends on population mass rather than fractional outcomes or lotteries.

So the negative case is weak at the point that matters. The reduction’s collapse is a useful boundary observation, not a reason the mirror is unworthy; the programme explicitly treats that kind of change as something to investigate. Nothing in the paper establishes that this continuous control problem is already computationally answered or that no sensible population regime exists. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor: under the stated criteria, its mirror survives.
