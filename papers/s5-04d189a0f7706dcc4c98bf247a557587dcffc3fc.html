The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), positive rational module requests \(d_t\le k\), and rational capacity \(1<\kappa<k\) per unit of population mass, find rational assignment masses \(x_{tj}\ge0\) for \(j\in\{0,\ldots,k\}\). Require \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,j}j x_{tj}\le\kappa\). With \(u_t(j)=\min(j/d_t,1)\) and \(u_t(z)=0\) for \(z<0\), require support-wise EF1: whenever \(x_{tj}>0\) and \(x_{s\ell}>0\), \(u_t(j)\ge u_t(\ell-1)\). Require Pareto efficiency against feasible reassignments that weakly improve almost every individual and strictly improve a positive mass, and require every bundle used by type \(t\) to satisfy \(u_t(j)\ge u_t(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor)\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of request types with rational masses \(\mu_t\); each individual receives an integral number \(j\) of modules, represented by type-to-bundle masses \(x_{tj}\). Total module use is at most \(\kappa\) per unit mass, and the allocation must satisfy EF1, Pareto efficiency, and the per-capita proportionality bound.

The objection that survived

The paper does not show that deployed stations sustain bounded request support as population size grows, so the low-support regime is plausible but supplied by the mirror rather than established by the study.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror captures Theorem 2’s instantaneous modular fairness guarantee; Theorem 1 also admits a conventional weighted-allocation mirror. The paper’s temporal SoC fairness metrics, arrival and departure dynamics, simulations, and runtime comparisons are outside these one-slot questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Formalize the replication correspondence for rational \(\mu_t\) and \(\kappa\), including the finite station-capacity assumption.
  • Give and analyze a type-level round-based algorithm with running time in \(\tau\), \(k\), and the input bit length.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is a one-slot allocation problem over a high-multiplicity EV population. The population is the continuous object; charging time, uncertainty, and outcomes need not be continuized. At the start of a slot, EVs report their requested power. For this paper’s fairness theorems, that request, together with the station-wide parameters, is a complete type: it determines the EV’s utility and feasible allocation. A type’s mass is its fraction of connected EVs.

A credible regime is a large charging hub or fleet depot with many active ports, where requests come from a small set of exact controller setpoints or recurring model-and-state-of-charge combinations. The number of EVs can then be in the hundreds or more while the number of distinct request types, \(\tau\), stays much smaller. This is a scenario for the paper’s per-slot allocation problem; it does not claim that every fast-charging station is a limit object.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper’s Appendix A. It says that FAIR-OPAP-C produces an allocation satisfying envy-freeness, Pareto efficiency, and proportionality for conventional FCSs, under \(u_i(p_i)=\min(p_i/p_i^{\mathrm{req}},1)\). The paper separately states that Algorithm 1 runs in \(O(|E|\log|E|)\) time. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result; the paper offers no comparable named hardness theorem.

I would ask the following continuous question.

Continuous Conventional FCS Fair Allocation (lead). An instance consists of a finite request-type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational requests \(r_t\le q\), a common per-port limit \(q\), and a rational station capacity \(\kappa<q\) per unit of population mass. Find rational per-EV allocations \(a_t\in[0,r_t]\) such that

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t\le \kappa, \qquad u_t(a_t)=\min(a_t/r_t,1), \]

and:

  • Envy-freeness: \(u_t(a_t)\ge u_t(a_s)\) for every \(t,s\in T\).
  • Pareto efficiency: no feasible type-level allocation \(b\) weakly improves every positive-mass type’s utility and strictly improves at least one.
  • Per-capita proportionality: \(u_t(a_t)\ge u_t(\kappa)\) for every \(t\).

The proportionality benchmark is deliberately stated as the utility from an equal per-capita share of capacity. The finite theorem defines proportionality using \(u_i(p^{CS})/|E|\), but its proof establishes the stronger intermediate bound \(u_i(p^{CS}/|E|)\). Under a replication with \(N\) EVs and total capacity \(N\kappa\), that proof supplies this mirror’s benchmark. The expected classification is Class A: a weighted water-filling rule, \(a_t=\min(r_t,\lambda)\), with \(\lambda\) chosen to use \(\min(\kappa,\sum_t\mu_t r_t)\) capacity, appears to give a solution in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The mirror covers the theorem’s instantaneous fairness guarantee, not the paper’s empirical claims about fairness across entire charging sessions.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here, in Appendix B. Under \(m^{CS}\ge m^{port}+(n^{port}-1)\), it says FAIR-OPAP-M guarantees EF1, Pareto efficiency, and proportionality for modular FCSs, for any sorting policy, using \(u_i(m_i)=\min(m_i/m_i^{req},1)\). The paper separately gives a running-time bound for Algorithm 2. Here the co-modification is essential: mass can be split across assignments, while every EV still receives an integer number of modules.

Continuous Modular FCS Fair Allocation. An instance has request types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational module requests \(d_t\le k\), an integer per-port module limit \(k\), and rational module capacity \(\kappa\) per unit mass. For a replication with \(N\) ports, take \(N\mu_t\) EVs of type \(t\) and \(N\kappa\) modules; restrict to the paper’s capacity regime, which is eventually met when \(\kappa>1\). Return rational masses \(x_{tj}\), where \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving exactly \(j\) modules:

\[ \sum_{j=0}^{k}x_{tj}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j=0}^{k}j\,x_{tj}\le\kappa. \]

Every individual receives an integer bundle; only the mass assigned to each bundle is fractional. Require support-wise EF1: whenever \(x_{tj}>0\) and \(x_{s\ell}>0\), \(u_t(j)\ge u_t(\ell-1)\), with \(u_t(z)=0\) for \(z<0\). Require Pareto efficiency against feasible reassignments that weakly improve almost every individual and strictly improve a positive mass. As a proportionality witness, require every bundle used for type \(t\) to give utility at least \(u_t(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor)\), the per-capita integer-share bound established in the finite proof. The expected direction is Class A, by adapting the round-based allocation over type masses, with complexity polynomial in \(\tau\), \(k\), and the input bit length. This is a distinct mirror from the conventional one: indivisible modules remain indivisible for each EV.

Both are problems the authors could plausibly recognize: they use the same request-based utilities, station caps, architecture constraints, and instantaneous fairness criteria. Existing work on fractional outcomes would not itself be a collision, since these questions make the population continuous. A useful follow-up is to formalize the replication dictionary, including when rational type masses and modular assignment masses can be realized exactly by finite populations.

The weakest point is the regime. A single ordinary FCS may have too few active EVs, and exact request types may proliferate with population size. The case rests on large hubs or depots with repeated request values, so \(\tau\ll N\). If requests are effectively all distinct, the type compression and the high-multiplicity motivation both weaken. Also, these anchors are positive algorithmic results, not discrete hardness that might dissolve in the continuum; the paper gives no such hardness result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that these are weak anchors for ChoCo’s complexity programme. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 prove that named policies satisfy fairness axioms; neither classifies the complexity of a stated decision or optimization problem. The running times appear in prose, and the paper offers no hardness result or complexity boundary. If the programme requires a mirror of a formal complexity result, the proponent has had to supply a new allocation problem, rather than continue one the paper itself posed.

That objection does not defeat the conventional mirror on its merits. For the theorem’s one-slot fairness question, an exact request is a complete type: the utility and fairness comparisons depend on it. A large hub with many ports and repeated digital request values is at least a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Scaling station capacity with population size is also an allowed co-modification. The narrower concern is that the paper studies a changing stream of charging sessions, while the proposed mirror captures a snapshot. A full-population account would have to represent the changing request masses as EVs arrive, depart, and change state. But the theorem’s guarantee is instantaneous, so a snapshot remains a defensible mirror.

The modular anchor is harder to dismiss. Its strongest formulation keeps each EV’s bundle integral and assigns masses of a request type to different module counts. That is a coherent continuous population model, and the station-capacity assumption can persist under linear scaling. The paper’s theorem then has a direct finite-population interpretation through replication. Calling the bundle masses fractional would not expose a flaw: the fractional quantity is the share of EVs receiving each integral bundle.

The practical regime is the negative case’s main weakness. Public fast-charging stations often have few ports, and requests may vary with state of charge and vehicle characteristics, so support size may approach population size. A large hub or depot with repeated requests is possible, though, and the theorem-level types need not encode charge curves or other attributes that the fairness guarantee does not use. The paper’s online setting also makes a sustained low-support regime less assured than a single-slot one. These points narrow the motivating instances; they do not rule them out.

I therefore cannot honestly defeat both anchors under ChoCo’s stated rules. The best case against is that the paper’s formal results are fairness guarantees rather than complexity classifications, and that the proposed low-support operating regime is not established by the paper’s small-station experiments. But neither point shows that the continuous questions are ill-posed or that no plausible high-multiplicity scenario exists. A red verdict would depend on a stricter requirement for what counts as a computational result to mirror; without that requirement, the negative case is weak.
