The strongest honest positive case is limited: a continuous, high-multiplicity version of this matching model sounds like a plausible research direction, but the paper gives no eligible computational result to anchor it.

There is no named computational result in the paper. Theorem 10, proved here, says that stable matchings under path-independent, cardinal-monotone choice functions form a distributive lattice and have polarity, unicardinality, and complementarity. Propositions 5–9 establish that structure. None asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm. The introduction’s suggestion that tracing all stable matchings “would be considerably simpler” is speculation, and the cited algorithm for finding stable matchings is external work.

A plausible setting for a future mirror would be a large two-sided market—say, recurring cohorts of employers and applicants—with agents grouped by complete choice-function type. Type masses would represent population shares; the decision would be a mass allocation across feasible partner bundles; the question could be whether a stable allocation exists or how to find one. That is a sensible high-multiplicity direction, but the paper neither studies that computational problem nor defines the continuous allocation and stability concepts it would need. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest candidate is Theorem 10, but it establishes a structural fact: stable matchings form a distributive lattice and satisfy polarity, unicardinality, and complementarity. Propositions 5–9 prove those properties. The paper gives no complexity result, algorithm, or computational model for finding or enumerating stable matchings. Its suggestion that tracing them “would be considerably simpler” is a conjecture about a possible algorithmic task, not a result to continuize.

The best reconstruction would use recurring cohorts of employers and applicants, grouped by complete choice-function type with rational population shares. One could ask for a stable mass allocation, or for the extremal points of its lattice. That is a plausible population regime; existing high-multiplicity work supports its relevance. But the paper leaves the computational question to be invented. “Does a stable allocation exist?” is already settled for the finite markets it studies. “How do we find one?” needs an input representation and algorithmic claim the paper does not supply. Asking for a welfare optimum over stable allocations would add an objective absent from the paper.

The continuous model also needs substantive choices about how type-level preferences act on partner bundles and how pairwise blocking works when allocations are represented by masses. A type-level flow alone may lose the bundle information that path-independent choice functions use. Bundle distributions or other richer representations could preserve it, but choosing one would define a new computational problem rather than mirror a problem in this paper.

So the strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper supplies no computational anchor: every named result is structural, and its only algorithmic remark is speculative. That makes the paper-specific case against a worthwhile mirror strong. The universal claim remains weaker: a carefully designed type-based matching computation could be valuable, but its value would come from the new model and question, not from continuizing a computational result established here.
