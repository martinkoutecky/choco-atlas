The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is an extension of Proposition 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that when every agent has symmetric single-peaked preferences, there is a matching rule that, paired with *any* efficient single-estate rule, produces a Pareto-efficient multi-estate allocation. The rule chooses a partition minimizing the sum, over estates, of the absolute differences between each estate’s endowment and the assigned agents’ total peaks.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large organization allocating workload or capacity across isolated projects or facilities. A type is a complete single-peaked preference relation; in the symmetric case, its rational peak \(p_t\) suffices. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu_t\) over finitely many such types. Individuals still go to one estate, while a type’s mass may be divided among estates. Let \(b_k\) be estate \(k\)’s resource supply per unit of total population, and \(x_{tk}\) the mass of type \(t\) assigned there. A continuous version of the proposition’s matching step is:

\[ \min_x \sum_k \left|b_k-\sum_t x_{tk}p_t\right| \]

subject to \(\sum_k x_{tk}=\mu_t\), \(x_{tk}\ge 0\), and enough assigned mass at each estate to receive its supply. For positive \(b_k\), that last condition is \(\sum_t x_{tk}\ge b_k\). The solution is a mass assignment; applying an efficient continuum extension of the chosen single-estate rule within each estate gives the allocation. In the symmetric-preference regime, this is an LP after introducing standard variables for the absolute values. The expected direction is tractable. The underlying efficiency argument is the paper’s: with symmetric preferences, Pareto improvements reduce total distance from peaks, contradicting the minimizing assignment.

This is a credible population mirror of the paper’s estate-assignment question: it preserves the split between matching agents to estates and allocating each estate’s resource, while making the population the continuous object. It is narrow, though. Theorem 3.1, proved here, rules out an efficient composition for every efficient, nondictatorial, strategy-proof, resource-monotonic and consistent single-estate rule. Theorem 3.2, also proved here, gives a no-go result for efficient, peak-only rules satisfying nonaccommodation of extremes. Those results motivate asking whether the no-go survives mass assignment, but neither states a computational complexity result.

That distinction is decisive under the requested anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or another complexity classification. Its numbered results concern existence and impossibility of Pareto-efficient rules; “efficient” means Pareto-efficient here, not polynomial-time computable. So the LP above is a concrete, plausible *new* computational mirror suggested by Proposition 3.1, but it is not a qualifying anchor from the paper, and I cannot honestly present it as one.

The weak point is that the authors study axiomatic rule design over finite societies, not algorithms for choosing an allocation. The most promising mirror also covers only symmetric preferences and needs a continuum single-estate rule to be specified. It makes a plausible case that their matching problem can inspire a ChoCo question, but it does not meet the paper-anchor requirement for this assignment.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper states no computational result. “Efficient” means Pareto efficient, not efficiently computable. Proposition 3.1 proves that a matching rule exists; Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 prove that certain rule compositions cannot achieve Pareto efficiency. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

Proposition 3.1 is the strongest candidate, and its proof gives a natural computational question: minimize \(\sum_k |b_k-\sum_t x_{tk}p_t|\) over mass assignments. In a large workforce divided among projects, with per-capita supplies, that is a coherent continuous model and a plausible LP. I would not dismiss it on grounds of multiplicity or scaling.

But the LP is a new algorithmic problem suggested by the proof, not a computational result mirrored from the paper. The proposition concerns an axiomatically efficient rule that works for every efficient single-estate rule \(f\). The LP computes one particular matching; it neither specifies how to compute \(f\) nor answers whether a rule with the paper’s desired properties exists. Turning Theorems 3.1 or 3.2 into complexity questions would likewise add a computational task to their axiomatic impossibility claims.

So the case against is strong only under the requested anchor standard: none of the paper’s named results supplies a computational result to mirror. It is much weaker as the universal claim that the paper cannot inspire worthwhile continuous computation. The symmetric-preference LP is a credible new research direction; I would call it inspired by this paper, not a mirror of one of its computational results.
