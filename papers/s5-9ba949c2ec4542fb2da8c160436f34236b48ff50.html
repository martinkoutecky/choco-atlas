The continuous mirror question

Given a finite agenda \(\mathcal A\) and a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of complete reflexive pairwise relations with positive rational masses \(\mu_R\) summing to \(1\), find an ordered partition \((X,Y)\) minimizing \(\sum_{R\in\mathcal T}\mu_R\left|\{(a,b)\in\mathcal A^2:\mathbf 1[(a,b)\in R]\ne\mathbf 1[(a,b)\in O_{X,Y}]\}\right|\), where \(O_{X,Y}\) is the DWO with \(X\) ranked above \(Y\) and ties within each block.

The model it lives in

Represent each relation \(R\in\mathcal T\) as a type carrying mass \(\mu_R\). Compute the mass-weighted pairwise quantities \(N_\mu(a,b)\) and \(E_\mu(a,b)\), then use the paper's min-cut construction with rational capacities and a sufficiently large finite capacity to enforce its linking arcs; return the cut's ordered partition and its expected disagreement. This takes time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's two-tier median optimization on complete pairwise relation types. It leaves incomplete reports and extensions to three or more tiers outside the stated mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the min-cut encoding extend to outputs with three or more tiers?
  • Can it handle types whose reports leave some pairs incomparable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead is a continuous two-tier median problem. Fix an agenda \(\mathcal A\) of \(m\) alternatives. An instance is a support of \(\tau\) complete, reflexive pairwise preference relations \(R\) on \(\mathcal A\), each with a rational mass \(\mu_R>0\), where \(\sum_R\mu_R=1\). “Complete” means each pair is either strictly ordered one way or tied; the relation need not be transitive. A solution is an ordered partition \((X,Y)\) of \(\mathcal A\), with \(X\) ranked above \(Y\) and alternatives within each block tied. It must minimize

\[ \operatorname{Med}^{(2)}_\infty(\mu) = \min_{(X,Y)} \sum_R \mu_R \left|\left\{(a,b)\in\mathcal A^2: \mathbf 1[(a,b)\in R]\ne \mathbf 1[(a,b)\in O_{X,Y}] \right\}\right|. \]

The answer is the minimizing partition and its expected number of ordered-pair disagreements per person. The output remains a dichotomous weak order; it is the population and the objective’s weights that are continuous.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that a minimum \(s\)-\(t\) cut in the constructed network gives a DWO of minimum disagreement, and conversely that every minimum-disagreement DWO lifts to a minimum cut: put \(s\), the alternatives in its first block, and the nodes \(v_{(x,y)}\) for alternatives \(x\) in the first block and \(y\) in the second on the source side; put the remaining nodes on the other side. Since minimum cut is polynomial-time solvable, this establishes the paper’s polynomial-time result. That is the full named result I rely on; the NP-hardness remarks in the introduction concern other input/output classes and are cited background, not additional numbered anchors.

The mirror is especially direct here. In the theorem’s network, replace each voter count \(N(a,b)\) and \(E(a,b)\) by its mass-weighted counterpart, for example
\[ N_\mu(a,b)=\sum_R\mu_R\mathbf 1[(a,b)\in R,\ (b,a)\notin R]. \]
For complete relations, strict preference in either direction and indifference exhaust the possibilities for each pair. The cut costs therefore represent the same expected disagreement objective. The resulting graph has \(O(m^2)\) nodes and arcs; its rational capacities can be handled exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. This gives a Class A, tractable mirror by the paper’s own cut structure.

A plausible regime is a large consultation on a fixed set of policy options where each participant reports a comparison or tie for every pair. Many participants can submit the same judgment relation, so the number \(n\) of participants may greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of distinct relations. A type is the participant’s full pairwise judgment; mass is the fraction of respondents with that judgment. Because this problem uses only the relation and its disagreements with the proposed output, participants of the same type are interchangeable. The authors should recognize the mirror: it keeps their alternatives, reports, DWO output, and disagreement objective, replacing a sum over respondents with the population average.

For a discrete electorate with \(n_R\) respondents of each type, set \(\mu_R=n_R/n\). The continuous optimum is exactly the discrete minimum disagreement divided by \(n\). Conversely, any rational instance can be realized by replicating respondents according to a common denominator. Thus the mirror supplies a clean high-multiplicity representation and an exact dictionary, though it does not claim a new complexity separation.

The scope is just Theorem 1 and the two-tier median problem. The weakest point is that this is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result whose difficulty might dissolve under continuization; the continuous algorithm is a rational-capacity version of the paper’s min-cut algorithm. Also, the paper states its input domain as reflexive relations, while its displayed \(N/E\) accounting distinguishes strict and indifferent comparisons. I have therefore specified the mirror for complete pairwise judgments, where those cases exhaust each pair; extending the cut argument to reports with missing comparisons would need separate justification. Nearby questions include whether the cut structure extends to outputs with three or more tiers, and what happens to the median problem when participants may leave pairs incomparable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat Theorem 1 as an anchor. It is a named computational result, and its input permits a credible high-multiplicity regime: a large consultation on a fixed agenda where many people submit the same complete pairwise judgment. A person’s identity is irrelevant once their judgment type is known.

The strongest case against is that this mirror appears to add little algorithmically. The cut uses only pairwise aggregate counts, so a continuous instance can replace them with pairwise masses and run the same minimum-cut construction. The type support disappears after those aggregates are computed. Since every rational mass vector can also be realized by replicating types according to a common denominator, the mirror has an exact discrete counterpart.

That makes this a low-yield candidate for new algorithm design. It does not, under the programme’s stated standard, make it an unworthy mirror: high multiplicity is precisely the regime at issue, and the exact dictionary is a programme deliverable. The paper’s reflexive-relation wording also leaves a question about incomplete reports, but restricting the mirror to complete pairwise judgments is a defensible version of the problem and avoids that issue.

So the negative case here is weak. I can argue for lower priority because the continuous result follows directly from the paper’s cut formulation; I cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
