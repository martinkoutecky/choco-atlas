The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses μ, linear valuations v_t, per-agent budgets B_t, per-capita supplies q, and ε > 0, compute a feasible type-level allocation x and prices p forming an approximate Fisher equilibrium, with |v_t · x_t - u_t★| ≤ ε for every type t with μ_t > 0.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the linear-utility Eisenberg–Gale and projected-gradient results in Corollary 1 and Theorem 5. It leaves the quasilinear, Leontief, proportional-response, general first-order, and experimental results unexamined.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a weighted-norm analysis give convergence and bit-complexity bounds polynomial in m, τ, input length, and log(1/ε), without dependence on μ_min?
  • Do the quasilinear and Leontief results admit analogous high-multiplicity formulations with useful guarantees?
  • How can the continuous equilibrium be rounded to a finite population of size N while preserving utility and market-clearing guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous-population version of the paper’s linear-utility Fisher market, anchored on Corollary 1. The corollary is proved in this paper; it is derived from the paper’s Theorem 5 rather than cited from elsewhere. The paper contains no named hardness result, so this is necessarily a Class A case rather than a test of whether discrete hardness dissolves.

Consider a very large fair-recommender or advertising market. There are \(N\) users or advertisers, but only \(\tau\) exact types, where a type records everything relevant to the market: its valuation vector, budget, eligibility, and any other utility parameters. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A realistic regime might have millions of agents but only hundreds or thousands of stable preference or campaign types. This is precisely a high-multiplicity regime: agents of one type are interchangeable for the market problem.

There are \(m\) divisible goods, with per-capita supply \(q_j\) of good \(j\). Type \(t\) has linear utility \(u_t(x_t)=v_t\cdot x_t\) and per-agent budget \(B_t\). The action variable is a per-agent allocation \(x_t\in\mathbb R_+^m\), while prices \(p\in\mathbb R_+^m\) are the dual market-clearing variables. Feasibility is

\(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le q_j\) for every good \(j\).

A continuous Fisher equilibrium requires each \(x_t\) to maximize \(v_t\cdot y\) subject to \(p\cdot y\le B_t\), and requires market clearance, with equality for every good having positive price. Equivalently, the equilibrium allocation solves the weighted Eisenberg–Gale program

\(\max_{x\ge0}\sum_t\mu_t B_t\log(v_t\cdot x_t)\)

subject to the supply constraints above.

This is a population continuization, not an outcome-space continuization. Goods remain divisible exactly as in the paper; the new continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\). The per-capita supply \(q_j\) is the natural co-modification when the number of agents grows.

The precise mirror question is:

Continuous Linear-Fisher Equilibrium Approximation. Given rational \(\mu\), \(V=(v_{tj})\), budgets \(B\), per-capita supplies \(q\), and \(\epsilon>0\), compute a feasible type-level allocation \(x\) whose utility for every positive-mass type satisfies

\(\lvert v_t\cdot x_t-u_t^\star\rvert\le\epsilon\),

where \(u_t^\star\) is that type’s unique equilibrium utility. An associated price vector may also be returned, but the allocation-and-utility guarantee is the one used by the paper’s corollary.

This is not a weakened surrogate. It is the same Fisher equilibrium problem, with indistinguishable buyers represented by mass rather than listed individually. If \(\mu_t=N_t/N\), expanding each type into \(N_t\) copies recovers the corresponding finite market. Conversely, the continuous instance is the high-multiplicity representation of that finite market.

The anchor is Corollary 1: for linear utilities, projected gradient computes a feasible allocation \(x^t\) satisfying \(\lvert\langle v_i,x_i^t\rangle-u_i^\star\rvert\le\epsilon\) for every buyer in \(t=O(\widetilde n\log\widetilde n\log(1/\epsilon))\) time, where \(\widetilde n=\operatorname{nnz}(v)\), and the hidden constant depends only on the valuations and budgets. The underlying Theorem 5, also proved here, establishes linear convergence of projected gradient for the modified Eisenberg–Gale program, with rate \(1-\mu_{\mathrm{sc}}/\max\{\mu_{\mathrm{sc}},L H_X(A)^2\lVert A\rVert^2\}\).

The same result applies directly to the type formulation. Set \(z_{tj}=\mu_t x_{tj}/q_j\). Each good then has its allocation represented by a simplex vector, and the problem becomes the paper’s finite-dimensional form with \(\tau\) rows, transformed valuations \(\widehat v_{tj}=q_jv_{tj}/\mu_t\), and transformed buyer weights \(\widehat B_t=\mu_tB_t\). Thus the first-order method operates on \(\tau\) types, not \(N\) named agents. In sparse form, its per-iteration work depends on the nonzero type–good incidences, and the inherited approximation guarantee is polynomial in the type representation and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), with no explicit dependence on population size \(N\).

I therefore expect this mirror to be tractable, Class A. The tractability is structurally meaningful: the population aggregation produces a weighted convex program whose feasible set remains a product of simplexes after rescaling, and the paper’s projected-gradient analysis survives. The result is not merely that an already-continuous outcome can be optimized; it is a computational statement about a society represented by a distribution over buyer types.

The main weakness is that this mirror may look like a weighted rewriting or compression of the paper’s finite market rather than a new problem. The paper already emphasizes large \(n\), sparse valuations, and scalable algorithms, so the continuous algorithm may be almost immediate once the type grouping is written down. Moreover, if the number of distinct types grows proportionally with \(N\), the mirror gives little benefit. The case depends on accepting a genuine high-multiplicity regime—millions of exchangeable agents over a much smaller type set—as a sensible instance class. In the recommender, advertising, and resource-sharing applications explicitly named by the authors, that regime is plausible, and the paper’s equilibrium question remains unchanged.

The natural follow-up questions are whether the same population formulation gives linear or sublinear guarantees for the paper’s Leontief and quasilinear results, how the constants depend on small type masses, whether exact rational equilibria can be represented efficiently, and how a continuous solution can be rounded when the finite population is large but not literally infinite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror is not a new continuous-population problem. It is exactly a finite weighted Fisher market already covered by the paper.

For every positive-mass type \(t\), define

\[ z_{tj}=\frac{\mu_t x_{tj}}{q_j},\qquad \widetilde v_{tj}=\frac{q_jv_{tj}}{\mu_t},\qquad \widetilde B_t=\mu_tB_t. \]

Then \(\sum_t z_{tj}\le 1\), and

\[ \sum_t\mu_tB_t\log(v_t\cdot x_t) = \sum_t\widetilde B_t\log(\widetilde v_t\cdot z_t). \]

Thus the alleged continuous problem is exactly the paper’s finite Eisenberg–Gale program with one buyer for each type, transformed valuations, and transformed budgets. This is an algebraic identification before taking any limit. The population measure is only a coefficient in the objective and feasibility constraints; it is not an optimization variable. Grouping repeated buyers is useful preprocessing, but it does not expose a new computational object, a separation problem, or a genuinely continuum-specific phenomenon.

This defeats both anchors. Corollary 1 already applies to the compressed instance with \(n=\tau\). Theorem 5 is likewise inherited verbatim after the above rescaling. Calling the resulting rows “types” rather than “buyers” does not create a new theorem. The paper is specifically about making finite Fisher computation scalable, so replacing repeated rows by weighted rows is especially hard to present as a missing continuous-computation problem.

The proponent also overstates the complexity consequence. Corollary 1 gives an iteration bound whose hidden constant depends on the valuations and budgets; it is not a uniform \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,\log(1/\epsilon))\) bit-complexity theorem. Under the proposed transformation,

\[ \frac{\widetilde B_t}{\|\widetilde v_t\|_1^2} = \frac{\mu_t^3 B_t}{\left(\sum_jq_jv_{tj}\right)^2}, \]

while the smoothness and operator-norm terms also deteriorate as \(\mu_t\) becomes small. Hence the claimed absence of dependence on population size is true only for the raw sparse arithmetic. The convergence constants can depend strongly on the smallest type mass, and if \(\mu_t=1/N\), that dependence can reintroduce \(N\) despite \(N\) having only logarithmic encoding length. A weighted-norm analysis might repair this, but that would be a new weighted-Fisher theorem, not an application of Corollary 1 or Theorem 5.

The best improved proposal would use an atomless distribution over valuation and budget vectors rather than finitely many types. That would evade the exact finite reduction, but it creates a different problem. A general distribution has no finite input representation. Finite mixtures collapse back to the weighted market above; oracle or succinct density representations make integration and distribution encoding—not population continuization—the main computational issue. Such a project could be worthwhile, but it is no longer a clean mirror of the paper’s named results.

So the negative case is strong against the submitted anchor: it is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but merely a compressed representation of the same finite convex program, with no new population-specific computation and no uniform complexity guarantee inherited from the paper. The universal claim “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” is weaker: a genuinely atomless valuation distribution could support a new paper, though not one justified by Corollary 1 as stated.
