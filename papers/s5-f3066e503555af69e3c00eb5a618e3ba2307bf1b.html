The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), distinguished candidate \(p\), committee size \(k\), deletion budget \(\ell\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(t\subseteq C\), does there exist \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{p\}\) with \(|D|\leq\ell\) such that \(p\) belongs to no maximizing \(k\)-committee in \(S=C\setminus D\) under \(\operatorname{SAV}_{\mu,S}(W)=\sum_{t:\,t\cap S\neq\varnothing}\mu_t\frac{|W\cap t|}{|t\cap S|}\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity SAV control model with approval-set types \(t\), population masses \(\mu_t\), integral candidate deletions \(D\), and integral committees \(W\); the objective is to maximize normalized SAV over \(k\)-committees after deletion and ask whether deletion can exclude \(p\) from every maximizer.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 19's SAV hardness, and analogous mirrors cover Theorem 20 and the ABCCV clause of Theorem 5. It leaves the paper's other control variants, axiomatic results, and parameterized bounds without continuous formulations.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact parameterized complexity of \(\operatorname{DCDC\text{-}SAV}_{\infty}\) beyond the inherited \({\sf W[2]}\)-hardness?
  • Can the type-mass formulation yield high-multiplicity compression or approximation results despite the Class B hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible continuous mirror, although it is primarily a Class B boundary result rather than a tractability result.

The common mirror is this. Fix a candidate universe \(C\). A voter type is an approval set \(t\subseteq C\), and the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\). Thus \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with exactly approval set \(t\), and \(\tau=|\mathcal T|\) may be much smaller than the population size \(n\). Committees and candidate-control actions remain integral: only the population becomes continuous.

This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for a large organization, public consultation, or participatory-budgeting body choosing \(k\) projects. Many citizens may share one of a relatively small number of standardized approval profiles, while the chair can add or delete candidate projects. The paper’s vote multisets already contain large repeated blocks; for example, Theorem 19 uses \(\Theta(|R|^2)\) voters but only \(O(|R|)\) distinct approval types. Replication can make the population arbitrarily larger without changing \(\mu\).

The continuous SAV score of a committee \(W\subseteq S\), where \(S\) is the currently available candidate set, is

\[ \operatorname{SAV}_{\mu,S}(W) = \sum_{t\in\mathcal T:\,t\cap S\neq\varnothing} \mu_t\frac{|W\cap t|}{|t\cap S|}. \]

For a finite election with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) divides every SAV committee score by \(n\), for every candidate set \(S\). Hence winners, ties, and control solutions are preserved exactly.

My lead anchor is Theorem 19, proved in this paper: DCDC for SAV and NSAV is \({\sf W[2]}\)-hard parameterized by \(\ell\), the number of deleted candidates, even with one distinguished candidate. I use its SAV clause.

Call the mirror \( \operatorname{DCDC\text{-}SAV}_{\infty}\). An instance consists of \(C\), a distinguished candidate \(p\in C\), committee size \(k\), deletion budget \(\ell\), and \(\mu\). The question is whether there exists \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{p\}\) with \(|D|\leq \ell\) such that \(p\) belongs to no maximum-SAV \(k\)-committee in \(C\setminus D\). Equivalently, after deletion, at least \(k\) candidates have strictly larger SAV singleton score than \(p\).

The authors should recognize this immediately: it is exactly their destructive control problem, with the finite voter multiset replaced by its population distribution. The reduction from Red-Blue Dominating Set survives by normalizing all vote multiplicities. Its combinatorics lies in selecting candidates to delete, not in distinguishing individual voters, so the expected classification is Class B: \({\sf W[2]}\)-hard parameterized by \(\ell\). This is a meaningful negative boundary for continuization: a large repeated electorate does not make candidate deletion under SAV easy.

A second strong anchor is Theorem 20, also proved here: for every \(\omega\)-Thiele rule satisfying \(\omega(2)<2\omega(1)\), DCDC for the sequential rule \(\operatorname{seq}\varphi\) is \({\sf W[1]}\)-hard parameterized by \(m-\ell\), the number of candidates not deleted, even with one distinguished candidate. This includes \(\operatorname{seqPAV}\) and \(\operatorname{seqABCCV}\).

The mirror \( \operatorname{DCDC\text{-}seq}\omega_{\infty}\) uses the same instance format, but after deletion the committee is built sequentially. If \(S\) is the current partial committee, the marginal contribution of an available candidate \(c\) is

\[ \Delta_{\omega,\mu}(c\mid S) = \sum_{t\in\mathcal T:\,c\in t} \mu_t\,\bigl(\omega(|t\cap S|+1)-\omega(|t\cap S|)\bigr), \]

with the paper’s fixed tie-breaking order. The solution is a deletion set \(D\) of size at most \(\ell\) such that \(p\) is not selected among the first \(k\) sequential choices.

Again, every marginal contribution in a finite election is merely multiplied by \(1/n\), so the sequence and all tie comparisons are unchanged. The Regular Set Packing reduction therefore transfers exactly. This is still Class B, with \({\sf W[1]}\)-hardness parameterized by \(m-\ell\). It is a useful independent anchor because the mirror preserves the paper’s genuinely sequential, non-additive decision process rather than replacing it by ordinary committee scoring.

A third anchor is the ABCCV part of Theorem 5, proved here: Nonwinning Testing for ABCCV is \({\sf W[1]}\)-hard parameterized by \(k\), even when every vote approves at most two candidates.

The continuous problem \( \operatorname{Nonwinning\text{-}ABCCV}_{\infty}\) takes \(C\), \(k\), \(p\), and \(\mu\), and asks whether \(p\) is contained in no maximum-ABCCV \(k\)-committee. Its score is

\[ \operatorname{ABCCV}_{\mu}(W) = \sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[t\cap W\neq\varnothing]. \]

A yes-instance is therefore one for which the best committee excluding \(p\) has strictly greater score than the best committee including \(p\). The restriction \(|t|\leq 2\) is preserved. The reduction from Independent Set on regular graphs transfers under normalization, so the expected class is again Class B and the problem remains \({\sf W[1]}\)-hard in \(k\).

These three mirrors cover a coherent part of the paper: winner robustness, destructive candidate control under an additive rule, and destructive candidate control under sequential Thiele rules. They do not claim a mirror for adding or deleting voter mass; those variants need separate care because an atomless amount of added mass can make “one more approval” arbitrarily cheap.

The weakest point is also clear: in all three anchors, population mass is mainly a coefficient, not an optimization variable. The continuous versions therefore inherit hardness by exact normalization rather than producing a new LP or a continuum-specific algorithm. That makes the scientific payoff modest. But it is still a faithful and author-recognizable high-multiplicity programme: the society is genuinely represented by type masses, the committees and candidate actions remain integral, and the results establish a clean Class B boundary showing where population continuization does not dissolve computational hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that all three proposed mirrors are exact coefficient lifts, not new continuous-computational phenomena. In SAV, ABCCV, and sequential Thiele rules, replacing \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) by \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) merely divides every committee score and every sequential marginal by \(n\). Thus the winner set, tie-breaking, and control solution are unchanged. The reductions’ combinatorics live entirely in candidate selection and committee formation; population mass is only a coefficient. No mass-transfer LP, pricing problem, approximation issue, or continuum-specific obstruction appears.

That criticism applies to Theorem 19. Its SAV reduction can be replicated arbitrarily many times, so it is not legitimate to object that the electorate lacks multiplicity. But replication leaves the normalized distribution and the entire control problem unchanged. The proposed \(\operatorname{DCDC\text{-}SAV}_{\infty}\) is therefore just the same candidate-deletion problem in a compressed encoding. The theorem establishes a Class B boundary, but contributes no insight that depends on a continuous society.

The same is true, even more transparently, of Theorem 20. With approval sets as types,

\[ \Delta_{\omega,\mu}(c\mid W) = \sum_t \mu_t\,\bigl(\omega(|t\cap(W\cup\{c\})|) -\omega(|t\cap W|)\bigr) \]

is exactly \(1/n\) times the finite marginal contribution. The sequential order is therefore identical. Replicating every voter block creates arbitrarily high multiplicity without changing anything. The result is a faithful high-multiplicity restatement of the paper’s candidate-deletion problem, but not a continuous optimization result.

Theorem 5 has the same defect. ABCCV becomes expected coverage,

\[ \operatorname{ABCCV}_{\mu}(W) = \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf 1[t\cap W\neq\varnothing], \]

which is again precisely the finite score divided by \(n\). Although the edge-voter types can be replicated to create a genuine large population, the Independent Set encoding remains entirely in the candidate universe and approval-type incidence pattern. The population continuum does no mathematical work.

This is the best available negative argument: these mirrors are formally sound but scientifically thin. They neither exploit fractional mass as a decision variable nor expose a new algorithmic boundary. If ChoCo required a continuous formulation to change the computational object rather than merely compress repeated voters, all three would be poor candidates.

But under the programme’s stated standard, that is not enough to defeat them. The programme explicitly accepts Class B mirrors, treats high-multiplicity work as supporting evidence, and says that an already-known answer does not invalidate a well-posed continuous question. Approval-set types are complete agent types; candidate deletion remains a legitimate integral control operation; and all three problems admit sensible high-multiplicity societies. Nor is this “already done continuously”: the paper itself never formulates the distributional problems.

So the honest conclusion is that the universal negative case fails. The proposed mirrors are not especially rich, and the first two are almost purely normalization results, but Theorem 19 alone survives the objections and supplies an author-recognizable continuous-population problem. A negative verdict would require an additional criterion—such as demanding population mass to be an optimization variable or requiring a continuum-specific algorithmic effect—that the programme expressly does not impose.
