The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a fully compliant anchor-based case from this paper: it has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification. The closest numbered results are convergence and structural properties. The computational claims are in the unnumbered Section IV.B: it gives RBS complexity \(O(JK^6N^6)\), Talmud-rule complexity \(O(K^6N^5)\), and says the procedures solve sequences of LPs. Those claims offer a real positive lead, but they cannot supply the numbered anchors your instructions require.

The strongest candidate I see is a replication-limit Talmud bargaining problem. Its input is a finite set of types \(T\), with \(\tau=|T|\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-person utility vectors \(u_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^K\), disagreement utilities \(d_t\), and per-capita asset supplies \(e_k>0\). For each integer \(q\) clearing the mass denominators, form a paper-model instance with \(q\mu_t\) players of type \(t\), and total supply \(qe_k\) of asset \(k\). This supply scaling is the per-capita co-modification: each member of a large population faces the same resource supply per person. Let \(X^{(q)}_{tk}\) be the average amount of asset \(k\) allocated to a type-\(t\) member by the paper’s Talmud bargaining solution.

The problem is to determine whether \(X^{(q)}\) converges as \(q\) grows and, if it does, compute its limit \(x\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tk}=e_k\). A nonconvergence answer would identify distinct accumulation points. This preserves the paper’s underlying allocation question and its Talmud solution at every finite replication; the continuous question is whether those solutions have a well-defined limit and how hard that limit is to compute.

A plausible regime is a large bankruptcy or compensation pool with many claimants, grouped by complete type: the same utility vector, disagreement position, claims, and other model parameters. Here \(q\) can be large while \(\tau\) stays moderate. The authors have already extended a bankruptcy rule to multiple assets and discuss a water-filling procedure with LP feasibility checks, so this population interpretation seems recognizably connected to their problem. I would expect Class A if the replication limit can be reduced to a mass-weighted water-filling rule and a finite collection of LPs over the \(\tau\) types. That is a conjecture for this mirror, not a consequence of the paper’s stated complexity bounds. The central further questions are whether the limit always exists, whether it equals a direct mass-weighted extension of their rule, and whether that extension is polynomial in \(K,\tau\), and the input bit length.

This candidate covers only the Talmud-rule computation, not the paper’s full bargaining framework. Its weakest point is that the paper’s rules bargain among finite players, and its complexity bounds do not analyze replication limits or mass-weighted type populations. The proposed limit question is concrete and plausibly theirs, but because the relevant complexity claims are unnumbered, it remains a positive case without the required numbered anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against this case is that it supplies no eligible named computational result to mirror. The numbered results concern Pareto convergence, contraction, and axiomatic properties; none establishes a complexity bound or classification. The running times in Section IV.B are unnumbered claims. In particular, the RBS bound contains the iteration count \(J\), while the procedure stops at a fixed tolerance, so the paper does not establish an exact bit-complexity result for computing RBS. The proposed replication limit therefore has no numbered computational result as its anchor.

The candidate also asks more than the paper defines. Its \(X^{(q)}\) records average asset allocations, but the rules can select a utility vector without uniquely selecting the bundles that realize it. One can repair that by asking for the limit of type-level utility awards instead. But this is then a new replication-consistency question: make \(q\) copies, scale supplies, apply a finite-player rule, and study the limit. A direct mass-weighted Talmud rule would be a better-defined alternative, but it too is an extension rather than a limit result established in the paper. The paper’s water-filling and LP methods suggest that extension may be quite direct; that weakens the case for a new complexity landscape, though an easy answer alone would not make the question unworthy.

The best substantive objection is that these are bargaining rules among principals, where fairness is defined across players. A large pool of claimants with repeated utility and disagreement types is plausible, but applying the rule to that pool changes the unit of fairness from named players to mass. The paper gives no indication that this is a computational problem its authors would recognize as central. Still, the paper’s allocation model and LP methods make the population version coherent, and nothing in the text rules out a meaningful claimant-pool regime.

So the negative case is strongest on the missing numbered complexity anchor and weaker on the modelling question. I can defeat the proponent’s submitted anchor as an anchor under the task’s standard, but I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A type-mass Talmud question remains plausible.
