The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) of complete participant types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), nonatomic valuation oracles \(v_t\), and one unit cake for each repeated local division, determine the minimum worst-case number of type-level marking/value queries needed to output connected pieces and an assignment that is exactly envy-free in every local division, and decide whether a finite guarantee exists; in particular, characterize \(T=\{A,B,C\}\) with \(\mu_A=\mu_B=\mu_C=1/3\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity batch of identical three-role cake divisions with \(T=\{A,B,C\}\), \(\mu_A=\mu_B=\mu_C=1/3\), valuation measures \(v_A,v_B,v_C\), one local cake per trio, reusable type-level oracle queries, and exact connected-piece envy-freeness as the objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow one: Stromquist’s paper supplies a computational impossibility anchor, but not an NP-hardness or polynomial-time anchor.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that no finite protocol can reliably find an envy-free division of a cake among three or more players when every player must receive one connected interval. “Finite” includes protocols whose running time is not bounded in advance, provided every execution terminates after finitely many marking queries. The impossibility remains true even when the players’ measures are promised to form a rigid measure system and the protocol may use that promise. The proof uses Lemma 2, which forces the unique correct cut locations in a rigid system, and Lemma 3, which lets an adversary alter the measures near those locations without changing any information revealed by the protocol so far.

My mirror would be Minimum-Query Continuous Connected Envy-Freeness, with the paper’s finite-protocol impossibility as its lead question.

A society consists of a fixed finite set of complete participant types. A type includes a role in a local division and a nonatomic valuation measure \(v_t\) on \([0,1]\), with strictly positive density as in Stromquist’s restricted class. The society is described by rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of a large population having that complete description. Thus, for a denominator \(N\), the instance represents \(N\mu_t\) identical participants of type \(t\). The intended regime is \(N\gg |T|\): for example, a large organisation repeatedly divides a contiguous resource among three departments, with many branches or committees sharing the same departmental valuation types.

The resource is scaled per capita by using one unit cake for each local division. This is important: a single fixed cake cannot give positive-length connected pieces to an atomless population. The continuous object is therefore the population of repeated, type-identical divisions, while each local division retains Stromquist’s original object: one cake, connected pieces, and exact envy-freeness.

The protocol has honest marking-query and value-query access to the valuation oracles. Its output must be, for every positive-mass local type profile, finitely many marks, an assignment of the resulting intervals, and a guarantee that every participant receives a piece they value at least as highly as every other assigned piece. The optimization version asks for the minimum worst-case number of type-level queries; the decision version asks whether this number is finite. Because identical participants share a type, one query can be reused across all \(N\mu_t\) copies.

The expected answer is negative: \(Q^*(I)=\infty\) whenever the support contains a three-role rigid measure system with positive mass. Taking three types of mass \(1/3\), with valuations \(v_A,v_B,v_C\) from Stromquist’s rigid construction, gives exactly the paper’s adversarial instance. No finite protocol can identify the required cuts \(x\) and \(y\), even though the population contains arbitrarily many repeated agents and only three distinct types.

This is plausibly the authors’ problem rather than a softened substitute. The mirror preserves the exact connected-piece requirement, the exact envy-free condition, arbitrary nonatomic valuation measures, honest oracle access, and the finite-termination question. It does not replace the problem by average welfare or fractional cake allocation. The authors would likely recognise it as their protocol problem deployed in the natural high-multiplicity regime of repeated homogeneous divisions.

I would classify the result as continuum-specific impossibility, or more precisely as an information-theoretic boundary rather than ordinary NP-hardness. It is not Class B: no discrete hardness is being transferred. The obstruction is exact recovery of real cut locations from finitely many oracle interactions, and it persists even with only three population types. The interesting follow-up is whether the obstruction disappears under approximation: define an \(\varepsilon\)-envy-free version, impose bounded densities, and study \(Q_\varepsilon(m,\tau,L)\), together with finite-population rounding and the effect of requiring success on only \(1-\delta\) population mass.

The weakest point is substantial. The population multiplicity does not cause Stromquist’s impossibility; the same obstruction already exists for three players. Moreover, the repeated-cake construction is a careful resource-scaling repair rather than the literal atomless limit of one cake. If “computational result” is restricted strictly to classifications such as NP-hardness, membership in P, or parameterized complexity, then this paper has no qualifying anchor. Under the broader query-complexity reading, however, Theorem 1 supports a precise and defensible continuous-population mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s mirror fails because the population is doing no computational work. Theorem 1 is a genuine named query-impossibility result, so the objection cannot be that the paper has no computational result. But Stromquist’s obstruction already occurs with three valuation oracles. Replacing each of those three players by a positive-mass class of identical copies leaves the problem unchanged.

In the proposed model, the envy-free constraints for types \(A,B,C\) are still

\[ v_t(S_t)\ge v_t(S_{t'}) \]

for every positive-mass type \(t\). The values of \(\mu_t\) never appear. Taking masses \(1/3\), \(1/100\), or \(0.999\) changes nothing; only the support matters. One query to a type can be reused by all its copies, but that is merely an optimization of the original three-player protocol. The theorem’s hard instance is not hard because a population must be compressed or manipulated. It is hard because an arbitrary real-valued measure cannot be identified from finitely many oracle answers.

The proposed “many repeated local divisions” does not repair this. It creates many independent cakes, each containing the same three-player problem. The resulting society is a batch of parallel instances rather than one continuous population sharing a resource. Scaling the cake supply per capita is therefore replication, not a population continuization that creates a new optimization or complexity landscape.

The more natural common-cake version fares worse. With one cake and an atomless population, finitely many cuts produce only finitely many positive-length intervals. An uncountable population cannot each receive a distinct positive-value connected piece. If members of a type share one interval, all members of that type are represented by a single player and the model collapses back to Stromquist’s finite-player problem. If the model permits fractional or probabilistic sharing, it has changed the allocation object toward divisible or randomized outcomes, precisely the sort of outcome-space continuization outside the programme’s target.

There is also a representation dilemma. If valuation types remain arbitrary nonatomic measures accessed by marking and value queries, the impossibility survives, but the type masses are irrelevant and the input is an infinite-dimensional oracle. If valuations are given by finite descriptions—say, rational piecewise-constant densities—then the adversarial indistinguishability argument of Lemma 3 no longer applies: the algorithm has a description from which the relevant cuts may be computed. That could yield a new finite-input complexity problem, but it would not be a continuous-population consequence of Theorem 1. Conversely, allowing a distribution over arbitrary valuation functions requires an infinite type space, abandoning the fixed finite \(T\) high-multiplicity model.

Approximate envy-freeness or success for all but a small population mass may be worthwhile fair-division questions, but they are not mirrors of the paper’s theorem. The paper proves exact recovery of particular cut locations under unrestricted oracle access; it supplies no approximation or mass-loss result to anchor those variants.

Thus the proponent has shown a formally defensible relabelling: repeated homogeneous committees can carry Stromquist’s oracle problem. But the continuous population is either decorative, the common-cake limit is degenerate, or the finite-description model removes the paper’s obstruction. The honest negative verdict is therefore strong, though not absolute: under an unusually broad notion of “mirror,” one could study the repeated-division query model. It is not, however, a worthwhile ChoCo continuization of this paper’s computational content.
