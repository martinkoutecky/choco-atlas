The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), compatible type pairs \(\Gamma\), and rational joint surpluses \(v_{tu}\ge 0\), determine and output a stable partnership allocation and payoff if one exists. Choose partnership masses \(x_{tu}\ge 0\), singleton masses \(s_t\ge 0\), and type payoffs \(p_t\ge 0\), with \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t:\{t,u\}\in\Gamma}x_{tu}+s_t=\mu_t\), \(p_t+p_u\ge v_{tu}\) for every compatible pair, \(x_{tu}(p_t+p_u-v_{tu})=0\), and \(s_tp_t=0\); for same-type pairs require \(2p_t\ge v_{tt}\).

The model it lives in

A continuum population with finitely many complete types, where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\); partnership flows pair individual units one-to-one, including \(2x_{tt}\) units used by same-type partnerships. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{\{t,u\}\in\Gamma}v_{tu}x_{tu}\), with singleton mass consuming remaining capacity, and \(p_t\) are dual payoffs.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers equilibrium existence and construction for type-mass populations. It leaves aside the paper’s price-adjustment trajectory and its iteration behavior.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can every rational type-mass equilibrium be implemented by a finite clone population, and what replication factor suffices?
  • How do the odd-cycle integrality obstructions characterize equilibrium existence at a fixed finite replication?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a type-mass stable partnership equilibrium for a large pair-programming or consulting platform. The agents are workers with standardized skill profiles; two workers’ compatibility and joint surplus depend only on their profiles. A population of, say, tens of thousands of workers might have only a few dozen such types. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of workers of type \(t\). This is a natural high-multiplicity regime, and the paper itself points to pair programming and cites Chiappori et al.’s model with multiple agents of a type as a close neighbor.

My single anchor is Proposition 2.2, credited to Talman and Yang (2011) and Biró et al. (2012), rather than proved as a new result here. It gives the paper’s LP formulation and the paper computes with it in Remark 2.4. For the finite-agent partnership problem, it says that: (a) an equilibrium exists if and only if (b) the fractional matching LP has an integral optimal solution; equivalently, (c) the maximum weight of an integral matching equals the LP’s optimal value. The LP maximizes total joint value subject to each agent being used at most once, and its dual assigns nonnegative payoffs \(p_i\) with \(p_i+p_j\ge v(i,j)\) for every compatible pair. This is a formulation anchor, not a numbered claim that the finite problem is in P.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Its input is a finite support \(T\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), an undirected compatibility relation \(\Gamma\) on types (including self-pairs where two different agents of the same type may partner), and binary-rational joint surpluses \(v_{tu}\ge 0\). Each type is complete for the model: its agents have the same compatibility and joint-surplus relationships with every other type.

For each allowed pair of types, choose \(x_{tu}\ge 0\), the mass of partnerships between those types, and choose \(s_t\ge0\), the unpartnered mass of type \(t\). Choose a nonnegative type payoff \(p_t\). The capacity constraints are
\[ 2x_{tt}+\sum_{\substack{u\ne t\\\{t,u\}\in\Gamma}}x_{tu}+s_t=\mu_t \qquad(t\in T). \]
The equilibrium conditions are
\[ p_t+p_u\ge v_{tu}\quad(\{t,u\}\in\Gamma), \qquad x_{tu}(p_t+p_u-v_{tu})=0, \qquad s_t p_t=0, \]
where the first inequality for a self-pair means \(2p_t\ge v_{tt}\). In words, no pair can block at the offered payoffs, every partnership used has its joint surplus fully paid out, and any unpartnered type receives payoff zero. The question is: output such an equilibrium, with the mass flows representing whole agents partnered one-to-one. Mass is divisible only in how the population of a type is split among partner types; no individual gets a fractional partner.

Equivalently, maximize total partnership surplus \(\sum_{\{t,u\}\in\Gamma}v_{tu}x_{tu}\) subject to the capacity constraints, and find an optimal dual payoff vector \(p\). Complementary slackness gives exactly the equilibrium conditions above. This is a rational LP with \(O(\tau^2)\) variables and constraints, so I expect Class A: an exact solution in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). The paper’s Theorem 3.5 and its equilibrium-finding algorithm are consistent with this target, but I would not present them as a second anchor or as a complexity theorem.

The paper’s own three-agent example shows why this is a real population mirror. With three equally sized types and surplus \(1\) for every pair, the finite society with one agent of each type has no equilibrium. In the type-mass model, set each of the three pair masses to \(1/6\), so each type’s mass \(1/3\) is fully paired, and set every payoff to \(1/2\). Each agent still has exactly one partner, and the pairwise stability conditions hold. This is the fractional matching obstruction in Proposition 2.2 becoming a legitimate allocation of a continuous population. The rational flow can also be implemented by a sufficiently replicated finite clone population.

The mirror covers equilibrium existence and equilibrium construction, not the paper’s adjustment-process trajectory or its iteration bounds. Further questions include which replication factors implement an equilibrium at a prescribed finite multiplicity, and how the odd-cycle integrality obstructions characterize failures at that multiplicity.

My weakest point is scientific payoff: the paper provides no numbered hardness or complexity-class theorem, and this is not an instance of known discrete hardness disappearing. The finite problem already has matching and LP characterizations. The case for inclusion is narrower: the authors’ equilibrium object has a faithful mass version, their cited type-based matching work supports the regime, and the finite example gives a concrete integrality barrier that continuous partnership mass removes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Proposition 2.2 is not a complexity result. It characterizes equilibrium existence by whether the fractional matching LP has an integral optimum. The paper’s algorithmic contribution is its reduction through an associated assignment game and its price adjustment process. The proposed mirror instead takes the fractional matching LP, gives it type-mass capacities, and interprets fractional flows as partnerships in a nonatomic population. That is a coherent question, but its computational core is a standard capacitated LP, not a new separation or algorithmic problem exposed by continuization.

The three-type example also makes a narrower case than it first appears to. Its mass solution can be implemented by a finite population of six agents: two of each type, with one partnership on each of the three edges. The example shows how duplication can remove the odd-cycle obstruction; it does not show that a continuum is needed. That weakens the claimed payoff, though the programme explicitly treats duplication effects as relevant rather than defective, so it does not rule out the mirror.

The broader objection is that low-support types require pair values and compatibility to be uniform across every pair of agents in each type block. That may be a poor fit for many real partnerships, where the value is genuinely relationship-specific. But standardized skill categories with type-pair surplus are plausible, and the paper itself cites a multiple-agents-per-type model. I cannot honestly turn either point into a fundamental obstruction. The proposed mirror is well-posed and the authors could recognize it as their problem; my negative case is that its likely contribution is modest because it repackages fractional matching in a mass-allocation interpretation. That is not enough to establish that no worthwhile mirror exists.
