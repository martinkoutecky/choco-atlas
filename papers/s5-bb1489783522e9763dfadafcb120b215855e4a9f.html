What the mirror covers

A type distribution can restate the majority-support shares in Tables 1–3 and the support distributions in Figures 1–2; the paper provides no computational result about bargaining or agreement selection.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its findings are empirical: for example, Table 2 reports that 68% of policies included in the coalition agreements lacked sincere floor-majority support, and Table 3 reports majority-supported policies omitted from agreements. Those are meaningful results, but they are not complexity anchors.

The nearest positive idea is to represent representatives as a distribution over complete policy-support types, with each type’s mass equal to its share of seats. For a given set of policy proposals and a given coalition agreement, one could calculate the shares of included policies lacking majority support and majority-supported policies left out. Large, disciplined legislatures with many representatives but relatively few distinct support types offer a plausible high-multiplicity setting.

That is a possible continuous representation of the paper’s measurements, but it does not yield a computational question the paper studies. Choosing agreements would require adding a model of coalition bargaining and feasibility that the authors do not provide; simply calculating the reported rates from a type distribution is a new, straightforward measurement task. So I cannot honestly name a continuous problem per anchor or predict its complexity class. The strongest case for a mirror is a prospective research direction, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its headline findings—such as the share of coalition-agreement policies without sincere majority support—are empirical comparisons, not the complexity of a decision problem. Table 2’s 68% figure and Table 3’s counts are evidence about coalition government, but the paper defines no algorithmic task whose complexity a continuous mirror could investigate.

The proposed support-type distribution is a sensible way to restate those measurements. Let \(v\) be a party’s support vector across the \(q\) proposals, and let \(\mu_v\) be the share of parliamentary seats held by parties of that type. Then proposal \(j\)’s floor support is \(M_j=\sum_v \mu_v v_j\). Given an agreement, one can recompute the reported shares from these masses. That captures the paper’s quantities, but it supplies no computational question beyond calculating descriptive statistics.

A more ambitious mirror might ask how to compute a coalition agreement from party types. But the paper does not specify the bargaining model such a problem needs: parties’ utilities over policy bundles, salience, bargaining power, feasible coalitions, or a solution concept. Many different mechanisms could produce the same observed agreement and support shares. Choosing one would make the complexity result about a newly supplied bargaining model, not a computational problem in this paper. Treating MPs as the population captures floor support, while the agreement is negotiated by a small number of party actors; treating parties as the population preserves those actors but does not make their vetoes divisible.

So the negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper*: the closest continuous representation restates its measurements, and a computational mirror requires adding the mechanism the paper leaves for future work. The universal claim is less secure. A separate, well-specified theory of coalition bargaining over a large population of parties could be worthwhile, and the authors might recognize it as related to their agenda. But that would be a new model prompted by the paper, not a computational result of it waiting to be continuized.
