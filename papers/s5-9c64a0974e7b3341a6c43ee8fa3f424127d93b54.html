What the mirror covers

The proposed questions cover Theorem 4.6's scoring-rule characterization and Theorem 5.3's uniform-feedback Condorcet impossibility; they leave the experiments and other results unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the paper’s own population model. A type is a complete ranking \(\sigma\in L(M)\); mass \(\mu_\sigma\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. In a large deliberation platform or public consultation over a fixed slate of proposals, many contributors may share rankings, so \(n\gg\tau\) is plausible. The decision is which candidate wins, based on exact aggregate improvement-feedback probabilities. This is a natural high-multiplicity setting: people are not targeted individually, and population shares are the quantity the feedback reveals.

My lead is Score Feedback Winner Set. Fix \(m\), \(t\), and the feedback distribution. Let \(A_t\mu=q\) denote the exact matrix of response probabilities induced by the population distribution \(\mu\). Given a rational matrix \(q\) promised to come from some rational mass distribution, compute
\[ W_s(q)=\bigcup_{\substack{\mu\in\Delta_{\mathbb Q}(L(M))\\A_t\mu=q}} \operatorname{argmax}_{c\in M}\mathrm{sc}_s(c,\mu). \]
Return the set and, for each member, a mass distribution witnessing that it can win. The question is whether the feedback identifies one winner, or which winners remain consistent with it.

The anchor is Theorem 4.6, proved here. For \(m\ge6\), uniform \(t\)-improvement feedback, every \(t\in[m-1]\), and any scoring vector \(s\), the theorem says: if \(s\) lies in the span of the feedback-induced scoring vector \(s_t^*\) and plurality’s vector, the winner can be found from the feedback for every profile; otherwise, there are indistinguishable profiles with different unique winners, and some profile defeats any randomized identification algorithm’s guarantee of success greater than \(1/m\). In the set formulation, the first case lets us recover the scores from \(q\); in the second, some \(q\) has \(W_s(q)=M\). The theorem is proved in this paper, though it builds on a construction from Halpern et al. (2024). This is the strongest anchor: it gives both a tractable slice and a sharp impossibility boundary across the full uniform-feedback range.

A second, separate mirror is Condorcet Feedback Winner Set: on the same input, return every candidate that is a unique Condorcet winner for some compatible mass distribution, with a witness for each. Theorem 5.3, also proved here, states that for uniform feedback, \(m\ge13\), and every \(t\in[m-1]\), no randomized algorithm can identify the unique Condorcet winner with success probability greater than \(1/m\) in the worst case. Its construction gives a response matrix compatible with profiles having each candidate as the unique Condorcet winner, so the possible-winner set can be all of \(M\).

For the underlying winner-identification questions, the expected outcome is tractability where the relevant score is recoverable, and a continuum-specific impossibility elsewhere: the response matrix loses information about the population. That is stronger than a runtime lower bound, and should not be mislabeled NP-hardness. The complexity of computing the full possible-winner sets is an additional question; the mass variables range over \(m!\) rankings, so it may turn on the same kind of pricing problem that appears in other continuous mirrors.

The authors would recognize these questions: their paper already defines a preference profile \(D\) as a distribution over rankings and treats its probabilities as population proportions. That makes the high-multiplicity interpretation unusually direct, and also makes novelty the weak point. The paper has already continuized the population; the proposed mirror is a computational reframing of its exact feedback-identification problem, not a new conversion from a finite electorate. This case covers the positional-scoring and Condorcet identification results in Sections 2–5, not the experiments or outcome-space continuity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has already made the population continuous. Its profile \(D\) is a distribution over complete rankings, and the mass on each ranking is explicitly the fraction of the population holding it. That is the programme’s central society model, not a finite electorate awaiting continuization. This is stronger than pointing to prior high-multiplicity work: the paper itself formulates and proves results over the continuous profile space.

For Theorem 4.6, the proposed Score Feedback Winner Set is the same identification question expressed as a set-valued output. The paper studies the fibres of the response map \(D\mapsto A_tD\): when the scoring vector lies in the stated span, the feedback determines the scores; otherwise, its construction yields indistinguishable profiles with different winners. In the proponent’s terms, that gives a response matrix with \(W_s(q)=M\). Theorem 5.3 does the analogous work for Condorcet winners: its indistinguishable profiles make every candidate possible for the constructed feedback data. Calling these outputs “possible-winner sets” does not create a new continuous-population result.

A stronger follow-up would compute \(W_s(q)\) for *every* feasible response matrix, with witness distributions, and do the analogous computation for Condorcet winners. That may be a worthwhile inverse problem, but its input is the feedback matrix \(q\), not the society’s support and rational masses. Giving the full society as input would make score or pairwise-winner computation direct; withholding it and supplying only \(q\) returns to the paper’s exact-feedback identification problem. The new contribution would therefore be a complexity classification of a latent-profile problem, not the population continuization that ChoCo is meant to chart. The paper’s theorems do not settle that broader classification, so they cannot by themselves establish that it is uninteresting.

Nor do the results supply the usual computational-complexity anchors: they impose information-theoretic limits while granting algorithms exact feedback, rather than classifying running time for society inputs. That does not diminish the paper’s results; it narrows what can be claimed as its continuous computational mirror. The main case against is thus a novelty collision, not that high multiplicity is implausible, nor that the answer to a proposed mirror would be negative. The negative case is weak on the user’s universal wording: the population regime is plainly sensible here, and a new inverse problem could still be valuable. But the two anchors offered are already results about a continuous population, and their possible-winner-set restatement is not a new continuization.
