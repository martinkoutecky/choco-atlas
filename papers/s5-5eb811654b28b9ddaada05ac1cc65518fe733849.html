What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror addresses only enemy-oriented core-stable partition search; it leaves the paper's other hedonic-game claims and its simulator contribution aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a core-stable measurable partition always exist for these type-level preferences?
  • When a stable partition exists, can it be represented finitely and found efficiently?
  • Which finite hardness reductions remain valid when type mass can be split?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains computational claims, but no numbered computational result that can serve as a required anchor. Its numbered items are Definitions 1–3. The complexity statements appear as unnumbered prose summarizing other papers: in §3.2, for example, it reports that enemy-oriented games always have a nonempty core, but finding a core-stable partition is NP-hard, citing Dimitrov et al. (2006). That is the strongest potential seed for a mirror, but it has no theorem number in this paper and is not proved here. I can’t present it as a compliant anchor.

The positive case around that near-miss is concrete. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete player types, where each type specifies which types its members regard as friends and enemies. Give each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A coalition is a measurable group of agents; its composition is the vector \(x\), where \(x_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) in it. Type \(t\) prefers coalitions lexicographically: first minimize the enemy mass \(\sum_{u\in E_t}x_u\), then maximize the friend mass \(\sum_{u\in F_t}x_u\). This preserves the priority encoded by the finite game’s enemy-oriented utility.

The candidate problem is Continuum Enemy-Oriented Core Search: given \(T\), the masses, and the friend/enemy relations, find a measurable partition of the population into coalitions such that no positive-mass group has every member strictly preferring that group to their assigned coalition; if no such partition exists, report that. A plausible regime is a large workforce or community whose members share a small number of compatibility profiles: the population is much larger than the number of distinct types. The finite game’s core-stability question survives, while the partition and the masses become genuinely continuous. Authors studying enemy-oriented core search could plausibly recognize this as their problem at high multiplicity.

I would tentatively expect the continuous problem to remain hard for continuum-specific reasons (Class C), rather than assume the finite hardness transfers. Type-level blocking still involves combinatorial choices, but divisible type masses also permit partitions unavailable in the finite game. Whether a stable measurable partition is guaranteed, whether stable partitions have finite descriptions, and whether any finite hardness reduction survives this splitting are natural next questions.

This proposed mirror covers only the enemy-oriented search claim, not the paper’s claims about fractional hedonic games, friend-oriented games, or altruistic games. Its weakest point is also its central modeling risk: friendship in the paper’s motivating story is individual, while this mirror requires people of the same type to agree on their relations to every other type. That is credible for some category-based settings, but it does not fit arbitrary friendship networks. And, regardless of that plausibility, the missing numbered anchor means this remains a positive near-case, not a case that meets the requested anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror of this paper is that it has no named computational result to anchor one. Definitions 1–3 are definitions. The complexity claims are unnumbered summaries of results by other authors, and the paper’s own contribution is a simulator for visualizing finite hedonic games. It states no complexity theorem for a problem of its own.

The proposed Continuum Enemy-Oriented Core Search is a coherent research question. A stronger candidate would be to continuize the reported \(\Sigma_2^P\)-completeness result for checking core emptiness in simple symmetric fractional hedonic games. Both, however, mirror results from Dimitrov et al. or Aziz et al., which Miles reports; neither mirrors a computational result established by this paper. A finite-type population with type-level friendships also changes the simulator’s setting: arbitrary friendship graphs may require as many types as players, while a fixed number of types restricts the model to block-structured friendships. That may be a worthwhile model in its own right, but the paper gives no computational finding about it.

The substantive negative case is weak on the universal claim. Category-based affinities in a large population are plausible, and a continuous core problem could be worth studying. What the paper supports is the narrower conclusion that no compliant, paper-anchored mirror has been identified. I cannot honestly establish that every such scenario is unworthwhile.
