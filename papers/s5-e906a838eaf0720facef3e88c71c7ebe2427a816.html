  • Theorem H.1 — survived: The mass-valued update computation is a narrow analogue of the amendment-decision step in the proven protocol, though it does not mirror BFT execution.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(s\in[1/2,1)\), \(D>0\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many types \((a_i,d_i)\) with binary-rational coordinates and masses, compute the amended pair \((s',D')\). Apply the \(h\)-rule to threshold ideals \(a_i\): raise to the maximal \(r>s\) with \(\mu(a\ge r)>r\), or lower to the minimal \(r<s\) with \(\mu(a\le r)>s\), retaining \(s\) if neither update applies; promise that any required extremum exists. For the timeout, let \(\rho=2s-1\); according to whether the weighted median of \(d_i\) lies above or below \(D\), remove mass \(\rho\) from the corresponding upper or lower tail and use the median of the remaining mass if it moves in that direction, otherwise retaining \(D\). Assume the relevant medians are unique.

The model it lives in

A society distribution \(\mu\) over supported ideal-point types \((a_i,d_i)\), with rational masses. The \(h\)-rule computes \(s'\); the Suppress Outer-\(f\) rule trims \(\rho=2s-1\) mass from timeout ideals to compute \(D'\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the threshold and timeout updates used in Theorem H.1’s amendment mechanism. It leaves membership changes and the theorem’s BFT consensus, identity, safety, and liveness guarantees unmodeled.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize when the \(h\)-rule’s strict feasibility sets attain the required extrema.
  • Quantify stability under mass perturbations and finite-\(n\) approximation when \(n\mu_t\) is not integral.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is computing constitutional parameter amendments for a high-multiplicity community. It covers the paper’s social-choice amendment rules for the supermajority threshold and message timeout, not its membership-change rule or its BFT consensus protocol.

The closest named anchor is Theorem H.1, “Constitutional Amendment Correctness,” proved in this paper. It says that Algorithm 1 with amendments from Algorithm 2 is correct: in any run where all valid amendments are safe, there is a constitutional sequence \(T\) such that correct agents’ outputs satisfy four properties. Consistency: each correct agent’s output is a subsequence of \(T\). Validity: an agent outputs an item only if it participates in that item’s epoch. Epoch liveness: each correct participant eventually outputs all transactions in its epoch; if that is the last epoch, \(T\) also includes every transaction input by a correct participant there. Amendment liveness: a valid amendment decision supplied by a correct agent eventually appears in \(T\).

That is a protocol-correctness theorem, not a complexity classification. The paper has no named theorem establishing that one of its problems is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified by computational complexity. Theorem H.1 is still a relevant, if limited, anchor: it is the paper’s proved result about the full amendment mechanism, and the computation below is the amendment-decision step that mechanism uses.

Call the mirror Continuous Constitutional Parameter Amendment. An instance gives the current parameters \(s\in[1/2,1)\) and \(D>0\), and a finite list of voter types \(t_i=(a_i,d_i)\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(q_i\), where \(\sum_i q_i=1\). A type records a member’s ideal threshold \(a_i\) and ideal timeout \(d_i\); \(\tau\) is the number of supported types. The question is to compute the amended pair \((s',D')\) using the paper’s \(h\)-rule and Suppress Outer-\(f\) rule. The solution is that exact pair, with the status quo retained for any parameter that does not change.

For the threshold rule, the raising condition at a proposed \(r>s\) is \(\mu(a\ge r)>r\); the lowering condition at \(r<s\) is \(\mu(a\le r)>s\). Use the paper’s maximal feasible raise or minimal feasible lowering, and leave \(s\) unchanged if neither applies. To make this continuous input problem well-defined, restrict instances to those where a required maximum exists. For the timeout rule, let \(\rho=2s-1\), the paper’s implied fault fraction. Compare the weighted median of the \(d_i\) values with \(D\). If it is above \(D\), remove mass \(\rho\) from the highest timeout ideals and take the median of what remains; if it is below \(D\), remove that mass from the lowest ideals and take the remaining median. Update only in the direction indicated by the original median. A unique-median promise fixes the output where weighted medians could otherwise be ambiguous. Fractional trimming of mass is the natural continuous counterpart of removing \(f\) votes.

A plausible regime is a grassroots digital community or federation with hundreds of thousands of members but only dozens or hundreds of recurring ideal-point pairs about timeout and quorum policy. Members still have distinct identities for authentication and consensus; for this amendment-computation question, members with the same pair of relevant ideals are interchangeable. If the masses are counts divided by a common \(n\), and \(\rho n\) is integral, the continuous rule agrees with the corresponding weighted vote calculation, subject to the stated median convention. Fractional masses also let the model express population shares directly.

I would expect this problem to be Class A: sort the supported ideal values, accumulate rational masses, and compute the threshold cutoffs and trimmed median in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the bit length \(L\). That is a precise and plausible continuous analogue of part of the authors’ problem, but its likely tractability comes from simple aggregation, not from a new optimization method. It generates follow-up questions about how amendment outcomes change under small perturbations of the masses, and how closely finite elections approximate the continuous rule when \(n\mu_t\) is not integral.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s central result concerns distributed consensus and the safe execution of amendments, while this mirror only computes two amendment parameters once votes are available. It omits membership changes, where individually named identities matter, and it does not transfer or dissolve any hardness result—the paper supplies no named hardness anchor to test. This is a defensible narrow mirror of the authors’ Section 4 amendment computation, but not a continuization of the paper’s main BFT contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem H.1 is the wrong anchor for a continuous complexity mirror. It proves that the combined protocol satisfies consistency, validity, epoch liveness, and amendment liveness under its safety assumptions. It does not classify the complexity of a social choice problem. The paper’s \(3\delta\) latency and \(O(n)\) communication claims concern a different object: identified participants sending and signing blocks.

The proposed parameter update is the best direct social choice mirror. It is a coherent high multiplicity question: given masses over ideal threshold and timeout values, apply the \(h\)-rule and the trimmed median rule. But it computes one component of the amendment procedure after consensus has already collected and ordered the latest authenticated votes. It does not mirror H.1’s result about whether members can maintain a consistent protocol history through Byzantine behavior and epoch changes.

That gap is fundamental to a mirror of the theorem. Its guarantees concern every correct participant and their transactions, signatures, and blocks. An atomless mass model cannot preserve those individual obligations: replacing “every participant” with “almost every participant” can discard a single member’s transaction, while retaining every public key and event restores an atomic population. Likewise, a type description complete for the protocol must include identity, since membership decisions, consent to join, equivocation, and message delivery depend on who acts. A mass distribution over such types offers no compression of those parts of the model.

For the threshold and timeout rules alone, masses over ideal points are sensible. The raising condition depends on \(\mu(a\ge r)>r\), the lowering condition on \(\mu(a\le r)>s\), and the timeout update is a trimmed weighted median after removing the prescribed mass. That is a valid continuous formulation of a narrow rule-evaluation task. Its input already consists of aggregate preferences, though: the finite rule depends on their weighted histogram, and normalizing vote counts into proportions changes the representation without changing what the rule uses. The paper does not pose a computational obstacle around that aggregation or study the effort needed to change its outcome.

A richer question, such as the minimum mass of members that must be persuaded to obtain a desired amendment, could fit ChoCo’s campaigning or bribery agenda. The paper supplies no intervention model, costs, or target-selection assumptions for that question. One can add them plausibly, but the resulting problem would be a new model built around the amendment rule, rather than a continuous version of the paper’s proved result.

So the strongest case against is that the offered anchor does not survive as a mirror of H.1, and the remaining direct slice is narrow and largely a change of representation. The universal claim remains weak: a large community with repeated ideal-point types is a sensible regime, and an author might recognize a carefully specified campaign problem as a useful extension. I would argue against calling this paper a strong continuous-mirror candidate, rather than claim that no worthwhile scenario can be constructed.
