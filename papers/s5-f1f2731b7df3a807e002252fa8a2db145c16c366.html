The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), and explicitly represented piecewise-constant nonatomic valuations \(V_t\) repeated on a per-capita replicated interval \(K_N\), compute a finite family of bundle templates and a feasible mass flow \(x_{t,P}\ge0\), with \(\sum_P x_{t,P}=\mu_t\), resource-capacity constraints, and an integral realization whenever \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nx_{t,P}\) are integral, such that all of \(K_N\) is allocated and, whenever \(x_{t,P}>0\), \(V_t(P)\ge V_t(Q)\) for every used template \(Q\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete explicit valuation descriptions; \(\mu\) is population mass; \(x_{t,P}\) assigns type mass to disjoint bundle templates; and the objective is an exact type-symmetric envy-free full allocation with an integral finite realization, while measuring schema complexity by \(\tau\), valuation-description length, and the encoding of \(\mu\).

The objection that survived

For unrestricted Robertson–Webb valuation oracles, collapsing agents to \(\tau\) types does not collapse geometric cut locations: even with \(\tau=1\), producing \(N\) disjoint pieces can require \(\Omega(N)\) oracle calls and \(N\)-scale output.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 4.11 and also yields a partial connected version of Theorem 5.2 with guarantee \(1/(3N)\); it does not establish the proposed type-only query bound in Theorem 4.4 or mirror every internal subprotocol separately.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the schema be computed with complexity polynomial in \(\tau\), valuation-description length, and the encoding length of \(\mu\), without enumerating named agents?
  • Which rounding conditions preserve exact envy-freeness and \(V_t(P)\ge V_t(K_N)/(3N)\) for every admissible finite \(N\)?
  • Does connectedness remain tractable on a per-capita replicated interval, or does exact type-level realization yield a continuum-specific hardness result?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity, type-oracle version of the paper’s cake-cutting problem. I would not argue that the cake is already continuous; that would continuize the resource rather than the population. The new continuous object is the population distribution.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete valuation types. A type \(t\) specifies the entire nonatomic additive valuation \(V_t\), including all information used by the protocol. The society is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents of type \(t\). The intended regime is \(N\gg |T|=\tau\): for every admissible \(N\), the finite approximation has \(N\mu_t\) indistinguishable agents of type \(t\).

Because a fixed cake shared by an atomless population gives each agent vanishing value, I would use the programme’s permitted per-capita normalization: either the total cake supply scales with \(N\), or utilities are multiplied by \(N\). Thus the paper’s guarantee \(V_t(X)\ge V_t(K)/(3N)\) becomes the nondegenerate normalized guarantee \(N V_t(X)\ge V_t(K)/3\).

The action is a type-level allocation schema. Given \(N\), it produces a finite partition of the cake and assigns exactly \(N\mu_t\) agents to pieces, but it is represented by mass flows \(x_{t,P}\), not by \(N\) named agents. Every realization must still give each unit of population an integral piece; fractional \(x_{t,P}\) records only the fraction of type \(t\) assigned to a piece template. The objective is exact envy-freeness, with query complexity measured in CUT and EVALUATE queries to distinct valuation types rather than repeated queries to identical agents.

My lead question is:

\[ \textsf{HM-EnvyFree}_\infty: \]

Given \(T\), \(\mu\), and oracle access to \(V_t\) for each \(t\in T\), compute a type-symmetric allocation schema such that, for every admissible \(N\), every type-\(t\) agent receives a piece \(X_i\) satisfying

\[ V_t(X_i)\ge V_t(X_j) \]

for every other allocated piece \(X_j\). The output must include a mass-flow description and a realization procedure for each finite high-multiplicity instance.

This mirrors Theorem 4.11, proved in the paper: “The Main Protocol allocates all the cake in an envy-free manner.” The theorem is not cited from elsewhere; it is established by the paper’s induction through the Core, Discrepancy, GoLeft, and recursive Main Protocols.

The continuous question is not merely the original theorem with names erased. It asks whether the protocol’s structure can be compressed to the finite type support and the mass vector \(\mu\), rather than operating separately on \(N\) agents. In a large fair-scheduling or resource-allocation population—say, many workers with one of a small number of complete preference profiles over time slots—this is exactly the natural representation. I expect the finite-type, piecewise-linear version to be Class A: the mass constraints should become a finite-dimensional allocation or flow problem, with the one-dimensional geometry of cake pieces providing useful structure. For unrestricted valuation oracles, a continuum-specific difficulty may remain.

The computational companion is:

\[ \textsf{HM-BoundedQuery-EF}_\infty: \]

On the same input, produce an exact envy-free allocation schema whose number of valuation queries is bounded by a function of \(\tau\), the valuation-description length, and possibly \(\log N\), but not by the number of named agents. Equivalently, determine whether the paper’s protocol can be batched over identical types and whether its output can be represented without listing all \(N\) pieces.

This mirrors Theorem 4.4, also proved here: the Main Protocol is bounded by the explicit quantity \(B'\) Robertson–Webb operations for \(n\) agents. The paper’s \(B'\) is a bound in the original number of agents; the mirror asks whether the relevant bound can instead depend on the number of types and the mass representation. I expect this to be Class A for structured valuation types, though it is a plausible place for a Class C result if exact mass arithmetic or type-level realization creates new hardness. A positive answer would be a genuine computational consequence of continuization, not a restatement of the theorem.

A second, weaker but cleaner mirror is:

\[ \textsf{HM-ConnectedPartialEF}_\infty: \]

Given \(T\), \(\mu\), and the valuation oracles, compute a type-level schema that, for every admissible \(N\), gives each agent a connected piece, leaves some cake possibly unallocated, preserves envy-freeness, and guarantees

\[ V_t(X_i)\ge \frac{V_t(K)}{3N} \]

for every type-\(t\) agent—or, under the per-capita normalization,

\[ N V_t(X_i)\ge \frac{V_t(K)}{3}. \]

This mirrors Theorem 5.2, proved in the paper: the ConnectedPieces Protocol allocates the cake partially in an envy-free way so that every agent receives a connected piece worth at least \(1/(3n)\) of the whole cake. The continuous problem retains all of the substantive constraints—envy-freeness, connectedness, partial allocation, and the quantitative guarantee—while replacing a long list of named agents by a distribution over repeated valuation types.

I would expect this version to be Class A, probably more readily than the exact full-allocation problem. The one-dimensional connectedness condition and the weaker partial-allocation requirement should interact well with mass flows and interval structure. It also generates useful follow-up questions: whether the constant \(1/3\) can be improved in the finite-type setting, whether exact connected allocations admit succinct certificates, and how rounding from a mass solution behaves for finite \(N\).

The paper contains no named NP-hardness, coNP-hardness, or parameterized-hardness anchor, so there is no honest Class B argument to make from its stated results. The positive case therefore rests on algorithmic anchors and asks whether their query and allocation structure survives high-multiplicity compression.

The weakest point is that cake cutting already has a continuous resource and is usually formulated through valuation queries, so a careless mirror could merely rename the cake’s divisibility as population continuity. The case survives only if the mirror keeps the cake-cutting problem intact while making \(\mu\) the computational input: many agents share complete valuation types, mass determines multiplicities and allocation flows, and the algorithm must produce realizable envy-free allocations rather than arbitrary fractional cake shares. Under that interpretation, the authors should recognize the questions above as genuine high-multiplicity versions of their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed continuization does not compress the object whose complexity the paper actually studies. It compresses the list of agents, while the protocol’s query cost is driven by the number of distinct cake pieces, trims, and cut locations.

A valuation type is not a reusable answer. Even when \( \tau=1 \), an oracle for one valuation may have to answer \(N-1\) different CUT queries to produce \(N\) equal-value pieces. For an arbitrary nonatomic valuation, those cut points can be independently unconstrained by previous answers. Counting all queries to the same valuation function as “one type query” therefore misstates the Robertson–Webb model: repeated agents disappear, but repeated geometric queries do not. If the answer is instead a symbolic instruction such as “partition into \(N\) equal-value pieces,” its realization still requires \(N-1\) oracle calls unless the valuation has an explicit special representation.

This defeats the proposed mirror of Theorem 4.4. The theorem’s boundedness is a bound on CUT and EVALUATE operations, not on the number of distinct valuation functions contacted. Replacing \(n\) by \(\tau\) is unjustified even for one type. A succinct output convention could produce such a bound only by changing the computational model into one of implicit-output generation or explicit valuation computation.

The same issue undermines the proposed mirror of Theorem 4.11. A mass flow \(x_{t,P}\) is meaningful only after the relevant pieces \(P\) have been generated. In a full envy-free allocation with \(N\) identical agents, every agent must receive a distinct positive bundle of equal value; assigning the same physical piece to a positive mass of agents violates integral, disjoint allocation. Thus the mass vector does not replace the \(N\)-piece geometry. A finite menu of templates either permits sharing, which is a different problem, or fails to represent generic valuation oracles. Running the original Main Protocol separately for each \(N\) is merely a uniform restatement of the theorem, not a population-level compression.

Theorem 5.2 is even less suitable. With a fixed cake, the guarantee \(V_i(X_i)\ge 1/(3N)\) tends to zero, and connected pieces shrink to measure-zero sets; under the paper’s nonatomicity assumption, the literal continuum limit gives them value zero. Replacing it by \(N V_i(X_i)\ge V_i(K)/3\) rescales utilities but does not prevent the physical pieces from shrinking or remove the need to construct \(N\) of them. Scaling the cake supply with \(N\) avoids that degeneration only by creating an \(N\)-dependent replicated resource; arbitrary valuations still require \(N\)-scale geometric output, while a highly periodic resource makes the problem easy for reasons unrelated to population continuization.

So the best negative conclusion is that all three proposed anchors become either a succinct-representation question, a changed oracle model, or a degenerate/replicated resource model. None yields the kind of finite-type continuous optimization problem exemplified by the ChoCo programme.

This case is not airtight universally. If one restricts valuations to explicitly represented piecewise-constant types and builds in a replicated per-capita cake, a sensible high-multiplicity cake-cutting problem can certainly be formulated. But that would be a new restricted model, not a compression of the paper’s arbitrary-valuation bounded protocol. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is therefore stronger than the evidence supports; the negative case is strongest against the particular mirrors proposed, especially the query-complexity and connected-piece versions.
