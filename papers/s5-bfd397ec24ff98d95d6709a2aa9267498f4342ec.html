The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\) of finitely many good types, integral bundle configurations \(B\in\mathcal{B}\), monotone valuations \(v_\theta\), and \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), compute \(x_{\theta,B}\ge0\) and residual masses \(z_g\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,B}a_g(B)x_{\theta,B}+z_g=\rho_g\), minimizing \(\sum_g z_g\), subject to supported bundles satisfying \(v_\theta(B)\ge(1-\varepsilon)v_\theta(B'\setminus\{g\})\) for every supported pair and copy \(g\in B'\), no envy of the residual multiset, and \(\mathrm{NW}_\infty(x)\ge\frac{1}{2e^{1/e}}\mathrm{NW}_\infty^\star\); determine whether the optimum residual mass is zero and compute such an assignment.

The model it lives in

A configuration-mass model over complete agent types \(\Theta\), with variables \(x_{\theta,B}\) assigning population mass to integral bundles, residual inventory \(z\), objective \(\sum_g z_g\), supported-bundle EFX constraints, and \(\mathrm{NW}_\infty(x)=\exp(\sum_{\theta,B}x_{\theta,B}\log v_\theta(B))\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s approximate-EFX-with-charity result.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved as a consequence of this paper’s new Theorem 3 and the earlier reduction of Chaudhury et al. Theorem 4 states that there is a polynomial-time algorithm which, for an \(n\)-agent instance, finds a partial \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX allocation \(X\) such that no agent envies the unallocated goods, the number of unallocated goods is \(\widetilde O((n/\varepsilon)^{1/2})\), and \(\mathrm{NW}(X)\ge \frac{1}{2e^{1/e}}\mathrm{NW}(X^*)\), where \(X^*\) maximizes Nash welfare. The paper contains no named NP-hardness result, so this algorithmic theorem is the appropriate anchor.

I would mirror it with the following problem, Continuous Approximate-EFX with Charity. An instance consists of a finite set of agent types \(\Theta\), a population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), a finite set of good types \(G\), per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\) for \(g\in G\), monotone valuation functions \(v_\theta:2^G\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\), and \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\). A type is complete: it includes the valuation function and every eligibility or constraint relevant to allocation. The regime is \(N\) students or residents with \(|\Theta|=\tau\ll N\) repeated valuation types, together with \(\rho_gN\) interchangeable copies of each course or good type.

The decision variable is \(x_{\theta,B}\ge0\), the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\), together with residual good mass \(z_g\ge0\). It must satisfy \(\sum_Bx_{\theta,B}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,B:g\in B}x_{\theta,B}+z_g=\rho_g\). Thus every infinitesimal agent still receives an indivisible bundle; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

The allocation is \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX when, for every \(x_{\theta,B}>0\), every \(x_{\theta',B'}>0\), and every \(g\in B'\), \(v_\theta(B)\ge(1-\varepsilon)v_\theta(B'\setminus\{g\})\). If \(U_z=\{g:z_g>0\}\) is the support of the residual inventory, the analogue of “no agent envies the unallocated goods” is \(v_\theta(B)\ge v_\theta(U_z)\) for every positively used \((\theta,B)\). The computational problem is to minimize normalized charity \(\sum_gz_g\), while obtaining the Nash-welfare guarantee. The natural continuum Nash welfare is \(\mathrm{NW}_\infty(x)=\exp(\sum_{\theta,B}x_{\theta,B}\log v_\theta(B))\), with the usual convention when a utility is zero.

This is genuinely a population continuization, not outcome-space fractionalization. A rational solution with denominator \(N\) corresponds exactly to \(N\mu_\theta\) agents and \(N\rho_g\) good copies; conversely, repeated-type finite instances normalize to such a mass instance. The paper’s charity bound becomes \(\widetilde O((N/\varepsilon)^{1/2})/N=\widetilde O((N\varepsilon)^{-1/2})\), so its natural continuum prediction is zero normalized charity, or arbitrarily small charity mass. The setting is plausible for course allocation, housing bundles, or aid packages: many agents share a small number of complete preference types, while each receives an integral bundle.

I would expect a Class A result for additive, Hana, or similarly structured valuations: the variables \(x_{\theta,B}\) form a configuration-LP model, Nash welfare is linear after taking logarithms, and the remaining issue is a bundle-separation problem. For unrestricted monotone valuation tables, I would leave the complexity open; the support condition for EFX may preserve genuine combinatorial difficulty.

A second, sharper anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper. It states that for three agents, if \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) are monotone and \(v_3\) is Hana, then an EFX allocation always exists. Hana means that for every set \(S\) and every two partitions \(A=(A_1,A_2)\), \(B=(B_1,B_2)\) of \(S\), \(\max(v_3(B_1),v_3(B_2))\ge\min(v_3(A_1),v_3(A_2))\).

Its continuous counterpart, Three-Type Exact-EFX\(_\infty\), has three positive-mass types \(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3\), per-capita supplies of integral goods, monotone \(v_1,v_2\), and Hana \(v_3\). The task is to find \(x_{i,B}\) using all good mass such that every bundle in the positive support satisfies \(v_i(B)\ge v_i(B'\setminus\{g\})\) for every other supported bundle \(B'\) and every \(g\in B'\). This asks whether the paper’s exact three-agent existence phenomenon survives when each of the three agents is replaced by a large homogeneous population that may be split across bundles. I would expect tractability in the structured three-type regime, but the mass-splitting question is itself a useful boundary problem.

The weakest point is that neither theorem automatically proves its continuous version. The paper’s proofs manipulate individual bundles and finite allocations; they do not establish that the EFX support constraints remain tractable after a type is split across many bundles. The residual-charity convention also needs to be fixed carefully. But that is a weakness of the prospective theorem, not of the mirror: the proposed problems preserve the paper’s indivisible bundles, EFX comparisons, charity notion, and welfare objective, while replacing a large repeated population by mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I do not think either anchor survives as a faithful, worthwhile continuization, although Theorem 4 is the serious weak point.

Theorem 10 is the easier case. It is an existence theorem for exactly three agents, not a computational result. Its proof relies essentially on manipulating three bundles. If each of the three valuation types receives one bundle, the masses do no computational work: they merely label three homogeneous blocks, so the result is still a three-agent allocation theorem. If a type is split across several bundles, every supported bundle becomes an effective agent in the EFX comparison. The problem is then an EFX problem with potentially many agents, outside the theorem’s three-agent hypothesis and much closer to the general open EFX problem. Replacing EFX by comparisons of average or aggregate utilities would restore linearity, but would no longer be EFX.

Theorem 4 is more plausible, but the proposed mirror loses precisely the content that makes the discrete theorem interesting. With \(N\) repeated agents and supplies scaled proportionally, its charity guarantee becomes

\[ r_N=\widetilde O\!\left((N/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\right), \qquad \frac{r_N}{N} =\widetilde O\!\left((N\varepsilon)^{-1/2}\right)\to 0. \]

Thus the continuum sees only zero residual mass. The theorem proves neither an exact zero-charity allocation nor a polynomial algorithm for that limiting problem. Asking whether zero charity is achievable is a new, stronger theorem; retaining \(N\) to preserve the sublinear rate simply retains the finite high-multiplicity problem rather than producing a continuum result.

There is also a modelling change hidden in the proposed rescue. If the paper’s goods remain a fixed finite set of indivisible objects, increasing the population makes almost everyone receive nothing and the fairness and welfare objectives degenerate. To avoid that, one must introduce \(N\)-scaled supplies of interchangeable good copies and valuations on multisets of copies. That is a sensible course-allocation model, but it is not merely the paper’s population made continuous; it is a new homogeneous-inventory fair-division problem.

Nor does the configuration-LP intuition solve this problem. The supply equations are linear, and the Nash-welfare objective is linear after taking logarithms, but EFX is a support condition:

\[ x_{\theta,B}>0,\ x_{\theta',B'}>0 \;\Longrightarrow\; v_\theta(B)\ge (1-\varepsilon)v_\theta(B'\setminus\{g\}). \]

Those implications are disjunctive in the variables \(x_{\theta,B}\). Preserving individual integral bundles therefore requires support selection, integer variables, or a combinatorial separation problem. Averaging the inequalities would give an ex ante fairness notion, not EFX. Theorem 4’s rainbow-cycle argument supplies no method for this new support problem.

The honest concession is that a repeated course-allocation population with integral bundle support is a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, and its exact-zero-charity question could be interesting in its own right. But that would be a new many-agent, multiset-valued EFX problem, not a computational mirror of either theorem as proved here. Theorem 10 supplies no computational anchor, while Theorem 4 survives only after its quantitative guarantee disappears and its model and target are substantially strengthened.
