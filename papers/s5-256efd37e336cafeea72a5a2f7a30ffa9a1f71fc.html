The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a follow-on computational problem prompted by this paper, rather than a mirror of one of its computational results. There are no qualifying anchors in the paper: none of its numbered results asserts that a computational problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Proposition 25 proves completeness of a logical axiomatization; that is not a complexity result. Proposition 14 is a substantive equilibrium result, but not a computational one. So I cannot honestly supply a per-anchor mirror under your rule.

Proposition 14, proved here by example, says that states with the same preference profile can have different conditional equilibria. In the paper’s \(t,u,v\) example, \(u\) and \(v\) have the same profile, yet there is no equilibrium in \(u\) where both voters vote for \(c\), while such an equilibrium exists in \(v\). The difference lies in what voters know. This is useful support for a continuous model: its types would have to encode epistemic information as well as preferences.

A plausible follow-on is Mass Conditional Equilibrium for a large electorate made up of homogeneous, coordinating blocs. An instance has candidates, a finite set of possible worlds \(\Omega\), a voting rule such as plurality with fixed tie-breaking, and a finite set \(T\) of complete epistemic types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\), summing to \(1\). A type records its sincere ranking in each world and its information partition of \(\Omega\); its ranking must be constant within each information cell. In each world, the mass of each type casts the ballot prescribed by its conditional strategy \(\sigma_t\), mapping information cells to ballots. The task is to decide whether there is a pure conditional equilibrium: for every type and information cell, switching that bloc’s ballot cannot produce a strictly preferred worst-case winner across the worlds in that cell. A solution is the collection of conditional strategies, with the equilibrium condition checked for every type and cell.

This is a credible population-level question about strategic voting under uncertainty, and the paper’s discussion of homogeneous coalitions lends it some support. Its weakest point is substantial: the paper’s equilibrium notion tests unilateral deviations by individual voters, whereas this formulation lets a positive-mass bloc coordinate. If each voter is instead infinitesimal under plurality by vote shares, an individual deviation has no effect on the outcome and strategic incentives can collapse. The paper supplies no reduction or algorithm for the bloc problem, so I would not predict its complexity class from these results. It is a plausible research extension, but it is not an anchored continuous mirror of a computational finding in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. None of the propositions classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity. Proposition 25 is about completeness of a logic; Proposition 14 establishes that two epistemic states with the same preference profile can have different conditional equilibria. Those are substantive results, but neither gives a computational anchor. The proponent acknowledges this, so the proposed “Mass Conditional Equilibrium” is a new problem prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its findings.

The proposed problem also changes what counts as a strategic actor. In the paper, a voter’s deviation changes one ballot. In an atomless electorate, a single voter has zero mass, so changing their ballot cannot change the plurality winner. With winner-based preferences, every ballot is then a best response, and conditional equilibrium no longer filters behavior in the way Proposition 14 describes. The difference in knowledge may still change beliefs, but it does not create an incentive to vote differently.

Treating each positive-mass type as a bloc repairs that degeneracy, but it makes the bloc’s coordinated deviation the strategic move. That is a meaningful weighted-coalition game, and the paper itself points toward coalitions. But it requires a coordination model beyond its individual-voter equilibrium: the authors note that coalition power depends on communication and distributed knowledge. The bloc formulation may merit study on its own; it does not preserve the strategic question behind Proposition 14.

So the negative case is strongest against an *anchored mirror*: the paper supplies no computational finding, and its central equilibrium phenomenon loses its individual strategic force in the usual continuum limit. This does not establish that no follow-on could be worthwhile. A computational study of coalitions, possible winners, or strategic disclosure in a large electorate might be valuable, but the paper’s results do not make that case, and each would need its own justification as a new problem.
