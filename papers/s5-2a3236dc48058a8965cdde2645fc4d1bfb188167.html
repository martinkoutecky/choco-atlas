What the mirror covers

The proposed mass-based reading would concern Theorems 2, 3, 18, and 20 as mechanism-property analyses, but none provides a computational anchor; the other domain and approximate-safety results likewise remain without one. Appendix C already treats random participation in a nonatomic population.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is about the population regime. A large online community voting on a fixed agenda can have many members but relatively few distinct preference types: complete rankings over \(m\) alternatives, for example, give at most \(m!\) types. Type mass is the fraction of the community holding each ranking. The authors would likely recognize this as their setting: they study virtual status quo votes whose weight, \(\tau\), is measured against the active electorate, and they motivate the model with online communities.

A natural design question is to choose the smallest \(\tau\) that guarantees safety for specified sybil and abstention bounds while preserving liveness. But it is not an eligible mirror under your anchor rule. Theorem 2 gives exact safety and liveness thresholds for binary \(\tau\)-SQ-MJ; Theorem 3 proves that no mechanism can provide both guarantees when \(3\sigma+2\mu\ge1\). Theorem 18 gives a safety threshold under random participation, and Theorem 20 gives one for delegation. These are substantive mechanism guarantees and impossibility results, but none asserts a computational complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. There is no qualifying named computational anchor, so I cannot honestly state a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor*.

There is also a scope wrinkle: Appendix C already studies a nonatomic population and proves safety and tradeoff results for it. That work supports the plausibility of mass-based voting as a sensible model, but a basic continuous restatement of the paper’s thresholds would largely repeat existing analysis. A new complexity question for richer inputs or mechanisms could be worthwhile, but it would be new research rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is a credible high multiplicity setting here: a large online community with many members sharing a relatively small number of preference types. The paper itself is framed around online communities, and its safety and liveness guarantees use population fractions. I would not reject the mirror on grounds that mass is unnatural or that voters need individual identities.

The problem is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational result. Theorem 2 gives safety and liveness thresholds; Theorem 3 gives a mechanism impossibility; Theorems 18 and 20 give probabilistic guarantees under random participation and delegation. They characterize when properties hold, but do not analyze the complexity of deciding or computing anything. The proponent is right that a question about choosing the smallest \(\tau\) is not an eligible computational anchor. In the paper’s settings, that would restate its threshold analysis. Nor does calling the input a distribution over types turn those results into algorithmic ones: the main bounds depend on aggregate fractions, and Appendix C already treats a nonatomic population for random participation.

The strongest alternative would be to ask for an algorithm that, given rational masses over preference and participation types, computes the least bias guaranteeing safety and liveness—or to synthesize a rule with the best such tradeoff. But for the named results, that asks for a computational wrapper around their existing characterizations. Making the question genuinely richer—for example, optimizing over a broader class of rules or a richer domain—could produce worthwhile research, but it would introduce a new computational problem rather than mirror a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo mirror: its plausible continuous-population questions are analytic, and the paper already includes a nonatomic treatment of one central branch. The honest limitation is that this does not prove no useful algorithmic follow-on can be invented; it shows that such a follow-on would need its own motivation and anchor.
