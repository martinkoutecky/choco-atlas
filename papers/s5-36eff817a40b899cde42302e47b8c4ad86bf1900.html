The continuous mirror question

Given indivisible projects \(C\) with sizes \(s_j\), budget \(b\), and \(\tau\) complete voter types \(\theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), where each monotone submodular utility \(u_\theta:2^C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) has a specified finite representation supporting value queries, compute an integral committee \(O\subseteq C\) with \(\sum_{j\in O}s_j\le b\) such that no nonzero mass vector \(0\le\lambda\le\mu\) and integral committee \(T\) with \(\sum_{j\in T}s_j\le b\sum_\theta\lambda_\theta\) satisfy \(u_\theta(T)>67.37\,u_\theta(O\cup\{q\})\) for every \(\theta\) with \(\lambda_\theta>0\) and every \(q\in C\); determine whether this is computable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A weighted high-multiplicity participatory-budgeting model in which complete utility types carry masses \(\mu_\theta\), committees and deviations remain integral, and a coalition mass \(\lambda\) receives purchasing power \(b\sum_\theta\lambda_\theta\); the task is to find a \(67.37\)-core committee.

The objection that survived

The input model remains underspecified: value oracles or compact representations do not establish a finite encoding or a \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) weighted algorithm, and the paper does not prove that whole-type elimination handles arbitrarily small masses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 1.3's \(67.37\)-core construction for monotone submodular utilities. Theorem 1.4 becomes a related additive follow-up, while Theorem 1.6 is only a consistency check; other lower bounds and proof machinery are not separately mirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a single mirror, which I would call \(\textsc{HM-Submodular-Core}_{67.37}\). It is a genuine population continuization, while keeping the public-good decision itself discrete.

An instance has a finite project set \(C\), project sizes \(s_j\), a budget \(b\), and finitely many voter types \(\Theta\). Type \(\theta\) has mass \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta=1\), and a complete monotone submodular utility function \(u_\theta:2^C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), with \(u_\theta(\varnothing)=0\). The type includes everything relevant to the problem: the entire utility function, not merely a demographic label. The input is represented by the \(\tau=|\Theta|\) types, their rational masses, the project data, and value oracles or compact representations for the utilities.

The decision variable remains an integral committee \(O\subseteq C\), satisfying \(\sum_{j\in O}s_j\le b\). Thus this is not a fractional-committee or divisible-outcome problem. A coalition is instead represented by a mass vector \(\lambda\), where \(0\le\lambda_\theta\le\mu_\theta\); its total population is \(|\lambda|=\sum_\theta\lambda_\theta\), and its purchasing power is \(b|\lambda|\). The committee \(O\) is an \(\alpha\)-core outcome if there is no nonzero coalition \(\lambda\) and no alternative integral committee \(T\subseteq C\) with \(\sum_{j\in T}s_j\le b|\lambda|\) such that, for every type with \(\lambda_\theta>0\) and every additament \(q\in C\), \(u_\theta(T)>\alpha u_\theta(O\cup\{q\})\).

So the problem is: given this continuous population, find an integral committee in the \(67.37\)-core. Its objective is not utilitarian welfare; it is to find a feasible committee immune to all proportional, mass-funded blocking coalitions up to the paper’s approximation factor.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1.3 (Main Theorem), proved in this paper. In the paper’s finite model, candidates have sizes and a total budget, voters have arbitrary monotone submodular utilities, and a committee is an \(\alpha\)-core outcome when no subset \(S\) can purchase a committee of size at most \((|S|/n)b\) that gives every member more than \(\alpha\) times the utility of the chosen committee even after any one additament. Theorem 1.3 states, in full, that for monotone submodular utilities a \(67.37\)-core is always nonempty, and one such committee can be computed in polynomial time. The implementation uses the paper’s multilinear-extension, randomized-rounding, and sampling framework.

The continuous question above is the high-multiplicity version of exactly that problem. The replacement \((|S|/n)b\mapsto b|\lambda|\) is not cosmetic: an atomless coalition can contain an arbitrary fraction of a type, and its purchasing power scales continuously with that mass. Rational masses also give the expected bridge back to finite instances: if \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\), the instance is the normalized \(N\)-voter election with \(n_\theta\) copies of type \(\theta\). Conversely, the compressed representation can describe an enormous electorate using only \(\tau\) utility types and \(O(L)\)-bit masses.

A plausible regime is participatory budgeting in a large municipality, university system, or public agency. There may be millions of residents but only a few hundred recurring service-demand templates: for example, residents whose utility is determined by the same pattern of school, transport, healthcare, park, or accessibility coverage. Within a type, agents genuinely agree on every project bundle, as high multiplicity requires. Thus \(n\) can be much larger than \(\tau\), while \(m\) remains the number of proposed projects. This is a more credible high-multiplicity setting than a small committee of individually distinctive voters.

I expect \(\textsc{HM-Submodular-Core}_{67.37}\) to be Class A, provided the paper’s proof is rewritten in weighted form. The paper’s Nash-welfare objective becomes \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\log U_\theta(x)\), where \(U_\theta\) is the multilinear extension used only as an internal relaxation. The local-search argument sums over types with weights \(\mu_\theta\); its coalition bound should become a mass inequality of the form \(\lambda(S)<\frac{\lambda(W)}{B(1-\varepsilon)}\frac{\operatorname{Cost}(y)}{\theta-1-2\varepsilon}\). Randomized rounding can be analyzed by the total mass of satisfied types, and the iterative elimination procedure can remove whole satisfied types while scaling the remaining budget. The intended running time would be polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the encoding length \(L\), and the required accuracy, rather than in the expanded electorate size \(n\).

This is also why I would not present the mirror as “the paper already studies continuous outcomes.” The paper’s fractional allocations and multilinear utilities are proof machinery. In the proposed mirror, projects remain indivisible and the returned committee remains integral; only the population and the blocking coalitions are continuous.

The paper’s Theorem 1.6 is a useful fidelity check, though I would not use it as a second computational anchor: it proves that for monotone submodular utilities a \(1.015\)-core can be empty. Its six-gadget witness can be represented by six voter types of mass \(1/6\), so the continuous model preserves the same lower-bound obstruction. The continuum therefore does not magically remove every fairness limitation; it preserves the substantive boundary while potentially improving the representation and algorithmic treatment.

The main further questions are whether the weighted extension really achieves polynomial time in \((m,\tau,L)\), whether the \(67.37\) factor can be improved, and what the optimal continuous factor is. The additive case is especially interesting: Theorem 1.4 proves that a \(9.27\)-core exists, but explicitly does not provide a polynomial-time implementation. A weighted high-multiplicity version would naturally lead to the question of computing a Lindahl-equilibrium-based mirror.

My weakest point is that the compressed weighted theorem is not literally proved in the paper. Theorem 1.3 is polynomial in the number of named voters, whereas replacing those voters by weighted types requires new arguments about weighted local search, atomless coalitions, tiny rational masses, and high-probability rounding. There is also a genuine plausibility cost in assuming that many residents share an entire submodular utility function. Still, the underlying question is exactly the paper’s core problem, the population mass is an active variable in its coalition condition, and the paper’s proof architecture strongly suggests that this is a meaningful Class A continuization rather than a tractable problem obtained by changing the question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror is not yet a well-specified computational problem. In the paper, a voter’s type is an entire monotone submodular function \(u:2^C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), represented only through a value oracle. “Many residents share a service-demand template” does not establish high multiplicity: two residents must agree on every one of the \(2^m\) committees, including all complementarities and saturation effects. If exact oracle identity is accepted, the mirror is merely a weighted finite-type restatement. For any fixed committee \(T\), a type either satisfies the blocking inequalities or does not; the optimal coalition simply takes the required mass from qualifying types. The atomless population contributes no new optimization structure.

One can repair the representation problem by restricting utilities to a compact language such as additive valuations or coverage functions. But then the result is no longer a mirror of Theorem 1.3 for arbitrary submodular utilities: it is a new, structured public-goods problem whose complexity may be governed by the chosen utility language rather than continuization. The paper itself proves only polynomial time in the number of explicitly named voters, not in the number of compressed utility types, and provides no weighted-oracle theorem with the required dependence on \(\tau\) and encoding length.

That objection is nevertheless not enough to defeat the anchor. The weighted version is mathematically natural: replace sums over voters by mass-weighted sums, \(|S|/n\) by \(\lambda(S)\), and the Nash-welfare objective by \(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta\log U_\theta(x)\). The coalition bound, rounding expectation, and iterative elimination all appear to admit precisely this replacement. The committee remains integral, so this is genuinely population continuization rather than outcome-space fractionalization.

The additive case makes the negative position weaker still. An exact utility type is then just a vector \((u_{\theta j})_{j\in C}\), and large cohorts with identical public-project valuations are quite plausible. Theorem 1.4 and the paper’s unresolved computation of Lindahl equilibria therefore yield a clean high-multiplicity computational question. Calling that “already continuous” would be wrong: the paper’s fractional committees and Lindahl prices are internal machinery, while the proposed mirror keeps the selected committee integral.

Theorem 1.6 is not a computational anchor, but its six-gadget witness does survive as six types with mass \(1/6\), and can be replicated to arbitrarily large electorates. It therefore supplies a consistency check, not an objection.

So the best negative case identifies a real modelling gap—exact utility-type multiplicity and the missing weighted input model—but it does not support the universal conclusion. Theorem 1.3, and especially the additive variant of Theorem 1.4, remain worthwhile continuous-population mirrors.
