The continuous mirror question

Given a finite object set \(O\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_a\), and a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies a strict ranking \(P_t\) and positive weight \(w_t\), determine whether a mass-popular assignment exists and return one if it does. An assignment is \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\), including \(a=\emptyset\), with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,a}\le q_a\) for each object. It is mass-popular if no feasible reassignment makes the total weight of the mass strictly preferring its new assignment greater than the total weight of the mass strictly preferring its old assignment.

The model it lives in

Represent the population as rational mass \(\mu_t\) on complete types \(t\), each carrying a ranking \(P_t\) and weight \(w_t\). A feasible allocation assigns each portion of a type to one whole object or to \(\emptyset\), subject to per-capita capacities. A challenger is a feasible flow \(z_{t,a,b}\) from current assignment \(a\) to new assignment \(b\); it defeats \(x\) when \(\sum_{t,a,b:\,b\mathrel{P_t}a} w_t z_{t,a,b} > \sum_{t,a,b:\,a\mathrel{P_t}b} w_t z_{t,a,b}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the existence of weighted-popular assignments, including instances with no popular outcome. It does not carry over Proposition 1’s universal cumulative-order criterion or the paper’s mechanism characterizations, strategy-proofness, Nash implementation, and dispute-resolution results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the decision and search complexities of CWPA as functions of \(|O|\), support size \(|T|\), and input bit length?
  • Which weight and mass profiles guarantee a mass-popular assignment for every admissible preference and capacity instance?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational complexity result in this paper: it establishes no P, NP-hardness, FPT, or similar classification. So there is no qualifying complexity anchor to lift. The strongest nearby anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper: for a finite weight profile, if the weights are cumulatively ordered—each weight is at least the sum of all weights below it—then every preference-and-capacity problem has a \(w\)-popular matching; otherwise, some such problem has none. This is a structural existence result, not a complexity result.

The lead mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous Weighted-Popular Assignment Existence (CWPA). An instance has a finite object set \(O\), rational capacities \(q_a\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete agent types. A type \(t\) specifies a strict ranking \(P_t\) over \(O\cup\{\emptyset\}\) and a positive voting weight \(w_t\); its population mass \(\mu_t\) is rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A feasible answer is a mass allocation \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\), including \(a=\emptyset\), with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,a}\le q_a\) for every object. Each person still receives at most one whole object; \(x_{t,a}\) records how much of a type cohort receives each object.

Call \(x\) mass-popular if no feasible reassignment has greater total weight among people who prefer their new assignment. Precisely, a challenger is a flow \(z_{t,a,b}\ge 0\) of type-\(t\) mass moving from current assignment \(a\) to new assignment \(b\), with \(\sum_b z_{t,a,b}=x_{t,a}\) and destination capacities respected. It defeats \(x\) if

\[ \sum_{t,a,b:\,b\mathrel{P_t}a} w_t z_{t,a,b} > \sum_{t,a,b:\,a\mathrel{P_t}b} w_t z_{t,a,b}. \]

CWPA asks whether any feasible \(x\) has no defeating challenger, and returns such an \(x\) when one exists. Atomless cohorts make every such flow realizable by assigning the corresponding portions of each type. There is no separate cost objective: the target is immunity to a weighted-majority challenge.

A plausible regime is a large university housing or office-allocation system. Many members share rankings over standardized office or housing categories and share voting weights tied to their role or ownership share. The population might have tens of thousands of members but only dozens or hundreds of complete types. This preserves the paper’s shared-ownership conflict while making population mass and per-capita capacities the natural quantities.

The existence issue does survive fractional mass in a simple case. Take one type of mass \(1\), equal voting weight, three objects each with capacity \(1/3\), and ranking \(a\succ b\succ c\succ\emptyset\). A popular allocation must fill all three capacities. From any such allocation, reassign the \(a\)-holders to \(c\), the \(b\)-holders to \(a\), and the \(c\)-holders to \(b\). Two-thirds of the mass gains and one-third loses, so the reassignment defeats it. Thus this continuous instance has no mass-popular allocation. It is a direct continuous analogue of the paper’s no-existence phenomenon, even with support size \(\tau=1\).

My expectation for unrestricted CWPA is Class C, provisionally. For a fixed \(x\), finding its strongest challenger is a linear program over type-to-object flows, so checking a proposed allocation is tractable in \(m,\tau,L\). Finding \(x\) is harder: its popularity condition quantifies over challengers whose feasible flows depend on \(x\). That may admit a structural or LP reformulation, but the paper does not supply it. Since the paper has no hardness result, there is no hardness transfer to claim.

This mirror covers the paper’s existence question, especially Proposition 1’s distinction between universal existence and instances with no popular outcome. It does not claim a continuous version of the cumulative-order criterion. Nor would I lead with Theorem 1’s strategy-proof mechanism result: in a nonatomic population, one person’s report has zero mass, so unilateral incentive claims risk becoming vacuous unless the model gives reports positive-mass effects.

The weakest point is therefore also clear: the paper’s central named result here is structural, not computational, and its cumulative-order condition relies on individually weighted agents in a way that may not survive a continuum of infinitesimal agents. The positive case is that CWPA remains a precise computational question about the population itself, and the paper’s no-existence result gives it a genuine problem to study. Further questions are whether CWPA admits a structural existence criterion, whether its decision problem is tractable, and what continuum analogue—if any—replaces cumulative ordering.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor itself: this paper has no named computational complexity result. Proposition 1 is a structural theorem about a finite list of individually weighted agents. It characterizes when a popular matching exists for every preference and capacity problem with that weight profile. The other theorems characterize mechanisms and implementation. None gives an algorithm or a complexity classification for a computational problem.

CWPA is well-defined, but it changes Proposition 1’s question. It asks whether a popular allocation exists for one specified society and capacity profile. That is a plausible algorithmic problem, but it is not the paper’s cumulative-order characterization. A closer continuation of Proposition 1 would ask which continuum weight distributions guarantee existence for every admissible instance. That condition appears to collapse: any positive mass of one type can be split across three equally capacitated objects, with everyone ranking them \(a\succ b\succ c\). Cycling the assignments lets two-thirds of that mass improve while one-third loses. So the universal-existence claim fails even with one type. This exposes a real limit of the finite theorem’s logic: a continuum has no first individual whose weight can dominate the tail.

The proponent’s \(\tau=1\) example is valid, and a large housing or office system with repeated preference and weight types is plausible. But the example is the familiar three-agent popularity cycle expressed in mass units. It establishes that the pointwise question survives fractional allocations; it does not show that the paper’s distinctive result has a useful continuous analogue. The proposed algorithmic challenge—searching for an allocation that passes every challenger—is new work attached to the paper, not a computational result the paper supplies. The fact that checking a fixed allocation uses an LP does not establish that the search problem has an interesting complexity boundary.

There is a further obstacle to the paper’s mechanism results: a single person has zero mass, so unilateral strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. One could study deviations by positive-mass groups, but that changes the incentive concept and asks for a new characterization.

I would not claim that no one could make CWPA worth studying. The honest negative case is narrower: this paper supplies no computational result to mirror, its strongest existence theorem loses its nontrivial universal criterion in an atomless population, and the pointwise CWPA problem is a plausible new direction rather than a demonstrated continuation of the paper’s contribution. That is the best case against; the housing regime and CWPA remain the weak point in any universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
