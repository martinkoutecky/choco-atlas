In a population model, \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of contributors of type \(t\), and their effort choices would determine each topic’s aggregate quality and produced value. That scenario fits the paper’s application, but it does not supply a qualifying anchor.

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your criteria require. The closest results are Theorem 2, proved here, which says that any sharing rule satisfying the marginal-contribution condition induces a universally \((1,1)\)-smooth game; Corollary 1, which gives at least half the expected optimum at the stated equilibria; Theorem 3, proved here, which gives the \(\frac{2^{1-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}\) welfare guarantee for proportional sharing under \(v_j(Q_j)=w_jQ_j^\alpha\); and Theorem 4, which bounds inefficiency according to cost elasticity. A continuous-population version could ask about equilibrium welfare in that Q&A regime, but it would be a new analytic question: when each person has vanishing mass, the model must specify how individual shares and incentives scale. I would not present it as a continuous computational mirror anchored in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is the proponent’s concession: this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 prove equilibrium welfare guarantees; Theorems 3 and 4 refine those guarantees for particular value and cost functions. None classifies a computational problem or gives an algorithm or runtime. The paper’s continuous effort choices and Bayesian distributions over individual players’ types do not themselves make the population a continuous object.

The high-volume Q&A example is plausible, and I would not reject it because contributors can be grouped into a finite set of complete types. The difficulty is finding a computational question anchored in the paper. Its guarantees rely on strategic deviations by individual players and their marginal contributions. In a population limit, an individual has zero mass, so preserving meaningful incentives requires formulating a per-capita or mean-field game. That could be a sensible extension, but it raises a new equilibrium question rather than mirroring a computational result here.

The best rescue would be to ask for an algorithm to compute an equilibrium, optimize sharing rules, or find the welfare optimum for a finite-support distribution of contributor types. Those could be worthwhile questions. But the paper does not study their computational complexity: its optimum is a benchmark for welfare comparisons, not a computational problem it analyzes. Defining the input encodings and the new algorithmic task would be the main research move.

So I would reject this paper as an anchor for a continuous computational mirror. I can’t honestly defend the broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile work: a mean-field Q&A model might be valuable if developed as a new project. That possibility does not supply a mirror of the paper’s results.
