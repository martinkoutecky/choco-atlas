The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-valid case for a continuous mirror of this paper. It contains no named computational-complexity result: Proposition 1 establishes equilibrium existence, Lemma 5 characterizes limiting acceptance under majority rule, and Proposition 4 gives limiting majority-rule payoffs. Propositions 5–10 compare voters’ and proposers’ welfare across voting rules. None classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. So there are no qualifying anchors, and I can’t honestly state one continuous problem per anchor.

The most plausible setting for a follow-on would be a very large creditor group with relatively few recurring information and preference profiles. A type would record a creditor’s signal profile and preferences; its mass would be its share of the group. A continuous model could ask how an uninformed proposer should choose an offer when acceptance requires at least an \(\alpha\)-fraction of the mass to vote yes. But the paper’s central mechanism is that each finite voter conditions on being pivotal. In a truly atomless population, an individual has zero effect on the outcome, so that strategic voting condition disappears. Preserving it would need additional modeling, and any complexity classification would be new research rather than a mirror of a named result here.

The positive case is therefore limited: the paper studies large electorates and endogenous proposals, making its subject a plausible source of population-based computational questions. Its weakest point is decisive for this task: the paper asks and answers analytic equilibrium questions, not computational ones.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right to name no anchors. The paper has named propositions, but none is a computational result: Proposition 1 proves equilibrium existence; Lemmas 3 and 5 and Proposition 4 characterize limiting acceptance probabilities and payoffs; Propositions 5–10 compare welfare across voting rules. The paper gives no algorithmic problem with an input, output, or complexity classification. So there is no computational result here for a continuous mirror to preserve.

The proposed creditor-group scenario is plausible as a high-multiplicity regime; that is not the objection. The harder issue is that the paper’s voting mechanism depends on each finite voter conditioning on being pivotal. In an atomless population, one voter’s ballot changes the accepted mass by zero. The pivotal event that drives the paper’s cutoff behavior disappears, and in an unrefined nonatomic game voters can be indifferent between ballots that lead to the same aggregate outcome. Treating blocs as voters, or adding a perturbation to restore pivotality, could repair the model, but each would change the game.

A strong follow-on could ask for the complexity of computing an equilibrium or an optimal proposal from a finite rational distribution of signal and preference types. That would be a legitimate computational project. It would, however, computationalize an analytic bargaining model; it would not mirror a computational result in this paper. The direct large-population analysis is already there, in the paper’s \(n\to\infty\) results, and answers analytic questions rather than complexity questions.

So the best negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper*: it lacks a computational anchor, and its central strategic mechanism does not carry over unchanged to an atomless electorate. The absolute claim that no worthwhile continuous computational project could grow from the paper is weaker. The text cannot rule out a new project its authors would recognize; it only gives good reason to call that project an extension, not a mirror.
