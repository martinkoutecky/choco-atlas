The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. Its only numbered formal result is Theorem 1, proved here: under the hard-triplet condition \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta_T\mid G]\le 0\), with strict inequality on a set of positive probability, the Bradley–Terry population risk satisfies \(R(B,\lambda)<R(B,1)\) for every \(\lambda\in[0,1)\). That is a risk comparison, not a result placing an algorithm in a complexity class or proving hardness for a computational problem. The accuracy gains and projection-rank findings are empirical, not numbered theorems, propositions, or corollaries.

A nearby mirror would distribute participant–triplet types by mass and choose a nuisance weight \(\lambda\) to minimize aggregate Bradley–Terry risk. But that largely restates the population-risk analysis already in Theorem 1; it does not supply the requested computational anchor or a new computational-social-choice question about a continuous society. So under the stated anchor requirement, there is no honest positive case to build from this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about one limitation: Theorem 1 is not a complexity result, and minimizing its Bradley–Terry risk over \(\lambda\) would mostly repeat the paper’s analysis. But that does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. Theorem 1 gives a formal reason to suppress nuisance geometry; the paper then uses preference geometry for an expressly computational social-choice purpose: selecting representative statements and forming fair slates (§8).

A stronger mirror is a population control or robustness problem for that slate selection. Fix a finite pool of statements and let each type specify its full utility over that pool under the learned ideal-point geometry, together with its intervention costs. Given rational masses \(\mu_t\), a target slate \(S^\star\), and costs for moving mass between types, ask for the cheapest transfer that makes \(S^\star\) optimal under a utilitarian or fairness objective. The type includes every property this problem uses, so differences in wording or identity can be ignored when they leave utilities and costs unchanged.

This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for online deliberation: a large platform can have many participants sharing the same ratings or utility profile over a fixed statement pool. The objective measures population shares, so the relevant changes are percentages of participants, not one-person perturbations. For utilitarian slate selection, each competing slate gives a linear inequality in the post-intervention masses. There may be exponentially many competing slates, making the problem’s tractability depend on the corresponding separation or optimization problem—the kind of computational question ChoCo is designed to chart.

That question goes beyond the paper’s risk comparison, while remaining close to its stated application. The absence of a named complexity theorem weakens the direct theorem-to-theorem case, but it does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror can be found.
