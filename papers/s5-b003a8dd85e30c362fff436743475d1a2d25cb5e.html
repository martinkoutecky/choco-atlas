The continuous mirror question

Given a matroid \(M=(E,\mathcal I)\), a supplied Hamiltonian cyclic ordering, and query access to a common monotone valuation \(v\), with \(\chi(M)\le 2\), find independent cyclic intervals \(A,B\) partitioning \(E\) such that \(\bigl||A|-|B|\bigr|\le 1\) and the two bundles satisfy EF1 for \(v\). Interpret the output as assigning mass \(1/2\) to bundle \(A\) and mass \(1/2\) to bundle \(B\).

The model it lives in

A society of mass \(1\) has one type \(t=(M,v)\). Each good has supply \(1/2\) per capita. The allocation assigns mass \(1/2\) to each of two whole, independent bundles \(A\) and \(B\), so each unit of mass receives an integral bundle and the total use of every good matches its supply.

The objection that survived

The \(1/2\)-\(1/2\) cohort split is fixed in advance, so population aggregation adds no search beyond the finite two-bundle cut; the proponent acknowledges this, and it bounds the mirror’s computational novelty.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s common-valuation EF1 bipartition for Hamiltonian matroids with \(\chi(M)\le 2\). It does not cover Theorem 1’s heterogeneous-valuation epistemic result or the paper’s conjectures and open problems.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest positive anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper, together with its constructive “Algorithmic reading” and Algorithm 2. The theorem says: if \(M=(E,\mathcal I)\) is a Hamiltonian matroid with \(\chi(M)\le 2\), and two agents have the same monotone valuation \(v\), then \(E\) can be partitioned into two independent sets that form an EF1 allocation and differ in size by at most one. The paper gives an explicit construction from a Hamiltonian cyclic ordering. It does not state a formal complexity classification; the \(O(m)\) value-query bound below is inferred from that construction. The paper has no named NP-hardness or other complexity-class result.

My lead mirror is Half-Supply Hamiltonian EF1. Given such a matroid, a cyclic ordering certifying Hamiltonicity, and a common monotone valuation \(v\), let a continuum of agents have one type \(t=(M,v)\) with mass \(1\). Each good \(e\in E\) has supply \(1/2\) per capita. Find independent sets \(A,B\) such that \(A\sqcup B=E\), their sizes differ by at most one, and
\[ v(A)\ge v(B\setminus\{g\})\quad\text{for some }g\in B,\qquad v(B)\ge v(A\setminus\{g\})\quad\text{for some }g\in A, \]
with the usual convention for an empty bundle. The allocation assigns mass \(1/2\) to the whole bundle \(A\) and mass \(1/2\) to the whole bundle \(B\). Thus every agent receives an integral, feasible bundle; only the type-level allocation is fractional. This is a feasibility problem, with no separate welfare objective.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many interchangeable teams, all with the same valuation, each assigned a feasible forest or other independent set of network resources. If there are \(N\) teams, take \(N/2\) copies of each good and assign half the teams bundle \(A\), half bundle \(B\). Each good’s supply and use are then both \(N/2\). The number of agents grows while the number of types stays at \(\tau=1\). The common valuation and constraint are genuine features of this regime, not information lost by aggregating types. The mass allocation is a cohort allocation, not a lottery that gives individuals fractional goods.

Theorem 2 proves this mirror feasible: its partition gives exactly the two bundle types required. Algorithm 2 searches cyclic intervals and needs \(O(m)\) valuation queries when the Hamiltonian ordering is supplied; with exact rational oracle values of bit length \(L\), the remaining work is polynomial in \(m\) and \(L\). I would therefore expect this narrowly specified problem to be Class A, tractable. The authors should recognize it as their common-valuation EF1 bipartition result lifted to replicated cohorts, though it is an extension through per-capita supply scaling.

This mirror covers Theorem 2 only. It does not cover Theorem 1’s heterogeneous-valuation epistemic EF1 existence result: Algorithm 1 depends on EF1-coloring and epistemic-bundle membership routines, and the paper does not establish their polynomial running times. Natural next questions are whether the mass formulation extends to several valuation types, whether it can handle type-specific matroid constraints, and whether larger support is needed for those extensions.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 2 is a constructive existence result, not a stated complexity theorem, and its continuous lift simply repeats the two-bundle solution across clones. The mass variables add little computational content, and no discrete hardness is available here to ask whether it dissolves. If the anchor rule requires a numbered theorem explicitly classifying a problem as being in \(P\) or hard, then this paper has no fully qualifying anchor; Theorem 2 and Algorithm 2 are the closest defensible basis for a positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with what this paper actually establishes. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are existence results, not complexity classifications. Theorem 2 does have a constructive algorithmic reading, so it would be too strong to call it nonalgorithmic: given a Hamiltonian ordering, the cyclic search uses \(O(m)\) valuation queries. But that bound is inferred from the procedure, not asserted in a numbered complexity result with an input model. Theorem 1’s algorithmic reading is conditional on EF1Color and IsEpiBundle routines whose complexity the paper does not establish. Under ChoCo’s computational brief, the paper offers no named complexity result to continue.

The proposed half-supply mirror is a coherent interpretation. Many interchangeable teams receiving replicated network resources are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and scaling supplies is an admissible co-modification. The difficulty is that the stated mirror has no population decision to compute: it fixes one type of mass \(1\), then asks for the same two bundles \(A,B\) as Theorem 2. The cohort split of mass \(1/2\) is imposed by the formulation. Its algorithm is therefore the paper’s cyclic cut, applied to the original finite instance. That makes it a reasonable application of the theorem, but a thin basis for a continuous computational result.

A richer version could let several agent types receive mass over feasible bundles, or consider more than two cohort bundles. That might produce a substantial continuous allocation problem. But it would need new modeling and new results: Theorem 2 covers only two agents with a common valuation, while Theorem 1’s constructive reading leaves its key subroutines unresolved. Those extensions are promising questions, but they are not computational findings of this paper that a mirror can carry over.

So the negative case is strongest on the programme’s stated standard: the paper supplies no established complexity result, and the one proposed mirror changes the interpretation of a finite constructive result more than it opens a new complexity landscape. It is weaker as a universal claim. The replicated-team scenario is sensible, and the paper’s explicit algorithm makes a continuous formulation easy to solve. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror; I can argue that this paper does not yet make a strong case for one.
