The continuous mirror question

Given firm types \(F\) and worker types \(W\), rational supplies \(\alpha_f\) and \(\beta_w\) summing to one on each side, shared strict rankings, acceptable pairs \(E\), and rational pair welfare values \(v_{fw}\), maximize \(\sum_{(f,w)\in E}v_{fw}x_{fw}\). Require \(x_{fw}\ge 0\), zero flow outside \(E\), and \(\sum_w x_{fw}\le\alpha_f\), \(\sum_f x_{fw}\le\beta_w\). For every \((f,w)\in E\), require either \(\sum_{i:\,f\succ_w i}x_{iw}=0\) or \(\alpha_f-\sum_jx_{fj}+\sum_{j:\,w\succ_f j}x_{fj}=0\), so no positive mass of employed workers and firms form a blocking pair.

The model it lives in

A rational mass matching over complete preference and welfare types, with supply capacities and exact worker-quasi-stability expressed by a disjunction for each acceptable type pair; optimize total welfare.

The objection that survived

The stated linear mass constraints admit a forbidden blocking pair; a faithful type-mass model needs a disjunction, whose complexity and relation to finite repeated-type markets the paper does not establish.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror asks about welfare optimization over repeated types under worker-quasi-stability. It does not transfer Theorem 2’s integral polytope or LP guarantee to the exact mass model, and leaves the paper’s finite-market geometric proof untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of welfare maximization for the disjunctive mass model in the number of types and the input bit length?
  • How does this model relate to finite markets formed by replicating the same types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror I see is worker-quasi-stable welfare maximization in a large market of repeated types. Picture a regional placement market with many applicants and positions but relatively few complete profiles: applicants share credential and preference profiles, while positions share employer preferences. For example, there might be hundreds of thousands of agents but only a few dozen types. A type includes every feature used by the model, including its preferences and welfare values.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that the extreme points of \(C_{\mathcal Q}\)—the polytope defined by the paper’s capacity, acceptability, and worker-quasi-stability inequalities—are exactly the incidence vectors of worker-quasi-stable matchings. The theorem is geometric rather than explicitly phrased as a complexity classification, but the paper’s conclusion draws the computational consequence: because this is a polynomial-size polytope, any linear objective over worker-quasi-stable matchings can be optimized in polynomial time. The paper has no comparable hardness result, so this is its strongest computational anchor.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Type-Mass Worker-Quasi-Stable Welfare Maximization. An instance has finite sets of firm types \(F\) and worker types \(W\), rational supplies \(\alpha_f\) and \(\beta_w\) for each type, strict rankings over the opposite side’s types, a set \(E\) of mutually acceptable type pairs, and rational welfare values \(v_{fw}\) for acceptable pairs. Normalize the total mass on each side to one. The decision is to find a mass matching \(x=(x_{fw})\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{(f,w)\in E} v_{fw}x_{fw}. \]
A solution is an optimal rational mass assignment satisfying one-to-one capacities,
\[ \sum_w x_{fw}\le\alpha_f,\qquad \sum_f x_{fw}\le\beta_w,\qquad x_{fw}\ge0, \]
with \(x_{fw}=0\) outside \(E\), and, for every \((f,w)\in E\), the paper’s worker-quasi-stability inequality in mass form:
\[ \sum_{i\succ_w f}x_{iw}+ \sum_{j\succ_f w}x_{fj}+x_{fw} \;\ge\; \sum_i x_{iw}. \]
This keeps the paper’s one-to-one market and its worker-quasi-stability condition; the action is a transfer of mass between type pairs, and the objective is social welfare.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. With the type lists explicit, the mass assignment is a linear optimization problem with \(O(|F||W|)\) variables and constraints, so it is polynomial in the type counts and the input bit length. The mirror covers the paper’s characterization and its consequence for linear welfare objectives. It does not claim that Theorem 2 proves integrality for the aggregated type-mass polytope: real-valued mass assignments need no integrality result to optimize this objective.

The weakest point is the link from the paper’s individual-agent model to this type-level one. The paper assumes strict rankings over named agents; the mirror ranks counterpart types, treating agents within a type as interchangeable. The mass inequalities are a direct type-level continuation of the paper’s constraints, but the paper does not prove that they exactly characterize limits or projections of worker-quasi-stable matchings in replicated finite markets. That is a meaningful follow-up: determine whether this mass polytope is the right aggregate model for pointwise no-blocking behavior, and establish a precise bridge to finite markets with many repeated types. The authors should recognize the question as a worker-quasi-stable matching problem, though they may view the type-level formulation and its bridge as the new work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 2, and it is a geometric characterization, not a complexity result for a named computational problem. The conclusion says linear objectives can be optimized over its polytope, but that follows directly from the finite-agent formulation, with variables for named firm–worker pairs. The paper gives no high-multiplicity input model or population-complexity question. So the proposed welfare problem is an addition to the paper, not a computational problem its theorem studies.

There is also a semantic problem with the proposed mass constraints. Consider firm types \(f,g,h\) with supplies \(1,\frac14,1\), and worker types \(w,a,b,z\) with supplies \(1,\frac12,\frac14,\frac12\). Let the acceptable pairs be \((f,w),(f,a),(g,w),(h,w),(h,b)\). Set preferences \(w:h\succ f\succ g\), \(f:a\succ w\), and \(h:b\succ w\); \(a,b,z\) have only the acceptable partners just listed. Assign mass
\[ x_{fa}=\tfrac12,\qquad x_{gw}=\tfrac14,\qquad x_{hw}=\tfrac34,\qquad x_{hb}=\tfrac14. \]
The proposed inequality holds for every acceptable pair. In particular, for \((f,w)\), its left side is \(\tfrac34+\tfrac12\), while its right side is \(1\). Yet the assignment has a positive mass of unmatched \(f\) firms and a positive mass of \(w\) workers employed at \(g\); those workers prefer \(f\), and those firms prefer them to being unmatched. That is a blocking pair involving employed workers, which worker-quasi-stability forbids.

A more faithful continuum definition would have to rule out such positive-mass blocking pairs directly. For each acceptable type pair, that is a support condition: either no employed \(w\)-mass is assigned below \(f\), or no \(f\)-mass is assigned below \(w\) or left unmatched. This is a disjunction, not the paper’s integral polytope. Theorem 2 does not establish that the resulting model is tractable or even that its feasible set has the same geometry. If one keeps the proposed linear constraints, the problem is an easy LP with the wrong behavioral meaning; if one repairs them, one has a new model whose central result is not a mirror of Theorem 2. And the finite-agent welfare problem already has the paper’s polynomial-time LP formulation.

That is the strongest case against this anchor, but it is not airtight. A well-specified atomless matching model with type-level preferences could still be a worthwhile adjacent problem. What the paper does not provide is a compelling computational gap that makes this particular theorem a strong reason for ChoCo to pursue it.
