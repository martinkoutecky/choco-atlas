The continuous mirror question

Given an explicit finite support \(\{(\Delta_t,g_t)\}_{t=1}^{\tau}\) of claimant-case types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), compute \(M=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{PCA}_{\mathrm{sst}}(\Delta_t,g_t)]\), the population share whose queried literal is possibly credulously accepted under semi-stable semantics; equivalently, for rational \(q\in[0,1]\), decide whether \(M\ge q\). Each \(\Delta_t\) is an explicitly represented incomplete argumentation framework, and completions remain internal possibilities within each type.

The model it lives in

A continuous distribution over complete claimant-case types, each consisting of an iAF and its query literal; the objective is the mass of types satisfying the paper’s possible credulous acceptance predicate.

The objection that survived

The population statistic decomposes into separate \(\mathrm{PCA}_{\mathrm{sst}}\) evaluations, and Theorem 6 supplies no shared intervention or algorithm across cases. This limits the mirror to population-level prevalence, but does not make that question ill-posed.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the possible credulous acceptance result under semi-stable semantics in Theorem 6, aggregated across claimant-case types. It leaves the paper’s other acceptance, verification, totality, determinism, functionality, equivalence, and probabilistic results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is for the paper’s possible credulous acceptance problem under semi-stable semantics, lifted from one incomplete argumentation framework to a high-volume population of argumentation cases.

The anchor is Theorem 6, a result of this paper; its full proofs are given in the technical report cited by the paper. The entry I rely on is: deciding whether a literal \(g\) is possibly credulously accepted under semi-stable semantics—whether there is some completion of the iAF and some semi-stable extension of that completion containing \(g\)—is \(\Sigma^p_2\)-complete.

A plausible regime is a large public-benefit appeals service applying recurring argument maps to many claimants’ cases. Each claimant’s case is represented by an iAF: arguments and attacks capture reasons for and against the claim, while uncertain arguments or attacks capture unresolved facts. The query literal is the same claim, such as “eligible,” across cases. Many claimants may have one of a relatively small number \(\tau\) of recurring argumentation types, even when the total population is very large. Here, two claimants have the same type exactly when their iAF and queried literal are identical. The mass of a type is the fraction of claimants with that case structure.

This yields a precise problem, Mass-PCA\(_{\mathrm{sst}}\). An instance consists of a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of types \((\Delta_t,g)\), each with its rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t=1\), and each \(\Delta_t\) is an explicitly represented iAF. For each type define \(z_t=1\) if \(g\) is possibly credulously accepted under semi-stable semantics in \(\Delta_t\), and \(z_t=0\) otherwise. The task is to return the exact accepted population share
\[ M=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t z_t. \]
Its threshold version also takes a rational \(q\in[0,1]\) and asks whether \(M\ge q\). The solution is the exact rational \(M\), or the yes/no answer for the threshold version. The relevant input measures are the graph descriptions, support size \(\tau\), and bit lengths of the masses.

The lift preserves the paper’s acceptance question within each case. The new quantity is the fraction of the population whose case admits a completion and semi-stable extension supporting the queried literal. Population mass is over claimants or cases; it does not replace the iAF’s completions with probabilities. A completion remains an internal possibility in one type. This distinction matters because the paper’s probabilistic acceptance model is not itself a continuous population.

I expect hardness to transfer. With support size one and mass \(1\), \(M\) is either \(0\) or \(1\), so computing it decides the original PCA\(_{\mathrm{sst}}\) instance. The threshold version with \(q=1/2\) does the same. Thus Theorem 6 gives \(\Sigma^p_2\)-hardness even in the one-type case. With arbitrary support, one can compute \(M\) using one PCA decision per type; this observation does not establish a better complexity bound. The mirror may support batching or reuse across recurring structures, but the anchor gives no such algorithm.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 6’s PCA\(_{\mathrm{sst}}\) result. It does not continuize the argument set, the completion space, or the probabilistic acceptance measure, and it does not claim to cover the paper’s totality, determinism, and functionality results.

The weak point is that the paper studies acceptance in an individual iAF, while this mirror measures acceptance across a distribution of cases. Its continuous object is the population of claimants, not the arguments inside a single dispute. Authors might regard the population-level coverage query as a useful deployment question built from their predicate rather than as their central problem. Still, it keeps their predicate and semantics intact, gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime with \(\tau\) recurring case types, and states a direct continuous-population question whose hardness follows from their named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is a genuine computational result: possible credulous acceptance under semi-stable semantics is \(\Sigma^p_2\)-complete. The strongest objection is to what Mass-PCA adds around it. For each type, acceptance is settled entirely inside that type’s iAF. No completion, extension, or argument from one type constrains another. The population masses then form a weighted average of the resulting yes/no labels. That is a coherent batch statistic, but the paper’s problem is still being solved separately for each represented case.

The claimant-service scenario makes repeated iAFs plausible, and exact repetition is a legitimate high-multiplicity regime. Yet the proposed output measures how often a case *admits some completion and extension* supporting the claim. It says nothing about how likely that completion is, or how cases should be decided collectively. The paper’s probabilistic acceptance machinery does not supply those missing interpretations: it assigns probabilities within a framework, while Mass-PCA weights cases. I would treat the proposed quantity as a potentially useful service metric, but a weak mirror of the paper’s acceptance problem.

A more consequential version could give an agency a shared budget to resolve uncertainty across case types and ask it to maximize the mass of cases where the claim is possibly accepted. That would make the population distribution matter to an optimization problem. But the paper supplies no review actions, costs, or shared intervention model. Adding them could motivate a new continuous control problem; Theorem 6 would provide a per-case subroutine, rather than anchor a continuization of a problem the paper studies.

A direct continuum of arguments runs into a different issue. Dung semantics use a graph of individually related arguments: attacks and defenses are pointwise, while preferred and semi-stable semantics involve maximal sets. Masses of argument types do not specify who attacks whom. Preserving that information calls for an attack kernel and a definition of extensions over it; an individual target argument also has zero mass in an atomless population. Giving the target a positive-mass cohort, or defining acceptance as a fraction of a cohort, is possible, but introduces new semantic choices. If types instead represent exact duplicates with identical interactions, their multiplicity does not act as a social weight in the paper’s semantics.

So the case against this anchor is that its clean population lift is mainly a weighted report over independent reasoning instances, while more coupled lifts require a substantial new model. That is a real weakness in the proponent’s case, not a proof that no worthwhile extension exists. A distributional audit or a carefully designed argumentation-control model might be worth pursuing; the paper alone does not establish either as a continuous mirror of Theorem 6.
