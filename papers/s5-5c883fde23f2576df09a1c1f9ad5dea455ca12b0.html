The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result: no theorem or proposition says that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Its closest named results are Theorem 2.2, proved here, which guarantees a committee approved by at least \(\binom{k}{j}/\binom{n}{j}\) of any voter distribution, and Theorem 5.1, also proved here, which gives a stronger guarantee for distributions supported in a sufficiently small Johnson-graph ball. Both are popularity guarantees, not computational results, so neither qualifies as an anchor under your criteria.

There is a natural computational follow-up: given a support \(S\subseteq\binom{[n]}{j}\) with rational masses \(P(\ell)\), find a \(k\)-element committee maximizing \(\sum_{\ell\subseteq C}P(\ell)\). But that is not a result established by this paper. Moreover, the paper already models voter data as a distribution over lists, with each mass equal to a fraction of voters. That makes the population-continuous interpretation natural, while also making it hard to present as a new mirror of the paper’s model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no result that fits ChoCo’s computational gate. Theorem 2.2 and Theorem 5.1 are existence guarantees for a committee’s approval proportion; neither classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithm. They are the closest named results, but making them anchors would turn a programme about computational questions into one about continuizing extremal theorems.

There is also no new population model to obtain from those theorems. The paper already represents voter data by a distribution \(P\) over lists, with \(P(\ell)\) the fraction of voters submitting \(\ell\), and states both guarantees in terms of that distribution. A mirror of either theorem would therefore restate a result already proved for a continuous population. This is a collision with the paper’s own model, rather than an objection to high multiplicity as a regime.

The strongest candidate follow-up is to compute a committee maximizing \(\pi_P(C)=\sum_{\ell\subseteq C}P(\ell)\). That could be a worthwhile computational project, but it is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper: the paper proves bounds on the optimum’s value, not a complexity result about finding an optimizer. Its population is already represented by \(P\), so asking for an algorithm over rational masses would add a computational question without adding a continuization.

This negative case is strongest against calling the work a continuous mirror. It cannot rule out the optimizer problem as a separate research direction; it shows why that direction would need to be justified on its own, rather than counted as a mirror of this paper.
