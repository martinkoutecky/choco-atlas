The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item types, bidder types \(t\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and explicitly represented XOS valuations \(v_t\), compute or approximate the maximum welfare \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\) subject to \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), \(\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\).

The model it lives in

A configuration-LP model with bidder types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), item capacities \(b_j\), and bundle-mass variables \(x_{t,S}\); each unit of mass receives an integral bundle \(S\), while welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). XOS pricing separates the dual constraints in polynomial time.

The mirror covers Theorem \(3.13\)'s XOS-language inapproximability result. It leaves the CF approximation algorithms, incentive-compatible mechanism, communication lower bounds, other XOS results, Theorem \(3.14\), and Theorem \(A.1\) outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s XOS welfare problem. I would make this the lead mirror because it preserves the paper’s main combinatorial object while turning its discrete bidder granularity into population mass.

The anchor is Theorem 3.13, proved in this paper. It states that it is NP-hard to approximate the optimal allocation among bidders whose valuations are given in the XOS language within any constant factor less than \(e/(e-1)\). The proof reduces from MAX-\(k\)-COVER: there are \(k\) bidders with the same XOS valuation, one additive clause for each set in the MAX-\(k\)-COVER instance, and value \(1\) for every item appearing in that clause. Choosing \(k\) covering sets corresponds to allocating bundles to the \(k\) bidders. Thus the theorem’s hardness comes from requiring an integral allocation of the items among individually represented bidders.

My continuous problem would be called XOS-Mass Welfare Allocation\(_\infty\).

There are \(m\) item types \(M=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), bidder types \(t\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational per-capita supply \(b_j\) of each item type \(j\). Type \(t\) has an explicitly represented XOS valuation \(v_t\), namely \(v_t(S)=\max_{\ell\in L_t}\sum_{j\in S}a_{t\ell j}\), for every bundle \(S\subseteq M\).

The decision variable is \(x_{t,S}\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(S\). It must satisfy \(\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\) for every item \(j\). The objective is to maximize \(W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).

The question is: given \((M,\mu,(v_t),b)\), compute an optimal feasible mass allocation, or approximate its welfare within a specified factor. A solution is a finite-support list of type–bundle pairs and their masses. Each individual agent still receives an integral bundle; only the measure of agents receiving each bundle is fractional.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the auction. For a finite population of size \(N\), take \(N\mu_t\) bidders of type \(t\) and \(Nb_j\) copies of item \(j\). The continuous problem is the limit in which these counts become mass variables. A natural scenario is a large marketplace serving many recurring cohorts of nearly identical customers, with a small number \(\tau\) of valuation templates and item inventories scaling with the cohort size. The paper’s own reduction is especially supportive: all \(k\) bidders have the same XOS valuation, so the mirror can have \(\tau=1\) while the number of agents tends to infinity.

The per-capita item supply is a necessary co-modification, not a switch to divisible outcomes. With finitely many indivisible items and a continuum of agents, almost every agent would receive nothing. Scaling item copies with the population preserves the auction’s integral bundle decision while making the population itself continuous.

I expect this mirror to be in Class A: the exact continuous problem is polynomial-time solvable for explicit XOS types. Its configuration LP is precisely the optimization problem above. The dual has a price \(p_j\) for each item and a variable \(u_t\) for each bidder type, with constraints \(u_t+\sum_{j\in S}p_j\ge v_t(S)\) for every bundle \(S\). Separation requires solving \(\max_S(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j)\). For an XOS valuation this equals \(\max_{\ell\in L_t}\sum_j\max\{0,a_{t\ell j}-p_j\}\), which is computable in polynomial time from the XOS expression. Ellipsoid or column-generation methods therefore solve the LP exactly, and a basic optimum has polynomially many positive bundle-mass variables.

Thus the continuous mirror predicts that the hardness in Theorem 3.13 does not transfer. The MAX-\(k\)-COVER set system remains present, as do the \(m\) item types, but the requirement that \(k\) named bidders each choose one integral bundle disappears. In the high-multiplicity market, mass can be distributed among bundles, while every unit of mass still receives an integral bundle. The discrete inapproximability barrier is therefore replaced by an exact pricing-based LP algorithm.

The mirror covers Theorem 3.13 only. It does not claim that arbitrary complement-free valuations, value-oracle access, or the paper’s communication lower bounds become tractable. Further questions include how rapidly finite-\(N\) integral auctions converge to the continuous optimum, whether one obtains explicit additive rounding guarantees, and where tractability fails for more general valuation classes or weaker oracle models.

The weakest point is the required scaling of item inventories. A referee insisting that the original auction must retain exactly one copy of each item could reasonably say that this is a different problem. The reply is that such scaling is forced by continuizing the bidder population while retaining meaningful integral bundles, and is explicitly the kind of co-modification the continuization programme permits. The resulting problem still has the paper’s bidders, XOS valuations, bundles, welfare objective, and approximation question; only the high-multiplicity regime has changed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 3.13 is not fundamentally about a population of interchangeable agents. Its reduction uses \(k\) bidders as \(k\) separate bundle choices in MAX-\(k\)-COVER. If the item set remains finite and indivisible while bidders become a continuum, almost all agents receive nothing and the welfare normalization degenerates. If item supplies are scaled with population size, as the proposed mirror does, the model becomes a fractional allocation or lottery over bundles with per-capita capacity \(1/k\). The paper’s auction has then been replaced by its configuration-LP relaxation; the discrete bidder constraint that creates the theorem has been removed by definition.

This objection is strongest when the application is meant to be an auction with a fixed catalogue of individually available goods. A continuum of customers does not naturally imply \(N\) copies of every item, and the proposed marketplace story does not establish that such synchronized supply scaling is economically intrinsic rather than mathematically convenient. Nor does an arbitrary XOS valuation template make the resulting mass distribution a compelling societal type space: it merely says that many agents have been declared identical so that their decisions can be split fractionally.

But this does not defeat the best mirror under the programme’s rules. Take \(N k\) identical bidders and \(N\) copies of every item. After normalization, the population has one bidder type of mass \(1\), each item has per-capita supply \(1/k\), and every individual still receives an integral bundle. As \(N\) grows, the empirical allocation converges to exactly the proposed mass allocation. The scaling is therefore a genuine high-multiplicity regime, not an illicit continuization of outcomes. Its configuration LP has polynomial-time separation for explicitly represented XOS valuations, so it is a meaningful Class A result: the paper’s MAX-\(k\)-COVER hardness disappears under high multiplicity.

Thus I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The paper contains clear computational results, bidder multiplicity is sensible, welfare is anonymous, and the continuum does not degenerate once supply is scaled. The negative case can question the economic naturalness of that scaling, but it cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
