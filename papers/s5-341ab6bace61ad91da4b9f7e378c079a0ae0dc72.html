The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational complexity result of the required kind, so I can’t give a compliant anchor-based case for a continuous mirror.

The closest positive foothold is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. It says that for fixed odd \(d\) and a slow-growth function \(f\), there is a \(p_0<1\) such that, whenever \(p_0<p<1\), every sequence of finite graphs \(G_n\in\bdd(f,d)\) with \(|V_n|\to\infty\) satisfies \(\delta(G_n,p)\to0\); it also says \(\delta(G,p)=0\) for every infinite \(G\in\bdd(f,d)\). This makes a large-population regime with bounded local structure a natural setting for studying information retention. But the theorem is a probabilistic statement about reconstruction, not an algorithmic or complexity classification. Theorem 7 is similarly about reconstruction on invariant random subgraphs.

So there is no valid anchor here, and thus no per-anchor continuous problem to state. A type-mass model for network positions might be worth exploring, but the paper’s dynamics depend on graph structure and correlations, which masses over types alone may not capture. More fundamentally, this paper asks analytic and information-theoretic questions; it does not chart the computational landscape the continuization programme targets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 8 gives a large-population regime: for certain bounded-growth graph families, the probability of reconstructing \(S\) from the eventual opinions tends to zero as the finite populations grow. But it is a probabilistic guarantee, not a theorem about the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Theorem 7 is likewise an identifiability result for random subgraphs. The other numbered results give combinatorial bounds or convergence claims; none classifies a computational task.

Those theorems do support the claim that large populations are sensible in this setting. They do not make the population continuous in ChoCo’s sense. The infinite graphs still consist of countably many individually located agents, and the finite-graph limit has no canonical distribution over types that captures the whole instance. A distribution over local agent types loses the graph’s wiring and the dependencies it creates. A richer continuum object could retain that information, but the resulting question would be about inference in a continuum network process. That could be an interesting new project; it would not be a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The best candidate for an algorithmic extension is control: choose initial opinions that make the dynamics preserve or reveal a desired truth. Theorem 4, however, only says that no finite set can force consensus on an infinite locally finite graph. In an atomless continuum, a finite set has zero mass, so its inability to control the population risks becoming a measure-zero observation. Asking for the minimum positive mass needed for a desired outcome would avoid that degeneracy, but it would introduce a new optimization problem absent from the paper.

So the case against a mirror is strong for this paper: Theorems 7 and 8 are the proponent’s most promising footholds, and neither supplies the computational anchor the programme requires. The claim that no worthwhile follow-on question could be built from these models would go too far; network control or inference might be worth studying. But that would be a new computational direction, not continuization of this paper’s results.
