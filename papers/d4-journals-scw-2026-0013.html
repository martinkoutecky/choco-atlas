What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror covers a result; the four implementation theorems are the only plausible anchors, while the value definitions and broader mechanism discussion are not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest case that meets the anchor requirement. The paper has no named computational result about an algorithm, running time, or complexity class.

The closest candidates are Theorems 3.1, 3.3, 3.5, and 3.6, proved in the paper. They establish that mechanisms A–D implement, respectively, the externality-free Shapley value, the expected stand-alone value, the consensus value, and the extended Shapley value in subgame-perfect equilibrium for zero-monotonic partition function form games. Those are results about strategic implementation, not the computational complexity of finding a value or solving an underlying problem. Treating one as a complexity anchor would misstate what it proves.

A continuous population version is imaginable: types could be complete payoff descriptions over coalitions and coalition structures, and mass could represent the fraction of players with each description. But the paper’s mechanisms choose individual proposers and recursively remove named players. Extending those mechanisms to a continuum would require substantial new modeling, and the paper offers no computational result to anchor that extension. So I have no qualifying mirror question to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the proponent’s own: the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 3.1, 3.3, 3.5, and 3.6 all say that, under zero-monotonicity, a specified finite-player mechanism has a specified value as its subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome. None asserts a complexity bound or an algorithm. Asking how to compute those equilibria or values in a continuum would add a computational problem the paper does not study; asking whether a continuum mechanism has an equilibrium would be an analytic implementation question, outside ChoCo’s stated focus. None of the four theorems supplies a qualifying anchor.

The best population-based reconstruction would restrict attention to games where players with the same payoff type are interchangeable, represent their shares by a distribution, and ask for limiting allocations as the number of players grows. That is a conceivable research direction, but it runs into the mechanism’s central feature: a single named proposer is chosen, rejected, and removed before bargaining continues among the remaining players. If agents are truly nonatomic, one proposer and one removal have zero mass, while the mechanism’s finite recursive sequence has no direct mass-level counterpart. If a whole type mass instead proposes, bids, or accepts as a bloc, the model gives type members joint strategic power that the paper’s individual players do not have. Keeping individual proposers preserves the finite strategic actors; replacing them with mass-level blocs changes the bargaining institution. Either route produces a new model, not a continuous mirror of one of these implementation results.

There may be worthwhile continuum bargaining models for symmetric partition-function games, or limits of the four values under particular payoff assumptions. The paper does not rule those out. But they would need their own motivating computational question and results; the proposed type distribution alone does not supply them. So the negative case is strongest on programme fit and weakest as a universal impossibility claim: it supports “no worthwhile mirror anchored in this paper,” not “no worthwhile continuous model could ever be built from these ideas.”
