What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers the geometry-free slate threshold in Theorem 2.4; it leaves the asymptotic slate result and the target-protection guarantee in Theorem 2.6 aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity model here, but the paper is a poor source of a *new* continuous mirror: it already models the population continuously. Also, it contains no named theorem asserting a complexity classification such as NP-hardness, membership in P, or FPT. The closest computational foothold is an exact solution theorem.

For a statewide idealization, let the two types be voters loyal to Player 1 and Player 2. Their masses are \(p\) and \(1-p\), with \(N\) residents represented by those masses and \(N\) much larger than the two-type support. A district has population \(1/n\). On each turn, one player partitions the remaining mass into equal-population districts; the other freezes one, and the process continues. Player 1 maximizes the number of districts in which their loyalty has at least half the mass; Player 2 minimizes it. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime if residents of the same loyalty type are interchangeable. It deliberately leaves geography out, as does the paper’s geometry-free slate analysis.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 2.4, proved in this paper. It states that for the paper’s \(n\)-district game on \([0,n]\), with Player 1’s loyal mass \(s\), the optimal-play slate satisfies \(\sigma(n,s)\ge k\) if and only if

\[ s \succeq \begin{cases} \dfrac{(n-1)!!\,(2k+[2\nmid n]-2)!!} {2(2k+[2\nmid n]-3)!!(n-2)!!}, & k\le n/2,\\[1.2em] n-\dfrac{n!!\,(2(n-k)-[2\nmid n]+1)!!} {2(2(n-k)-[2\nmid n])!!(n-1)!!}, & k>n/2. \end{cases} \]

Here \([2\nmid n]\) is \(1\) for odd \(n\) and \(0\) otherwise, and \(\succeq\) means \(\ge\) when ties favor Player 1 and \(>\) when they favor Player 2.

The corresponding prover-ready question is ICYF-Threshold\(_\infty\): given \(n\), a rational loyalty fraction \(p\), a tie-breaking rule, and \(k\), does Player 1 have a strategy guaranteeing at least \(k\) seats under this alternating cut-and-freeze protocol? A solution is the yes/no answer, equivalently the exact optimal-play slate. Theorem 2.4 answers this question for the geometry-free continuous model by the displayed threshold, with \(s=np\). Its formula gives an exact algorithm polynomial in \(n\) and the rational input length when \(n\) is unary or treated as a parameter; the theorem itself does not claim a complexity class for binary-encoded \(n\).

The expected direction is therefore tractable in this continuous model, and already settled by the paper. Further questions would add connected or compact districts, or compare this continuous answer with the finite-resident game, including whether rounding finite populations changes the optimal slate.

That is the strongest positive case I can make: the model is natural for a large population with few loyalty types, and the authors plainly recognize the mass-based problem. Its weakness is decisive for novelty. The paper already studies the continuous-population question and proves its answer, so this would largely restate their result rather than expose a new complexity landscape. It also supplies no discrete hardness anchor whose hardness might dissolve in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s Theorem 2.4 anchor is also the strongest reason against a *new* continuous mirror. This is a genuine model collision: the paper already treats the state as divisible mass, tracks each party’s loyalty by measure, and solves the optimal-play slate game over those masses. In its notation, the proposed two-type society is just the same game with \(s=np\). Theorem 2.4 gives the exact threshold for whether Player 1 can guarantee \(k\) seats. Calling that game \(\mathrm{ICYF\text{-}Threshold}_\infty\) makes the population interpretation explicit, but does not create a new computational question. This is not merely an answer found elsewhere to a fresh mirror; the paper’s own model is already continuous.

A finite-population version is the best attempt to make the anchor distinct: define indivisible voters of two loyalty types, require equal-size districts, and ask whether the continuous threshold approximates the finite game as the population grows. That could support a useful bridge result. But the paper does not formulate that finite game, and the choices needed to define it—especially how indivisible voters may be assigned on each turn—create a new variant. Its continuous side is already solved. This would be a comparison with the paper’s model, not a new continuous-population problem or a new complexity landscape.

Switching to target protection does not uncover a new mirror either. Theorem 2.6 already bounds how much of an arbitrary target subset can be concentrated in one district, including in the geometric model with population density. A sharper geometric slate analysis is a plausible follow-up, but it extends an already continuous model; the paper itself identifies richer geometric models as an open direction. That is the honest weakness in the negative case: I cannot rule out that such an extension would be worthwhile. I can say that it does not rescue this anchor as evidence for a missing continuization. The paper already makes the population continuous and proves exact results about the resulting game.
