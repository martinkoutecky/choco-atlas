The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the strongest negative case: this paper states no computational problem whose complexity it studies. Results 1–4 are experimental comparisons, and Appendix C’s propositions derive behavior from assumptions about responsibility aversion. None gives a tractability, hardness, or algorithmic result to mirror.

The most promising candidate is Result 2: represent agents by a distribution over complete types, then ask whether voluntary entry amplifies the difference between defaults. Results 3 and 4 offer related questions about which types enter. But those are behavioral comparative statics, not computational questions. With groups fixed at three and two possible defaults, expected outcomes can be computed by summing over type triples under the population distribution. With \(\tau\) types, direct enumeration takes \(O(\tau^3)\); the entry and composition calculations are similarly small. That could make a useful analytic or empirical model, but it does not expose the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo studies.

A more computational version would optimize group assignments, vary group size, or design new decision rules. Those may be worthwhile questions in their own right, but they replace the paper’s random matching and fixed three-person voting setup with a planner’s problem the authors did not study. Taking group size to grow with the population also changes the object: it shifts the paper’s individual pivotality mechanism toward a limit in which each person’s influence may vanish. That is a new model, not a stronger mirror of these results.

The negative case is therefore strong for this paper and this programme’s scope, though it cannot prove that no related computational question would interest the authors. A continuum over types could sharpen their account of selection and group composition. The case against is that doing so produces behavioral aggregation, while making it computational requires adding a problem—such as optimal assignment—that is absent from the paper.
