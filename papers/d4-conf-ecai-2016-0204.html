The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible case that this paper’s market model has a continuous-population version, but it has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor. The numbered results are Properties 1–4: they establish bounds on provider tariffs, incentives to use cooperative energy, and protection against one prosumer’s overconsumption. None asserts that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The paper does present LPs and an algorithm, but not as a numbered complexity result. I can’t honestly present one of the Properties as the requested anchor.

The strongest candidate mirror would be annual contract allocation for a large cooperative. Let each type \(t\) describe a complete prosumer profile: demand bounds, forecast errors, storage and generation capacities, and other parameters used by the mechanism. Let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s share of the cooperative. Given provider costs and capacities, choose each provider’s per-capita contract bounds to meet the cooperative’s aggregate demand limits while minimizing annual subscription and deviation-penalty costs. A solution is a feasible set of per-capita contract bounds and its resulting minimum cost. A plausible regime is a large housing cooperative with many similar homes and relatively few distinct profiles.

That is a sensible high-multiplicity object, and it extends the paper’s annual-contract LP in Section 4.3. But it is not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper, so I can’t assign it an honest tractability or hardness-transfer verdict as an anchor. My weakest point is therefore decisive for the requested argument: the paper’s results concern market properties, not the computational complexity of the underlying problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that there is no qualifying result to mirror. Properties 1–4 establish tariff bounds and incentive claims; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper gives LP formulations and an algorithm, but does not state a complexity result for them. So the proponent supplies no anchor of the requested kind.

The annual-contract LP is the closest candidate, and the population regime is credible: many similar households could be grouped by their full demand, storage, generation, and forecast profiles. But the proposed mirror asks a new question about the complexity of an optimization problem; it does not continue a computational classification proved in this paper. The LP’s decisions are provider contract bounds, while households enter through aggregate quantities such as \(\sum_t \mu_t Q_{\min,t}\). That makes the model a plausible starting point for new work, but not a mirror of a named computational result here.

A stronger version could include type-level daily schedules as well as annual contracts. It would still be a worthwhile question someone could ask about this market design, but the paper does not establish the algorithmic guarantee that such a question would mirror. Recasting the incentive properties as type-level claims would have the same problem: they are economic properties, not complexity results.

I would not claim that no continuous-population scenario is sensible. The paper itself describes large numbers of small prosumers, and a distribution over household types fits that setting. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no named computational-complexity result for which that plausible continuum is a mirror. The annual-contract extension may merit study, but calling it a mirror of a computational result in this paper would overstate what the paper proves.
