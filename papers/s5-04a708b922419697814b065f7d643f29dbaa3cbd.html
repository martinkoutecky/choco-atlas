The paper has no eligible named anchor: the supplied text contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. The plausible but unanchored mirror would treat each crowd member’s task subset and ordinal and numerical reports as a type, with mass equal to that report type’s share of the crowd. The decision is an aggregate numerical vector \(r\), with its induced ranking, minimizing the mass-weighted sum of the paper’s ordinal and numerical disagreement distances. That is a coherent continuous-population version of the aggregation problem, but this paper supplies no qualifying named result to anchor it. I would not present it as a compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is the anchor rule: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational result. Its claims about the elicitation interfaces, crowd accuracy, and aggregation methods are experimental findings. The NP-hardness claim about COA is presented as prior work, not as a result proved here. So the proponent’s case supplies no eligible anchor.

The best repair is a distribution over complete report types: each type records the participant’s assigned image subset and their paired ordinal and numerical estimates. With mass \(\mu_t\) on type \(t\), one could minimize the mass-weighted version of the paper’s COA objective over \(r\) and its induced ranking. That is a coherent population model, and it preserves the link between each participant’s two estimates. But it is a new formulation of the aggregation problem, not a result of this paper. With rational masses, scaling by a common denominator turns it into weighted aggregation of a discrete multiset. It offers a possible follow-on question, but does not mirror a computational finding made here.

That formulation also misses what the paper actually establishes: differences in accuracy between elicitation designs, and the value of collecting paired inputs from the same people. Those are empirical comparisons shaped by sampling, task assignment, and participants’ response behavior. A fixed distribution of reports can support an aggregation problem, but it cannot by itself recover those comparisons. Modeling them would require a further question about response distributions and expected accuracy, which the paper does not formulate as a computational problem or solve.

There is a practical concern as well. A complete type must retain the assigned subset, the ordinal report, and the numerical estimates, since the aggregation objective uses them all. Those joint reports can have many distinct values, so the support may grow nearly as fast as the crowd. Coarsening types to reduce that support risks changing the distances the paper uses. A recurring task with a genuinely small set of report types could avoid this problem, but it would make the mirror a new weighted-aggregation study rather than an extension of a named result here.

So the case against a mirror *of this paper’s results* is strong: the required computational anchor is absent, and the proposed substitute is a plausible new problem rather than a mirror of a result. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous study could arise in any related scenario is much weaker; I cannot rule out one. The proponent’s candidate shows why that broader claim would be hard to defend, but it does not meet the stated anchor requirement.
