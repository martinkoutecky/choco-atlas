The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(S\) of partial orders on \(C\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_P\) summing to \(1\), and a target \(c^\star\), decide whether there are nonnegative rational masses \(x_{P,\rho}\), for \(P\in S\) and each linear extension \(\rho\) of \(P\), such that \(\sum_{\rho\in\operatorname{LE}(P)}x_{P,\rho}=\mu_P\) for every \(P\), and \(\sum_{P,\rho}x_{P,\rho}(m-\operatorname{rank}_{\rho}(d))\leq\sum_{P,\rho}x_{P,\rho}(m-\operatorname{rank}_{\rho}(c^\star))\) for every \(d\ne c^\star\), where \(m=|C|\).

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over partial-order types. The decision variables allocate each type’s mass across its legal complete rankings; feasibility means the target’s aggregate Borda score is at least every other candidate’s score.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Borda possible-winner determination. It leaves aside the necessary-winner optimizations, the plurality and veto procedures, the RSM generator, and the paper’s empirical performance results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the exact complexity of this feasibility problem in \(m\), support size \(\tau=|S|\), and input bit length \(L\), including whether separation over the linear extensions can be done efficiently.
  • Characterize when a feasible rational mass allocation can be converted into a discrete high-multiplicity profile without changing the possible-winner answer.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is possible-winner determination when the electorate is a distribution over incomplete-preference types. It preserves the paper’s question—can this candidate win under some completion of the voters’ stated preferences?—while making the population, rather than a list of named voters, the continuous object. The paper’s RSM is relevant context, but it generates partial orders for experiments; it does not itself model the population as a distribution of types.

The main anchor is Theorem 1 (Classification Theorem). It is a summary of results cited from earlier work, not a classification proved by these authors. In full, it says that for every pure positional scoring rule, the set of necessary winners can be computed in polynomial time; possible-winner determination is polynomial for plurality and veto, and NP-complete for every other pure positional scoring rule; and the same classification holds for necessary-unique and possible-unique winners. The Borda possible-winner case is therefore NP-complete. That is the result I would lead with.

Here is the continuous Borda question. Fix candidates \(C\), with \(|C|=m\). Let \(S\) be a finite, explicitly listed support of partial orders on \(C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_P\), where \(\sum_{P\in S}\mu_P=1\). The type \(P\) is an agent’s complete partial preference for the purposes of this problem. For every type \(P\) and every linear extension \(\rho\) of \(P\), choose a nonnegative mass \(x_{P,\rho}\). Require \(\sum_{\rho\in\operatorname{LE}(P)}x_{P,\rho}=\mu_P\). Given a target \(c^\star\), ask whether there is such an allocation for which, for every \(d\ne c^\star\), \(\sum_{P,\rho}x_{P,\rho}(m-\operatorname{rank}_{\rho}(d))\le \sum_{P,\rho}x_{P,\rho}(m-\operatorname{rank}_{\rho}(c^\star))\). A solution is a finite rational list of type, completion, and assigned mass; there is no objective beyond feasibility. Although there may be exponentially many linear extensions, this specifies the question exactly.

A plausible regime is a large election or consultation in which many people submit partial rankings through the same ballot format—for example, ranking a familiar subset of candidates while leaving the rest unranked. Millions of participants may then share the same exact partial-order type, with \(\tau=|S|\) much smaller than the population. Each person still receives a complete ranking; \(x_{P,\rho}\) records what fraction of a type’s mass receives each completion. The authors should recognize this as a continuous form of their possible-winner problem: the preferences are incomplete, the admissible completions are the same, and the target wins by the same aggregate score comparison.

The paper’s Theorem 5, proved there, reinforces the fit. It states that a candidate is a possible winner exactly when the paper’s 0–1 ILP system has a solution. That system assigns each candidate one rank and each rank to one candidate in every voter’s ballot, preserves the partial-order comparisons, and imposes the score inequalities; it has \(O(m^2n)\) binary variables and constraints. The continuous counterpart is the same underlying possible-winner question, with each type’s mass allocated across its legal completions. For this anchor, the precise search question is: given the rational type masses and Borda target, output such an allocation if one exists, and otherwise report that none exists. This mirrors the computation without treating the encoding itself as the research question.

For both anchors, my provisional expectation for unrestricted Borda is Class C, though this is a forecast, not a hardness claim. The finite NP-completeness result alone does not establish hardness after mass can be split within a type. But the continuous feasibility problem still has to reason about linear extensions and candidate scores; its natural pricing question is to optimize weighted Borda scores over the extensions of a partial order. That leaves substantial candidate-order structure in the problem. If that structure admits efficient pricing and an exact LP method, the result could instead be Class A; if the discrete reduction survives fractional splitting, it would be Class B. Useful next questions are whether the exact problem is tractable for partial chains or partitioned preferences, whether pricing over their linear extensions is efficient, and how much a feasible continuous solution can differ from a finite electorate’s solution.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is concrete. From a profile with \(n\) voters, assign each partial order \(P\) mass equal to its frequency divided by \(n\); every discrete completion then gives a feasible mass allocation. Conversely, a rational continuous allocation can be realized exactly by a sufficiently large replication of that profile. Thus the mirror has a clear high-multiplicity interpretation, even though feasibility need not agree at the original electorate size.

My weakest point is that exact partial-order types can be numerous: if \(\tau\) approaches the number of voters, the high-multiplicity motivation fades. Also, fractional allocation can make a candidate possible who would not win in the particular finite electorate. The proposed regime addresses the first limitation by focusing on repeated ballot types; the second is the intended change in granularity, and the replication bridge makes it explicit. The scope here is Borda possible-winner determination and its witness problem, not the paper’s necessary-winner optimizations or its RSM generator.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest skeptical case is that this paper’s practical contribution may not carry over. Its necessary-winner work speeds up a polynomial algorithm on explicit voters; its possible-winner experiments rely on pruning and a heuristic before invoking a binary ILP. In the proposed mirror, the central object changes: each partial-order type’s mass can be divided among its linear extensions. For Borda, the question becomes whether those divisions can make the target’s aggregate score at least every opponent’s. The resulting LP has a potentially enormous set of extensions, and its pricing problem optimizes a weighted sum of ranks over a partial order. That is a difficult combinatorial problem even with just one type. So there is little reason to expect the paper’s practical techniques to transfer, and no obvious reason that high multiplicity makes the hard part easier.

That concern applies to both anchors, but it does not defeat either. Theorem 1 is a classification summarized from earlier work, rather than a new complexity theorem proved by these authors. Theorem 5 is a generic 0–1 ILP encoding, not a distinct winner problem. Still, each points to a well-defined continuous question: whether a target can win when mass on each incomplete-preference type is allocated among its legal completions. The encoding’s binary variables do not settle that question; replacing them with exact mass allocations is a genuine modeling and complexity step. The two anchors reinforce one mirror rather than providing two separate reasons to reject it.

The most plausible regime is a large consultation where participants share a small set of incomplete ballot templates. That is not the paper’s most natural empirical setting: its real datasets contain hundreds or thousands of user-specific partial profiles, and its synthetic experiments emphasize varied posets. But standardized templates or shared tiers of known and unknown candidates could produce a small support of repeated types. Treating those participants as mass is sensible, and allowing different portions to receive different completions follows from the nonatomic population model. The change in granularity does not make the question illegitimate.

Nor can I use the likely persistence of hard rank-order structure as an objection. It may mean the mirror is Class C, even for one type, but establishing that boundary is part of the programme. The paper’s RSM and Mallows models are not an existing continuous computational treatment of possible-winner determination, and the cited discrete classification is not a novelty collision.

So the negative case is weak. The best I can argue is that this mirror has a real risk of producing a hard, technically separate problem with little payoff for the paper’s empirical algorithms. I cannot honestly conclude that no scenario makes it worthwhile: the possible-winner question remains well posed for a repeated-type society, and even a continuum-specific hardness result would be informative.
