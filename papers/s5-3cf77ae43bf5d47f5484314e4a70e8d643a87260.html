The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no qualifying anchor: it has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Its numbered items are algorithms, and Section 10 reports simulations. The claims about maximum-cardinality exchanges are not proved as named results. So there is no anchor-specific continuous question to present under your rules.

The strongest plausible mirror is still a large, centrally pooled kidney exchange. Let a type be a complete patient–donor profile for everything the mechanism uses: compatibility data, preferences, societal acceptance, priorities, and the current filter state. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the pool of type \(t\). This regime is plausible when a national registry has many pairs but repeated effective profiles; the paper’s simulations use pools up to 10,000 pairs, though their generated data do not show that real registries have \(\tau \ll n\).

A candidate problem is IKEP\(_\infty\) Maximum-Mass Cycle Packing. Its input is rational masses \(\mu_t\), a directed compatibility graph whose edges pass the specified acceptance and filter rules, and a cycle-length bound \(L_{\max}\). For each allowed cycle \(C\), choose mass \(x_C\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{C\ni t}x_C\le\mu_t\) for each type \(t\); maximize \(\sum_C |C|x_C\). Each \(x_C\) represents a subpopulation whose members all take part in that cycle, with each transplant still integral for each person. I would expect this relaxation to be tractable: it is an LP over cycle variables, and its pricing question is a bounded-length weighted-cycle search.

The weakness is substantial: the paper proposes top-trading cycles, whereas this optimization can use acceptable lower-ranked exchanges and need not preserve the mechanism’s strategic properties. The authors may not recognize it as their problem. The positive case is therefore that a continuous population model is plausible for their national-pool setting—not that this paper supplies a named complexity result supporting it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no result-specific anchor here. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational complexity result. Its algorithms are proposed procedures, and its simulations report outcomes under generated data; neither supplies a complexity claim to mirror. The proponent acknowledges this, then offers a new optimization problem instead.

The proposed maximum-mass cycle-packing LP is coherent, but it changes the paper’s mechanism. It may choose any acceptable cycle, including exchanges that are not patients’ top choices. IKEP instead runs top-trading cycles with priority tie-breaking, societal preferences, and rules that change after a pair rejects an offer. The LP treats those preferences largely as edge feasibility and omits the strategic reports and hospital withholding that motivate the paper’s game-theoretic framing. One could add constraints to better reflect those features, but that would be a new model rather than a continuous version of a result the paper proved.

A more faithful mirror would aggregate full patient–donor profiles and define a mass-valued version of IKEP. The difficulty is that a type must preserve every distinction the mechanism uses: HLA data, age, kidney size, location, societal preference rankings, and potentially waiting and rejection history. The paper’s 10,000-pair simulations do not establish that many pairs share such complete profiles; they regenerate demographic marginals and randomize societal preferences. Coarser types could make aggregation plausible, but could also change compatibility rankings and priority outcomes.

That makes a negative case against this paper reasonably strong: it offers no computational theorem to mirror, and the attractive cycle-packing formulation does not preserve the proposed mechanism. But the universal claim is weak. A large national pool could plausibly contain repeated effective profiles, and an aggregate TTC or cycle-packing question might be one the authors recognize. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every scenario; the case against it rests mainly on the gap between the paper’s stated mechanism and the proposed optimization.
