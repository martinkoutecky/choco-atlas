The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. Its numbered results concern game-theoretic properties: Theorem 1S, proved here, says that if \(\vartheta>H\), \(0<B<\vartheta-H\), and a refund scheme satisfies contribution and time monotonicity, then at equilibrium the project is funded exactly at \(C=H\), there is no race condition, and the game is sequential. Theorem 1N gives necessary conditions under a uniqueness assumption; the PPRG theorem gives a subgame-perfect-equilibrium characterization. None classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. The smart-contract gas comparison is computationally relevant, but it is not a numbered complexity result. I can’t honestly present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

There is still a narrow, plausible continuous research question. Picture civic projects funded by many small donors arriving in recurring cohorts, with relatively few complete types: a type records valuation and arrival cohort. Represent the society by rational masses \(\mu_s\) over finitely many types, \(\sum_s\mu_s=1\). Use per-capita quantities, and let type \(s\) have valuation \(\theta_s\) and time factor \(q_s=e^{-y_s}\). For the PPRE scheme, if its cohort contributes \(x_s\) at arrival, aggregate contribution is \(c=\sum_s\mu_s x_s\), and its per-capita refund in the underfunded case is

\[ r_s=\frac{b(x_s+kq_s)}{c+k}, \]

where \(b\) and \(k\) are the per-capita counterparts of the bonus budget and \(K_2\). This keeps the population continuous while retaining the paper’s time-based refund rule; time monotonicity motivates contributing on arrival.

A concrete candidate is Mass-PPRE Equilibrium: given those type masses, valuations, arrival order, and mechanism parameters, determine whether there is a pure subgame-perfect strategy profile that funds the target \(h\), and, if so, return the contribution strategy for each type after every observed funding history. To make this a coherent finite-support game, each type cohort must act as a strategic bloc choosing a common per-capita contribution. I would initially expect that restricted problem to be tractable, because the paper’s monotonicity argument and equilibrium bounds may reduce it to support-level constraints. That is a conjecture for the proposed mirror, not a result of this paper. The mirror covers only online refund schemes and funding equilibrium, not the paper’s full gas comparison or civic-crowdfunding design.

The soft spot is substantial: a type is not automatically a coalition. In a literal atomless population of individually strategic donors, one donor has no effect on whether the threshold is crossed. The pivotal incentives used in Theorem 1S may disappear, so the continuum game could behave differently or fail to have the desired equilibrium. The cohort-bloc version needs a convincing institutional story—such as pooled pledges managed by donor associations—and a proof that its equilibrium question is one the authors would accept. So there is a credible population-continuous extension to investigate, but no qualifying computational anchor in the paper supports a strong positive verdict under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the absence of a computational problem in the paper. Theorem 1S characterizes equilibrium behavior under contribution and time monotonicity; Theorem 1N gives necessary conditions under a uniqueness assumption; the PPRG result gives a family of subgame-perfect equilibria. None asks for an algorithm to solve, optimize, or approximate a computational task. The gas comparison counts operations per contributor for specified refund formulas. That is useful implementation analysis, but it does not supply a complexity problem for a continuous-population mirror.

The proposed Mass-PPRE Equilibrium makes that gap visible. In a literal atomless donor population, an individual’s contribution does not change aggregate funding \(c\), so the individual cannot be pivotal in crossing \(h\). The equilibrium argument in the paper relies on agents’ contributions affecting the provision point. A continuum game can be defined, but it needs a new account of individual incentives and aggregate outcomes; it becomes a mean-field game question rather than a continuous version of the paper’s equilibrium computation.

Treating each type cohort as a strategic bloc restores pivotality only by making the bloc a coordinated actor. A pooled pledge managed by a donor association is conceivable, but its decisions, incentives, and internal coordination are additional assumptions. That model may be worth studying on its own. It does not arise from representing many independent donors of the same type by mass, because type equality does not give those donors joint agency. The candidate also needs a finite description of its input and output: a strategy “after every observed funding history” is a function over a continuum of histories, not an algorithmic output until a compact representation is specified.

The paper’s own computational angle does not supply a stronger mirror. Its gas figures charge for processing each contributor’s refund calculation. A continuous distribution does not remove those transactions. Aggregating donors by type could reduce the number of calculations only if the platform processes pooled type-level contributions, which again changes the mechanism and its actors. For PPRE, the continuous mass formula is easy to write, but the paper has no computational objective or decision problem about that formula for a distribution of types.

One could formulate a new optimization problem—for example, choosing a refund schedule subject to budget and equilibrium constraints for a specified type distribution. The paper does not define that objective, input representation, or feasible schedule class. Its results establish conditions for a refund scheme and compare several schemes’ gas costs. Turning those into continuous mechanism design would be a further research agenda, rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest against a *computational* continuous mirror: the paper offers no named computational-complexity result to anchor one, and the plausible Mass-PPRE proposal needs either atomless incentives that depart from the paper’s pivotal logic or coordinated blocs that alter the strategic model. The universal claim remains less secure at the boundary: donor associations and pooled pledges are plausible, and the resulting continuous game could be interesting. The material here does not show that such a model is worthless; it shows that its value as a continuous-computational-social-choice mirror of this paper is weak.
