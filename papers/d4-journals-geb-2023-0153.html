The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under your anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational result asserting a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, and it gives no algorithmic formulation that it computes with.

The closest foothold is Proposition 2, proved here. It characterizes the broker’s maximum expected surplus under efficient trade and the stated incentive and participation constraints by the discounted sum \(U_1=\sum_{t\ge1}\delta^{t-1}G_B(t)G_S(t)\tau_t\). It then shows that a blind, budget-balanced efficient mechanism exists exactly when \(U_1\ge0\); overlapping value and cost supports at every date are sufficient for \(U_1<0\). Proposition 1, also proved here, says the maximum is attained by running a sequence of static VCG-* mechanisms, with the same surplus attainable in a public mechanism. These are substantive mechanism-design characterizations, but they do not classify the computational complexity of a problem.

A plausible extension would model a large two-sided market with finitely many buyer and seller types, each type specifying its arrival and payoff processes, and rational masses specifying population shares. The question could be whether efficient trade is implementable under incentive, participation, and budget-balance constraints. That is a concrete continuous-population question, and the paper’s §4.5 says its insights extend to allocation problems with more than two agents. But it changes the paper’s bilateral setting, and Proposition 2 does not establish a computational result for that market problem. I would not present it as an anchor.

The main obstacle is the scope mismatch: the paper’s continuities are over private values, costs, and arrival times, while its strategic population remains one buyer and one seller. Continuizing time or the distributions of those individuals would not supply the required population mirror. Without a named computational anchor, there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the paper’s strongest foothold, but it is an economic characterization, not a computational result. It gives the broker’s maximum expected surplus as \(U_1=\sum_{t\ge1}\delta^{t-1}G_B(t)G_S(t)\tau_t\), then characterizes when budget-balanced efficient trade is feasible. Its inputs describe the arrival and payoff processes of two traders; the paper gives no algorithmic problem, complexity claim, or computational method to mirror. Treating the randomness in those processes as population mass would confuse uncertainty about an individual with the fraction of a society belonging to a type.

The proponent’s stronger repair—a market with buyer and seller cohorts, rational type masses, and a question about efficient budget-balanced trade—is plausible as a research question. Section 4.5 even points toward multi-agent allocation problems. But that repair changes the bilateral model into a market whose allocation and incentive constraints couple many agents. It does not extend Proposition 2’s result to a continuous society; it asks for a new result about a different model. The paper’s multi-agent discussion supports the economic extension, but supplies no computational anchor for ChoCo.

That is the best negative case: none of the paper’s named results identifies the computational landscape of a population problem, and continuizing its existing probability distributions does not fill that gap. The case is strongest against treating this paper as a ChoCo anchor. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous computational question could arise nearby: the cohort-market extension is sensible, and the paper gives some reason to think its authors would recognize it. But the positive case itself stops short of showing that this is a mirror of a computational result in the paper.
