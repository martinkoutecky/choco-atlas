The continuous mirror question

Given hospitals \(H\), rational per-capita quotas \(q_h\), and a finite support \(T\) of single-applicant and couple types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), complete application preferences, and hospital priority classes, compute a stable mass assignment or determine that none exists. Assignment variables \(x_{t,e}\) give the mass of type \(t\) assigned to application \(e\); each type's assigned and unmatched mass totals \(\mu_t\), and hospital loads respect \(q_h\). Stability follows the paper's quota-responsive blocking rule: no positive mass can move from an unmatched or less-preferred assignment to a preferred acceptable application that the involved hospital or hospitals can admit, including the contribution-two capacity use of a combined application. What is the complexity as a function of \(|H|\), \(|T|\), and input bit length?

The model it lives in

A stable-allocation model with one vertex per applicant or couple type, bound \(\mu_t\), one vertex per hospital, bound \(q_h\), and an application edge for each acceptable contract. Edge flow is mass receiving the whole contract; joint applications consume one unit at each hospital, and combined applications consume two units at one hospital.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the stable-matching reductions in Theorems 3 and 4, including combined applications in Theorem 4. It leaves the experiments' comparative heuristic performance and success counts unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of computing a stable mass assignment in terms of \(|H|\), \(|T|\), and input bit length?
  • Do the combined-application contribution constraints change the complexity relative to the version without combined applications?
  • Can a stable mass assignment be rounded to an integral matching while preserving stability under stated conditions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem asserting a complexity classification. Its NP-hardness statement is cited in the introduction, not proved as a numbered result here. The strongest anchors it does prove—and then use in the Scarf experiments—are Theorem 3 and Theorem 4, the reductions from HRC to stable allocation. I would use Theorem 3 as the lead: it gives an exact correspondence, while Theorem 4 reaches the richer model with combined applications.

My proposed setting is a large, centralized allocation market: for example, a yearly cohort applying to a fixed set of hospitals or programmes, including couples who submit joint lists. Many applicants share the same complete profile—preferences and hospital priority class—so \(N\), the number of matching-side entities, is much larger than \(\tau\), the number of profiles. A couple counts as one matching-side entity, and its type includes its joint preferences. Each type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); hospital capacities become rational per-capita quotas \(q_h\). A type must include the hospital priority information too. The scenario is plausible where priorities are class-based and same-class applicants are interchangeable. If every identity-level tie-break is essential, the number of types could grow with \(N\), and this case weakens substantially.

Theorem 3 (proved here; lead anchor) says that an HRC instance with no combined applications reduces to an ISA instance with no edge capacities, with a one-to-one correspondence between its stable matchings and integral stable allocations. Singles, couples, and hospitals become vertices; applications become edges; a single or joint application uses one unit at each relevant vertex. This is the cleanest bridge for a mass version.

I would call that mirror Continuous HRC Mass-Stability, without combined applications. Its input is a finite hospital set, rational quotas \(q_h\), and a finite support of single and couple types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), explicit acceptable application lists, and strict priorities over types. Its variables are \(x_{t,e}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to application \(e\), plus unmatched mass. Each type’s assignments sum to \(\mu_t\); each hospital’s load is at most \(q_h\). A solution is such an assignment with no blocking mass: there is no positive amount of a type, currently unmatched or assigned to a less-preferred application, that can move to an acceptable application while every hospital involved has spare capacity or can replace lower-priority assigned mass. A couple’s joint application blocks only if both hospitals can accept it. The task is to find such an assignment, or report that none exists; there is no welfare objective in the paper to import.

Here fractional \(x_{t,e}\) means a fraction of the type’s people receive the whole application, not that each person gets a fractional job. That distinction makes this a population mirror rather than an outcome-space continuation. I would expect the exact computational status to be open; my first guess is Class C for the full problem, because couples’ complementary applications and the resulting blocking conditions remain in the type/application structure. That is a forecast, not a hardness claim: the paper’s cited hardness for discrete HRC does not by itself transfer to a divisible mass assignment. The useful questions are whether computation is polynomial in \(|H|,\tau,L\), whether hardness persists with small \(\tau\), and whether a mass-stable assignment can be rounded while preserving stability.

Theorem 4 (proved here; second anchor) extends the reduction to HRC with combined applications. A combined application is represented by a contract in ISAC in which the couple contributes one unit and the hospital contributes two. Integral stable allocations yield stable matchings, but the converse need not hold; the paper gives an example where a stable matching does not translate back to an integral stable allocation. This is a genuinely broader result, though weaker as a correspondence.

Its mirror, Continuous HRC Mass-Stability with Combined Applications, has the same input and assignment variables, now allowing a couple to apply to two distinct hospitals or to two positions at one hospital. A mass unit of a combined application consumes two units of that hospital’s quota; a joint application consumes one at each of two hospitals. Stability uses the paper’s quota-responsive hospital priorities: no positive mass of couples prefers a combined application that the hospital could admit by using two vacancies or displacing lower-ranked mass. I would also expect this version to be Class C, and the further question is whether the contribution-two constraint creates hardness beyond the no-combined case.

The scope is deliberately narrow: these mirrors cover the stable-matching and stable-allocation results, not the paper’s empirical claim about which heuristic performs best. The paper’s own fractional stable-allocation machinery makes a mass formulation recognizable to its authors, and its experiments already compute fractional outputs. Its cited warning that Scarf-style stable-allocation search can be PPAD-hard also suggests that a continuous formulation will not automatically be easy.

My weakest point is the priority structure. The paper assumes strict rankings over named applicants, while a useful high-multiplicity regime needs many interchangeable applicants with shared priority profiles. Class-based priorities are natural in some allocation systems, but they narrow the setting and may require extending the paper’s strict individual rankings. That caveat limits the mirror; it does not erase the exact reduction in Theorem 3 or the contribution-aware extension in Theorem 4.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on novelty and weakest on the claim that no useful scenario exists. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 are structural reductions, not complexity classifications: neither gives a running-time bound or proves the complexity of a computational problem. The paper cites HRC’s NP-hardness, but does not establish it here. Its other computational contribution is an experimental comparison of heuristics. So the proponent’s anchors do not supply the kind of complexity result the programme normally continuizes.

For Theorem 3, a high-multiplicity version is conceivable: group applicants and couples by their complete preferences and priority information, give each group a rational mass, and scale hospital quotas. But under the paper’s strict rankings over named applicants, exact priority information distinguishes applicants, so this grouping may leave almost as many types as people. To get a small support, one must coarsen priorities into classes or allow ties. That could be a reasonable extension, but it changes the paper’s strict-priority model.

More importantly, with such classes, the proposed mass-stability problem has the form of the stable-allocation model the paper already invokes. Give each type a vertex with bound equal to its mass, each hospital a vertex with its per-capita quota, and each application an edge; the variables assign mass to edges. That is the real-valued stable-allocation framework described in Section 2.3 and attributed to Biró and Fleiner [3]. The resulting question may have interesting parameterized complexity, but it is not a new continuous HRC model supplied by Theorem 3. The theorem’s one-to-one correspondence concerns *integral* solutions; it does not establish a new correspondence for mass assignments.

Theorem 4 is weaker as an anchor. It gives only a forward correspondence, and its combined applications are already represented in the paper’s contribution model: a combined application consumes two units of hospital capacity. A mass version assigns mass to those same contracts, with contribution two at the hospital. That again lands directly in the stable-allocation framework, rather than furnishing a distinct computational problem. The theorem’s failure of the converse also means its finite result offers less of an exact bridge to a mass problem than Theorem 3 does.

The strongest repair is to ask for the complexity of finding a stable mass assignment as a function of support size, market size, and input bit length. That is well-posed, and the paper’s Scarf algorithm and its cited hardness result do not settle the complexity of every HRC-derived subclass. But it is then a complexity study of a restricted stable-allocation model, not a continuization motivated by either theorem’s result. The paper itself supplies the main fractional representation and the relevant algorithmic framework.

This case cannot honestly rule out all worthwhile work. A market with repeated joint preference lists and genuinely shared priority classes is plausible, and a new efficient algorithm or a precise hardness result for its mass version could matter. The objection is that the paper’s own assumptions make substantial type compression difficult, while the natural workaround maps straight into the fractional stable-allocation model it already uses. That makes these two theorems a weak basis for a new continuous mirror, even though it does not prove that no useful scenario can be constructed.
