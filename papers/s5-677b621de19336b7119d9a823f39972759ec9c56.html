The continuous mirror question

Given two unit-mass societies with finite supports \(R_A\) and \(R_B\), rational type masses \(\mu_r\) and \(\nu_s\), and strict rankings of opposite-side types, find a nonnegative assignment \(x_{rs}\) with row and column sums \(\mu_r\) and \(\nu_s\), respectively, such that for every \(r,s\), \(\left(\sum_{s':\,s\succ_r s'}x_{rs'}\right)\left(\sum_{r':\,r\succ_s r'}x_{r's}\right)=0\).

The model it lives in

Two finite sets of preference types carry rational masses \(\mu_r\) and \(\nu_s\), each summing to \(1\). Choose mass \(x_{rs}\ge0\) matched between types \(r\) and \(s\), respecting both marginal masses and having no pair of types with positive mass on both sides assigned below one another in their rankings. The task is to find such a stable coupling, with complexity measured in support size and mass bit length.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the stability part of the paper, not of its equitable optimization goal.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: *if Algorithm 1 terminates, its final matching is stable*. The theorem does not prove that the algorithm terminates, bound its running time, or guarantee a low sex-equality cost. The paper does say that minimizing sex-equality cost is strongly NP-hard, citing Kato [10], but that claim is not stated as a numbered theorem here, so I would not present it as a qualifying anchor under your rules.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Stable Marriage. An instance has two sides, with finite sets of types \(R_A\) and \(R_B\), rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) of equal total mass \(1\), and a strict ranking of opposite-side types for every type. A type is a complete matching-relevant description: its members have the same preferences and are indistinguishable for the problem. The task is to find a nonnegative mass assignment \(x=(x_{rs})\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{s\in R_B}x_{rs}=\mu_r,\qquad \sum_{r\in R_A}x_{rs}=\nu_s. \]
There is no optimization objective in this anchor’s mirror: the solution is a feasible assignment with no blocking pair of types. Precisely, for every \(r,s\), the mass of type \(r\) matched to types it ranks below \(s\) and the mass of type \(s\) matched to types it ranks below \(r\) cannot both be positive. Otherwise some positive mass on each side would prefer to match with the other.

A plausible regime is a large centralized matching round—say, a market with many applicants and positions—in which applicants share a modest number of preference types, and positions share a modest number of preference types. The relevant comparison is \(N\gg |R_A|+|R_B|\): the market is large, while the supported type sets are comparatively small. This version assumes agents care about counterpart types and are indifferent among members of a type. That is a real modeling choice, but it preserves the two-sided stability question rather than replacing it with fractional outcomes or a probabilistic profile.

I would expect this feasibility problem to be tractable, likely through a mass-based deferred-acceptance procedure whose work depends on the supported types and the bit lengths of their masses. A prover would need to establish the algorithm and its bound; Theorem 1 itself supplies neither. This is a sensible computational question even where continuum matching is familiar as an analytic or economic model: the target here is an algorithm and its dependence on support size and input precision.

The natural next question, closer to the paper’s named ESMP, is to minimize the mass version of its sex-equality objective over stable assignments:
\[ \min_x \left| \sum_{r,s}x_{rs}\operatorname{rank}_r(s) - \sum_{r,s}x_{rs}\operatorname{rank}_s(r) \right|. \]
That is the right place to investigate whether the cited discrete hardness dissolves, transfers, or is replaced by continuum-specific hardness. I would not claim Theorem 1 establishes anything about that optimization problem.

The mirror’s weakest point is also its boundary: the paper’s central ambition is an *equitable* stable matching, while its sole numbered theorem guarantees only stability conditional on termination. The continuous feasibility problem is a credible mirror of that theorem and of the underlying SMP; by itself, it does not mirror the paper’s equity contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not establish the kind of result ChoCo is looking for. Theorem 1 says that if this particular finite-agent procedure terminates, its output is stable. It gives no termination guarantee, running-time bound, or equity guarantee; the paper itself says termination is not intrinsic and reports success only on tested instances. The proponent’s Continuous Stable Marriage problem asks for a stable mass assignment. That is a different search problem, not a mirror of the theorem’s conditional correctness claim. A mass version of ESMA would need its own definitions for split proposals, proposer scheduling, and termination; showing that ordinary deferred acceptance finds a stable mass assignment would not establish anything about ESMA’s alternating proposal process.

The mass model is sensible, but its basic stability question already has substantial continuum matching context. For example, Greinecker formulates two-sided matching in terms of distributions over agent characteristics and joint distributions of matched types, and relates stable outcomes to limits of finite markets. That work is primarily about existence and economic structure, so it is not a computational collision under ChoCo’s rules; it does show that the proponent’s feasibility question is not a new computational direction supplied by this paper. [Greinecker, *Pairwise Stable Matching in Large Economies*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA16228)

The stronger mirror is to minimize the mass version of sex-equality cost over stable assignments, \(\min_x \left|\sum_{r,s}x_{rs}\operatorname{rank}_r(s)-\sum_{r,s}x_{rs}\operatorname{rank}_s(r)\right|\). A large market with many applicants and positions sharing rankings over a modest set of counterpart types is plausible, and this objective preserves the paper’s equity question. It is not defeated by saying the answer might be easy, and the continuous matching literature above does not settle its computational complexity. The objection is narrower: it is a newly posed optimization problem, not a mirror of Theorem 1’s stated result.

So I can make a strong case against the submitted stability anchor, but not against every worthwhile mirror. The mass-based equity optimization remains a credible candidate, and the honest negative case is weak at that broader claim.
