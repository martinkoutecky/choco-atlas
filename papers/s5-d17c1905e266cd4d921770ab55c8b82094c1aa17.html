The strongest *unanchored* mirror is a claims allocation for a large population of regulated facilities. Let type \(t\) describe facilities with the same per-facility projected emissions claim \(c_t\), and let \(\mu_t\) be their share of all facilities, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Given a per-facility emissions budget \(E<\sum_t\mu_t c_t\), find permitted emissions \(y_t\) satisfying \(0\le y_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t y_t=E\). For the paper’s CEA rule, \(y_t=\min(c_t,\lambda)\), where \(\lambda\) is chosen to exhaust the budget. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime if many installations share the same claims; the finite-support version is tractable by sorting the \(\tau\) claim values.

The proposed facilities model is the strongest alternative. A large population of facilities grouped by per-facility claims is a plausible high-multiplicity claims problem. But it changes the paper’s agents from EU member states to regulated installations, and its national projections into facility-level claims. For CEA, the mass allocation is just \(y_t=\min(c_t,\lambda)\), with \(\lambda\) chosen so that \(\sum_t\mu_t\min(c_t,\lambda)=E\). With finite support, sorting the claims finds \(\lambda\); the other rules likewise have direct mass analogues. That could be a useful application of claims allocation, but it supplies a new problem and a routine computation, not a continuous mirror of an algorithmic contribution here. Recasting the 27 states themselves as types does not help: each remains a distinct national decision maker, so assigning them artificial masses creates no meaningful multiplicity.
