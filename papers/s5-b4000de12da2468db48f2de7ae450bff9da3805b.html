The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result that qualifies as an anchor. The paper surveys core nonemptiness, convexity and stability conditions; it does not state complexity bounds or analyze an algorithm for a computational problem.

The closest results are Proposition 3.27, cited from Lekeas and Stamatopoulos (2014), which says that under the stated symmetric Cournot assumptions the \(f\)-game has a nonempty core exactly when \(n \ge 12\); and Proposition 4.21, proved here, which says that for each fixed \(r>0\), the symmetric Bertrand \(\delta\)-game has an empty core for all sufficiently large \(n\). These are structural thresholds for an economic property, not computational results of the kind your anchor rule requires.

One could formulate a new problem about core existence for a continuum of firms, but that would not mirror a qualifying named result in this paper. So I can’t honestly provide a continuous problem per anchor under the requested criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that the paper gives no \(P\)-versus-\(NP\) classification or running-time bound. Proposition 4.21 still gives a strong anchor: it decides the core-nonemptiness question for a named family of Bertrand games, and Proposition 4.20 supplies the coalition values used to study that question. A continuous mirror can ask how to decide core nonemptiness, and find a supporting allocation when one exists, for a society of firms described by a finite support of rational types.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Consider many small producers sharing a finite set of cost, capacity, and technology profiles. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of firms of type \(t\). A coalition is described by its type masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Extending the paper’s \(\delta\) rule gives a value \(v_\mu(x)\) from the strategic interaction between that coalition and its complement. A type-anonymous payoff allocation \(a_t\) is in the core when
\[ \sum_t x_t a_t \ge v_\mu(x)\quad\text{for every }x\in[0,\mu], \qquad \sum_t \mu_t a_t=v_\mu(\mu). \]
Deciding whether such an allocation exists, or finding one, is a precise computational problem in support size and input bit length. Its separation problem is to maximize \(v_\mu(x)-\sum_t x_ta_t\) over coalition mass vectors. That puts the mirror directly in reach of continuous optimization.

The continuum does not erase the blocking question in Proposition 4.21. In its symmetric model, the coalition’s size enters through \(s/n\). When \(s/n\) tends to a positive fraction \(\alpha\), the coalition value divided by \(n\) has a finite, nonzero limit that varies with \(\alpha\). Thus positive-mass coalitions retain meaningful core constraints. The individual firms become negligible, while coalitions with positive mass still matter—the object the paper studies.

Proposition 3.27 is a weaker anchor because its \(f\) rule depends on counting outsiders’ discrete coalitions. A continuum version would need a specified law over mass partitions. The case for a mirror need not rest on that extra choice: Proposition 4.21 already supplies a natural mass-based question through \(\delta\). So the paper supports a worthwhile continuous computational follow-up, even though it lacks a theorem explicitly classifying computational complexity. If “mirror” is restricted to continuizing an existing complexity theorem, the proponent’s objection stands; under the broader question posed here, the claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is too strong.
