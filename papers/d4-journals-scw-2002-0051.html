The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is unanchored: this paper has no named computational result of the kind the brief requires. Its main result, Theorem 1 (proved here), says that the ratio correspondence is the unique solution on the paper’s family of finite-agent public-good economies satisfying one-person rationality, consistency, and converse consistency. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity classification or algorithm. The lemmas establish properties used in that characterization; they do not supply computational anchors either.

There is a plausible continuous-population computation one could develop from the paper’s underlying ratio-equilibrium problem. Let the finite support contain types \(t\), where a type consists of an endowment \(w_t\) and utility function \(u_t\), and let \(\mu_t\) be its rational population mass. Given a common public-good cost function \(f\), ask for a common provision level \(y\), private-good consumption \(x_t\), and per-agent cost ratio \(r_t\) such that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t r_t=1,\qquad x_t+r_t f(y)=w_t, \]
and \((x_t,y)\) maximizes \(u_t\) among bundles affordable at ratio \(r_t\). A solution is such an equilibrium, or a certificate that none exists. For a computational version, the utilities and cost function would need a finite encoding, for example rational piecewise-linear functions.

A plausible regime is a large jurisdiction with many households sharing a relatively small set of income and preference types, so \(N\) is large while \(\tau\ll N\). Agents of each type share the same ratio and allocation, which is the natural high-multiplicity symmetry. This preserves the paper’s ratio-equilibrium question and continuizes the population. Its weakest point is decisive for this assignment: the proposed problem computes an equilibrium, while the paper’s named theorem characterizes a solution by axioms. The authors might recognize the equilibrium model as theirs, but there is no result here to anchor a claim about its computational landscape. I therefore cannot honestly classify this proposed problem as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard on the basis of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the classification gate: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes a solution correspondence by one-person rationality, consistency, and converse consistency. Its proof relies on induction over finite economies, with the one-agent case as its base. Nothing in the paper states a complexity, algorithmic, or approximation claim.

The proposed continuum equilibrium problem is coherent. A large jurisdiction with finitely many household types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and assigning each type a common ratio and allocation respects the paper’s model. But that proposal asks a new computational question about the underlying equilibrium correspondence; it does not continue a computational result from the paper. The paper allows arbitrary strictly increasing utility functions and non-decreasing cost functions, without prescribing finite encodings or an output task. Restricting these inputs to rational piecewise-linear functions and asking to find an equilibrium or certify none would supply choices the paper did not make. That might be worthwhile research, but its value would have to stand on its own.

Trying instead to continuize the theorem itself runs into a different mismatch. Its axioms concern economies formed by withdrawing finite coalitions, and its characterization is anchored at one-person economies. In an atomless population, a single person has zero mass; replacing singleton rationality with a local condition and finite coalitions with measurable subpopulations would change the axiomatic question. Such a reformulation could be interesting, but it would be a new axiomatization, not a computational mirror.

So the proponent raises no named-result anchor that survives the brief’s test. The best negative conclusion is that this paper offers no computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror, and the plausible equilibrium-computation extension requires a separate, substantive modeling choice. The stronger claim—that no version of that extension could be worthwhile—is not established: the paper’s underlying equilibrium problem remains a sensible candidate for independent computational study.
