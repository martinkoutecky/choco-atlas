The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a new continuous version of the paper’s stability measure, but it has no qualifying result to anchor it. The paper’s numbered Assertions 1 and 2 concern when rules are unstable; they do not assert a complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The reported comparisons are simulation results. So there are no eligible anchors here, and I cannot honestly give a per-anchor tractability or hardness prediction.

A plausible setting is a large election with \(m\in\{3,4,5\}\) alternatives and many voters sharing a relatively small set of complete rankings. Let \(T\) be the \(m!\) rankings and \(\mu\) their population fractions, with support size \(\tau\le m!\). For a rule \(F\), fix a rational error mass \(\varepsilon>0\), and assume every supported type has mass at least \(\varepsilon\). Choose type \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t\), choose a different ranking \(t'\) uniformly, and transfer mass \(\varepsilon\) from \(t\) to \(t'\). The resulting robustness is
\[ R_\varepsilon^F(\mu)= \sum_{t\in T}\frac{\mu_t}{m!-1} \sum_{\substack{t'\in T\\t'\ne t}} \mathbf{1}\!\left[ F(\mu)=F\!\left(\mu-\varepsilon e_t+\varepsilon e_{t'}\right) \right]. \]
A concrete computational question is: given \(F,\mu,\varepsilon\), compute this exact rational value. It preserves the paper’s focus on whether random preference errors change the collective choice, and the regime is a credible high-multiplicity one.

The weakness is the change in error scale. A single voter’s error has mass \(1/N\) in a population of size \(N\), which vanishes as \(N\) grows; fixed \(\varepsilon\) models a batch of errors instead. The paper does not study that regime or the complexity of computing its index. This is a plausible, narrow mirror of its subject, but it is an unanchored research question—not a continuization of a named computational result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying anchor in the paper. Assertions 1 and 2 are claims about when a rule is unstable; neither says anything about computational complexity. The reported results are simulation estimates and comparisons, not complexity theorems. So there is no named computational result here whose continuous complexity mirror this paper motivates. That is the strongest negative case.

The proposed \(R_\varepsilon^F(\mu)\) is a sensible population-level robustness measure, but fixed \(\varepsilon\) changes the experiment: it models a batch of errors, whereas the paper measures one voter’s error. For the literal one-voter analogue, the transferred mass is \(1/N\). At any fixed society with a positive margin between the winner and its competitors, sufficiently small transfers cannot change the winner, so the limiting index is \(1\). One could instead study boundary cases, rescale the vanishing effect, or use a fixed-mass noise model. Each is a plausible extension, but each requires choosing a new question the paper itself does not resolve.

For the paper’s actual range, \(m\in\{3,4,5\}\), the fixed-mass index is also straightforward to compute exactly: enumerate the at most \(120\) rankings and the transfers between them, evaluate the rule, and sum the rational weights. That may replace simulation with an exact calculation, but it offers little complexity landscape. Letting \(m\) vary could make implicit enumeration over \(m!\) rankings a genuine computational problem. That is the strongest version of the mirror—and also a new generalization, rather than a complexity result anchored in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on the absence of an eligible result and weak on the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists. The high-multiplicity election setting is quite credible; what this paper supplies is a robustness statistic, not the computational result the programme is built to continuize.
