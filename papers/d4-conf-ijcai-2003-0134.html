What the mirror covers

The proposed model tries to extend Theorem 1’s WLO and Pareto guarantee to fleet-wide average preferences; it does not cover the paper’s single-network results for its broader preference classes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high multiplicity version of temporal preference optimization for repeated mission executions. Imagine a large fleet or campaign running many instances of a rover observation plan. One unit is one mission execution; its type is the complete temporal problem it faces: event structure, hard constraints, and local preference functions. A type’s mass is the fraction of executions with that profile. Thousands of executions could share a modest number \(\tau\) of profiles. Each execution still receives one complete feasible schedule; the mass assigned to different schedules can vary continuously, so type-level average preferences can be fractional.

My sole anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, assuming a weakest-link constraint is found at each iteration, WLO+ returns an STP whose solutions are contained in the intersection of the original STPP’s WLO-optimal and Pareto-optimal solutions. Section 4 also establishes that the returned set is nonempty whenever the original STPP is feasible. This is a correctness result for the algorithm, not a named theorem saying the problem is in P. The paper has no named hardness result or explicit complexity-class theorem to anchor on. Its polynomial-time claim comes from the surrounding analysis: WLO+ makes at most one pass over the soft constraints, and each WLO solve is polynomial using the earlier STPP method cited from Khatib et al. Theorem 2 and Corollary 2.1, both proved here, support that account: with continuous domains and preference functions whose upper-level sets are convex, a weakest-link constraint exists for a WLO-optimal set; under those conditions WLO+ finds one at each iteration.

Here is a population question that keeps the temporal problem intact. Call it Continuous-Population Pareto STPP. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of mission profiles with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a shared event template with criterion edges \(E\). Each type \(t\) has a bounded STP-feasible set \(S_t\) and, for each \(e=(u,v)\in E\), a rational piecewise-linear concave preference function \(f_{t,e}(x_v-x_u)\). A solution is, for every type, a finite distribution \(\pi_t\) over complete schedules in \(S_t\). The distribution records what fraction of that type’s executions receives each schedule; it does not give any execution a fractional schedule. Its society-wide preference on criterion \(e\) is
\[ \bar p_e=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\int_{S_t} f_{t,e}(x_v-x_u)\,d\pi_t(x). \]
The requested output is an allocation maximizing \(\min_{e\in E}\bar p_e\), with a Pareto-undominated preference vector among those max-min optima. Thus all temporal constraints remain jointly enforced for every execution, while the objective concerns per-capita preferences across the population.

I expect this restricted mirror to be Class A, tractable. The attainable average-preference vectors form a convex set because each type can divide its mass among feasible schedules. A master optimization can maximize the weakest average preference, then choose an undominated point on the optimal face. Its schedule-pricing step optimizes a weighted sum of the local preferences over an STP-feasible schedule. For the stated piecewise-linear concave functions, that pricing problem can be represented as a linear program; rational input and bounded domains put the setup within standard separation and ellipsoid machinery. A finite-support allocation suffices to represent the output. The natural parameters are the number of temporal variables and constraints, \(\tau\), and the input bit length—not the number of mission executions.

This is a credible, but bounded, extension of the paper’s question. Theorem 1 itself concerns the preferences of one temporal network. The mirror instead evaluates averages across many networks and uses distributions over schedules. That is a real change in the aggregation level, though it preserves the paper’s central object: jointly feasible temporal assignments evaluated by local temporal preferences, with a weakest-link objective and Pareto refinement. The restrictions also matter. The paper’s broader preference class is not necessarily concave piecewise-linear, and it explicitly observes that utilitarian optimization appears intractable. Whether pricing remains tractable outside this subclass is an open question, not a consequence of its theorems.

The weakest point is author recognition: the authors might regard population-wide averages over repeated plans as a portfolio problem added to their STPP, rather than as the continuous form of their original problem. The fleet scenario helps because it supplies genuine high multiplicity with few complete mission types, but it cannot erase that change. The most useful follow-up questions are whether WLO+’s weakest-link and Pareto guarantee extends to these aggregate vectors, how to round the mass allocation for a finite fleet, and whether the pricing boundary for the paper’s broader preference functions yields tractability or continuum-specific hardness. The continuized object here is the mission population; temporal time was already continuous in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only strong anchor, but it is a correctness result: WLO+ returns a nonempty set of schedules that are both WLO-optimal and Pareto-optimal for one STPP. It does not state a complexity classification. The polynomial-time claim comes from applying the earlier STPP solver a bounded number of times; Theorems 2 and 2.1 establish when weakest links exist. So the paper offers no named complexity result whose population analogue can be charted. That alone does not rule out a new computational question, but it makes Theorem 1 a weak anchor for one.

The fleet scenario is the best population mirror available. Treating each complete mission profile as a type is coherent, and a large fleet could have many executions per type. But if runs are independent, the paper’s problem separates by type: solve once for each profile and reuse the result. The mass records repetition; it adds no computational structure. If the goal is to preserve the paper’s per-schedule Pareto guarantee, each run can receive a Pareto-optimal schedule for its type without a population model.

The proposed aggregate objective does add structure, but changes what is being optimized. In the paper, every local preference is evaluated on the same complete schedule. The fleet model instead permits one execution’s poor score on a criterion to be offset by another execution’s good score, then seeks a Pareto-undominated population average. A distribution over schedules is a sensible way to define that portfolio problem, and its tractability may be useful. But the paper’s WLO+ guarantee does not establish it: the guarantee concerns schedules of one network, not aggregate vectors produced by mixing schedules across executions. The LP formulation gets much of its tractability from that mixing and from the imposed piecewise-linear concavity assumptions.

A coupled fleet is a stronger candidate mirror: shared resources could make assignments across runs genuinely interact. Yet those interactions must be represented somehow. If a typewise distribution over schedules omits which runs compete for which resources, it may lose the relevant constraints. Encoding the full interaction structure in the types can make the number of types grow with the fleet, removing the compression that made high multiplicity attractive. Special exchangeable or aggregate resource constraints could avoid that, but they would add a substantial model beyond this paper.

So the proposed mirror is a plausible adjacent research problem, especially for campaign planning. The negative case is weakest there: the authors might recognize it as a natural extension. Still, the paper itself studies a planner’s multiobjective temporal network, not a population whose members receive schedules and trade off their average preference scores. I would not count the proposed aggregate LP as a continuous mirror of Theorem 1 without evidence that the authors regard that portfolio objective as their problem.
