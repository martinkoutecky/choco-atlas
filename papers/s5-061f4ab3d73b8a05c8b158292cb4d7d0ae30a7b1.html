The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) goods, \(\tau\) valuation types \(v_t\in\mathrm{GGS}(2,M)\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and positive rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_j\), compute nonnegative prices \(p_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), where each \(S\subseteq\Omega\) is an integral bundle. Require \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le\beta_j\), \(x_{t,S}>0\Rightarrow S\in\arg\max_{R\subseteq\Omega}(v_t(R)-p(R))\), and zero price for every good with unused supply.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic market with a finite set of goods, a distribution \(\mu\) over complete GGS\((2,M)\) valuation types, and per-capita supplies \(\beta_j\). Each person receives an integral bundle, while assignment variables \(x_{t,S}\) may divide a type’s mass among bundles; prices support a Walrasian allocation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the GGS\((2,M)\) equilibrium computation in Theorem 4.9 and its associated existence question. It leaves aside the paper’s gross-substitutes auction equivalence, the broader GGS\((k,M)\) programme, and strategic properties.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper’s particular ascending auction admit a convergent mass-weighted update rule using only aggregate type demands, and what bound holds on its price updates?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s GGS\((2,M)\) Walrasian equilibrium computation problem, with the population made continuous and each person still receiving an integral bundle.

A plausible regime is a large cohort of firms competing for a finite menu of permits. Firms fall into a small number \(\tau\) of stable valuation types, with \(N\gg\tau\). A type’s valuation is a complete description of its preferences: in the paper’s GGS\((2,M)\) class, it has type-specific values for individual permits and value \(M\) for every bundle of at least two permits. The mass of a type is its population share. Goods have per-capita supplies \(\beta_j\); for the paper’s exact one-copy-per-good auction with \(N\) bidders, the normalized supply is \(\beta_j=1/N\). In a recurring market with stock scaled to the cohort, \(\beta_j\) can instead remain fixed. Each individual gets a whole subset of goods; only the mass of a type can be divided among different bundles.

The best computational anchor is Theorem 4.9, proved in this paper. The paper gives no numbered NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification, and Theorem 4.9 itself does not claim a polynomial running-time bound. It proves that, for GGS\((2,M)\) valuations under the paper’s integral-price auction assumptions, the described auction terminates at a Walrasian allocation and equilibrium. Starting at zero prices, it first runs the induced gross-substitutes auction when the players who demand only singletons cannot be allocated. Once they can be allocated, it finds a maximum matching to singleton items. If \(n'\) players and \(m'\) items remain unmatched, it stops when \(2n'\le m'\), so the remaining players can receive disjoint pairs; otherwise, it raises the prices of all minimum-price items and repeats. The theorem is proved here, using the paper’s equilibrium-existence result and its intervening propositions.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is GGS\((2,M)\)-nonatomic Walrasian equilibrium computation. Its input is a finite set of \(m\) goods, \(\tau\) valuation types \(v_t\in\mathrm{GGS}(2,M)\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and positive rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_j\). Its output is prices \(p_j\ge0\) and assignment masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\). A valid output must satisfy \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le\beta_j\), and \(x_{t,S}>0\Rightarrow S\in D_t(p)\); any good with unused supply must have price zero. For the exact single-copy normalization, \(\mu_t=N_t/N\) and \(\beta_j=1/N\).

I expect this problem to be Class A. It is the configuration LP and its dual for the same welfare-and-prices question the paper studies. For GGS\((2,M)\), demand can be found by comparing the empty set, singletons, and pairs: bundles of size at least two all have value \(M\), and at nonnegative prices a pair is a best representative among them. Thus pricing can be handled in \(O(m^2)\) comparisons per type, giving a polynomial-time rational LP algorithm in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). This gives a precise computational mirror in terms of the paper’s central equilibrium object, rather than making outcomes or time continuous. It covers the equilibrium computation in Theorem 4.9 and its associated GGS\((2,M)\) existence question; it does not attempt to mirror the paper’s broader search over GGS\((k,M)\), its gross-substitutes auction equivalence, or its strategic-auction discussion.

The weak point is that nonatomic assignment lets a type’s mass split across bundles. That convexification makes equilibrium existence follow from LP duality for far more valuation classes than GGS\((2,M)\), so it removes the paper’s surprising discrete phenomenon: a non-gross-substitutes class where equilibrium is nevertheless guaranteed. The continuous question remains the same allocation-and-pricing problem, and GGS\((2,M)\) gives it an efficient succinct representation, but the paper’s special existence result is no longer what distinguishes the mirror. A more faithful follow-up would ask whether the paper’s particular ascending auction has a weighted-mass version using only aggregate type demands, and how many price rounds it needs. Theorem 4.9 does not establish that extension or a polynomial bound.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s market does not keep a positive per-capita supply of goods as the bidder population grows. It has \(m\) distinct goods, one copy of each. With population mass normalized to \(1\), the exact supply of each good is \(1/N\), which tends to zero. For \(M>0\), a positive mass of GGS\((2,M)\) bidders wants a nonempty bundle at zero prices. In the zero-supply limit, none can receive one; but if goods are unused, Walrasian prices must be zero. That limit has no equilibrium. Holding supply per capita fixed avoids this collapse, but changes the market to one whose total inventory grows with its population. The programme explicitly permits such co-modifications, so this is a real qualification, not a decisive objection.

The proposed equilibrium computation also goes beyond the paper’s stated result. Theorem 4.9 proves that a particular auction terminates at equilibrium; it gives no running-time bound in \(n,m\), or input bit length. Its steps depend on integral prices and counts of unmatched players and goods. The proposed continuous problem instead uses fractional assignment masses and arbitrary per-capita supplies. Its assignment LP is closely related to the winner-determination LP and dual prices already displayed in the paper. For GGS\((2,M)\), demand reduces to comparing the empty set, singletons, and pairs, so pricing is easy and the continuous equilibrium can be computed by standard LP methods. Since mass splitting makes equilibrium existence a generic consequence of LP duality, the paper’s special existence result for GGS\((2,M)\) no longer distinguishes the continuous model.

That makes the proposed mirror look routine, and less faithful to the auction theorem than its framing suggests. A closer follow-up would study a weighted version of the paper’s ascending auction, including whether it converges and how many price updates it needs. But the paper does not provide that result, and the distinction between indivisible counts and fractional masses leaves genuine work to formulate it.

These points weaken the case for this as a rich continuization result, but they do not defeat its best version. A recurring market with stable valuation types and supplies scaled to its cohort is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The equilibrium computation is a well-posed population-continuous problem, and the paper’s algorithmic equilibrium result is a clear anchor for it. I cannot honestly defend the universal negative: this anchor survives, even if its continuous solution is generic and likely straightforward.
