The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\rho_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational expected-bill table \(\ell_{tk}\) for each type \(t\) and tariff \(k\in\Gamma\), find mass assignments \(x_{tk}\geq 0\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k\in\Gamma}x_{tk}\ell_{tk}\), subject to \(\sum_{k\in\Gamma}x_{tk}=\rho_t\). Report the tariff masses \(s_k=\sum_{t\in T}x_{tk}\); types are exact classes with identical forecast parameters and tariff eligibility, and tied types may split.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic market with type masses \(\rho_t\), where each type has a common forecast profile and tariff eligibility, and each unit of its mass assigned to tariff \(k\) incurs expected bill \(\ell_{tk}\). Choose mass flows \(x_{tk}\) to minimize total expected bills while assigning all type mass, then report market shares.

The objection that survived

Individual choices are independent, so the continuous assignment only aggregates choices into market shares and adds no interaction or substantial algorithmic novelty; the proponent acknowledges separability but does not remove that limitation.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest positive mirror is the paper’s individual tariff choice problem in Section VI. A continuous population of household types choosing among POU tariffs is a sensible object, and the paper already studies the corresponding finite market segmentation. There is a qualification about the requested anchors: the paper has no numbered theorem that classifies a problem as polynomial-time or hard. Its polynomial-time claim appears with Algorithm 1 in Section VIII. The best clean numbered anchor for a computational mirror is Lemma 1, proved in this paper.

Lemma 1 says: after dominated and duplicate tariffs are removed, form the thresholds between every pair of tariffs, sort them as \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_q\), where \(q=|\Gamma|(|\Gamma|-1)/2\), and let the intervals between consecutive thresholds include the two unbounded end intervals. For each such interval, every customer whose coefficient of variation \(\sigma/\mu\) lies in that interval minimizes expected payment under the same tariff. This is a tariff-choice characterization, not a complexity theorem.

My lead mirror is Continuous POU Market Segmentation. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of consumer types, with rational masses \(\rho_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has the same expected consumption \(\eta_t\), forecast-error standard deviation \(\sigma_t\), tariff eligibility, and risk attitude as every other member of that type. A finite menu \(\Gamma\) offers each type a choice of tariff. Let \(a_k\) be tariff \(k\)’s baseline rate and \(\beta_k\) its expected penalty coefficient per unit of standard deviation, as in the paper’s expected-payment formula. Choose masses \(x_{tk}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_k x_{tk}=\rho_t\), to minimize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k\in\Gamma}x_{tk}(a_k\eta_t+\beta_k\sigma_t). \]
The solution is the assignment \(x\) and the resulting market shares \(s_k=\sum_t x_{tk}\). A type can split across tariffs only when there is a tie; otherwise all its mass goes to a minimum-cost tariff. For a conventional bit-complexity statement, the effective coefficients can be supplied as rationals; with coefficients computed from tariff parameters, the corresponding question can ask for an \(\epsilon\)-optimal assignment using certified approximations.

This is Class A: the objective separates by type, so a direct algorithm checks each type against each tariff in \(O(\tau|\Gamma|)\) arithmetic operations, where \(\tau=|T|\). Lemma 1 adds a useful structure: tariff choice is constant across intervals of the coefficient-of-variation axis, so the market can also be described by threshold segments. The mirror preserves the paper’s actual decision—choosing among POU tariffs to minimize expected bills—while making the society a distribution over types.

A plausible regime is a large retailer or collective-switching service with many household accounts and a small catalogue of standardized forecast classes. A type’s description must match exactly on the attributes the model uses; merely similar households are not one type. This could arise when a forecasting provider assigns repeated accounts to the same certified demand profile and risk class, with identical tariff eligibility. The number of accounts can then be much larger than the number of types. The paper’s own 3,000-customer evaluation makes market shares a natural object, although its individual customers are not thereby a continuous population. The normal forecast-error model is retained here; the continuized object is the distribution of households, not the uncertainty model.

I would keep the scope to Section VI’s individual-choice result and its market-segmentation experiment in Section IX-A. I would not present the group-buying dynamic program as an equally strong anchor: its polynomial claim is attached to Algorithm 1 rather than a numbered complexity theorem, and the unrestricted grouping section itself gives an example where the monotone assignment pattern can fail. A useful next question is whether continuous type-mass group buying admits an exact algorithm whose running time is polynomial in support size and tariff-menu size, without that restriction.

The weakest point is that this mirror covers the paper’s separable individual-choice problem, not its more distinctive coalition-formation contribution. Its optimization becomes easy because consumers do not interact when choosing individually. The type regime also requires exact repeated forecast classes, which is plausible in a standardized service but narrower than the paper’s heterogeneous measured dataset.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that the proposed anchor, Lemma 1, is not a complexity result. It says that customers with coefficients of variation in the same interval choose the same tariff. The paper’s polynomial dynamic program appears elsewhere, for group buying under a monotone assignment restriction; the paper also gives an unrestricted example where that restriction can fail. So the proposed mirror takes a structural characterization and recasts it as an optimization problem, rather than continuizing a computational result established by Lemma 1.

For the proposed finite-support model, let \(\ell_{tk}\) be type \(t\)’s expected bill under tariff \(k\). The objective reduces to \(\sum_t \rho_t \min_k \ell_{tk}\), with each type’s mass assigned to a minimizing tariff. The masses do not interact: the paper’s individual threshold characterization already determines every choice, and the continuous formulation just weights those choices to report market shares. Rational masses can also be represented as counts in a finite market by clearing denominators. This is a valid high-multiplicity encoding, but it adds no population-level decision to the individual-choice problem. If the type distribution instead has a continuous density, computing market shares becomes an integration problem whose difficulty depends on how that density is represented, not on the tariff-choice result.

The more ambitious group-buying mirror runs into a different problem. Under the paper’s independence assumption, a group of \(N\) customers has expected consumption proportional to \(N\), while its aggregate prediction-error standard deviation grows as \(\sqrt{N}\). The risk penalty per customer therefore shrinks as \(1/\sqrt{N}\). In a large-population limit, the pooling benefit that motivates group buying disappears relative to baseline costs. A shared forecast shock could preserve nonvanishing aggregate risk, but it changes the stochastic structure and the pooling effect; scaling tariff penalties with population size would likewise change the tariff model. Those might be reasonable new models, but they are not continuizations that preserve this paper’s central group-buying mechanism.

The negative case is weakest on plausibility. Large services could have many households in identical standardized forecast classes, making a continuous population of types sensible. I cannot honestly rule out that mirror. The strongest case against it is narrower: Lemma 1’s mirror is a distributional restatement with no new computational question, while the paper’s richer computational contribution loses its independent-error pooling benefit in the natural continuum limit.
