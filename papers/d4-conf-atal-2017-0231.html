The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\ge0\) summing to \(1\), a directed compatibility relation \(A\subseteq\Theta\times\Theta\), and a cycle cap \(L\), choose nonnegative masses \(\lambda_q\) of feasible directed cycle patterns \(q\) of lengths \(2,\ldots,L\) to maximize \(\sum_q |q|\lambda_q\), subject to \(\sum_q a_{\theta q}\lambda_q\le\mu_\theta\) for every \(\theta\in\Theta\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete patient-donor compatibility classes; \(\mu_\theta\) is their population mass, \(\lambda_q\) is mass assigned to an integral cycle pattern, and the objective maximizes the fraction of pairs receiving kidneys under per-type capacity constraints.

What the mirror covers

It covers Theorem 2 and its \(3\)-Exchange and \(4\)-Exchange corollaries for static bounded-cycle exchange without altruistic chains; it leaves the chain heuristics, local-search implementation, empirical guarantees, and arbitrary pair-specific graphs aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass LP admit useful column generation when \(L\) is part of the input, and what is the complexity of its pricing problem?
  • Which clinically plausible finite type systems make compatibility type-determined, and how do altruistic chains or stochastic compatibility alter the mass formulation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a bounded-cycle, high-multiplicity kidney-exchange mirror. I would use one anchor only: Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that, for every \(\epsilon>0\), there is a polynomial-time \( \bigl(1+L(L-2)/6+\epsilon\bigr) \)-approximation algorithm for \(L\)-Exchange. Here \(L\)-Exchange asks, given a directed graph, for vertex-disjoint directed cycles of length at most \(L\) covering as many edges as possible. Theorem 2 is proved here using Lemma 1 and Theorem 1, the latter being cited as Theorem 1.2 of [19]. Its printed special cases are Corollary 1, a \( (3/2+\epsilon) \)-approximation for 3-Exchange, and Corollary 2, a \( (7/3+\epsilon) \)-approximation for 4-Exchange.

The paper does mention NP-hardness in its introduction, citing Abraham, Blum, and Sandholm, but it does not state that hardness as a numbered theorem of this paper. I therefore would not pretend to have a hardness anchor. The positive case is instead that the paper’s named approximation result has a natural population-continuous counterpart whose aggregate problem becomes an exact linear program.

Call the mirror Typed \(L\)-Exchange\(_\infty\). An instance consists of:

  • a finite set \(\Theta\) of pair types;
  • a rational mass distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of patient-donor pairs of type \(\theta\);
  • a directed compatibility relation \(A\subseteq\Theta\times\Theta\);
  • a cycle cap \(L\).

A type is a complete description of a patient-donor pair for the static clearing problem: all pairs of type \(\theta\) have the same outgoing compatibility relation, the same incoming compatibility relation, and the same transplant value. In a kidney interpretation, \(\theta\) could encode the relevant blood, tissue, sensitization, clinical, and administrative classes. The setting is a large national or cross-border clearinghouse in which many pairs share each such complete profile, while the number \(\tau=|\Theta|\) of profiles is moderate and \(N\gg\tau\).

A feasible cycle pattern is a sequence \(q=(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_\ell)\), with \(2\le\ell\le L\), such that \(A_{\theta_i,\theta_{i+1}}=1\) cyclically. Repeated types are allowed, provided there is enough mass of that type. Let \(a_{\theta q}\) be the number of times type \(\theta\) occurs in \(q\). The decision variable \(\lambda_q\ge0\) is the mass of actual exchange cycles having pattern \(q\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_q a_{\theta q}\lambda_q\le\mu_\theta \qquad\text{for every }\theta\in\Theta. \]

The objective is

\[ \max \sum_q |q|\lambda_q. \]

Thus the question is: what is the maximum fraction of the population that can receive a kidney through vertex-disjoint cycles of length at most \(L\), when the population is represented by masses of interchangeable pair types?

This is not fractional treatment of an individual. Each unit of mass is assigned to an integral cycle pattern; \(\lambda_q\) records how much population participates in such cycles. The continuity is entirely in the population, exactly as required by the programme.

The high-multiplicity bridge is direct. Given a finite graph with \(N\) vertices and \(n_\theta\) copies of each type, set \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\). Every discrete cycle packing gives a feasible continuous solution by dividing its cycle counts by \(N\). Conversely, a basic optimum of the continuous LP has at most \(\tau\) positive cycle-pattern variables. Rounding \(N\lambda_q\) down for those variables produces a discrete packing losing fewer than \(L\tau\) covered vertices. Hence the normalized difference is \(O(L\tau/N)\). As \(N/\tau\) grows, this is genuinely the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s problem, not merely an analogy.

The authors should recognize this as their problem because it is exactly \(L\)-Exchange on a graph consisting of many interchangeable clones. The objective, cycle constraint, and compatibility semantics are unchanged. What changes is that the population counts become masses, which is precisely what makes an aggregate cycle-packing LP exact. This is not continuizing the outcome space: a kidney is still indivisible for every infinitesimal pair. Nor is it merely using the LP encoding from Section 4; the underlying question is still maximum-cardinality barter exchange.

For fixed \(L\), the expected classification is Class A. There are \(O(\tau^L)\) cycle patterns, so the displayed LP can be written and solved exactly in polynomial time in the explicit type description. In particular, the continuous 3-Exchange problem is exactly solvable, and therefore certainly satisfies the paper’s Corollary 1 guarantee of a \( (3/2+\epsilon) \)-approximation. The same applies to the \(L=4\) case of Corollary 2 and to Theorem 2 for every fixed \(L\).

The more interesting computational questions arise at the boundary: can the LP be solved by column generation when \(L\) is part of the input? Its pricing problem is a maximum-weight directed simple cycle with a length bound, which may become hard for reasons tied to the type graph and cycle cap rather than to population multiplicity. One can also ask whether altruistic chains, weighted transplants, fairness constraints, stochastic compatibility, or dynamic arrivals preserve the LP structure. Those questions are outside this anchor, but they are natural continuations.

My weakest point is the regime itself. Real kidney-pair graphs may contain highly idiosyncratic compatibility edges, and if almost every pair has a unique effective type, the high-multiplicity interpretation is unconvincing. The mirror is strongest for a deliberately typed clearinghouse—many repeated immunological and clinical profiles, with compatibility determined by those profiles—and it covers the paper’s static no-chain \(L\)-Exchange result, not its full empirical framework. Still, that is a credible instance regime for the paper’s abstract problem, and Theorem 2 gives a precise named algorithmic anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. Theorem 2 has a legitimate continuous-population mirror, and the proponent has chosen the right regime.

The strongest objection is that the mirror applies only to blow-ups of a type graph, not to arbitrary directed graphs from the paper. In a completely general exchange graph, making a type a complete description of compatibility may give every vertex a distinct type, so \(\tau\) grows with the population and the high-multiplicity gain disappears. Real kidney-exchange graphs also contain pair-specific immunological and clinical information: coarse types may not determine compatibility, while sufficiently refined types may be nearly unique.

That objection weakens the application, but it does not defeat the proposed scenario. A clearinghouse with compatibility determined by finitely many clinical classes is a coherent high-multiplicity instance regime. The authors would recognize the resulting problem as bounded-cycle exchange on a population of interchangeable clones. It is not fractional treatment of kidneys or an outcome-space relaxation: every unit of mass still belongs to an integral cycle pattern.

Nor does the LP merely assume away the finite problem illegitimately. For rational masses, a basic optimum uses at most \(\tau\) cycle patterns; rounding their counts down loses fewer than \(L\tau\) transplants. Thus the normalized loss is \(O(L\tau/N)\), giving a genuine high-multiplicity bridge. For fixed \(L\), the continuous problem is an exact polynomial-time LP, which is itself a meaningful computational result even if simpler than the paper’s discrete approximation theorem.

So the negative case can say that the mirror is narrower and less empirically secure than the paper’s arbitrary-graph formulation. It cannot support the requested universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 2 remains a surviving anchor.
