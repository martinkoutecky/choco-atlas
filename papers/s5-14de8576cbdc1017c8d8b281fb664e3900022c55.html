The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets \(P,Q\) of agent types with positive rational masses, integer capacities at most \(C\), compatibility relations, and weak rankings of counterpart types, compute a Pareto-stable assignment in an atomless two-sided market in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau\), \(C\), and input bit length \(L\). Each individual receives an integral bundle of counterpart types. A solution may be represented by rational masses \(y_{t,B}\) of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(B\), with pairwise link balances \(\sum_B y_{t,B}k_u(B)=\sum_{B'}y_{u,B'}k_t(B')\). Stability forbids positive-mass groups on opposite sides that are both free to match each other; Pareto efficiency forbids a feasible reassignment that weakly improves almost every agent and strictly improves a positive mass.

The model it lives in

An atomless two-sided market with finite rational-mass type support. Each type fixes its side, capacity, compatibility, and weak ranking of counterpart types; variables \(y_{t,B}\) distribute mass among integral bundle patterns, subject to link-balance, stability, and Pareto-efficiency conditions.

The objection that survived

Stability becomes disjunctive across bundle patterns, and Pareto efficiency is a global condition; finite bundle configurations and mass flows alone do not establish a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau,C,L\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10’s Pareto-stable assignment result and asks for its type-compressed computational counterpart. It leaves the cited prior hardness of maximum-size stable matching and rank-maximal matching as a separate subproblem outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For bounded \(C\), is there an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\) for finding a Pareto-stable assignment, or does the type-compressed problem become hard?
  • Do rational type masses always admit a rational bundle-mass representation with a measurable Pareto-stable realization?
  • How does the complexity change when capacities are unbounded?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mirror of Theorem 10, proved in this paper: every finite many-to-many matching market with ties has an assignment that is both stable—there is no blocking edge under the paper’s free-endpoint definition—and Pareto efficient under its responsive preferences over bundles. The theorem is an existence result, not a complexity classification. Section 7.1 separately gives the paper’s algorithm a polynomial running time in the explicit graph size, but that runtime is not stated as a numbered theorem. So there is no numbered result here that establishes a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Theorem 10 is the nearest anchor; it does not meet the requested anchor standard.

The mirror I would pursue is Finite-Type Atomless Pareto-Stable Assignment. Its regime could be a large hiring or placement market: millions of people on both sides, but relatively few recurring combinations of preferences and capacities. A type is a complete description for the market—side, capacity, compatibility, and ranking of counterpart types. People of the same type are interchangeable for those purposes, and are indifferent among individuals within a counterpart type. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s many-to-many model.

Here is the problem precisely. Give each type \(t\) a rational mass \(\rho_t\), a positive integer capacity \(c_t\), and a weak ranking of compatible types on the other side. Each type consists of an atomless population of that mass. For a type \(t\), let \(\mathcal B_t\) contain the integral bundle vectors \(\mathbf k\) with one coordinate per compatible counterpart type and \(\sum_u k_u\le c_t\). Let \(y_{t,\mathbf k}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(\mathbf k\). Require
\[ \sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathcal B_t}y_{t,\mathbf k}=\rho_t \]
for every type, and, for each compatible pair \(t,u\),
\[ \sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathcal B_t}y_{t,\mathbf k}k_u = \sum_{\boldsymbol\ell\in\mathcal B_u}y_{u,\boldsymbol\ell}\ell_t. \]
The equality says that both sides count the same mass of links between the two types. A solution is a rational \(y\) that can be realized by a measurable matching, is stable, and is Pareto efficient. Stability means there are no positive-mass groups of bundle patterns on opposite sides that are both free to match each other: an agent is free to a type if they have spare capacity or strictly prefer that type to one of their current partners. Pareto efficiency means there is no feasible reassignment that weakly improves almost every agent’s bundle and strictly improves a positive mass of agents. Individual bundles remain integral; only the masses assigned to bundle patterns are fractional.

This is recognizably the paper’s problem: it preserves many-to-many links, capacities, ties, stability, and Pareto efficiency, while making the population the continuous object. The continuous question is whether, given the rational type masses, one can produce such an assignment with running time polynomial in the number of types \(\tau\), the capacity bound \(C=\max_t c_t\), and the input bit length \(L\). My provisional expectation is Class A for bounded capacities, using the finite bundle configurations and their mass flows. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 10. The related questions are whether every such atomless market has a Pareto-stable assignment, whether rational solutions always suffice, and how the result changes when capacities are unbounded.

The weakest point is the gap between the anchor and this proposed problem: Theorem 10 proves existence only for finite graphs, and the paper’s explicit-graph runtime does not establish a bound polynomial in \(\tau,C,L\). The continuum model also needs a careful treatment of stability up to null sets. Those are substantive questions for the mirror to answer. They do not make the regime implausible; they mean this is a proposed continuous research problem, not a result already contained in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Theorem 10 is not a computational-complexity result: it asserts existence. The paper gives an algorithm and an explicit polynomial running time in Section 7.1, but no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishes a complexity classification. Under the programme’s stated standard for a named computational anchor, Theorem 10 does not qualify.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror, though. The paper’s algorithm for finding a Pareto-stable assignment gives a clear problem to ask about in a high-multiplicity regime. Repeated types with the same capacities and rankings over counterpart types are plausible in a large placement or hiring market. The paper’s runtime in the explicit graph size gives no bound polynomial in the number of types and the bit length of their masses; expanding rational masses into individual agents could be exponentially large in that bit length. Finding a compressed algorithm, or establishing that none is possible, is a real computational question.

The proposed configuration formulation also does not by itself yield the claimed LP: blocking constraints are disjunctive, and Pareto efficiency is a global nondominance condition. But that weakens the proponent’s tractability conjecture, not the case for studying the mirror. Even the better-defined, fixed-capacity version remains a sensible high-multiplicity problem whose answer could be tractability or hardness.

So my negative case is narrow: the proponent’s named anchor fails the prescribed bar, and their “finite bundle configurations and mass flows” sketch understates the constraints. I cannot honestly turn that into a case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The paper’s unnumbered algorithm and runtime support a repaired mirror, and the proposed type regime is credible.
