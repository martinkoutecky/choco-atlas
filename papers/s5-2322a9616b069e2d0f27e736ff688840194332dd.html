What the mirror covers

The nearest analogue covers Proposition 1's robustness conditions over a distribution of agent types; it does not cover Theorem 1's latent-state stability claim or the empirical accuracy comparisons as computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper already treats corruption as a population share: its agents have operational types such as model and prompt condition, and a large ensemble of repeated deployments could have \(N\) agents but only a few distinct types. For that scenario, \(\mu_t\) could represent each type’s fraction of the ensemble. The paper’s use of \(\rho=|C|/N\) makes a continuous-population version recognizable to its authors.

But I can’t honestly supply a qualifying anchor under your rule. The paper’s named formal results are Proposition 1 and Theorem 1, both proved here. Proposition 1 says that, on a prompt where one corrupted agent alone returns the distractor, no anonymous, symmetric outcome-level aggregator can be both mostly robust to minority corruption—returning the correct answer more than half the time—and mostly robust to slight-majority corruption—returning it at least half the time. Theorem 1 says that, under its stated assumptions, a fixed-chunk round-robin system is locally Lyapunov stable when \(\rho<\rho_{\max}(K)\), and that sufficiently small chunks and adversarial drift can give \(\rho_{\max}>0.5\). These are impossibility and stability claims, not named results about computational complexity or algorithms.

The nearest continuous question would give a mass distribution over complete agent types—such as model, prompt condition, and answer behavior—and ask whether an anonymous, symmetric aggregator can satisfy the paper’s two robustness guarantees over specified corruption shares. Proposition 1 suggests the same impossibility in that setting. But this is an aggregation-rule feasibility question, not a complexity result of the kind the programme asks us to mirror; the paper’s proposition already ranges over finite ensembles, so passing from counts to masses may add little beyond replacing frequencies with proportions. Theorem 1 is an even weaker anchor for this purpose: its continuous object is the latent semantic state \(h_t\), while the agent population remains finite and turn-ordered.

So there is a plausible population interpretation, especially for large ensembles of repeated agents with few operational types. The weak point is decisive for this assignment: the paper contains no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo complexity question to, and I would not present either formal theorem as one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is an impossibility result about aggregation axioms; Theorem 1 is a stability claim about a dynamical system; the accuracy results are experiments. None asks for an algorithm, establishes a complexity classification, or defines an encoded computational problem. That is a decisive mismatch with the programme’s focus.

Proposition 1 is the closest anchor for a population model. A distribution over complete agent types—model, prompt condition, and response behavior—could make corruption share \(\rho\) a mass. The resulting question would be whether an anonymous, symmetric aggregator can meet the stated robustness guarantees. That is a coherent continuous-population question, but it is an axiomatic feasibility question, not a computational one. A stronger computational variant might ask for the minimum mass or cost of corrupted agents needed to force a wrong output. The paper, however, supplies no cost model or optimization problem for choosing whom to corrupt; adding those would create a new problem rather than mirror Proposition 1.

Theorem 1 does not supply a better anchor. Its continuous object is the latent state \(h_t\); the agents remain finite and take turns in an order that affects the trajectory. Replacing the roster with an average over agent types would change the sequential operator product into a different model. Preserving the relay while using a population distribution would require additional inputs such as type-specific transition rules and a scheduling model. That could lead to a new computational question, but the theorem itself is an analytic stability claim, not a complexity result. The experiments’ use of \(\rho\) as a fraction makes a large-ensemble scenario plausible; it does not turn the reported accuracy comparisons into computational results.

I would not claim that no sensible high-multiplicity model of repeated LLM ensembles could ever be worth studying. That is where the universal negative case is weakest. But for this paper, the proposed mirrors of Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 miss the programme’s computational scope, and stronger variants require adding the very computational problem the paper does not pose.
