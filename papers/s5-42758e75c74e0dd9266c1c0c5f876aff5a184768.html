The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest case is a high-multiplicity version of unrelated-machine scheduling in a large compute fleet. A plausible regime is many independently operated servers drawn from a small number of hardware and service profiles, processing recurring batches of job classes. The point is that the population is large while the number of distinct processing-time vectors, \(\tau\), stays comparatively small.

My anchor is Theorem 33, proved in this paper. It states that for scheduling tasks on \(n\) unrelated machines, every deterministic truthful mechanism has approximation ratio at least \(1+\sqrt{n-1}\), with no restriction on its running time. This is a mechanism-design approximation lower bound, not a theorem about membership in \(P\) or NP-hardness; the paper has no numbered result of that latter kind. The theorem is still a computational result in the mechanism-design sense, and it gives a clear problem to mirror.

I would call the mirror the Continuous Nisan–Ronen Ratio Problem. An instance has \(m\) job classes, a rational per-capita supply \(q_j\) for each class \(j\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over processing-time types \(t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\), with \(|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|=\tau\). Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of machines with processing-time vector \(t\). At population scale \(N\), there are \(N\mu_t\) machines of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) indivisible jobs of class \(j\), for scales \(N\) where these counts are integral.

Each individual machine receives an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\), with load \(\sum_j t_j b_j\). In the limit, \(x_{t,b}\) records the fraction of the population of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=q_j\). Thus the aggregate assignment can be fractional even though every machine gets an integral bundle. Processing-time vectors are private; a mechanism takes the machines’ reports, assigns bundles, and makes payments, with utility equal to payment minus the machine’s actual processing time. It must be deterministic and dominant-strategy truthful at every finite scale \(N\).

The question is to determine the best worst-case makespan ratio \(\rho_{\mathrm{HM}}(\tau)\) achievable by such a family of truthful mechanisms as \(N\) grows, over job supplies and societies with at most \(\tau\) types. A solution would be either a mechanism family with a proved asymptotic ratio, or a lower-bound proof ruling out a claimed ratio for every such family. The natural test suggested by Theorem 33 is whether the barrier depends on the number of types—for example, whether \(\rho_{\mathrm{HM}}(\tau)\ge 1+\sqrt{\tau-1}\)—or whether mass splitting lets a mechanism do better.

This is a credible analogue of the paper’s problem: the agents are still machines, types still describe their full cost vectors, the objective is still makespan, and truthfulness is still an individual incentive requirement. Only the population becomes the continuous object. Scaling job supply with population size makes the fleet regime meaningful; assigning fractions of a type to different integral bundles describes the resulting aggregate without making individual jobs divisible. I would mirror only Theorem 33, not claim coverage of the paper’s randomized or fractional-mechanism results.

My provisional expectation is Class C, in the programme’s closest sense: any surviving barrier would arise from the continuous model’s own incentive geometry under mass splitting. I would not predict that Theorem 33 transfers just by replacing \(n\) with \(\tau\). The theorem counts individually manipulable machines, while this mirror has many machines sharing each type. That gives the prover useful questions: does the ratio depend on \(\tau\), on the smallest positive type mass, or on both? Can fixed support allow the ratio to approach \(1\) as \(N\) grows?

The weakest point is that the limiting aggregate \(x_{t,b}\) does not, by itself, express what happens when one machine deviates. That is why the mirror is defined through truthful finite replications and their asymptotic performance, rather than by treating an LP over \(x\) as the whole problem. Even with that care, the paper’s lower-bound construction may lose force when many machines share a type and can spread jobs among themselves. So this is a sensible continuous problem closely tied to the theorem, but I would not present its proposed ratio bound as an expected transfer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 33 is the only serious anchor, but it bounds the approximation ratio of every deterministic truthful mechanism even when computation is unlimited. It does not give a running-time bound or identify an input problem whose complexity the theorem studies. In ChoCo’s stated sense—complexity and algorithms for problems on a society distribution—that makes it a poor anchor for a continuous computational mirror. Its subject is the expressive limits of truthful allocation rules.

The natural continuum model makes that mismatch visible. If a mechanism sees only type masses \(\mu\) and returns aggregate assignments \(x_{t,b}\), a single machine’s report changes no mass and no aggregate assignment. The reported model therefore cannot express the individual deviation on which Theorem 33 rests. One could add a report-contingent assignment and payment menu for a representative machine, with incentive constraints for every possible processing vector. That is a coherent continuous mechanism-design model, but its central problem is still characterizing truthful mechanisms. The aggregate variables alone do not turn it into the kind of LP-and-pricing question ChoCo is organized to study.

The proponent’s finite-replication version repairs individual truthfulness: for each \(N\), use \(N\) machines and impose the original dominant-strategy constraints, then study the limiting worst-case ratio. That is a sensible high-multiplicity research question, and the fleet scenario makes the regime plausible. But the limit is a question about a sequence of discrete mechanism-design problems. Exact incentive constraints still compare one machine’s reports across full profiles; an aggregate assignment \(x\) does not encode them. A cohort-as-agent alternative would preserve the cohort’s ability to affect the outcome, but it would change individual truthfulness into a group or coalition incentive requirement.

So the strongest case against is about fit, not whether the proposed model is legitimate: these versions do not turn this paper’s result into a computational problem over a continuous society in ChoCo’s sense. The case is not airtight. An asymptotic ratio for truthful mechanisms with fixed support could be worthwhile on its own, and the large-compute-fleet example is credible. I would argue that it belongs to high-multiplicity mechanism design rather than this programme’s continuous complexity landscape; I would not claim that no worthwhile mirror could be formulated.
