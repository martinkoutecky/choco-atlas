The continuous mirror question

Given resident types \(a\in\mathcal A\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), hospital types \(b\in\mathcal B\) with rational supplies \(\nu_b\), and a strict ranking of hospital types for each resident type, assume every hospital has quotas \([1,2]\), is indifferent among residents, and \(2\sum_b\nu_b>1\). For each hospital type \(b\) and integral assignment pattern \(k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\mathcal A}\) with \(\sum_a k_a\le2\), let \(z_{b,k}\) be the mass of hospitals assigned that pattern; require \(\sum_k z_{b,k}=\nu_b\) and \(\sum_{b,k}k_a z_{b,k}=\mu_a\). Set \(x_{a,b}=\sum_k k_a z_{b,k}\), \(D_{a,b}=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{a,b'}\), and \(V_b=\sum_{k:\,\sum_a k_a<2}z_{b,k}\). Among assignments satisfying, for every \(a,b\), \(D_{a,b}=0\) or \(V_b=0\), maximize \(\sum_b\sum_{k:\,\sum_a k_a\ge1}z_{b,k}\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic stable matching market with resident-type masses and hospital-type supplies. Each hospital unit receives an integral bundle of at most two residents; the decision variables \(z_{b,k}\) give the mass of units assigned each bundle, and the objective maximizes the mass of hospitals receiving at least one resident subject to no positive-mass blocking pair.

The objection that survived

The positive-mass stability condition can ignore a single vacant hospital that would block in every corresponding finite market, and the proponent leaves its relationship to replicated finite instances open.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 14, proved in this paper in Section 6 (with the reduction in Appendix D.3). It says HRT-MSLQ is NP-hard even when every hospital has quotas \([1,2]\), every resident has a complete strict preference list, and all hospitals are indifferent among all residents. In this subcase, a hospital’s satisfaction ratio is \(1\) when it has at least one resident and \(0\) when it is empty. So the hard problem is to choose a weakly stable matching that occupies as many hospitals as possible.

A plausible mirror is a large placement market with many residents and many small facilities, such as a centralized rural-clinic staffing system. Clinics need one resident to operate and can take at most two; after a common eligibility screen, they treat eligible residents interchangeably. Residents fall into cohorts with the same ranking of facility classes. The market can have \(N\) residents and \(N'\) hospitals, with both growing while the numbers of distinct resident and hospital types remain comparatively small. This is a narrower scenario than ordinary hospital recruitment, but it matches the theorem’s indifference assumption.

Call the mirror Continuous HRT-MSLQ with \([1,2]\) quotas and indifferent hospitals. Its input is finite sets of resident types \(\mathcal A\) and hospital types \(\mathcal B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), and a strict, complete ranking \(\succ_a\) of hospital types for each resident type \(a\). Normalize \(\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\mu_a=1\); \(\nu_b\) is the mass of hospital units of type \(b\) on the same population scale. Every unit hospital has quota \([1,2]\) and is indifferent among residents. A type records the complete preference information used by the model, so residents of the same type share a ranking and residents treat hospitals in the same type as interchangeable.

A solution is a mass distribution over integral hospital assignments. For \(k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\mathcal A}\) with \(\sum_a k_a\le2\), let \(z_{b,k}\) be the mass of type-\(b\) hospitals assigned exactly \(k_a\) residents of each type \(a\). Require \(\sum_k z_{b,k}=\nu_b\) for every \(b\), and \(\sum_{b,k}k_a z_{b,k}=\mu_a\) for every \(a\). Thus individual hospitals still receive zero, one, or two residents; fractional values arise only in the mass of hospitals with each assignment pattern.

Write \(x_{a,b}=\sum_k k_a z_{b,k}\), \(D_{a,b}=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{a,b'}\), and \(V_b=\sum_{k:\,\sum_a k_a<2}z_{b,k}\). Define stability by requiring, for every \(a,b\), that \(D_{a,b}=0\) or \(V_b=0\): there cannot be positive mass of type-\(a\) residents assigned below \(b\) while positive mass of type-\(b\) hospitals has a vacancy. The question is to find a stable \(z\) maximizing \(\sum_b\sum_{k:\,\sum_a k_a\ge1}z_{b,k}\), the mass of hospitals meeting their lower quota. This is the continuous optimization question mirroring Theorem 14’s underlying problem, not a continuous-outcome or encoding question.

The mirror passes the recognition test for me: it preserves stable matching, the \([1,2]\) capacity structure, and the lower-quota objective. Grouping indistinguishable agents into types is the high-multiplicity move; indifference among facilities in a class is its natural preference co-modification, and ties are already part of HRT. My expectation is Class A for the intended high-multiplicity regime: with few types relative to the population and capacity two, there are only \(O(|\mathcal A|^2)\) local assignment patterns per hospital type. The hard part is whether the stability conditions can be handled in time polynomial in the type counts and input bit length. I would treat that as a conjecture to investigate, not as a result. Further questions are whether a polynomial-time algorithm exists in \(|\mathcal A|,|\mathcal B|,L\); whether the continuous optimum has a precise limit or approximation relationship to large replicated discrete instances; and whether hardness survives when the type supports stay small compared with population size.

The mirror covers only Theorem 14’s uniform-\([1,2]\), indifferent-hospital subcase. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s other hardness or approximation results, its strategy-proof algorithm, or its results for other quota regimes.

My weakest point is that the paper’s hardness proof reduces from minimum Pareto-optimal matching, whose combinatorics may rely on many distinct preference lists. Theorem 14 therefore does not establish hardness when many agents share a small set of types, and the configuration count alone does not resolve the stability constraints. Also, hospitals indifferent among residents is a special placement scenario, not a general account of hospital recruitment. Those limitations weaken the predicted complexity result, but they do not make the continuous question a restatement: it asks whether the same stable lower-quota objective changes when the market consists of large, genuinely repeated cohorts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection I can make is a limit problem in the proposed mirror’s stability condition. With quotas \([1,2]\), a finite hospital has a vacancy whenever it has fewer than two residents. In a market with \(N\) residents and hospital counts scaled with \(N\), one vacant hospital has mass \(1/N\). The proposed condition \(D_{a,b}=0\) or \(V_b=0\) cannot see that vacancy in the limit, even though in the finite market any resident of type \(a\) assigned below \(b\) would form a blocking pair with it. So the mirror captures blocking by a positive share of hospitals, not every block in the paper’s weakly stable matching.

That makes the mirror depend on a modeling choice the paper does not settle: should stability in a continuum market rule out only positive-mass blocking, or preserve the finite model’s sensitivity to a single vacancy? A support-based condition could retain the latter, but the mass variables \(z_{b,k}\) do not record a vacancy that occurs on a set of measure zero. Tracking such vacancies would add information beyond the continuous type distribution. Scaling capacities with the market could avoid vanishing hospitals, but it changes the fixed \([1,2]\)-quota setting of Theorem 14.

This is a real weakness in the proposed formulation, but I cannot make it defeat the anchor. Positive-mass stability is a coherent asymptotic question, and the configuration variables preserve each hospital’s integral assignment while allowing the population of hospitals and residents to scale. Ranking facility classes, with residents indifferent among units in a class, is also a plausible cohort model for large placement systems. The paper has a named computational result for that regime, and I see no continuous computational treatment here.

So the negative case is weak: Theorem 14 remains a worthwhile mirror candidate, provided the authors are asked to justify the stability notion and its relation to large finite instances. I cannot honestly argue that no scenario or reasonable type space produces a useful mirror.
