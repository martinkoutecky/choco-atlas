The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational distribution \(\mu\), \(m\) resource kinds, common values \(\alpha>\beta>\gamma\ge0\), type utilities \(u_t(o)\in\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}\), and initial bundles \(e_t\in\{0,1\}^m\), define \(u_t(B)=\sum_o u_t(o)B_o\) and \(s_o=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t e_t(o)\). Decide whether there exist masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), for \(B\in\{0,1\}^m\), satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_o=s_o\), \(u_t(B)\ge u_t(e_t)\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\), and \(u_t(B)>u_t(e_t)\) for positive total mass.

The model it lives in

A configuration LP over integral bundle columns \(B\in\{0,1\}^m\), with type masses \(x_{t,B}\), per-capita resource supplies \(s_o\), weak improvement for every used bundle, and objective equal to the mass receiving strict improvement.

The objection that survived

The model changes individually named goods into repeated resource kinds, permits type mass to split across bundles, and has no finite-to-continuous theorem showing that this is the unique or canonical high-multiplicity limit of the paper's economy.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's three-value additive Pareto-optimality test. It leaves the other hardness, pseudo-polynomial, lexicographic, bivalued, conservative, possible, and necessary Pareto-optimality results untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration LP be solved in time polynomial in \(m\), \(|T|\), and the encoding length using a valid pricing oracle for the three utility classes?
  • For unrestricted additive values, does pricing become knapsack-hard and yield a continuum-specific hard case?
  • What exact or approximate rounding theorem maps rational \(\mu\)-solutions back to \(K\)-agent markets?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Pareto-optimality test.

Consider a large entitlement exchange with \(K\) agents and \(m\) kinds of indivisible one-use resources. There are only \(\tau\) recurring agent types, with \(K\gg\tau\). A type \(t\) includes both an additive utility vector \(u_t\) and an initial bundle \(e_t\); agents of the same type are indistinguishable in every respect relevant to reallocation. Assume, in the strongest version of the mirror, that all utilities use the same three values \(\alpha>\beta>\gamma\geq0\).

The continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over types. Resource supplies scale with the population: resource \(o\) has per-capita supply \(s_o=\sum_t\mu_t e_t(o)\). Each individual still receives an integral bundle \(B\in\{0,1\}^m\); only the population and aggregate resource counts are continuous. Thus a mass \(x_{t,B}\) means that this fraction of type \(t\) receives the integral bundle \(B\). It is not a fractional allocation of an object to an individual.

I would call the resulting problem \(\mathrm{3V\mbox{-}PO}_\infty\). Its instance consists of \(T,\mu,(u_t,e_t)_{t\in T}\), and the induced resource supplies. We ask whether there is a feasible mass reassignment \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_o=s_o\) for every resource \(o\), and \(u_t(B)\geq u_t(e_t)\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\), with strict inequality for a positive total mass of agents. Equivalently, maximize \(z=\sum_{t,B:u_t(B)>u_t(e_t)}x_{t,B}\); the initial society is continuously Pareto optimal exactly when the optimum is \(0\).

This is a direct high-multiplicity analogue of the paper’s underlying problem. A finite \(K\)-agent market with \(K\mu_t\) clones of each type and \(Ks_o\) resource copies maps to the continuous instance, and conversely rational continuous data can be realized by a sufficiently large clone market. The Pareto condition remains individual: every member of every type must weakly improve, rather than merely receiving a better average utility.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that testing Pareto optimality of a given assignment under additive utilities is strongly coNP-complete even when there are only three utility values. The reduction is from \((3,\mathrm{B}2)\)-SAT. For each variable it creates two agents, for each clause a clause agent, and two additional agents \(A\) and \(H\); it constructs an initial assignment \(q\) using variable, clause, and auxiliary goods. The only utility values are \(1\), \(t\), and \(\varepsilon=1/(6t)\). A satisfying truth assignment yields a Pareto improvement of \(q\), while any Pareto improvement of \(q\) induces a satisfying truth assignment. Hence \(q\) is not Pareto optimal exactly when the formula is satisfiable. The theorem therefore gives a genuine hardness anchor, not merely a formulation.

I expect \(\mathrm{3V\mbox{-}PO}_\infty\) to be in Class A, even though the discrete problem in Theorem 3 is strongly coNP-complete. For each type \(t\), let \(U_t\) be the family of bundles with \(u_t(B)\geq u_t(e_t)\). The continuous problem is a configuration LP whose variables are the masses \(x_{t,B}\). It has exponentially many bundle variables, but its pricing problem has a simple structure under three utility values.

Given resource shadow prices, pricing asks for the cheapest bundle meeting a utility threshold. Partition the resources for type \(t\) into the three utility classes. For every possible number of selected resources from each class, choose the cheapest resources in those classes. Enumerating the three counts gives a polynomial-time pricing algorithm; the strict-improvement columns are handled by the same calculation with a strict threshold. Standard column-generation or separation machinery should therefore solve the LP in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length.

This is exactly the kind of dissolution the programme is looking for: the discrete SAT hardness is encoded through the identities of individual agents and the integral assignment, whereas the continuous version replaces those identities by mass flow between finitely many complete types. The three-value restriction makes the resulting pricing problem especially promising. The expected classification is therefore Class A, with Theorem 3’s coNP-hardness disappearing in the population continuum.

The mirror covers Theorem 3 only. It does not claim that every result in the paper has a useful continuum analogue. It also generates natural follow-up questions: whether unrestricted additive values make pricing knapsack-hard and produce a Class C problem; whether the result changes when agents may receive multiple copies of a resource kind; and how exactly a continuous solution rounds to a \(K\)-agent allocation.

My weakest point is that the mirror must scale the resource supply and treat repeated copies of a good as one resource kind. A critic could say that the paper’s original objects are individually named and that the three-value pricing argument is a restriction rather than a theorem about the full additive model. I accept that limitation. But it is the honest high-multiplicity regime: the underlying question remains Pareto-improving reallocation of indivisible bundles under additive utilities and initial endowments, while only the population is replaced by mass. The fact that the original SAT gadget does not itself consist of repeated types is not a defect; the programme asks whether this problem has a sensible high-multiplicity regime, not whether every discrete reduction survives it unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 3 is not really a population-complexity result. Its \((3,\mathrm{B}2)\)-SAT gadget is built from individually exceptional agents and individually named goods. Once a type includes the complete utility vector and initial bundle, essentially every gadget role is a different type. In the original one-copy economy, two agents cannot even share the same nonempty endowment, since each object is available only once. Thus the theorem itself supplies no natural \(K\gg\tau\) regime.

The proposed rescue—replace objects by resource kinds and scale their supplies per capita—is mathematically coherent, but it changes the exchange economy in a substantive way. The SAT construction relies on whole objects such as \(h_i\), \(v_i^\ell\), and \(w_i^\ell\) carrying the logical coupling. With repeated copies and mass variables \(x_{t,B}\), a type can split across several integral bundles: some of its mass can consume \(h_i\), while another part releases positive and negative literal resources. Clause mass can also split across different literals. To retain the original gadget, one would need all-or-nothing type-cohort decisions or indivisible organizational blocks. Those constraints restore the very agent-level granularity that the continuum was meant to remove.

So the proposed model is better described as a new atomless entitlement-exchange problem than as the continuous version of the paper’s given-assignment problem. Its configuration LP may be interesting, but the paper supplies no finite-to-continuous theorem, rounding guarantee, or structural reason that this particular market is the right high-multiplicity interpretation of its Pareto test. The three-value pricing argument is also generic: it follows immediately by enumerating how many resources are chosen from each utility class, rather than exposing a distinctive phenomenon in the paper.

That is the best negative case, but it is not decisive under the programme’s rules. The entitlement-exchange scenario is plausible, per-capita resource scaling is explicitly an allowed co-modification, and each unit of mass still receives an integral bundle. The LP’s pricing oracle appears genuinely polynomial, so this is a legitimate Class A question even though it is not a faithful continuation of the SAT gadget. I therefore cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the proponent’s Theorem 3 anchor survives the strongest objection.
