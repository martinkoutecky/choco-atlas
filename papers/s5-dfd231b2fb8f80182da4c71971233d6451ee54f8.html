The continuous mirror question

Given a network \(G=(V,E)\), rational per-capita edge capacities \(\kappa_e\), and a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type \(t\) has a terminal pair \((s_t,z_t)\), demand \(d_t\), and value \(w_t\), compute a welfare-maximizing mass assignment to simple routes. Choose \(x_{t,P}\ge 0\) to maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{P} w_t x_{t,P}\), subject to \(\sum_P x_{t,P}\le\mu_t\) for each \(t\) and \(\sum_{t,P:e\in P}d_t x_{t,P}\le\kappa_e\) for each edge \(e\). Each unit of mass takes one whole route; the mass of a type may be divided among routes.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity unicast access market with standardized terminal pairs, demands, and values. Route-mass variables \(x_{t,P}\) give the welfare objective, while per-capita capacities \(\kappa_e\) scale with population; equivalently, an edge-flow LP routes \(q_t\le d_t\mu_t\) for each type and maximizes \(\sum_t (w_t/d_t)q_t\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the unicast welfare-optimization component of Theorem 2; leaves its DA payment and weak group strategy-proofness guarantees, its finite-instance approximation bound, and the multicast Steiner-tree case outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What additive or rounding bounds relate the mass optimum to finite populations with many contracts per type?
  • Does the analogous multicast mass-assignment problem admit an efficient algorithm, or does the Steiner-tree subproblem preserve its computational difficulty?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Continuous Unicast Bandwidth Reallocation\(_\infty\), for the unicast part of Theorem 2. The theorem states that Algorithm 4 is a DA-implementable \(\left(\frac{\bar e\gamma C}{C-1}m^{1/(C-1)}\right)^{-1}\)-approximation for network bandwidth reallocation, where \(m\) is the number of edges, \(C=\min_e c(e)>1\), and \(\gamma=1\) for unicast or \(1.55\) for multicast. The paper proves the DA-implementability construction; its proof refers the approximation guarantee to Briest et al. [2].

Here is the mirror problem. The input is a network \(G=(V,E)\) with per-capita edge capacities \(\kappa_e\), and a finite set \(T\) of firm types. A type \(t\) specifies a terminal pair \((s_t,t_t)\), demand \(d_t\in(0,1]\), value per retained contract \(w_t\ge 0\), and population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A solution assigns mass \(x_{t,P}\ge 0\) of type \(t\) to each simple route \(P\) between its terminals, subject to

\[ \sum_{P}x_{t,P}\le \mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t,P:e\in P}d_t x_{t,P}\le\kappa_e \quad\text{for every }e. \]

The goal is to maximize \(\sum_{t,P}w_t x_{t,P}\). Each unit of mass still represents a firm whose entire demand uses one route; the population of a type can be divided among routes because it represents many distinct firms. A solution is an optimal feasible mass assignment.

The regime is a large market of standardized network-access contracts: for example, many business connections sharing the same metro-area endpoints, bandwidth tier, and value class. There may be millions of contracts but far fewer distinct endpoint–demand–value types. Capacity scales with the population, so \(\kappa_e\) is capacity per unit mass; in a finite lift with \(n\mu_t\) contracts of type \(t\), the corresponding edge capacity is \(n\kappa_e\). This capacity scaling is part of the mirror: keeping total capacity fixed as the population grows would make the retained fraction vanish.

I expect the unicast mirror to be Class A. Its route-mass formulation is a multicommodity-flow LP. Equivalently, send flow \(q_t\le d_t\mu_t\) for each type, maximize \(\sum_t(w_t/d_t)q_t\), and impose the edge capacities and flow-conservation constraints. A flow can be decomposed into routes, so this LP exactly describes how a continuum of firms can each take one unsplittable route. The path-based formulation may have many variables, but the edge-flow LP has polynomial size in the network and \(\tau=|T|\). This gives an exact continuous optimization problem, not merely an approximation guarantee. The paper’s Appendix C already writes an LP relaxation for the finite routing problem; the mirror’s point is that, with a genuinely large population of each type, route-mass assignments make that kind of LP the population’s actual feasible outcome.

This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ problem because it retains their firms, access rights, terminal pairs, demands, capacity constraints, and welfare objective. It covers the welfare-maximization routing problem underlying Theorem 2’s unicast clause; it does not claim a continuous extension of the theorem’s weak group-strategyproof mechanism. The multicast clause suggests a further question: can the same mass interpretation make tree assignments tractable, or does the Steiner-tree pricing problem preserve the combinatorial difficulty? A second useful question is how sharply finite instances with many contracts per type approach the mass optimum, including rounding or additive-error bounds.

My weakest point is the market regime: if the real instances have few large firms, or each firm has distinctive endpoints, demand, or value, then \(\tau\) may be close to the number of agents and the high-multiplicity interpretation is much less persuasive. There is also a precise boundary in the model: aggregate flow may split across routes, while each infinitesimal firm still uses one route. That is justified by many firms per type, but not by a small set of individually important firms. The paper’s prose notes NP-hardness for the maximum-weight \(k\)-colorable subgraph problem, but it is not a numbered hardness result, so I do not use it as an anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror carries over the welfare optimization problem from Theorem 2 but drops the paper’s main mechanism-design guarantee: weak group strategy-proofness. In a continuum, a single firm has measure zero, so unilateral incentive constraints can become vacuous. To preserve the paper’s contribution, one would need a meaningful continuum version of the auction, its payments, and its group deviations. That is a new mechanism question, not simply the paper’s DA auction with more firms.

But this does not defeat the anchor the other analyst actually raised. Theorem 2 includes a computational welfare problem, and the analyst explicitly limits the mirror to that problem. Nor does the route-splitting objection work: an atomless cohort can be partitioned among routes so each firm takes one whole route while the cohort’s aggregate assignment realizes fractional route flows. With standardized terminal pairs, demands, and values, and per-capita capacities, the resulting multicommodity-flow LP is a coherent continuous-population model. Capacity scaling is an allowed co-modification, and the finite-lift interpretation makes the high-multiplicity connection clear.

The best version of the mirror is therefore stronger than the stated one: ask for the exact mass optimum and how well finite populations approximate it. The flow LP gives an exact continuous answer, while the finite-to-continuous approximation question tests what that answer says about large discrete instances. The fact that standard optimization machinery may answer the first question quickly, or that the answer may differ from the paper’s discrete guarantee, is not a reason to reject the question under this programme’s rules.

I cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The incentive guarantee is a real limitation on how much of the paper’s contribution survives, but it does not erase the optimization mirror. Claims that type aggregation loses individual routing, that capacities cannot scale, or that an existing flow LP makes the question already done would not overcome the stated scope rules. The negative case is weak precisely at its strongest anchor.
