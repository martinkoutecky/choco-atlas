The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many political-interest types \(S\) with rational masses \(\mu_s>0\), \(\sum_{s\in S}\mu_s=1\), the paper's quasi-distributional policies and payoffs with its cost and coalition assumptions scaled to mass, and an explicit finite proposer schedule, compute the pure-strategy subgame-perfect outcome from any initial policy. Votes follow the paper's vote-as-if-pivotal convention and a proposal passes when supporting mass exceeds \(1/2\). In particular, does the outcome equal \(\{s_T\}\), the final proposer's bloc policy, when that bloc's mass is at least the size-biased median bloc mass and either the mass of blocs represented among proposers is at least \(1/2+\mu_{s_T}\) or that bloc had an earlier proposer?

The model it lives in

A policy game with a finite set of political-interest types \(S\), rational population shares \(\mu_s\), and the paper's quasi-distributional utilities. Keep the finite recognition schedule and pure-strategy equilibrium for proposal choices; tally voter support by mass using the paper's vote-as-if-pivotal convention.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result that can serve as an anchor: its theorems characterize equilibrium outcomes, efficiency, and dictatorship, but none classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. I can’t make an anchor-compliant case for a continuous computational mirror from this paper.

The closest foothold is Theorem 9, proved in the paper. In a quasi-distributional policy setting, if the last proposer belongs to a group at least as large as \(N^*\), then the group’s policy is the outcome when either enough people are represented among proposers—\(|J_0|\ge M+|I_{r(i(T))}|\)—or someone from the last proposer’s group proposed earlier. That suggests a plausible high-multiplicity scenario: a large population divided into a few recurring political-interest blocs, with each bloc’s share represented by a rational mass. One could ask for the equilibrium outcome of sequential proposal and voting in that society. But this would be a new computational question based on an analytic result, not a complexity result established by the paper.

That is the strongest positive lead I can defend. Its weak point is decisive for the requested standard: the authors study equilibrium properties, not the computational complexity of finding equilibria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 9 is the proponent’s only named foothold, and it is an equilibrium-selection theorem, not a computational result. It says when the last proposer’s group determines the policy; it gives no complexity claim about finding that outcome or solving an optimization problem. Turning it into “compute the equilibrium outcome for a continuous society” would define a new algorithmic problem, not mirror a computational result in this paper. Under the programme’s stated test, that is a decisive gap.

The proposed bloc model is sensible as a high-multiplicity electorate: rational masses can represent groups with shared interests, and majority support can be expressed as a mass threshold. But Theorem 9 also turns on who proposes last and who proposed earlier. Those are positional roles. A mass distribution over voter types does not preserve them unless the model adds a finite schedule of designated proposers or a process for selecting proposers. Either choice may be a reasonable model, but it adds a strategic structure that the continuous electorate alone does not supply.

The strongest repair is to ask for the complexity of computing subgame-perfect equilibrium given rational masses over payoff types, a finite policy space, and explicit proposal roles. That could be a worthwhile problem, and the authors would likely recognize its subject matter. Still, it is a new computational question built from their model, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 9. So the case against this anchor is strong; the broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous computational problem is much weaker. A continuum of voters with a finite agenda-setting structure is plausible, but this paper does not establish a computational result to anchor that mirror.
