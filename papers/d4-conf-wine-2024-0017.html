The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is a literal continuum version of the paper’s large-election model. Let \(T\) be a finite support of complete voter types, where each type records a contingent preference and its cardinal utility vector, and let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s population mass. In state \(\omega\), mass \(\mu_t P_s^\omega\) has type \(t\) and signal \(s\). A strategy maps \((t,s)\) to a report distribution; an anonymous mechanism maps the resulting report masses to an outcome.

A precise candidate question is: given rational type masses, signal probabilities, utilities, and an explicitly represented anonymous mechanism, does there exist a strategy profile that implements the informed-majority decision in each state and is a strong Bayes Nash equilibrium against every measurable coalition of voters? A solution would be such a strategy profile. A plausible regime is a very large electorate with many repeated preference and utility types, such as residents sharing a small set of interests and information channels. That is a sensible population model, and the authors would recognize the strategic aggregation question.

But I cannot honestly attach this question to a qualifying anchor. The paper’s numbered results are about equilibrium existence and mechanism feasibility, not computational complexity. For example, Theorem 1, proved in this paper, gives the majority-vote threshold \(\theta_{\mathrm{maj}}=1/(2M)\): above it, approximate strong equilibria exist and all such equilibria aggregate the informed majority decision with probability tending to one; at or below it, the stated nonexistence result holds. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives a mechanism with threshold \(\theta^*=1/(\Delta+1)\), above which truthful reporting is an approximate strong equilibrium that aggregates the decision. Theorems 3 and 4, proved here, give impossibility results below that threshold for anonymous mechanisms, respectively ruling out truthful aggregation and symmetric equilibrium aggregation under their stated assumptions. These are substantial structural results, but none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

There is a further weakness: this continuum formulation largely restates the paper’s own asymptotic analysis. The authors explicitly let \(n\) grow without bound, define configurations that allow arbitrarily large \(n\), and derive thresholds from limiting population proportions. The candidate question therefore has a plausible continuous population, but its natural answer is already the paper’s analysis. Its thresholds are given in closed form; that does not turn the paper’s theorems into named complexity results.

One could pose a new computational question about mechanism synthesis for a compactly encoded class of anonymous mechanisms. But the paper does not specify such an encoding or study that algorithmic task, so I would not present it as a mirror of one of its results. Under ChoCo’s computational scope and the required anchor rule, this paper offers no qualifying anchor for a positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the anchor rule: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 gives the equilibrium threshold \(\theta_{\mathrm{maj}}=1/(2M)\); Theorem 2 gives a mechanism with threshold \(\theta^*=1/(\Delta+1)\); Theorems 3 and 4 give impossibility results for anonymous mechanisms under stated assumptions. These are equilibrium and mechanism-design characterizations, not claims about algorithmic complexity. The finite-\(n\) bounds use concentration inequalities, but they do not analyze an algorithm’s running time.

The best repair for Theorem 1 would be to ask whether a given rational configuration admits a good strong equilibrium under majority voting. But the paper already characterizes that question in the large-population limit and gives the threshold and a strategy explicitly. Turning the characterization into a decision procedure over encoded rational inputs would add a computational wrapper; it would not supply a missing computational result from the paper. Theorem 2 and the impossibility theorems fare no better: a new problem that searches a restricted, succinctly represented class of mechanisms might be worthwhile, but the encoding and search task would have to be supplied from outside the paper.

The proposed question about arbitrary explicitly represented anonymous mechanisms does not fix the gap. A finite representation for the mechanism, report space, strategies, and allowable deviations is needed before it defines a computational problem. Choosing those restrictions could yield a legitimate research question, but it would be a new mechanism-synthesis or equilibrium-computation problem, not a continuous mirror of one of these theorems.

The high-multiplicity regime itself is credible here: a large electorate with repeated preference and information types is a natural setting. Nor would I reject a mirror merely because the paper studies \(n\to\infty\). But its main results already express equilibrium behavior in terms of population proportions and signal distributions, so a mass-based restatement would sit very close to the analysis it has done.

I can defend a strong case against a ChoCo-style computational mirror anchored in this paper’s results. I cannot honestly establish the broader claim that no useful continuous computational question could arise from this model: a carefully specified complexity study of atomless coalition equilibria could be worthwhile. It would be a new problem, though, rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.
