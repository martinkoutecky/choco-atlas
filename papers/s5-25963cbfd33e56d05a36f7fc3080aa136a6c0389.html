The strongest positive follow-on I can make is a search problem motivated by Theorem 1(ii) and Lemma 1(ii), though those results are not valid computational anchors under your rule. Call it High-Multiplicity Pareto-Improving Rule Search. An instance has \(k\) constituency classes, rational population shares \(p_g\) and voting weights \(W_g\), and a rational, piecewise-constant, full-support density for their joint vote-margin vector \(\Theta\in[-1,1]^k\). In each realization \(\theta\), class \(g\) consists of continuous mass \(p_g\), split between the two preference types in shares \((1+\theta_g)/2\) and \((1-\theta_g)/2\). Thus the voter-type masses are explicit, while the paper’s uncertainty over realized margins is retained.

The requested output is coefficients \(\lambda_g\in[0,1]\), not all zero, defining rules \(\phi_g(\theta_g)=\lambda_g\theta_g\). They induce decision \(d_\lambda(\theta)=\operatorname{sgn}\sum_g W_g\lambda_g\theta_g\), with fair tie-breaking. The output must satisfy
\[ \mathbb{E}[\Theta_g d_\lambda(\Theta)] \ge \mathbb{E}[\Theta_g d_{\mathrm{WTA}}(\Theta)] \quad\text{for every }g, \]
with strict inequality for at least one class, where \(d_{\mathrm{WTA}}(\theta)=\operatorname{sgn}\sum_g W_g\operatorname{sgn}(\theta_g)\). The question is whether such a profile exists and, if so, to produce one. This preserves the paper’s binary decision, weighted groups, rule choice, and group welfare objective. It asks for an explicit Pareto improvement in a society whose members are represented by masses; it does not replace population continuity with continuous outcomes or lotteries. Lemma 1’s characterization gives a reason to search among generalized proportional rules.

I would leave its complexity classification open. If it proves hard, my initial guess is that the hard part will be the number of constituency types and the geometry of the weighted decision boundaries, so discrete hardness may transfer. The further question is whether that search admits an efficient algorithm when the support size is moderate.

The soft spot is substantial: this is a computational extension of the paper’s welfare theorem, not a computational result the authors establish. The paper already takes limits in group size and studies asymptotically many groups, but neither fact supplies the missing complexity anchor. So the positive case is that the model gives a natural, concrete high-multiplicity welfare-search problem; it does not show that this paper itself has a qualifying computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own first paragraph removes the paper’s possible anchors: it contains no numbered computational result. Theorem 1 is about dominance and Pareto inefficiency; Theorem 2 is about asymptotic welfare; Lemma 1 characterizes efficient outcomes. None states a complexity claim for an algorithmic problem. Under the requested standard, there is no computational result here to mirror.

The proposed High-Multiplicity Pareto-Improving Rule Search does not supply one. For its promised inputs, the existence question is already settled by Theorem 1 and Lemma 1: when no group is a dictator, a Pareto-improving generalized proportional profile exists. Finding explicit coefficients could be an algorithmic follow-on, but its computational difficulty would concern the number of strategic groups and integrating over their joint uncertainty. The voter-mass parameters \(p_g\) do not enter the decision rule or the stated payoff constraints. Removing them leaves the search unchanged, so they do not make this a high-multiplicity computation.

There is also a deeper fit problem. The paper’s strategically relevant players are groups, each of which chooses a rule. Its group margins are already continuous fractions: Assumption 1 gives them absolutely continuous distributions over \([-1,1]\), and Remark 1 explicitly motivates them as limits of large groups of members. Describing the members instead as masses of the two preference types would reproduce that model. The paper already studies the population-continuous object at that level.

Continuizing the groups themselves changes the strategic problem. If each group becomes an infinitesimal member of a diffuse population of groups, a single group’s rule has zero influence on the decision, so the paper’s unilateral incentives disappear. Keeping groups with positive influence keeps the finite strategic actors that the paper already has. The asymptotic analysis as the number of groups grows does not create a new computational mirror: it studies limiting payoffs analytically.

A search for a constructive Pareto-improvement certificate might still be worthwhile as a separate algorithmic project. Its strongest version would ask for coefficients or a weighted-majority rule achieving specified welfare guarantees, with an explicit representation of the joint distribution. That would be a new computation over this game’s uncertainty and strategic groups; the paper gives no discrete-to-continuous computational problem whose high-multiplicity relaxation it would be. The negative case is strongest on this task-fit point, rather than as a claim that no interesting algorithm could ever be built from the model.
