The continuous mirror question

Given recipient types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), priorities \(\rho_t\), per-capita envy limits \(\Lambda_t\), and sample access to an item-valuation distribution \(\alpha\) on \([0,\bar{x}]^{|T|}\), compute an \(\epsilon\)-optimal welfare-maximizing allocation policy \(q_t(x)\) subject to the type quotas and per-capita envy constraints; characterize the algorithm's dependence on \(|T|\), \(\epsilon\), and the input representation.

The model it lives in

Each item has valuation vector \(x\) across types, and \(q_t(x)\) is the probability it is assigned to type \(t\). Require \(\int q_t\,d\alpha=\beta_t\), where \(\beta_t=\mu_t\rho_t/\sum_s\mu_s\rho_s\), and maximize \(\int\sum_t x_tq_t(x)\,d\alpha(x)\). With \(A_{ts}=\int x_tq_s(x)\,d\alpha(x)\), type \(t\)'s per-capita envy constraint is μ_t^{-1}\max_s\{(\rho_t/\rho_s)A_{ts}/\mu_s-A_{tt}/\mu_t\}\le\Lambda_t\).

The objection that survived

Exact equality of recipient profiles may leave few useful type classes in realistic donation markets, and the paper's sample-based convergence does not establish bit-complexity for arbitrary representations of α.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the offline envy-constrained allocation structure of Theorem 1 and the paper's sample-based approximation analysis; it leaves incentive compatibility, finite-population rounding, and ex-post fairness unanswered.

Open questions for a prover

  • What running-time and approximation bounds hold when α is given by an explicit representation rather than sample access?
  • Can the ex-ante type quotas and envy guarantees be rounded to assignments for a finite recipient population while preserving useful ex-post guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a recipient-side high-multiplicity mirror. The paper already represents its item pool by a distribution \(\alpha\); in the blood-donation story, that pool consists of users or donor opportunities. I would not count that item-side distribution as a new continuization. Instead, I would continuize the recipients.

Call the proposed problem Type-EnvyOT\(_\infty\). A population has a finite set \(T\) of recipient types, with mass \(\mu_t>0\) for each type and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type is complete for this problem: its members share the same valuation of every item, target priority \(\rho_t>0\), and envy budget. An item is described by its type-valuation vector \(x\in[0,\bar x]^\tau\), where \(\tau=|T|\), drawn from distribution \(\alpha\). The type’s target share is
\[ \beta_t=\frac{\mu_t\rho_t}{\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s\rho_s}. \]
A solution is a measurable allocation policy \(q_t(x)\ge 0\), with \(\sum_t q_t(x)=1\), assigning each item to a recipient type, and satisfying \(\int q_t\,d\alpha=\beta_t\). The planner maximizes
\[ W(q)=\int\sum_{t\in T}x_tq_t(x)\,d\alpha(x). \]
For per-capita fairness, require for every type \(t\)
\[ \max_{s\in T}\left\{ \frac{\rho_t}{\rho_s}\frac{1}{\mu_s}\int x_tq_s(x)\,d\alpha(x) -\frac{1}{\mu_t}\int x_tq_t(x)\,d\alpha(x) \right\}\le \Lambda_t. \]
Here \(\Lambda_t\) is the envy limit in the type-average, per-capita normalization. In a finite population of \(N\) recipients, type \(t\) has about \(N\mu_t\) members; items assigned to that type are distributed symmetrically among them. Thus the mass policy permits fractional type averages while the individual recipients still receive items. A plausible regime is a large donation platform with many branch-level recipient organizations and recurring operational profiles: \(N\) could be in the thousands while the number \(\tau\) of distinct complete profiles is much smaller. The precise scenario is an extension of the paper’s small-recipient experiments, not one they demonstrate.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Section 4 and Appendix A.1. It says that when the item measure \(\alpha\) is continuous and the recipient target measure \(\beta\) is discrete, the envy-constrained transport optimum \(L(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)\) equals the finite-dimensional dual maximum \(\max_{g,\gamma\ge0}E(g,\gamma)\), and an optimal allocation is the partition into generalized Laguerre cells \(L_{y_i}(g^*,\gamma^*)\). In the mirror, the underlying question is: given the type population, item distribution, priorities, and envy limits, find a welfare-maximizing feasible allocation policy. For finite \(T\), the theorem applies with \(n=\tau\): dual prices determine which recipient type receives each item. The expected classification is tractable (Class A) in an oracle or sample-based model, with polynomial dependence on \(\tau\) and approximation precision under suitable boundedness assumptions. The theorem itself is a structural result, not a bit-complexity classification; Section 5’s projected stochastic optimization is the computational route that makes it relevant.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here, in Section 6 and Appendix A.2. Under Assumption 1, which bounds the coefficients of the optimal dual solution, it states that with probability \(1-\delta\), the empirical maximizer from \(m\) independent samples has population dual-objective gap
\[ O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{(\log m)^3+\log(1/\delta)}{m}}\right). \]
The appendix’s bound includes a dependence on \(n\) (through \(n\log n\)), and the paper summarizes the resulting sample requirement as \(\tilde O(n/\epsilon^2)\). The corresponding Sample-Based Type-EnvyOT\(_\infty\) question supplies the type data and an i.i.d. sampler for \(\alpha\), and asks for dual prices \((\hat g,\hat\gamma)\) whose population objective is within \(\epsilon\) of optimum. Its expected classification is again tractable in the learning sense, with sample complexity roughly \(\tilde O(\tau/\epsilon^2)\), independent of headcount \(N\). This is a guarantee about dual-objective quality; it does not by itself guarantee a close policy in a policy-distance metric or ex-post fairness for a finite population.

Theorem 1 is the stronger anchor because it supplies the allocation structure itself; Theorem 2 adds a sample-access guarantee. Neither is a hardness result, and the paper gives no named NP-hardness or other hardness anchor to test whether recipient-side continuization removes hardness. The further questions are whether a finite population can be rounded while preserving quotas and envy, how dependence on small type masses and priority ratios affects the bounds, and how much of the result survives when recipients within a nominal type have meaningful differences.

The weak point is the regime. The paper’s experimental example has only five recipients, and exact type equality requires recipients to share their full valuation profile, priority, and envy parameters. Many real centers may be too heterogeneous to group without losing relevant distinctions. That limits the mirror’s reach, but it does not make the question incoherent: standardized branches or programs can share complete profiles, and the paper’s allocation problem remains recognizable when each such class is treated as a mass of recipients. The mirror covers the paper’s offline envy–welfare allocation and its sample-based approximation result; it does not cover its incentive-compatibility question, which the paper leaves for future work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed recipient-side mirror is coherent, but its two anchors reduce exactly to the paper’s existing optimization problem.

Let \(K=\sum_t\mu_t\rho_t\), and write \(A_{ts}=\int x_tq_s(x)\,d\alpha(x)\). Set the paper’s recipient share to \(p_t^*=\beta_t=\mu_t\rho_t/K\). Then the mirror’s envy expression, multiplied by \(\mu_t\), is

\[ \max_s\left\{\frac{\mu_t\rho_t}{\mu_s\rho_s}A_{ts}-A_{tt}\right\} = \max_s\left\{\frac{\beta_t}{\beta_s}A_{ts}-A_{tt}\right\}. \]

That is precisely the paper’s envy expression for recipient \(t\). Its bound \(\Lambda_t\) maps to the paper’s \(\lambda_t=\mu_t\Lambda_t\). The welfare objective and quota constraints also coincide. Thus Theorem 1, the generalized Laguerre-cell structure, and the paper’s optimization method apply directly with \(n=\tau\). In every exchangeable type regime where headcount \(N\) is much larger than the number of complete profiles, \(N\) disappears from the computation. The mirror is a sensible population interpretation, but it adds no new structural or complexity question for ChoCo to chart.

Theorem 2 reduces in the same way. Its samples are draws from the item-valuation distribution \(\alpha\); replicating recipients within a type creates no new sample requirement. Substituting \(n=\tau\) gives the corresponding type-level sample bound—the appendix makes the \(n\)-dependence explicit. This is not a new result about learning a continuous recipient population. It is the paper’s existing sample guarantee applied to the same allocation problem under a type interpretation.

A more ambitious version could study finite-population rounding, ex-post guarantees, or meaningful differences between recipients in the same nominal type. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they are not what the two proposed anchors establish. The first requires additional finite-population structure; the second breaks the exact type aggregation that makes the mirror equivalent to the paper’s model. If recipients remain identical for all features the problem uses, aggregation gives the reduction above. If they differ in those features, the types must distinguish them, potentially eliminating the headcount advantage.

So the strongest case against is redundancy, not incoherence: for the best exact high-multiplicity mirror, the paper already supplies the relevant machinery and guarantees. I cannot honestly claim that no useful applied version exists—large platforms may have many exchangeable recipient organizations—but on the programme’s computational-landscape criterion, this mirror appears to be a relabeling of a solved problem, not a new mirror to prioritize.
