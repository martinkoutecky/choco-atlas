What the mirror covers

The proposed type-support formulation carries over the proportionality bounds of Corollaries 4.3 and 5.1 with support sizes in place of group sizes, but neither bound addresses computation; the discrepancy, envy-freeness, and consensus-division results are not covered.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result establishing a complexity classification such as NP-hardness, membership in P, or fixed-parameter tractability. It proves named quantitative bounds about discrepancy and fair-allocation guarantees. Those are meaningful computational results, but they do not meet the anchor requirement you set, so I cannot make a positive case with a qualifying anchor.

The strongest plausible mirror is group proportionality up to \(c\) goods. Let group \(i\) have a distribution \(\mu_i\) over additive utility types \(t\), where a type specifies an agent’s utility vector \(u_t\) over the goods. Given the goods and these distributions, choose a partition \((A_1,\ldots,A_k)\) minimizing the integer \(c\), subject to this condition: for every type \(t\) with \(\mu_i(t)>0\), there is a set \(B_{i,t}\subseteq G\setminus A_i\), with \(|B_{i,t}|\le c\), such that
\[ u_t(A_i)\ge \frac{u_t(G)}{k}-u_t(B_{i,t}). \]
The output is the allocation, the value \(c\), and witnesses \(B_{i,t}\). A plausible regime is large groups—say, many members of each organization or consumer segment—with many agents sharing each utility type, so the number of supported types \(\tau_i\) is much smaller than the headcount.

The closest numbered anchors are Corollary 4.3 and Theorem 5.1, both proved in this paper, but neither is a complexity anchor. Corollary 4.3 says that for \(n_1\ge\cdots\ge n_k\ge1\), the worst-case proportionality guarantee satisfies
\[ c_{\mathrm{PROP}}(n_1,\ldots,n_k) \ge \Omega\!\left(\max_{i^*\in[k]}\frac{i^*}{k}\sqrt{n_{i^*}}\right). \]
Theorem 5.1 says that every such instance has a proportional allocation with
\[ c_{\mathrm{PROP}}(n_1,\ldots,n_k)\le O(\sqrt{n_1}). \]
The first is the more useful lead for the programme: it is a lower-bound result, though a lower bound on fairness quality rather than computational hardness.

There is a faithful high-multiplicity reading of both bounds. Because the requirement applies to every positive-mass type, duplicating a type changes nothing: only the support matters. Thus the mirror’s worst-case guarantee for support sizes \(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_k\) is exactly the finite-agent guarantee with \(n_i=\tau_i\). In that reading, Corollary 4.3 gives the same lower bound with \(\tau_i\) in place of \(n_i\), and Theorem 5.1 gives the corresponding upper bound. For balanced support sizes, these bounds match at order \(\sqrt{\tau_1}\).

The exact computational question for either result is: given finite goods and rationally described distributions over utility types, find a partition minimizing \(c\) under the condition above; what is its complexity in \(k\), \(|G|\), the support sizes, and input bit length? The paper does not answer that question. My expectation would be that any hardness of the finite type-support problem transfers, since a finite instance embeds by assigning positive rational mass to each type. The paper provides no hardness result to establish that premise.

That is also the mirror’s weakest point. The masses \(\mu_i(t)\) disappear from the fairness constraints, and the paper’s lower-bound constructions use many distinct utility vectors. So this is a sensible high-multiplicity formulation of *their* proportionality problem, but it does not reveal a population-mass effect or a new tractability gain. The honest positive case is that the problem has a clean type-distribution mirror and its quantitative guarantees carry over exactly; it is not a paper-backed continuous complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper proves no complexity classification for a computational problem. Its results bound worst-case discrepancy and the fairness parameter \(c\); they do not classify the complexity of finding a coloring or allocation. The proposed search for a minimum-\(c\) allocation is a reasonable problem to study, but it is a new algorithmic question, not a continuous mirror of a complexity result in the paper.

The proportionality bounds are the strongest candidate, and the proponent’s type-based model is coherent. But with the paper’s requirement that every agent satisfy the guarantee, a type’s mass has no effect: identical utility types impose identical constraints. Any two distributions with the same support produce the same feasible allocations and optimum \(c\), regardless of their rational weights. Replacing group sizes \(n_i\) by support sizes \(\tau_i\) therefore amounts to removing duplicate constraints from a finite instance. It captures how many distinct utility vectors there are, not how much population mass each type has. This is not an objection to high-multiplicity work generally; it is a feature of this particular fairness condition.

The alternatives do not rescue the mirror. Requiring the guarantee for almost every member of a diffuse population can let exceptional agents’ complaints disappear; requiring it for every type in the support restores the mass-insensitive formulation. A weighted or \(1-\epsilon\) population guarantee would make the masses matter, but it changes the paper’s individual fairness requirement, so Corollary 4.3 and Corollary 5.1 (the upper bound the proponent calls Theorem 5.1) would no longer be the results being mirrored.

The discrepancy lower bound offers no better route. If columns are treated as an atomless population of agents and coloring is assigned individually, each type’s mass can be split equally among the \(k\) colors, balancing every row exactly. Keeping whole types indivisible preserves a nontrivial weighted coloring problem, but then the paper supplies no complexity result for it; treating columns as a continuously divisible population also shifts continuity to the objects being colored.

So the negative case is strongest against calling this paper a worthwhile *continuization* target: its main results are not computational classifications, and the most faithful population model erases the masses. I cannot rule out a useful study of algorithms for fair allocation on compressed utility supports, or of a new population-weighted fairness notion. Either could be worthwhile on its own, but neither is established by the paper’s results as a continuous mirror.
