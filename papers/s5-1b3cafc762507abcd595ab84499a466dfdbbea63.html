The continuous mirror question

Given three candidates \(p,a,b\), binary-rational masses of fixed-vote types \((r,w)\) and manipulator types \(w\), with total mass \(1\), ask whether the manipulators’ mass can be assigned among the \(15\) nonempty top-truncated ballots so that \(p\) is a co-winner under Borda-down. Let \(x_{w,r}\geq 0\) be the mass of weight-\(w\) manipulators assigned ballot \(r\), constrained by \(\sum_r x_{w,r}=\nu_w\). A complete ballot scores \((2,1,0)\) by rank, a two-candidate ballot scores its listed candidates \(2,1\) and the unlisted candidate \(0\), and a singleton ballot scores its listed candidate \(1\) and the others \(0\). Compute whether an assignment makes each resulting weighted score of \(p\) at least the corresponding score of \(a\) and \(b\).

The model it lives in

Types record role and weight \(w\), plus the fixed ballot \(r\) for nonmanipulators; each type has mass. Decision variables \(x_{w,r}\) split each manipulator type’s mass among allowed reports, and the objective is feasibility of the two linear winner inequalities.

The objection that survived

The cited work already gives a polynomial-time algorithm for divisible-vote manipulation under positional scoring rules, which answers the mirror’s computation; this is a prior answer, not a reason the question is unworthy.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the Borda instance of Theorem 4; it leaves the other scoring schemes, elimination rules, destructive manipulation, and uncertainty results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a finite group of \(N_w\) manipulators of each weight \(w\), what additive winner-margin guarantee follows when a feasible mass allocation is rounded to integral reports?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous 3-Candidate Borda-Down Constructive Manipulation, anchored on Theorem 4, which the paper proves here. The theorem says that for every three-candidate positional scoring rule \(X\) not isomorphic to plurality or veto, deciding whether a weighted coalition can make its preferred candidate a winner using top-truncated ballots under \(X^\downarrow\) is NP-complete. The paper’s default is the non-unique-winner model. Borda, with scoring vector \((2,1,0)\), is one instance covered by the theorem.

In the continuous version, the candidates are \(p,a,b\). A society consists of fixed-vote agents and manipulators, with each agent’s type recording their role and voting weight \(w\); a fixed-vote type also records its top-truncated ballot \(r\). The mass \(\mu_{r,w}\) is the fraction of the population in fixed-vote type \((r,w)\), and \(\nu_w\) is the fraction of manipulators of weight \(w\). These are binary rationals summing to one. For any fixed finite set of allowed weight classes \(W\), there are at most \(15|W|\) fixed-vote types and \(|W|\) manipulator types, since three candidates have 15 nonempty top-truncated orders.

The decision variables are \(x_{w,r}\geq 0\): the fraction of the population in manipulator weight class \(w\) assigned to report \(r\). They must satisfy \(\sum_r x_{w,r}=\nu_w\) for every \(w\). Use exactly the paper’s Borda-\(\downarrow\) scores: a complete ballot gives scores \(2,1,0\); a ballot listing two candidates gives them \(2,1\) and gives the unlisted candidate \(0\); a singleton ballot gives its listed candidate \(1\) and the others \(0\). The resulting score is \(S(c)=\sum_{r,w}\mu_{r,w}w\,s^\downarrow_r(c)+\sum_{w,r}x_{w,r}w\,s^\downarrow_r(c)\). The question is whether there is an allocation \(x\) such that \(S(p)\geq S(a)\) and \(S(p)\geq S(b)\). A solution is such an allocation; the objective is the source problem’s yes/no feasibility goal.

This is an exact, finite-dimensional LP: each weight class has 15 report choices, and the winner constraints are linear. It is solvable in polynomial time in the support size and the input bit length. The answer I expect for this mirror is therefore tractable (Class A). A concrete regime is a large three-candidate election with a coalition drawn from many agents who share a weight and the same target, and with nonmanipulator reports drawn from a small set of ballot types. Then the number of agents can be much larger than the number of occupied types. Each infinitesimal agent still submits an integral top-truncated ballot; it is the mass of a homogeneous cohort that can be divided among reports.

The mirror preserves the paper’s manipulation question, ballot domain, voting rule, and winner condition. It replaces indivisible voter counts within each type by mass allocation, the population change at the heart of continuization. Its scope is deliberately narrow: it covers Theorem 4’s constructive-manipulation result for three-candidate scoring rules under round-down, not the paper’s other scoring schemes, elimination rules, destructive manipulation, or uncertainty results.

The main weakness is also clear: Theorem 4’s hardness is for weighted manipulation, and its reductions use binary-encoded weights. In the continuous model, members of a large same-weight cohort can split across ballots; an individual weighted voter in the paper cannot. So this does not claim that the theorem’s hard instances become easy while preserving every indivisible weighted voter. It asks the corresponding population question in a regime with many agents per type. The useful next question is how well the LP’s solution rounds back to finite elections: can one give additive guarantees as the coalition grows, and identify when growing the number of candidates turns this small LP into a pricing problem?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed large-cohort story does not line up with where Theorem 4 gets its hardness. Its reductions use the \(k_i\) values from Partition as manipulator weights. With a fixed set of weight classes \(W\), as in the proposed high-multiplicity scenario, that item-by-item structure disappears. If \(W\) grows to include the reduction’s distinct weights, the types may be singletons, so splitting their mass is a fractional relaxation of individual manipulators rather than a limit supported by repeated agents of each type. That weakens the connection to Theorem 4, though it does not invalidate a different, homogeneous-weight instance regime.

There is also a substantial priority concern: divisible-vote manipulation already allows manipulators to use convex combinations of complete rankings, and gives a polynomial-time algorithm for positional scoring rules. The proposed Borda-down ballots fit that model at the level of winner feasibility: a singleton \((p)\), scored \((1,0,0)\), can be replaced by a mixture of complete rankings scoring \((5/3,2/3,2/3)\), which differs only by a common score offset. The existing algorithm therefore covers the same continuous computation. [Xia, Conitzer, and Procaccia, “A Scheduling Approach to Coalitional Manipulation”](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf)

But the programme’s prior-art rule matters: an answer already available elsewhere does not by itself make a well-posed mirror unworthy. And the mirror is well-posed: weight and coalition status suffice for these computations, and a large cohort with common weight is plausible. So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The negative case is weak; its best point is that the stated cohort regime does not capture the theorem’s hardness construction, not that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
