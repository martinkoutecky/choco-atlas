What the mirror covers

The proposed assignment mirror covers none of the paper’s named results; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize TTC and bipolar serial dictatorship, while the other menu and welfare results are also non-computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive setting is a large housing exchange: many residents and one unit per resident, with many residents sharing the same preference ranking and initial endowment type. Write their shares as a rational distribution \(\mu\). In a type-level assignment, \(x_{t,o}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving object \(o\); its row sums are \(\mu_t\), and its column sums are the per-capita supplies. Fractional type averages are natural even though each resident receives an integral object.

To preserve the paper’s notion of freedom, I would add a tagged resident to this atomless background and ask which objects they can obtain as their reported ranking varies. For power over another resident, tag a second resident and record the objects they can receive under those same report changes. A natural follow-up would ask whether the tagged resident’s menu can be computed from the rational type distribution, or whether the full-menu and bounded-bilateral-power characterizations survive in this limit.

That is a plausible continuous-population research question, but it is not an anchor permitted by your brief. The paper contains no named computational result: its results characterize mechanisms and relate menus to welfare; none gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic formulation that the authors compute with. In particular, Theorem 2, proved here using the trading-cycle representation characterized by Pycia and Ünver, says that for assignment rules with at least four agents that are efficient and group-strategy-proof, being TTC is equivalent both to every agent having some opposing profile where their menu contains all objects, and to every ordered pair having some opposing profile where the first agent’s menu for the second contains all objects. Theorem 3, proved in the appendix, says that among efficient, group-strategy-proof assignment rules, all bilateral menus having size at most two at every profile is equivalent to being a bipolar serial dictatorship. Both are characterization theorems, not computational results under the stated anchor rule.

The weakness in the proposed mirror is substantial: a zero-mass tagged resident’s report may have no effect in an ordinary continuum mechanism, while giving them a special role requires an extension of TTC that the paper does not define. Replacing the individual menu with a cohort’s average allocation would change the object being measured. So the assignment setting offers a credible direction for a new continuous follow-up, but I cannot honestly present a precise problem as the continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against has one strong anchor: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 characterizes TTC through full-menu properties, and Theorem 3 characterizes bipolar serial dictatorship through bounded bilateral menus. Both are structural equivalences about mechanisms. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational problem with a specified input representation. Turning either into “compute a resident’s menu from a rational type distribution” would define a new problem; the paper does not analyze that problem.

The large housing-exchange version is the best rescue, especially with a tagged resident whose report varies against a fixed mass distribution of everyone else. But the aggregate allocation variables \(x_{t,o}\) record how much mass receives each object. They do not specify the tagged resident’s attainable menu or the outcomes they can induce for another tagged resident—the objects Theorems 2 and 3 characterize. Keeping those residents as atoms preserves the menus, but makes the question depend on exceptional individuals; giving a cohort positive mass instead studies collective influence. If the number of object types also grows, the paper supplies no canonical continuous counterpart to “the full menu” or “menu size at most two.” Each choice could support a useful follow-up, but none is forced by the results here.

So no proposed anchor survives as a continuous mirror of a computational result. The universal claim that no scenario could make such a follow-up worthwhile is harder to defend: a tagged resident facing a continuous housing market may be a sensible new research question.
