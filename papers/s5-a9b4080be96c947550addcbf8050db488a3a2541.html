The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Proportional Robust Stable Marriage, a continuous-population version of the paper’s existence problem for supermatches.

The anchor is Corollary 1, proved here from the preceding restricted-family reduction. It states that both \(\mathrm{P}_1\)—given \(a,b\) and a Stable Marriage instance, decide whether it has an \((a,b)\)-supermatch—and \(\mathrm{P}_2\), the special case \(a=b=1\), are NP-complete. Here a supermatch is a stable matching such that, after any choice of \(a\) non-fixed pairs is broken, some stable matching avoids those pairs while changing the partners of at most \(b\) additional men. The paper’s reduction to this result encodes satisfiability in the rotation-poset structure; Corollary 1 is not merely reporting hardness from earlier work.

For the continuous mirror, take two atomless populations of equal total mass, men and women, each divided into finitely many preference types. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), summing to one on each side, and each type’s ranking of acceptable types on the other side. A large hiring or placement market is one plausible regime: many applicants and positions, but relatively few recurring applicant profiles and employer-role profiles with shared rankings. Mass is the fraction of the relevant population. A matching is a flow \(\pi_{ij}\) of mass from male type \(i\) to female type \(j\), with unmatched mass allowed. Each infinitesimal agent still gets one partner; the flow records how a cohort is distributed across partner types.

Here is the precise decision problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Proportional Robust Stable Marriage. Its input is the type lists, rational masses, and rational disruption and repair limits \(\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]\). A matching flow \(\pi\) is stable when there is no acceptable type pair \((i,j)\) for which both a positive mass of type \(i\) prefers \(j\) to its current assignment and a positive mass of type \(j\) prefers \(i\) to its current assignment. Among the stable flows, let \(\ell_{ij}\) be the minimum possible flow on \((i,j)\); thus \(\pi_{ij}-\ell_{ij}\) is the portion of that relation not forced across all stable matchings.

The question is whether there is a stable flow \(\pi\) such that for every disruption \(r\) with \(0\le r_{ij}\le \pi_{ij}-\ell_{ij}\) and \(\sum_{ij}r_{ij}=\alpha\), there is a new stable flow \(\pi'\) that repairs it while changing at most \(\beta\) additional mass of men’s partner types. Formally, the repair can be represented by transition masses \(\eta_{ijj'}\): mass of male type \(i\) moving from old partner type \(j\) to new partner type \(j'\), with an unmatched partner represented by \(\bot\). Their row and column sums must agree with \(\pi\) and \(\pi'\); at least \(r_{ij}\) mass must move from \(j\) to a different partner type; and
\[ \sum_i\sum_{j\ne j'}\eta_{ijj'}-\alpha\le\beta. \]
The output is the initial flow \(\pi\) witnessing existence. The problem is a decision question, as in the paper, rather than a minimization problem.

This retains the central object: choose a stable matching robust to arbitrary breakups, with bounded repair. The thresholds are per-capita versions of \(a\) and \(b\): in an \(N\)-agent discretization, \(a\) and \(b\) correspond to \(\alpha=a/N\) and \(\beta=b/N\). Fixed mass is excluded from disruption just as fixed pairs are excluded in the paper. Treating agents within a type as interchangeable, and measuring changes by partner type, is the needed population-level co-modification. Fractional flows describe cohort assignments; they are not lotteries that give an individual a fractional partner.

I would tentatively investigate this as Class C, continuum-specific hardness. Corollary 1’s reduction suggests that the rotation and preference structure carries substantial combinatorics, and the continuous problem still has an adversarial disruption followed by a stable-repair requirement. But that is a conjectural direction, not a hardness transfer: fractional flows could make the robust condition tractable. The main follow-up questions are whether stability and repair admit a separation or LP formulation, and whether the discrete reduction survives fractional splitting or instead dissolves in the continuous model.

The weak point is the type model’s treatment of preferences. The paper ranks named individuals in strict lists; this mirror ranks counterpart types, making members of a type interchangeable. That is plausible for cohort-based markets, but it is a substantive modeling choice. If named identities or within-cohort rankings are essential to the authors’ problem, the mirror becomes less convincing. The case rests on their accepting proportional disruption and repair as the natural high-multiplicity form of the same supermatch question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is Corollary 1: the paper states a genuine NP-completeness result for deciding whether an \((a,b)\)-supermatch exists. So the case against cannot rest on a lack of a computational result to mirror. The problem is what that result measures. A supermatch must avoid the particular named pairs that broke up, and \(d(M,M')\) counts men whose actual partners differ.

The proposed flow model forgets those identities. Its \(\ell_{ij}\) measures how much flow between two types is forced across stable flows; that is not the same as a fixed named pair. And its repair cost counts changes of partner *type*, not changes of partner. If a man is paired with a different woman of the same type, the original distance counts a change, while \(\eta_{ijj'}\) does not. Conversely, requiring disrupted mass to move to a different type forbids re-forming the same type relation with different people, which the paper’s prohibition on repeating a named pair does not forbid.

That gap is especially consequential in an atomless cohort model. Suppose preferences depend only on type, and a disruption selects mass \(\alpha\) of matched pairs. Within each type-pair cell, the disrupted men and their former partners have equal mass. They can be rematched to one another through a measure-preserving reassignment that avoids every disrupted individual pair while leaving all type flows unchanged. Since preferences and stability depend only on types, the new matching remains stable; exactly the disrupted men change partners, so no additional mass needs to move. The resulting identity-faithful proportional version is therefore \((\alpha,0)\)-robust whenever such a disruption is possible. The proponent’s flow condition avoids this degeneracy by treating a type relation as the broken pair, but that changes the paper’s failure event.

There is a route to retaining strict preferences over named individuals: give the continuous model identity-level preference data and track matching maps, with distance equal to the mass of men assigned a different individual. But then finite type rankings no longer specify the relevant preferences and repair constraints. One needs identity-level rankings or a new succinct model of rankings over a continuum, rather than a distribution over a fixed finite set of types. That is a substantial change to the input and problem, not a high-multiplicity relaxation supplied by the paper’s model.

So the strongest negative case is that Corollary 1 has no convincing *direct* mirror under the programme’s finite-type population model: preserving exact-pair robustness makes type-based versions degenerate, while the proposed nondegenerate version changes what counts as a disrupted pair and a repair. The weak point is real: the authors might endorse type-pair relations as the unit of disruption. If they do, Proportional Robust Stable Marriage could be worthwhile as a new problem. The paper alone does not establish that they would recognize it as their problem.
