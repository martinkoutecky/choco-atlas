The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result. Its results characterize game maps and values; they do not classify the complexity of a problem or give an algorithm with a stated running time.

The closest foothold is Theorem 4.16, proved here. For a finite player set \(N\), game \(u\), and coalition \(S\), it identifies the value at \(N\) of the Poisson-equation solution \(u_S\) as
\[ u_S(N)=\frac{u(N)-u(N\setminus S)+u(S)}{2^n}=\chi'_u(S). \]
Theorem 4.17 identifies the corresponding \(k=1\) construction with the Shapley value, \(u_i^1(N)=\phi_i(u)\), connecting it to earlier work. These are mathematical identities, not claims that evaluating either underlying problem is in P, hard, or tractable under a specified input model. The paper also does not analyze the equation as a computational encoding with complexity guarantees.

A continuous-population extension is imaginable: treat coalitions as measurable subsets of a nonatomic population and extend \(u\) to those subsets. But the paper supplies neither that model nor a computational question about it. Its finite-player results range over all coalitions of \(N\), and specify no type-mass representation or high-multiplicity regime. Defining such a version would start a new research question, rather than mirror a named computational result here.

So there are no eligible anchors, and I cannot state a continuous problem per anchor. The strongest honest observation in favor is only that the coalitional formula may invite a continuous analogue; it does not support a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch with ChoCo’s computational scope: the paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 4.16 identifies the Poisson-equation solution’s value at \(N\) with \(\chi'_u(S)\); Theorem 4.17 identifies a related construction with the Shapley value. Those are mathematical identities, not claims about algorithms, running time, or hardness. The axiomatic characterization and the other propositions are structural results too. So neither theorem supplies a computational result for a continuous mirror to extend.

The best repair would be to model a coalition by its masses \(x\in[0,\mu]^T\) over finitely many types, give it a worth function \(U(x)\), and extend the map as
\[ \chi'_U(x)=\frac{U(\mu)-U(\mu-x)+U(x)}{2^{|T|}}. \]
That is a coherent population-continuous question. But it needs choices the paper does not make: how \(U\) is represented, what computation is required, and how running time is measured. With an oracle, a circuit, or a structured optimization model for \(U\), one gets different computational problems. Any of those might be worthwhile, but none is specified by the paper’s result.

The Hodge theorem does not close that gap. Its graph has one vertex for every coalition of a finite \(N\); extending it to a continuum of coalition masses requires a new graph or operator and a finite input representation. The Shapley identity is also a possible starting point for a nonatomic allocation theory, but defining a meaningful per-capita or type-level allocation would add another model absent from the paper.

So the negative case is strong on eligibility: none of the proposed anchors is a computational result to mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous computational paper. Structured, type-based cooperative games could support one; that would be a new ChoCo project inspired by this paper, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result it establishes.
