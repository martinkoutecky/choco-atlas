The continuous mirror question

Given worker types \(i\in B\) with rational masses \(p_i\) summing to \(1\), position types \(j\in C\) with rational masses \(q_j\) summing to \(1\), a strict order \(\succ_i\) over position types for each \(i\), and a relation \(R_j\subseteq B\times B\) for each \(j\), does there exist a nonnegative coupling \(x_{ij}\) with row sums \(p_i\) and column sums \(q_j\) such that for every \(i,j\), \(U_{ij}=0\) or \(V_{ij}=0\), where \(U_{ij}=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}\) and \(V_{ij}=\sum_{k:\,(k,i)\notin R_j}x_{kj}\)?

The model it lives in

A coupling between two atomless populations represented by finitely many worker and position types with rational masses. Each worker type ranks position types, each position type compares worker types by an arbitrary relation, and \(x_{ij}\) is the mass paired across types; a blocking pair exists exactly when both \(U_{ij}\) and \(V_{ij}\) are positive.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s core problem, SMG, not the more elaborate Stable Extension result. Its central question—whether a matching exists with no blocking pair—survives naturally when both sides of a large market consist of many agents in a small number of preference types.

A plausible regime is a large hiring market with repeated cohorts of workers and positions. A worker type is a complete ranking of position types; a position type is its complete comparison relation over worker types. The many agents might number in the tens of thousands, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is much smaller. Employers’ comparisons need not be transitive: they could reflect conflicting attributes of applicants, as in the paper’s motivation for general preferences. Each actual worker still gets one actual position. The masses and matching below describe the shares of each type and how those shares are paired.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: deciding whether an SMG instance has a stable matching is NP-complete. The instances have \(n\) men with complete strict rankings of the women, and \(n\) women whose preferences are arbitrary binary relations over the men; a matching is stable if it has no blocking pair under the paper’s definition. The authors prove the result by a polynomial-time reduction from the known NP-complete problem of deciding whether an SMTI instance with ties only on the women’s side has a perfect stable matching [18].

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: SMG-Mass Existence. An instance consists of finite sets \(B\) and \(C\) of worker and position types, positive rational masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, a strict total order \(\succ_i\) over \(C\) for each \(i\in B\), and a relation \(R_j\subseteq B\times B\) for each \(j\in C\). As in the paper, \((k,i)\in R_j\) means a type-\(j\) position regards a type-\(k\) worker as at least as good as a type-\(i\) worker.

A solution is a nonnegative mass assignment \(x_{ij}\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=p_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=q_j\). For every pair \(i,j\), define \(U_{ij}=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}\), the mass of type-\(i\) workers assigned to positions they rank below \(j\), and \(V_{ij}=\sum_{k:\,(k,i)\notin R_j}x_{kj}\), the mass of type-\(j\) positions assigned to worker types they do not regard as at least as good as \(i\). Call \(x\) stable exactly when, for every \(i,j\), \(U_{ij}=0\) or \(V_{ij}=0\). If both were positive, positive mass on each side could form blocking pairs; if either is zero, that type pair cannot block. The decision question is whether such an \(x\) exists. The input size is governed by the numbers of types, the preference and relation data, and the binary encoding length \(L\) of the masses.

This keeps the paper’s underlying problem intact: existence of a matching immune to blocking under one strict preference side and one general-relation side. It preserves the arbitrary relations rather than replacing them with scores or transitive rankings. The continuous change is that an assignment is a coupling of population masses, so a type’s agents can be divided among partner types.

My provisional expectation is Class C: continuum-specific hardness, with the combinatorics likely living in the support types and their relations. That is a target for investigation, not a consequence of Theorem 1. The main follow-up is whether the problem is NP-hard in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or whether the continuous splitting instead makes existence tractable. Other natural questions are whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and whether the paper’s asymmetric-preference tractability result extends to these mass assignments.

The weakest point is also the essential one: Theorem 1’s reduction does not establish hardness here. In the mass model, a type can split across positions, so an instance with no discrete stable matching might still have a stable mass assignment. Also, the paper ranks named individuals, whereas this mirror ranks partner types; that is the natural anonymous formulation for a high-multiplicity market, but it is a real co-modification to the model. This case covers Theorem 1 only; it does not claim continuous counterparts of the asymmetric-preference algorithm or the Stable Extension results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about marginal payoff. Theorem 1 is a clear computational anchor, and a large market with repeated worker and position cohorts is plausible. In the proposed mass model, a coupling can be realized by matching atomless members of each type. Its stability condition also captures blocking correctly: if both \(U_{ij}\) and \(V_{ij}\) are positive, there are positive masses on each side that can form blocking pairs. So the mirror is not defeated by lack of multiplicity, identity dependence, or a vanishing limit.

The type-level formulation does change the paper’s semantics. Men in SMG strictly rank named women, and women’s relations compare named men. Preserving that detail may require types to encode rankings and relations over individual counterparts; as the market grows, that can make the type description grow too. A finite type table instead treats same-type counterparts as interchangeable, introducing equivalence or ties that the paper’s strict rankings do not have. But the authors already study ties, and this is a plausible high-multiplicity extension. It is not a decisive objection.

There is a sharper challenge to the proponent’s expected Class C story. Fix, for every type pair \((i,j)\), one of the zero conditions that prevents blocking: either \(U_{ij}=0\) or \(V_{ij}=0\). Each choice deletes transport arcs; the original stable coupling remains feasible on the resulting support. After clearing rational masses to integer margins, the restricted transportation polytope is integral, so it has an integral feasible point. Thus a stable mass assignment can be converted to a stable matching among clones with the same type-level preferences. In particular, setting all type masses equal gives the paper’s SMG instance directly. This suggests a short hardness-transfer argument and rules out calling the difficulty continuum-specific.

That observation makes the proposed mirror less novel, but the programme explicitly treats exact high-multiplicity transfers as valid work: the classification and the discrete-to-continuous dictionary are deliverables. Related work already studied continuous weakly stable matching and transport variants, including support-preserving integrality ([continuous stable-matching and transport complexity](/nfs/home/koutecky/research/brain/knowledge/concepts/continuous-stable-matching-transport-complexity.md)). Those results narrow the novelty claim; they do not settle SMG with arbitrary relations or make its mass version a collision.

So the strongest case against is that the likely payoff is modest: the defensible mirror appears to be a typed clone version of an existing NP-complete problem, not a new continuous-optimization problem. That is a case for restrained priority and for correcting the expected Class C label. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. On the material here, I cannot honestly defeat Theorem 1’s best type-mass mirror; the universal negative is weak.
