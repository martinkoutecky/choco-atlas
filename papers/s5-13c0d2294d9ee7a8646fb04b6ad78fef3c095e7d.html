The continuous mirror question

Given a rational interval \(I\) with uniform unit mass, output a finite partition into interval jurisdictions \(J\). Each jurisdiction chooses its median project and charges each resident \(1/\mu(J)\). Can an algorithm produce a partition that no measurable coalition \(B\) of positive mass can block, where \(B\) uses the paper's mean-of-extreme-medians rule and almost every \(x\in B\) strictly lowers cost from \(|x-m(J_x)|+1/\mu(J_x)\) to \(|x-m(B)|+1/\mu(B)\)?

The model it lives in

A uniform distribution of residents over an interval; jurisdictions are measurable groups of mass, projects are selected by the median rule, and each resident pays \(1/\mu(J)\). The output is a finite interval partition that is core-stable against measurable positive-mass coalitions.

The objection that survived

The opponent notes that core deviations in a continuum must be defined as positive-mass coalitions; the proposed mass model does not specify that blocking rule, though it is a natural extension of the paper's core definition.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3.3's constructive core-stability result for equidistant societies. It leaves the paper's other existence, nonexistence, Nash-stability, and consecutiveness results aside.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its numbered propositions concern existence or nonexistence of stable partitions and their structure; none establishes NP-hardness, membership in P, fixed-parameter tractability, or another complexity result.

The closest positive foothold is Proposition 4.5, proved here: under the equal-share and consistent-median rules, every finite society has a Nash-stable partition. The authors prove this by minimizing a potential over the finite set of partitions. That is an existence proof, not an efficient algorithm or a complexity classification. Proposition 4.2, proved here, says that under the equal-share and arbitrary-median rules, a core-stable structure may fail to exist; it likewise does not establish computational hardness.

A plausible future mirror would describe residents by a distribution over ideal locations and jurisdictions by measurable groups of population mass, with the equal-share charge normalized as \(1/\mu(S)\). A setting with many residents but relatively few location cohorts could make that population model credible. But choosing and computing stable partitions for it would be a new computational question, not a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. I therefore can’t honestly supply the requested per-anchor problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent concedes: this paper has no computational result to mirror. Propositions 4.2 and 4.5 concern whether stable partitions exist; the other propositions concern stability and consecutiveness. None studies the complexity of finding a partition, gives an algorithm, or establishes a complexity bound. Proposition 4.5’s potential function proves existence by minimizing over the finite set of partitions. That is not a computational classification. Proposition 4.2 gives an example where the core is empty, not a hardness result.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity setting here: many residents can share a location type. One could represent them by a distribution \(\mu\) over locations, partition that mass into jurisdictions, choose medians, and—granting the programme’s permitted scaling of project costs—retain a meaningful equal-share charge such as \(1/\mu(J)\). But this makes a new equilibrium problem. In an atomless society, one person moving has zero effect on a jurisdiction’s mass or median, so Nash stability needs a Wardrop-style interpretation; core deviations become positive-mass coalitions. The paper’s finite potential argument and stability definitions do not settle either version. Proposition 4.2 could motivate asking whether its blocking cycle persists, but that remains a question about equilibrium existence, not computational complexity.

One could go further and define a search problem: given rational location masses, compute a stable mass partition under a specified deviation rule. That may be worthwhile ChoCo research. But the paper neither poses nor answers it; choices about split types, stability, and output representation would define the new problem. So the negative case is strong as a verdict on this paper’s fit for the programme. It is weaker as the universal claim that no nearby continuous computation could be worthwhile: the location-cohort model is plausible. What it fails to provide is an anchored computational mirror of its results.
