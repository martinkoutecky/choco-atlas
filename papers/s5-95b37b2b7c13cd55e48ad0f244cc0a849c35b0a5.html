What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror reaches Proposition 1 and Lemma 1, but neither has a surviving continuous-population version; the paper's \(O(2^n)\) remark about MessageComputing is not a named computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: for \(n\ge3\), if the designer uses the Maskin mechanism for a monotonic, no-veto social choice rule, messages pass through indirect channels, and condition \(\lambda\) holds, then in state \(\bar t\) the agents can sign the agreement ComplexMessage so that the Pareto-inefficient outcome \(F(\bar t)\) is not yielded in Nash equilibrium. The supporting Lemma 1, also proved here, says that under \(\lambda\), when the first \(n-l\) agents choose \((\theta,\phi)=(0,0)\) and agents \(n-l+1\) through \(n-1\) choose \((0,\pi/l)\), agent \(n\)’s best response is \((0,\pi/l)\).

Neither result is a computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Proposition 1 is about Nash implementation; Lemma 1 establishes a best response. The paper mentions that MessageComputing takes \(O(2^n)\) time and space, but that is a remark about its algorithm, not a named result classifying the complexity of a problem it solves. I therefore have no eligible computational anchor, and no honest way to state one continuous computational problem per anchor.

There is a plausible setting for a *different* question: a large online decision body whose members have preferences drawn from a small set of rankings over outcomes. One could describe it by a distribution \(\mu\) over preference types and ask whether an agreement among members can prevent an implementation mechanism from producing its prescribed outcome. That gives the population a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation. But it does not preserve this paper’s result: the Maskin mechanism’s rules distinguish exact finite-player cases such as unanimity, one dissenter, and the lowest-index agent; ComplexMessage takes inputs from each of the \(n\) agents; and the paper explicitly motivates its approach as suitable for small cases, giving examples below 20 agents. In a nonatomic population, a single agent’s deviation has zero mass, and extending those rules would require designing a new aggregate mechanism.

So the honest positive case is only a research lead: preference types could be aggregated in a large population, and implementation vulnerability could be studied there. The weakest point is decisive for this assignment: the resulting question would be a new continuum mechanism-design problem, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. I would not assign it a tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness prediction without first specifying that new mechanism.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 and Lemma 1 are the paper’s clearest anchors, but neither states a computational result. Proposition 1 gives a strategic possibility under conditions \(\lambda\); Lemma 1 verifies a best response in the finite-player game. The \(\mathcal{O}(2^n)\) remark concerns the implementation subroutine, which computes a distribution over message profiles. It does not classify the complexity of a social-choice problem. So the paper supplies no named computational problem for a continuous mirror to answer.

The best version of the proposed mirror would ask whether a mass-based agreement can prevent a mechanism from producing its prescribed outcome, given a distribution \(\mu\) over preference types. But \(\mu\) leaves out information this result uses: the agents’ individual channels, the possibility that exactly one agent deviates, the lowest-index tie-break, and the distinguished \(n\)-th agent whose payoffs enter \(\lambda_4\) and \(\lambda_5\). Put those roles into the types and nearly every agent may need a separate type, so the high-multiplicity representation disappears. Merge agents by preference type and the mechanism’s strategic distinctions disappear instead.

The deeper problem is that the result turns on a unilateral deviation. In a society described only by nonatomic masses, one person’s deviation leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. If the mechanism depends only on aggregate messages, that deviation cannot trigger the agreement’s all-agents response or change the outcome. If it does respond to a single person’s action, the population measure alone is not its input. Scaling up a finite population does not resolve this: one person’s mass tends to zero, while the mechanism’s exact one-deviator conditions remain central.

One could replace unilateral Nash deviations with positive-mass coalition deviations and design an aggregate monitoring rule. That could be a worthwhile new mechanism-design problem. But it changes the strategic question that Proposition 1 and Lemma 1 answer, and the paper provides no computational result for that new problem to mirror. The negative case is not an impossibility proof; it is a case that this paper offers little foundation for a ChoCo mirror. The subroutine’s running time is the one possible computational lead, but studying its complexity from aggregate preference data would likewise require a new input problem, rather than continuizing a result in the paper.
