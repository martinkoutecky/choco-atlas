The case FOR (proponent)

I find no named computational-complexity result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. It contains named theorems, but none classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar, and none analyzes running time on encoded instances.

The closest results are Theorems 5 and 6, both proved in the paper. Theorem 5 says that, with at least two sectors, rich marginal preference domains, and Property \(\alpha\), the sector-wise core solution for sector coalitional game environments is Nash implementable in PE. Theorem 6 gives the corresponding result for the sector-wise VCG solution in VCG game environments. Theorem 4 supplies a sufficient characterization under decomposability, indistinguishability, sector-wise Maskin monotonicity, unanimity, weak no veto-power, Property \(\alpha\), and rich marginal domains, for \(n\ge3\). These are results about whether mechanisms exist and what they implement—not computational-complexity results. The canonical mechanism in the proof of Theorem 4 has abstract, infinite message spaces and no runtime analysis.

There is a plausible population-continuum extension of Theorem 5: let \(T\) be a finite set of complete preference types, let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s mass, and describe a coalition by the mass \(q_t\le\mu_t\) it contains of each type. Give each sector a feasibility correspondence from coalition mass vectors to feasible outcomes. Then ask whether the sector-wise core—an outcome is excluded if some coalition mass vector can obtain a feasible outcome strictly preferred by all its participating types—can be Nash implemented by separate sector mechanisms. A replicated market or public allocation programme with many households sharing preference types could make that population regime credible. But this would be a new continuous implementation question, not a continuous version of a named complexity result in the paper.

So the strongest honest positive observation is that the paper offers a plausible implementation problem to continuize, especially through its core result. It offers no qualifying computational anchor, however, and I cannot claim that any discrete hardness dissolves or that a continuous algorithm follows from these theorems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would keep this paper outside ChoCo, even though a large population with repeated preference types is a plausible setting for it. The decisive problem is that its results are about existence and characterization of mechanisms, not computational complexity. Theorem 4 characterizes Nash implementation under abstract domain conditions; Theorems 5 and 6 apply that characterization to the core and VCG solutions. None gives a finite encoded input, an algorithm, or a running-time or hardness result. The canonical mechanism in Theorem 4 even uses abstract message spaces built from preference profiles, outcomes, and nonnegative integers. As the proponent acknowledges, turning these results into a complexity landscape would require supplying a new computational problem.

The strongest proposed mirror is Theorem 5. A replicated school or housing market with finitely many complete preference types and masses \(\mu_t\) is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. A sector’s feasible outcomes could depend on a coalition’s mass vector \(q\), and the core could exclude allocations blocked by a coalition of positive mass. That is a coherent continuous cooperative-core question. But the paper leaves the feasibility correspondence and social choice rule abstract; it gives no finite representation of them or procedure for finding the core. Asking whether this mass-based core is Nash implementable would extend the paper’s existence theory. Asking how to compute it would require choosing and encoding a particular market model. Neither is a computational mirror of a result the paper proves.

There is also a real mismatch in the proposed Nash implementation. In a mass-only, anonymous model, a single agent has measure zero. If the outcome depends only on aggregate type masses, that agent’s unilateral deviation does not change the societal outcome. The paper’s Nash incentive conditions then lose their force at the level of mass outcomes, while the core’s blocking condition concerns coalitions with positive mass. One can retain finitely many agents, make whole type cohorts strategic players, or preserve individual assignments so that a person’s report changes their own outcome. Each choice changes the strategic object: respectively a finite high-multiplicity game, a game with coordinated mass blocs, or an outcome that retains identity-level information. Those may be worth studying, but they are not a straightforward continuation of the paper’s population-level Nash implementation.

Theorem 6’s auction setting offers a similarly plausible population story: finitely many bidder types in a large replicated market. Yet its VCG result is also an implementation-existence claim, not a computation result. With fixed scarce items and atomless bidders, an individual’s effect on aggregate allocation can vanish; scaling supply and transfers with population size can restore a substantive market, but defines a new continuum auction model. Theorem 4 has the same gap in general form.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo’s stated computational programme, not as a claim that continuous implementation theory could never be valuable. The paper supplies no computational anchor, and the most natural mass-based extensions either remain existence questions or need new modelling choices before there is an algorithmic problem to study.
