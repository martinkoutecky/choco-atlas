The continuous mirror question

Given a matroid \(\mathcal M=(E,\mathcal I)\), a marked set \(S\subseteq E\), and a rational inventory \(\lambda\) in the base polytope with \(\sum_{e\in E}\lambda_e=r\), find a distribution \(\pi\) over integral bases \(B\in\mathcal B(\mathcal M)\) satisfying \(\sum_{B\ni e}\pi_B=\lambda_e\) for every \(e\), such that every \(B\) with \(\pi_B>0\) has \(|B\cap S|\in\{\lfloor\lambda(S)\rfloor,\lceil\lambda(S)\rceil\}\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of interchangeable recipients receives integral bases of \(\mathcal M\); \(\lambda_e\) is the per-capita supply of item type \(e\), and \(\pi_B\) is the population mass receiving base \(B\).

The objection that survived

Because all recipients have the same valuation type, \(\mu=(1)\), and much of the nontrivial variation lies in the inventory vector \(\lambda\), the mirror may be viewed as compressed matroid decomposition rather than a rich population-composition problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.1 and its homogeneous binary-valuation fair-division application, but not the two-set near-equitability theorem, tri-valued EF1 result, heterogeneous bi-valued MMS result, or the paper's structural conjectures.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the equitable distribution be computed in time polynomial in the matroid-oracle complexity and the encoding length of \(\lambda\), without expanding denominator-cleared copies?
  • Do analogous compressed algorithms exist for two marked sets with the near-equitability guarantees of Theorem 4.1?
  • What guarantees remain possible with several valuation types or welfare objectives?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a single-anchor mirror based on Theorem 1.1. The paper contains no named NP-hardness or parameterized-hardness result; the hardness statements in its related-work discussion are cited background, not results proved here.

The relevant continuous problem is a high-multiplicity version of matroid-constrained fair division. Let \(\mathcal M=(E,\mathcal I)\) be a rank-\(r\) matroid whose elements are item types, and let \(S\subseteq E\) be the set of “valuable” item types. The society has mass \(1\), consisting of many interchangeable recipients: each recipient has the same complete valuation type \(\theta_S\), with binary additive value \(v(B)=|B\cap S|\) for a bundle \(B\). Thus \(\tau=1\), or more generally there may be several named types that are identical on this valuation and differ only in irrelevant attributes.

A continuous inventory is a rational vector \(\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{\geq 0}^{E}\) with \(\sum_{e\in E}\lambda_e=r\), where \(\lambda_e\) is the number of copies of item type \(e\) available per unit population mass. Assume \(\lambda\) lies in the matroid base polytope, so that the inventory can be decomposed into bases. Every individual recipient must still receive an integral base \(B\in\mathcal B(\mathcal M)\); only the population assigned to each base is fractional.

The decision variable is a distribution \(\pi=(\pi_B)_{B\in\mathcal B(\mathcal M)}\), where \(\pi_B\) is the mass receiving base \(B\). It must satisfy \(\sum_B\pi_B=1\) and \(\sum_{B\ni e}\pi_B=\lambda_e\) for every \(e\in E\). Put \(q=\lfloor\lambda(S)\rfloor\), where \(\lambda(S)=\sum_{e\in S}\lambda_e\). The problem is:

Continuous Equitable Matroid Allocation. Given \(\mathcal M\), \(S\), and \(\lambda\), find such a distribution \(\pi\) in which every base with \(\pi_B>0\) satisfies \(|B\cap S|\in\{q,q+1\}\).

Equivalently, the objective is to minimize the maximum disparity in the number of \(S\)-items received by any positive-mass group, with the target guarantee that the disparity is at most one and the only used values are the floor and ceiling of the population average.

This is a genuine population continuization. The bundles remain indivisible for every individual recipient. The fractional object is the mass of recipients receiving each integral bundle. The scaling of supplies is forced by high multiplicity: with \(N\) recipients and rational \(\lambda\), there are \(N\lambda_e\) copies of item type \(e\). If the denominators divide \(N\), a distribution \(\pi\) is exactly equivalent to a finite allocation of those copies to \(N\) recipients. Conversely, any such finite allocation gives a rational \(\pi\). In the special case \(\lambda_e=1/N\) for every original item, this reduces exactly to partitioning the original ground set into \(N\) bases.

The scenario is plausible for a large institution distributing matroid-constrained bundles to many interchangeable households, employees, or beneficiaries. The number \(N\) of recipients is very large, while the number of item types, the matroid description, and the number of valuation types remain comparatively small. A matroid can encode incompatibilities or coverage requirements within each bundle; \(S\) can represent premium or scarce categories. The paper’s own identical-binary-valuation fair-division application, Corollary 1.7, makes this homogeneous high-multiplicity regime particularly defensible.

The anchor is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper. In full, it says: if a matroid \(\mathcal M=(E,\mathcal I)\) has a ground set partitionable into \(k\geq 1\) disjoint bases, then for every subset \(S\subseteq E\), there is a partition \(E=B_1\cup\cdots\cup B_k\) into \(k\) bases such that every \(i\in[k]\) satisfies \(\lfloor |S|/k\rfloor\leq |B_i\cap S|\leq\lceil |S|/k\rceil\). Moreover, such a partition can be found in polynomial time.

The continuous problem above is the same question after replacing \(k\) named recipients and one copy of every ground element by a high-multiplicity population and per-capita item supplies. For rational \(\lambda\), clearing denominators produces a finite parallel-copy matroid; applying Theorem 1.1 gives the required balanced allocation, and grouping equal projected bases yields \(\pi\). The theorem therefore strongly suggests a Class A outcome: the continuous problem should admit an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|E|\), the matroid-oracle complexity, and the encoding length of \(\lambda\), without expanding the denominator. The natural route is a compressed base-decomposition or configuration-LP algorithm, with only \(O(|E|)\) bases in the output.

This is also where the mirror produces a real computational question rather than merely renaming the theorem. The paper’s algorithm is polynomial in the explicitly expanded number of copies; the continuous task asks whether the same guarantee can be obtained in time polynomial in the bit length of the compressed population description. Further questions include whether the guarantee survives several valuation types, whether two marked sets can be balanced with the near-equitability bounds of Theorem 4.1, and whether one can optimize costs or welfare subject to equitable mass allocation.

My weakest point is that the lead regime is homogeneous: \(\tau=1\). Moreover, the continuous problem is expected to be tractable, so it does not demonstrate discrete hardness disappearing. A referee could reasonably view it as a compressed decomposition problem rather than a deep new social-choice model. Still, it preserves the paper’s actual objects—matroid bases, indivisible bundles, a marked subset, and equitable allocation—while making the population itself continuous. It therefore gives a defensible, precise Class A mirror of Theorem 1.1, without claiming coverage of the paper’s heterogeneous or multi-set results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has found the paper’s only genuinely strong anchor, and the negative case is therefore narrow.

The best objection is that the proposed model does not actually continuize the society. In the programme’s notation, every recipient has the same type, so \(\mu=(1)\) in every instance. The nontrivial vector is \(\lambda\), a per-capita inventory of item types; \(\pi\) is a distribution over bundles. Thus the continuous object is the resource supply or the allocation outcome, while the population itself has no varying composition. A randomized single recipient with marginal inventory \(\lambda\) has essentially the same mathematical description.

The natural high-multiplicity lift of Theorem 1.1 makes this especially clear. Replicating the original instance \(N\) times gives parallel copies of every item and \(N\) copies of the original recipient population. After normalization, the recipient type distribution is still \(\mu=(1)\), and \(N\) merely clears denominators. Allowing arbitrary \(\lambda\) introduces a new fractional-inventory problem: it changes the relative supply of item types and replaces the original matroid by a parallel-copy extension. That may be a sensible resource-allocation problem, but the interesting continuity lies in the inventory, not in the population. The programme permits co-modifying supplies when population continuization forces it; here the arbitrary fractional supply vector is doing the substantive work.

Trying to repair this with several recipient types does not preserve the anchor. If all types value the same \(S\), their distribution again disappears. If type \(\theta\) values a different set \(S_\theta\), the question becomes simultaneous type-specific fair division, which Theorem 1.1 does not state or imply. It is a new heterogeneous allocation theorem, not a continuous reading of the paper’s equitability result. The same applies to a stronger MMS-based construction: it could make a worthwhile high-multiplicity fair-division problem, but it would be a new anchor from the paper’s applications rather than a population-sensitive mirror of Theorem 1.1.

This objection is not airtight. The proposed \(\pi\)-model is mathematically coherent, preserves integral bundles for each individual, and its rational high-multiplicity interpretation is legitimate. If the programme accepts a homogeneous population together with fractional per-capita supplies as sufficient, then this anchor survives and the universal negative case fails. I cannot honestly claim that the paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror; the strongest defensible negative claim is that the proponent’s mirror continuizes a compressed matroid-inventory decomposition rather than the society itself.
