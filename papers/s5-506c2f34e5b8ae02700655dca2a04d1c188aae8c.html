What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-up concerns only link-additive weight manipulation in Theorem 3; it does not cover the alpha-stability results in Theorems 1–2 or the restrictions and optimal variants discussed with Theorems 3–4.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: every procedure that returns a link-additive stable matching is strictly w-manipulable. The authors exhibit a two-men, two-women instance where a woman changes her scores while keeping her ranking and complete preference list; the unique stable matching changes to one giving her a strictly better partner. Because both instances have unique stable matchings, the result applies to every such procedure.

That is a useful strategic result, but it is not a computational-complexity anchor of the kind this task requires. Theorem 3 says that manipulation is possible; it does not classify the complexity of finding a manipulation or computing a matching. The paper’s conclusion explicitly leaves the computational complexity of w-manipulation for future work. I therefore cannot honestly present a result-backed continuous complexity problem for this paper.

The most plausible follow-up would be a typed-population version of Theorem 3. Imagine large resident and hospital cohorts, with many agents but relatively few complete preference-and-score types. Represent each side by finitely many types \(I,J\), with rational masses \(a_i,b_j\) summing to one. Type \(i\) has a ranking and score vector over hospital types; type \(j\) has the corresponding information over resident types. A matching is an allocation \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with row and column sums equal to those masses. Its link strength is \(\ell_{ij}=p_i(j)+q_j(i)\); call it stable when no positive mass from a type pair can be rematched along a link that both sides prefer to their current links.

One precise question is Positive-Mass Link-Additive Weight Manipulation: given such an instance and a fixed matching procedure \(F\) that returns a stable allocation, can a positive-mass type change its score vector while preserving its ranking so that \(F\)’s new allocation is weakly better for that type and strictly better for some positive mass of it? For fractional outcomes, “better” could mean that the type’s distribution over partner types first-order stochastically improves, with strict improvement at some rank cutoff. A witness would be the altered score vector and the resulting allocation. This covers only the link-additive manipulation theme of Theorem 3; it does not mirror the paper’s results about \(\alpha\)-stability or establish a complexity classification.

The weakest point is central: Theorem 3’s manipulator is one named person, while a person in a continuum has zero mass and cannot change aggregate matching allocations. Giving a whole type the ability to misreport turns individual manipulation into coalition manipulation. The typed model also changes preferences over named partners into preferences over partner types and allows fractional allocations. Those choices make a coherent research question, but they are not licensed by the theorem. So the paper suggests a plausible follow-up in continuous matching, yet it supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and I would not assign this candidate to the programme’s tractable / hardness-transfer / continuum-specific-hard trichotomy on the evidence here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper proves strategic possibility results, not computational ones. Theorem 3 says every procedure returning a link-additive stable matching is manipulable; it does not classify the complexity of finding a manipulation. The paper explicitly leaves that complexity question for future work. And the theorem quantifies over arbitrary procedures, without specifying an algorithm whose running time could be studied. So there is no named computational result here for the programme’s complexity landscape to continuize.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 3 also runs into a deeper problem than how to define fractional stability. In the paper, one named woman changes her weights and thereby changes the matching. In a nonatomic society, one person has mass zero: changing her report leaves the population distribution unchanged, so a rule that depends on that distribution cannot react. Treating a positive-mass type as the manipulator restores an effect, but changes the strategic actor from an individual to a coordinated group.

That group version is not incoherent. Indeed, the paper’s \(2\times2\) example can be scaled to cohorts, and a whole cohort could change its weights while preserving its ranking. But the resulting question concerns coalition manipulation in a fractional matching market. To make it computational, one must also choose a specific matching procedure and represent its computation; the paper’s theorem supplies neither. Even if that follow-up proved worthwhile, it would be a new problem built around the paper’s theme, not a continuous mirror of its result.

The negative case is weakest precisely there: large markets with repeated preference-and-score types are plausible, and positive-mass coalition manipulation may be worth studying. What fails is the closer claim that the paper offers a computational result whose individual manipulation question survives continuization. The natural population limit removes the individual’s leverage; restoring leverage changes the question.
