Neither classifies the complexity of solving a computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case offers no positive anchor: it says Theorem 2 and Proposition 2 are analytical results, not computational ones. I agree with that diagnosis, and it is the strongest reason against a mirror here. The paper’s named results characterize limit beliefs, variance, and network geometry. They do not classify the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Turning them into a question about computing or optimizing those quantities would introduce a new computational problem, rather than continuize one the paper studies.

There is a deeper obstacle to making that new question a population mirror. The results depend on who is connected to whom and on distances across the network: those determine diffusion, Voronoi regions, and which seeds influence the limit belief. A distribution over coarse voter-like types would discard that structure. A type detailed enough to preserve it would need to encode a node’s network position and relations; on generic networks, that can leave nearly every node as its own type. Graph limits might preserve some structured network families, but then the continuous object must carry the network geometry as well as the population. That may support an analytic limit theory, but it does not supply the missing computational-complexity anchor.

The best alternative would be a new problem such as choosing seed locations to minimize the variance of the limit belief. That could be worth studying, but the paper does not pose or solve it, and a continuum version would be a further research direction rather than a mirror of one of its results. The honest negative case is therefore strong for this paper under ChoCo’s computational brief, though it cannot establish that no continuous-network or seed-optimization project would ever be worthwhile.
