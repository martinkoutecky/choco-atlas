What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible population setting is a large public-school district: many students, a finite set of schools, and recurring combinations of preference rankings, priority categories, and eligibility status. Let each complete type \(t\) include those characteristics, and let rational \(\mu_t\) denote its share of students. School capacities would be expressed as shares of the population. A natural follow-on question is whether the paper’s credibility ranking between deferred acceptance and Boston persists when assignments and capacities are represented by masses.

The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For every observation structure, if student-proposing deferred acceptance has a safe deviation at a preference profile, then the Boston mechanism also has one; the comparison is strict because there is an environment where Boston has a safe deviation and deferred acceptance does not. This is a strong mechanism-comparison result, but it does not assert an algorithmic or complexity result. Theorem 1, Theorem 3, Propositions 1–5, and the numbered lemmas likewise concern credibility properties or proof tools. The paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under the stated criterion.

That leaves no eligible anchor for a continuous problem per anchor. The proposed mass version also needs new semantics before it can be handed to a prover: the paper asks whether each individual student can explain an observed assignment by changing other students’ reports. In an atomless population, one student has zero mass, and changing that student’s report does not affect the aggregate mechanism outcome. Treating an entire type as the observer whose reports stay fixed would give the type a bloc role absent from the paper. The large-population setting is plausible, and the co-scaling of capacities fits the programme; the missing computational anchor and the unresolved explanation semantics make this a research lead, rather than an anchored continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only plausible anchor the proponent names, and it does not qualify for ChoCo’s computational programme. It says that whenever deferred acceptance has a safe deviation, Boston does too. That is a structural comparison of mechanisms, proved using stability, student optimality, and Pareto dominance. The paper gives no computational problem, algorithm, or complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1, Theorem 3, and Propositions 1–5 have the same character. A mass version of these claims would be a continuous matching-theory result, not a complexity landscape.

The strongest population version is still easy to describe: model a large district by shares of preference and eligibility types, scale school capacities with population, and represent assignments as measurable allocations. That handles the obvious objection that an individual has zero mass; safe explanations and welfare comparisons could be redefined pointwise or almost everywhere. But exact priority rank is part of a student’s type for these mechanisms. With strict priorities, ranks distinguish students, so a finite set of complete types does not capture repeated types. Grouping students by priority category changes the strict-priority model; preserving exact rank calls for a continuous, uncountable type space. These are real modeling costs, though not by themselves fatal.

The more decisive issue is that this repaired model still mirrors a structural credibility claim, not a computational result. One could invent a new task—such as deciding whether a proposed mass reassignment is a safe deviation—but the paper establishes neither the finite-instance complexity of that task nor an algorithmic structure to carry into its continuous version. That would be a new computational project inspired by the paper, rather than a mirror of one of its named results.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored ChoCo contribution: it contains no named computational result, and its central results are outside the programme’s computational scope. The universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile work is weaker. A complexity study of safe deviations in high-multiplicity school choice might be worthwhile, but the case for it would need to be made independently of this paper’s results.
