Why no mirror

The proposed high-multiplicity CPA-evaluation problem is a coherent extension of the crossed-claims model, and the aggregation objection limits novelty rather than invalidating the question. However, every result invoked by the proponent is structural or axiomatic; none is a numbered computational theorem, algorithmic guarantee, or hardness result. What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers exact evaluation of CPA for a finite-support mass distribution and related representation or sensitivity questions; it does not computationalize the paper's axiomatic characterizations or independence result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest position is qualified: this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it contains no qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are axiomatic or structural. Proposition 1 proves properties of the CPA iteration; Theorem 1, proved in this paper, shows that CPA satisfies PEFF, ETE, GMA, CONS, NMS, and NMRM; Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes CPA as the unique rule satisfying PEFF, ETE, GMA, CONS, and NMS; and Proposition 2 proves logical independence of those axioms. None asserts membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), hardness, parameterized tractability, or an algorithmic formulation with a complexity claim. Thus I would not honestly present Theorem 1 or Theorem 2 as a ChoCo computational anchor.

Subject to that caveat, my lead mirror would be Continuous Crossed-Claims CPA Computation. A type is a pair \( \theta=(c_\theta,A_\theta) \), where \(c_\theta\) is a claimant’s scalar claim and \(A_\theta\subseteq I\) is the set of issues that claimant requests. A society is a finite-support distribution \( \mu\) over such types, with \( \mu_\theta\) denoting the fraction of claimants of type \(\theta\). The available amount of issue \(i\) is a per-capita supply \(e_i\); for a population of size \(n\), the corresponding finite supply is \(n e_i\).

The decision variable is an award \(a_\theta\in[0,c_\theta]\) per claimant of each type, or equivalently an awarded mass \(y_\theta=\mu_\theta a_\theta\). Feasibility requires

\[ \sum_{\theta:i\in A_\theta} y_\theta\le e_i \qquad\text{for every issue }i. \]

The problem is: given rational \(I,\Theta,\mu,c,A,e\), compute the unique award profile prescribed by the continuous CPA rule. At each round, among the currently active types, choose the largest common proportional factor \(\lambda\) such that awarding \(\lambda\) times every active type’s residual claim respects every residual issue capacity. Award that fraction of the residual claims, remove exhausted issues and fully satisfied types, and repeat. The output is the accumulated award \(a_\theta\) for every positive-mass type.

This is a genuine population continuization, not merely another fractional-allocation problem. The paper already has divisible resources; the new continuous object is the claimant population itself. The relevant regime could be a large population of households, firms, or radio users in a few dozen or few hundred recurring demand classes. Members of one type have exactly the same claim \(c_\theta\) and request exactly the same issue set \(A_\theta\), so they are indistinguishable for the problem. The number of agents may be millions while the number of types is \(\tau\ll n\). The per-capita scaling of \(e_i\) is the necessary co-modification that makes the model meaningful as population size grows.

The high-multiplicity correspondence is exact. If \(n_\theta\) finite claimants have type \(\theta\), set \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/n\) and divide every resource by \(n\). Every sum in the paper’s CPA iteration becomes the corresponding mass-weighted sum. Equal members of a type receive equal awards, as supported by Theorem 1’s ETE conclusion. Conversely, a rational distribution can be expanded into a finite MAC instance by clearing denominators. The authors should therefore recognize this as the high-multiplicity version of their crossed-claims problem, rather than as a different allocation story.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. The CPA procedure has at most \(|I|\) rounds because each nonterminal round exhausts at least one issue, as described around Proposition 1. With explicit type support, each round requires only sums over \(m\tau\) type–issue incidences, and exact rational arithmetic remains polynomial in the input length. A natural new computational theorem would therefore be an exact algorithm running in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), together with approximation and sensitivity results for nonrational or sampled population distributions.

The further questions are meaningful: how accurately can CPA be estimated from samples; how does the allocation change under Wasserstein or total-variation perturbations of \(\mu\); can one represent a very large implicit type space efficiently; and do the consistency and non-manipulability properties in Theorem 1 survive when coalitions are measurable sets of population mass rather than finite claimant subsets?

The weakest point is decisive: this is not a mirror of a named computational result in the paper. It is a plausible new computational study suggested by the paper’s computed rule and its finite-round structure. Moreover, because the paper’s outcome is already divisible, an opponent could argue that the population continuization adds compression and a high-multiplicity interpretation but not a substantial new computational phenomenon. The positive case therefore survives as a sensible ChoCo research question, but not as a fully compliant anchor-based case under the programme’s strict gate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Proposition 1 is a structural fact about the iteration; Theorem 1 verifies axioms; Theorem 2 gives an axiomatic characterization; and Proposition 2 proves independence of axioms. None states a complexity classification, an algorithmic guarantee, an approximation result, or even a computational problem. Changing the finite claimant set into a population distribution cannot turn any of these results into a computational theorem. The proposed “CPA computation” would therefore be a new algorithmic study inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a result proved in it.

The proponent’s population model is nevertheless mathematically sensible, and I would not object that high multiplicity is implausible. The stronger negative point is that, for this particular rule, the population continuum contributes almost no new structure. For each issue-set \(A\subseteq I\), define the aggregate claim mass

\[ q_A=\int c\,d\mu(c,A). \]

At every CPA round, all claimants with the same \(A\) receive the same proportional factor on their residual claims. Consequently, the next round depends only on the finitely many quantities \(q_A\), not on the distribution of claim sizes within \(A\). The individual output has the form \(a(c,A)=\rho_A c\), with \(\rho_A\) determined by those aggregate claim masses and the issue capacities. Thus even an atomless distribution over claim sizes collapses to a finite vector indexed by issue-subsets. With explicit support, the proposed continuous algorithm is just the paper’s finite iteration after grouping homologous claimants and dividing all quantities by population size.

That grouping is not an accidental simplification: it is exactly what NMS and NMRM are designed to guarantee. The paper already proves invariance under splitting and merging homologous claimants. The continuous model therefore adds a normalization and a type-level representation, but no new population-level computational object. A succinctly represented distribution over \(2^I\) could make the aggregate quantities difficult to evaluate, but that would be a succinct-input or counting problem, not difficulty caused by continuizing the population. Adding prices, identities, locations, or other data would create a different allocation problem.

The other possible anchors do not rescue it. Theorem 1’s ETE, NMS, and NMRM extend almost tautologically to type masses; PEFF and GMA remain allocation axioms. Consistency is especially weak in an atomless society if interpreted as removing one claimant, since a singleton has measure zero. Replacing it by consistency for arbitrary positive-measure subpopulations may be interesting, but it is a new measure-theoretic axiom rather than a computational mirror. Theorem 2’s proof also fundamentally uses induction on the finite number of claimants and deletion of one claimant. Proposition 2 is likewise purely axiomatic.

Sampling questions, Wasserstein sensitivity, and robustness under perturbations of \(\mu\) would introduce an observation model or a metric absent from the paper. They may support a later distributional-allocation project, but they are not continuizations of the paper’s results. The same is true of measurable-coalition versions of splitting and consistency.

So the negative case should not pretend that the proposed high-multiplicity scenario is nonsensical; it is quite natural. Its weakness is different and stronger under ChoCo’s rules: the paper contributes no computational result to mirror, while its rule is already invariant under precisely the population refinements the proposed continuum would formalize. If the programme accepts any new algorithmic question suggested by an axiomatic paper, this is a legitimate but modest Class A project. Under the stated anchor requirement, however, it should not be greened as a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
