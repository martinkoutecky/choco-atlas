What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a qualifying mirror: Proposition 1 is an equilibrium-accuracy comparison, and Experimental Results 1–4 report laboratory behavior and outcomes.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named result, Proposition 1 (proved in Appendix A), says that for every \(n\ge2\), \(\lambda\in(0,1)\), and \(p\in(0,1)\), there are thresholds \(C'<C''<C'''\) such that delegation gives higher decision accuracy at low information costs, individual consultation gives higher accuracy when \(C''<C\le C'''\), and the regimes have equal accuracy when \(C>C'''\). That is an equilibrium and accuracy characterization, not a result about computational complexity or an algorithm for a computational problem.

A continuum of experts grouped by competence type is a possible population interpretation. But taking an atomless limit also makes any one expert nonpivotal, which risks removing the individual incentives central to Proposition 1. Keeping finite committees and considering many similar committees avoids that issue, but then the continuum aggregates outcomes across committees rather than continuizing the paper’s decision problem.

So the paper has no named computational result on which to base the required continuous mirror. Calling Proposition 1 an algorithmic or complexity anchor would overstate what it proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only plausible anchor, but it is an equilibrium and accuracy comparison, not a computational result: it gives no algorithmic problem, input encoding, or complexity claim. The experimental results likewise report behavior and outcomes. The paper’s two competence types, binary state, and scalar parameters already yield explicit equilibrium thresholds. Turning those formulas into a continuous-type version and asking how to compute its equilibria would require adding a new type space and a new computational question; that would be an extension of the paper, not a computational mirror of a result it establishes.

The strongest population mirror would put a continuum of experts inside one committee, preserving the paper’s type masses and costs. But that limit removes the incentive trade-off that drives the paper. Its cutoff for information acquisition under delegation is
\[ C''(n,\lambda)=\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}}{2-(1-\lambda)^n}, \]
which tends to zero as \(n\to\infty\), while the consultation cutoff is \(C'''=\lambda/(1+\lambda)\). Thus, at any fixed \(C\) with \(0<C<C'''\), a sufficiently large committee acquires no information under delegation, while competent experts still acquire it under consultation. With a positive mass of competent experts, consultation’s majority decision becomes accurate with probability tending to one; delegation’s uninformed decision has accuracy \(1/2\). That is a clear analytic limit, but it is a direct asymptotic consequence of the paper’s thresholds—not a computational landscape. Scaling costs or reputational stakes to preserve individual pivotality would change the model’s incentives and lead to a different equilibrium problem.

A second tempting construction is a continuum of small, similar committees. It preserves each expert’s incentives, but then the paper’s finite-committee game remains intact: aggregate accuracy is just an average over those committees. That makes the population continuous across institutions, while leaving the decision problem the paper studies finite. Neither construction supplies ChoCo with a computational question arising from one of the paper’s results.

I can’t rule out a worthwhile continuous economic model inspired by this paper. The negative case is strongest within ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper offers no computational result to mirror, and its natural large-committee limit yields analytic comparative statics while erasing the delegation-side incentive mechanism. An algorithmic treatment would need substantial new structure beyond the paper’s model.
