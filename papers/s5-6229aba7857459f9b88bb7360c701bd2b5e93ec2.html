The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible, focused mirror: the price of envy-freeness for a continuous population with indivisible personal bundles and item supplies scaled with population size. The mirror concerns the same allocation problem and welfare ratio; it does not turn the bundles themselves into fractional goods.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: when \(m=n+\Theta(\sqrt n)\) and \(m\ge n\), the worst-case price of envy-freeness satisfies \(p_{\mathrm{envy}}(n,m)\in\Theta(\sqrt n)\). The paper also proves the boundary estimates in Theorem 3.5 and Lemma 3.6: for \(m=n\),
\[ p_{\mathrm{envy}}(n,n)\le \tfrac12\sqrt n+O(1) \quad\text{and}\quad p_{\mathrm{envy}}(n,n)\ge \tfrac12\sqrt n-\tfrac12. \]
These are quantitative results about the allocation objective, not runtime classifications. The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time result; its ILP formulation in Section 3 is relevant computational content, but is not itself a numbered complexity theorem.

Consider a large public allocation programme with many recipients and a catalog of \(K\) indivisible item classes. There are \(N\) recipients but only \(\tau\) complete valuation types, with \(N\gg\tau\): for example, recipients belong to a small number of standardized household or eligibility profiles, each specifying the same additive values for every item class. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of recipients of type \(t\). Give each class \(q_k=Q/K\) units per capita, so the total stock is \(Q\) items per recipient. Along a finite realization, each person receives an integral bundle even though the population’s allocation averages can be fractional.

Here is the precise problem I would hand to a prover. For a type \(t\), let \(v_{tk}\ge0\) be its value for one unit of class \(k\), normalized so that
\[ \frac QK\sum_{k=1}^K v_{tk}=1. \]
A bundle is \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^K\). Let \(\pi_{t,b}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\). Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_b\pi_{t,b}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,b}\pi_{t,b}b_k=\frac QK\quad\text{for each }k. \]
The allocation is envy-free if every bundle used by positive mass gives each type at least as much utility as every other bundle used by positive mass: \(v_t\cdot b\ge v_t\cdot b'\). Its welfare is
\[ W(\pi)=\sum_{t,b}\pi_{t,b}\,v_t\cdot b. \]
Let \(W_{\mathrm{all}}\) be maximum welfare over feasible allocations and \(W_{\mathrm{EF}}\) the maximum over feasible envy-free allocations. For instances where an envy-free allocation exists, define
\[ P^{\mathrm{HM}}_{K,\tau}(Q) = \sup_{\substack{\mu,\,(v_t)\\|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\le\tau}} \frac{W_{\mathrm{all}}}{W_{\mathrm{EF}}}, \]
with the normalization above. The continuous problem is to characterize or compute this extremal price, and to return an extremal society and its optimal allocations. Equivalently, one can ask its threshold version: given \(\lambda\), does an admissible society have price at least \(\lambda\)?

For Theorem 4.1, the lead question is what happens when \(Q=1+\epsilon\) and \(\epsilon\) tends to zero, particularly when \(\epsilon=\Theta(K^{-1/2})\). In the paper’s regime, \(Q=m/n=1+\Theta(n^{-1/2})\), \(K=m\) is of order \(n\), and the discrete price is \(\Theta(\sqrt K)\). The continuous question is whether \(P^{\mathrm{HM}}_{K,\tau}(1+\Theta(K^{-1/2}))\) has comparable growth when the population is represented by a small number of types, or whether splitting type mass across integral bundles changes the rate. At the unit-supply boundary, Theorem 3.5 and Lemma 3.6 motivate the separate question of how \(P^{\mathrm{HM}}_{K,\tau}(1)\) depends on item-class count \(K\) and type support \(\tau\).

I expect Class C for the general problem when \(K\) and \(\tau\) vary: the population counts become continuous, but the choice of integral bundles and the support-wide envy constraints remain combinatorial. Fixing a set of bundle patterns makes welfare and resource constraints linear, which suggests a configuration-LP route; finding the right patterns or pricing them is the computational bottleneck. I would treat tractability for fixed \(K,\tau\) as a plausible subcase, not claim it is established. The paper’s theorems do not provide hardness that could transfer.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it retains additive utilities, individually integral bundles, envy-freeness, and the same ratio of unconstrained to best fair welfare. The high-multiplicity modification is explicit: recipients with the same type are indistinguishable, and the stock scales with population. It covers the price-of-envy-freeness results, especially Theorem 4.1 and the \(m=n\) bounds; it does not try to mirror every open question or fairness criterion in the paper.

The weakest point is that the paper treats its \(m\) items as distinct, while this mirror represents goods through classes with per-capita supplies. Also, the paper’s extremal profiles may require many distinct valuation types. Neither issue invalidates the question, but both could change the answer: the mirror asks whether the price law survives when a large population has few complete types and type mass can be split continuously. A useful next result would determine the dependence on \(K,\tau,\epsilon\), and establish whether the paper’s extremal constructions can be represented with bounded type support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the three anchors is a computational result. Theorem 4.1, Theorem 3.5, and Lemma 3.6 bound the worst-case price of envy-freeness; they do not classify the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The ILP in Section 3 is a formulation, and the reported computations for small \(n\) are not a complexity theorem. So the proposed \(P^{\mathrm{HM}}_{K,\tau}(Q)\) is a new extremal-price problem, not the continuous mirror of a computational result established in this paper.

That objection applies to Theorem 4.1 even in the better version of the mirror. Its \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\) claim concerns the finite regime \(m=n+\Theta(\sqrt n)\). The proposed question substitutes item-class count \(K\) for the population parameter \(n\): \(P^{\mathrm{HM}}_{K,\tau}(1+\Theta(K^{-1/2}))\). But in a continuous society there is no population count \(n\); asking for this asymptotic in \(K\) varies the item side. One could instead study dependence on type support \(\tau\), but that is a reasonable new question, not a direct continuation of Theorem 4.1.

The same limitation applies to Theorem 3.5 and Lemma 3.6. They bound the price at \(m=n\), where the finite allocation is a matching of individual agents to individual items. With continuous type masses and item supplies, the corresponding allocation can split mass across bundles. At unit supply, the allocation problem has a fractional transportation structure. That may make a useful algorithmic question, but the theorem and lemma do not establish its complexity or provide a computational claim for the continuous model to extend.

There is a stronger candidate than the proposed extremal-price threshold: given \((\mu,v)\) and item supplies, compute the maximum welfare among envy-free allocations of integral bundles. That is a well-posed high-multiplicity allocation problem, and a configuration LP with a pricing question is plausible. But it changes the anchor from the paper’s bounds on a supremum over utility profiles to an algorithmic problem for a fixed society. It may be worthwhile; it simply is not supplied by the named results.

So the negative case defeats the claimed anchoring, but it does not honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The proponent’s model retains additive valuations, integral personal bundles, envy-freeness, and the welfare ratio, and the paper’s ILP makes it plausible that its authors would recognize the associated allocation problem. The case against is therefore weak exactly at the universal claim: there is a credible continuous research direction here, even though this paper gives no computational theorem to anchor it.
