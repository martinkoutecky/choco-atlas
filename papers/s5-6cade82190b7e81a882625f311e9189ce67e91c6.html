Why no mirror

The named results guarantee existence of stable partitions, but the paper does not state a qualifying computational result: its constructive proof supplies no input model, runtime bound, or complexity claim. The proposed continuous team-mass problem is a plausible new project, but it requires anonymous type-composition preferences, a coalition-size bound, and positive-mass deviations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is that this paper does not provide a qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s rule. It contains no named theorem proving NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), FPT, W[1]-hardness, or a comparable complexity result. Its algorithmic remarks and cited results do not change that. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Theorem 3 are existence theorems.

The best substantive mirror would nevertheless be based on Theorem 1, proved in this paper using the Top Covering Algorithm of Alcalde and Revilla and Dimitrov and Sung:

Top responsiveness and mutuality together guarantee the existence of an SSNS partition.

A natural continuous version is a high-multiplicity research-team population. Let \(T\) be a finite set of role-types, such as theorist, experimentalist, and engineer, and let \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) give the fraction of the workforce of each type. Types are complete: agents of the same type have the same preferences over team compositions. Let \(q\) be the maximum team size and define

\[ \mathcal A_q=\left\{a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{T}:1\le \sum_{t\in T}a_t\le q\right\}. \]

Here \(a\) describes a team containing \(a_t\) agents of type \(t\). A continuous coalition structure is a vector \(x=(x_a)_{a\in\mathcal A_q}\), where \(x_a\ge0\) is the mass of teams of composition \(a\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{a\in\mathcal A_q}a_t x_a=\mu_t \qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]

The preferences of each type are lifted from individual players to team compositions. Top responsiveness means that every type has a unique best subcomposition \(ch(t,a)\), and mutuality means that the induced “chosen collaborator” relation between types is symmetric.

The problem is:

Continuous Top-Responsive SSNS. Given \(T\), rational \(\mu\), \(q\), and type-level preferences satisfying top responsiveness and mutuality, output a feasible \(x\) for which there is no positive-mass group of agents that can reorganize into new teams while every deviator weakly improves and a positive mass strictly improves, with non-deviators retaining their coalition relations.

The decision variable is the mass distribution \(x\); the objective is feasibility of strict strong Nash stability, not welfare maximization. A rational \(x\) is also a finite high-multiplicity object: if its denominator is \(N\), then \(N x_a\) is the number of teams of composition \(a\), and \(N\mu_t\) is the number of type-\(t\) agents.

This is plausible as the continuous analogue because the paper itself motivates top responsiveness through research teams. The regime is a large organization with many interchangeable agents in a small number of roles, not a seven-person committee. I would expect this mirror to be Class A when \(q\) is fixed or coalition templates are explicitly represented: the Top Covering argument should aggregate to a type-level choice graph, followed by a finite flow or configuration calculation. With succinct preferences and unbounded \(q\), the separation and stability-verification questions remain open.

The weakest point is substantial. In the finite paper, a partition records named players and exact non-deviator relationships. A mass vector \(x\) forgets those identities, and fractional team mass may permit recombinations unavailable in one finite partition. A fully rigorous model therefore needs a measurable realization of \(x\) and positive-mass deviations, not merely the displayed linear constraints. That makes this a credible new high-multiplicity research problem, but not a theorem already supplied by the paper.

Theorem 2 (bottom responsiveness implies SIS) and Theorem 3 (strong bottom responsiveness plus mutuality implies SNS) could be mirrored similarly, but they are also only existence results. I would not present them as qualifying computational anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong at the programme’s gate: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Theorem 3 are existence theorems. The Top Covering Algorithm is used constructively, but the paper gives no input representation, running-time bound, oracle model, or complexity classification for computing a stable partition. Its closing algorithmic remarks explicitly present such questions as future work. A continuous problem about computing a stable object would therefore be a new problem inspired by the paper, not a continuization of one of its computational results.

Theorem 1 is the strongest possible rescue, but the proposed mirror changes too much at once. The finite theorem concerns arbitrary named players, arbitrary coalitions, and a unique named choice set \(ch(i,X)\) for every subset \(X\). A high-multiplicity population instead requires preferences over anonymous type-compositions. Those are not equivalent assumptions. If two agents have the same type, a preference that selects one of them but not the other violates anonymity; yet the original top-responsiveness condition demands a unique maximizing subset. Replacing \(ch(i,X)\) by a unique best composition vector is a sensible repair, but it is a new “anonymous top responsiveness” condition whose relation to the paper’s Top Covering proof must be established from scratch.

The proposed bounded team size \(q\) is another substantive addition. The paper imposes no such bound: coalitions are arbitrary subsets of \(N\). Without a bound, the continuous model has infinitely many possible team compositions and no finite configuration vector of the displayed kind. With a bound, it becomes a different, restricted coalition-formation model. Neither choice is illegitimate, but neither is supplied by the paper.

There is also a mismatch in stability. SSNS forbids every nonempty deviating set \(H\), including a single agent or a finite group. A positive-mass deviation deletes precisely those deviations: every individual has measure zero. Preserving the paper’s notion requires a pointwise measurable partition with incidence information, whereas the proposed vector \(x\) records only masses of team compositions. Requiring positive-mass deviations instead defines a bulk version of SSNS, not SSNS itself. That bulk problem might be interesting, but it is a newly designed stability concept rather than the continuous form of Theorem 1.

The bottom-responsiveness results do not provide a fallback. SIS is defined around unilateral deviations, so in an atomless population it is either vacuous if null deviations are ignored, or becomes a different bulk-stability notion if a positive mass of agents must move together. SNS permits larger deviations, but the same problem remains for every finite blocking coalition. The finite proofs’ lexicographic arguments over integer coalition-size vectors also do not yield an algorithm for a continuum of team masses; one would need a new existence and representation theory.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer might be easy, or that the theorem might remain true. Those would be acceptable outcomes under ChoCo. The objection is that the paper supplies no computational question to continuize, while the most promising proposed mirror requires new anonymous preference axioms, a coalition-size regime, a new deviation semantics, and a new algorithmic problem. Theorem 1 can plausibly inspire a worthwhile future project, but the paper itself does not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror. For Theorems 2 and 3, the null-deviation obstruction is even more direct.
