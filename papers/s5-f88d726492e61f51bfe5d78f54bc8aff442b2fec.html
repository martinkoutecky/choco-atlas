The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses μ_t summing to \(1\), per-person reform advantages \(d_t\), and per-capita lobby budgets \(b_0,b_1\), lobby 1 chooses offers \(p_t\ge0\). Find the minimum spend \(V=\min\sum_tμ_t p_t\) subject to \(\sum_t z_t(p_t+d_t)\ge b_0\) for every blocking mass vector \(0\le z_t\leμ_t\) with \(\sum_t z_t\ge1/2\); return an optimal offer vector and determine whether \(b_1\ge V\).

The model it lives in

A sequential two-lobby vote-buying game over a unit-mass population with finitely many preference-intensity types. Type \(t\) has mass \(μ_t\) and per-person reform advantage \(d_t\); lobby 1 pays \(p_t\) per supporter. Reform passes with a strict majority, and lobby 0 can counteroffer to any blocking mass vector. The objective is to minimize lobby 1's total spend while securing reform.

What the mirror covers

The mirror carries Proposition 4's majority cutoff and offer allocation to finite-type populations, and covers the weighted-quota slice of Proposition 1. It leaves Proposition 1's arbitrary simple games and the paper's broader hypergraph, least-core, and non-majority analyses without a mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the finite-electorate optimum converge to the mass optimum when type counts approximate \(μ\), with an explicit rounding-error bound?
  • Can a polynomial-time algorithm compute an exact optimal offer vector from rational finite-support input?

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no numbered result claiming an algorithm is in P or a problem is NP-hard. Its computational anchors are optimization results: Propositions 1 and 4 formulate and characterize the underlying minimum-spend problem. Both are proved in the paper. Proposition 4 is the stronger mirror for a majority electorate; the authors also credit Young with independently deriving it.

Lead — continuous majority vote buying, mirroring Proposition 4. Proposition 4 considers an odd legislature with \(n=2k-1\) members, whose reform-preference intensities are ordered as \(\alpha_1\le\cdots\le\alpha_n\). When lobby 1’s willingness to pay is large enough, an optimal offer can target a prefix of \(m^*\) legislators and equalize their net payoff from supporting reform: \(t_{i1}^*+\alpha_i\Delta W=t_{j1}^*+\alpha_j\Delta W\) for all targeted \(i,j\). The cutoff \(m^*\) depends on the preference intensities and the ratio of lobby 0’s budget to the policy-value gap. Specifically, if \(W_0/k>\alpha_k\Delta W\), it is the smallest \(m\) for which \(W_0/k\le\alpha_m\Delta W\), if one exists, and \(n\) otherwise. If \(W_0/k\le\alpha_k\Delta W\), it is the smallest \(m\le k-1\) satisfying \(W_0<\Delta W[\sum_{\ell=m+1}^k\alpha_\ell+m\alpha_{m+1}]\). The proof constructs an optimal schedule with no offers above the cutoff. This is a characterization of the underlying bribe allocation, not a result about the complexity of computing it.

The continuous counterpart I would hand to a prover is Atomless Majority Vote-Buying Security. Take a population of mass \(1\), divided among \(\tau\) preference types \(t\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Everyone has one vote; type \(t\) has reform-preference intensity \(\alpha_t\) and per-person money-equivalent preference \(d_t=\alpha_t\delta\), where \(\delta>0\) is the per-capita value gap. Two informed lobbies move in sequence as in the paper. Let \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) be their per-capita budgets. Lobby 1 chooses nonnegative conditional offers \(p_t\) per person of each type. Reform passes with a strict majority, so an opposition mass vector \(z\) blocks it when \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t z_t\ge 1/2\). The problem is to find the minimum spend \(V\) and an optimal offer vector:
\[ V=\min_{p_t\ge0}\ \sum_t\mu_t p_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t z_t(p_t+d_t)\ge b_0 \ \text{for every blocking }z. \]
A solution is an optimal \(p\), its value \(V\), and whether lobby 1 can secure reform, which is exactly when \(b_1\ge V\). Type-constant offers are without loss here: agents within a type are interchangeable, and averaging offers within each type preserves feasibility.

The regime I find plausible is a very large member-based association or union voting on a binary proposal, with equal voting rights, a small number of stable preference-intensity classes, and two organized factions that campaign sequentially. Then the number of members is much larger than \(\tau\), and fractional mass is the natural quantity for describing support and campaign spending. Treating the budgets and utilities per capita is a necessary scale adjustment for the population limit. The problem keeps the paper’s central question—how much must the first mover spend to withstand a countercampaign, and how should it allocate that spend—while making the population continuous.

I expect this majority mirror to be Class A. For fixed \(p\), checking the constraints amounts to finding the least expensive blocking mass: choose half the population from types with the lowest \(p_t+d_t\), allowing a fractional amount of a type at the boundary. That is a fractional-knapsack calculation. With rational finite-support input, it gives a separation procedure for the offer-optimization problem, suggesting polynomial dependence on \(\tau\) and the input bit length. That is a claim about the proposed mirror, not something Proposition 4 proves.

Broader anchor — continuous victory threshold, mirroring Proposition 1. Proposition 1 treats an arbitrary finite simple game and preference vector. Write \(\mathcal B\) for its blocking coalitions and \(\Delta W=W_1-W_0\). It says lobby 1’s minimum cost is characterized by the maximum, over nonnegative subbalancing coefficients \(\lambda_S\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\ni i}\lambda_S\le1\) for every legislator, of \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal B}\lambda_S[W_0-\Delta W\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_i]\). If \(W_1\) is at least that maximum, lobby 1 makes an optimal offer and wins while lobby 0 makes no offer. If it is below the value for some such coefficients, lobby 1 abstains and lobby 0 can defeat reform with an offer to a blocking coalition at cost below \(W_0\). The proposition derives this characterization by LP duality from the minimum-offer problem.

A precise type-level analogue is Weighted-Rule Mass Security. An instance has rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-person voting weights \(w_t\), intrinsic reform advantages \(d_t\), a quota \(q\), and per-capita budgets \(b_0,b_1\). Reform passes if its supporting weight exceeds \(q\). An opposition mass vector \(z\) blocks when \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t w_t(\mu_t-z_t)\le q\). The question is to find the minimum spend \(V=\min_{p_t\ge0}\sum_t\mu_t p_t\), subject to \(\sum_tz_t(p_t+d_t)\ge b_0\) for every blocking \(z\), and return an optimal offer vector and whether \(b_1\ge V\). This is the mass version of the same first-mover security question, now allowing type classes with different institutional voting weights. For a finite-type weighted quota rule, I also expect Class A: checking a proposed offer requires optimizing a linear objective over a box with one weighted constraint, again a fractional-knapsack problem. More general compact descriptions of voting rules could have hard separation problems, so this expectation should not be extended to arbitrary rule encodings without analysis.

These mirrors cover the paper’s minimum winning budget, counteroffer resistance, and allocation of offers. They do not claim to continuize every result about finite legislatures or every simple-game rule. The paper itself notes that much of Groseclose and Snyder’s 1996 analysis assumes continuum voters. That is evidence that this regime is intelligible, not a collision with this paper’s finite-legislator Propositions 1 and 4.

The weakest point is also the main modeling risk: in an atomless electorate, no individual is pivotal, while the paper’s institutional structure is expressed through discrete winning and blocking coalitions. The mirror has to preserve those coalition-level counteroffer constraints as constraints on masses; it cannot merely replace each legislator by a fraction and assume the strategic meaning survives. The next questions are whether the finite cutoff in Proposition 4 becomes a preference quantile with a fractional boundary type, and whether finite electorates with type counts approximating \(\mu\) converge to \(V\) under an explicit rounding error. The large-association scenario and the mass-level blocking rule make that a serious question, though less direct than the authors’ original finite legislature.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Proposition 1 is that its central object is the legislature’s particular simple game: the full family of winning and blocking coalitions. Population masses do not encode that structure. The proposed weighted-quota model retains only a restricted class of rules; to preserve arbitrary simple games in a limit, one would need to carry along a limiting description of the coalition rule, which could retain as much individual structure as the finite model. That makes the weighted-quota problem a narrower new model, rather than a continuization of Proposition 1’s general result.

For Proposition 4, the corresponding objection is weaker. A continuum of equal-vote members with finitely many preference-intensity types is a sensible regime, and the union or association scenario works. Its mass version also has a direct computational formulation: security against every blocking mass reduces to a lower-tail constraint, expressible as a finite LP for finite type support. That makes it a plausible Class A question, but also makes it look like a type-compressed version of the paper’s existing optimization, rather than a new complexity problem. The paper’s note that much of Groseclose and Snyder’s analysis assumes continuum voters reinforces the model’s naturalness; it is not, by itself, a collision or a reason to reject the mirror.

I would not rest the negative case on the absence of complexity-class theorems. Propositions 1 and 4 are named optimization results, and minimum-cost campaigning is computational in ChoCo’s sense. Nor can I object that the continuum formulation preserves the same answer, or that an individual has zero mass: the relevant constraints concern masses, and the per-capita scaling is coherent.

So the best case against is that Proposition 1’s genuinely distinctive generality resists type aggregation, while Proposition 4’s majority case appears to yield a direct, compact LP. But that does not defeat the stronger Proposition 4 mirror. It is well-posed, fits a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and asks a computational question about the population itself. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case: that anchor survives.
