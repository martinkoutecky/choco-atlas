The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of types \(t=(x_t,p_t,\rho_t)\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), \(x_t\in[0,1]\), \(\rho_t\geq 0\), and \(p_t\in\{0,1\}\), find a type-symmetric pure default policy \(d:\mathcal T\to\mathcal T\cup\{\bot\}\). The policy must be a Nash equilibrium: no individual can increase their expected utility by changing their initial delegate type or choosing \(\bot\), with all other individuals following their type's policy. A type-\(t\) nonvoter receives \(\rho_t-|x_t-x_q|\) if their route reaches a certain voter of type \(q\), and \(0\) if it reaches no caster; a certain voter receives \(\rho_t\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population with finite support \(\mathcal T\), where each type carries mass \(\mu_t\) and records position, deterministic participation, and tolerance. Each type uses one default-delegation policy; individual utility follows the paper's delegation routes, while masses represent population shares and weight aggregate welfare.

The objection that survived

The paper's general-position assumption excludes repeated positions, so repeated cohorts require relaxing that restriction and specifying tie behavior; the construction in Theorem 1(i) otherwise applies.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers deterministic-participation equilibrium existence and computation in Theorem 1(i); it leaves the stochastic equilibrium results, cycle structure, welfare bounds, and experiments aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a single lead mirror anchored in Theorem 1(i). The paper proves this result itself: when every voter is deterministic, meaning \(p_i\in\{0,1\}\), a pure Nash equilibrium exists and can be computed in polynomial time. Its construction has every certain voter self-nominate; each voter who never votes delegates to the closest certain voter within their acceptability set, or self-nominates if there is no such voter. The paper’s theorem covers other special cases too, but this deterministic case is the cleanest one to carry over.

Call the mirror Deterministic High-Multiplicity Default-Delegation Equilibrium. An instance is a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of types \(t=(x_t,p_t,\rho_t)\), with \(x_t\in[0,1]\), tolerance \(\rho_t\geq 0\), and \(p_t\in\{0,1\}\), together with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\), and the support size \(s=|\mathcal T|\) may be much smaller than the population. Type records the attributes the paper’s utility uses: position, participation, and tolerance.

A type-symmetric default policy is a map \(d:\mathcal T\to\mathcal T\cup\{\bot\}\), where \(\bot\) means no default delegate. All members of a type use the same policy. Delegating to a type means selecting a member of that cohort; since its members share a deterministic participation status and the same type policy, identity within the cohort does not change the resolution. A delegation route that enters a cycle of never-voting types never reaches a caster and yields utility \(0\). If a type-\(t\) voter’s route first reaches a certain voter of type \(q\), their utility is \(\rho_t-|x_t-x_q|\); a voter who is certain to vote gets \(\rho_t\). The question is: given the type masses and attributes, find a type-symmetric pure Nash equilibrium—a policy \(d\) such that no individual can improve their utility by choosing a different initial delegate type or \(\bot\), with the other types’ policies fixed. A solution is such a policy, not a welfare-maximizing one. The associated aggregate welfare could be reported as \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t U_t(d)\), but welfare is not what the anchor computes.

This is a plausible large-platform regime: millions of users fall into a much smaller number of stable ideological, tolerance, and turnout classes, and users in a class follow the same default-delegation rule. The mirror preserves the paper’s central question—whether a default-delegation choice is a best response—while treating society as type masses. It is not a continuization of participation uncertainty: the anchor has \(p_t\in\{0,1\}\), and the continuous object is the population distribution.

The mirror is in Class A, tractable. For each never-voting type \(t\), scan the certain-voter types and choose the closest one with \(|x_t-x_q|\leq\rho_t\); choose \(\bot\) if none exists. Certain voters can choose \(\bot\). This is an equilibrium: an acceptable caster gives positive utility and the closest one is best; routing through another type cannot reach a better caster than the closest acceptable one. If no acceptable caster exists, any reached caster gives negative utility, while \(\bot\) or a route that never reaches a caster gives \(0\). With \(s\) support types and binary-rational inputs of bit length \(L\), the scan takes polynomial time in \(s\) and \(L\), for example \(O(s^2)\) rational comparisons. The mass values describe the society and its aggregate welfare; they do not affect these best responses, just as the paper’s individual utility depends on the voter’s representation rather than the amount of incoming voting weight.

This covers only the deterministic-participation branch of the paper. I would not stretch it to the paper’s cycle-structure or welfare results, nor claim a hardness result: the supplied paper does not establish a named computational hardness classification. Natural next questions are whether type-symmetric equilibria can be found when \(p_t\in(0,1)\), and how equilibrium computation behaves when agents of the same type may use different policies. The paper’s Theorem 2 already cautions that equilibrium existence is not automatic in its finite stochastic model.

The weak point is the aggregation semantics. The paper gives each named voter an individual delegation choice, while this mirror asks for symmetric type policies and has agents delegate to a type. The deterministic case makes within-type identity harmless, and the paper’s own assumptions already use position, participation probability, and tolerance to determine utility. Still, an author or referee could reject the type-level policy as too restrictive if large cohorts would in practice choose different delegates. The positive case holds for a platform where these are credible behavioral classes; it does not establish a mirror for the paper’s full individual-choice model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that its population masses do no computational work. A complete type here must include \(x\), \(p\), and \(\tau\). The paper assumes distinct positions, so in its stated instances no two voters share a complete type: the proposed high-multiplicity cohorts require relaxing that assumption. The paper says the assumption is there to avoid ties, so allowing co-located cohorts with a tie rule is a plausible repair. But after that repair, Theorem 1(i)’s construction still chooses each nonvoter’s closest acceptable certain voter independently. For fixed positive support, changing the rational masses does not change the equilibrium choices; masses only weight a welfare total reported afterward. The computation is a support scan, with no mass-transfer decision or continuous optimization.

A stronger mirror could retain stochastic participation and ask for type-symmetric equilibria, so cycles and uncertainty remain. That improves fidelity to the paper’s central model, but it still reduces to a finite delegation game on the type support: the masses affect aggregate welfare, while each type’s equilibrium incentives depend on the type attributes and delegation paths. If support is small, this may be useful high-multiplicity computation; if support grows with the electorate, the compression disappears. This makes the mirror’s payoff narrower than the proponent suggests, though it does not make the question incoherent.

I cannot honestly turn those limits into a decisive objection. Theorem 1(i) is a named polynomial-time equilibrium result, and a large platform with repeated ideological, tolerance, and turnout classes is plausible. Dropping the tie-avoidance assumption is a modest, author-recognizable change. The resulting mirror is narrow and computationally simple, but the programme permits such tractable mirrors. The negative case against this sole anchor is therefore weak: it questions its scientific payoff, but does not defeat the mirror.
