What the mirror covers

The proposed type-mass model would extend Theorem 3's axiomatic question; it does not supply a computational mirror of Theorem 3 or Theorem 4.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is for a dense wireless deployment with many interchangeable users: \(T\) is a finite set of complete channel and interference profiles, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users with profile \(t\), and the mechanism assigns each type a per-user power \(p_t\). With a per-capita power budget, feasibility is \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\le P\); type \(t\)’s SINR depends on its own allocation and interference generated by the mass-weighted allocations \((\mu_s p_s)_{s\in T}\). Users report SBU utility functions, and the mechanism returns a type-level allocation.

A precise extension question would be: given rational type masses and a finite representation of the type-specific interference functions and utility family, does there exist an allocation rule that is strategy-proof, Pareto-efficient, continuous in reported utilities, and gives type \(t\) zero power when its reported utility is zero? A positive result would construct such a rule; a negative result would prove that no such rule exists for the given model. This preserves the paper’s SINR coupling and its axioms, so the authors could recognize the connection. A plausible deployment has many devices but relatively few recurring channel profiles.

The paper’s closest named anchor is Theorem 3, proved here: an SCF in the paper’s SBU framework that is strategy-proof and efficient cannot be both continuous and satisfy non-participation. Theorem 4, also proved here, says that an SCF is implementable in Nash equilibrium if and only if it is constant, and gives the same characterization for dominant-strategy implementation. Both are relevant analytical results, but neither asserts a computational complexity result. The paper contains no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. So I cannot honestly offer either theorem as an anchor under ChoCo’s stated criterion, or claim a complexity direction for the extension.

That is the weakest point, and it is decisive for whether this is a mirror rather than a new research direction. The extension also needs a real modelling choice about incentives: if users are infinitesimal, an individual deviation may have no effect on the aggregate; making each type’s report a cohort decision restores strategic force but changes who acts. The continuum-user model is plausible, but without a qualifying computational anchor, this paper supplies motivation and axiomatic questions—not a computational result whose population-continuous form can be defended.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 3 is an impossibility statement about strategy-proofness, efficiency, continuity, and non-participation. Its continuity is continuity of an SCF as utility functions change; it does not make the population continuous. Theorem 4 characterizes which SCFs can be implemented in Nash or dominant strategies. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a computational problem. In both, “efficient” means Pareto-efficient, not polynomial-time.

A serious population model is easy to imagine: many wireless users fall into finitely many channel classes, with interference described by a type-to-type coupling kernel and power budget scaled per capita. But the proposed question—whether a rule over those classes can satisfy the paper’s axioms—is still an axiomatic existence question. Making it computational would require adding a finite input model for utilities and interference, then asking for an algorithm to synthesize or evaluate such a rule. The paper supplies neither that representation nor that algorithmic task. With arbitrary continuous utility and interference functions, the computational input is not even specified. A parametric version could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new computational problem motivated by this paper, not a continuous mirror of either theorem.

That defeats both anchors, including their strongest plausible population versions. It does not establish that wireless allocation has no worthwhile continuous computational model: the finite-class scenario is plausible, and I would not rely on a claim that wireless populations cannot be grouped into types. The narrower verdict is firmer: this paper is a poor ChoCo mirror candidate because its results are analytical and axiomatic, not computational.
