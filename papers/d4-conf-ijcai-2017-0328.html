The continuous mirror question

Given a finitely typed measured treeing \(\mathcal G\), type masses \(\mu_t\), valuation densities \(v_t\), a globally consistent edge mass-transport law, and one normalized cake copy per finite replica, compute a distribution \(\Lambda\) over finite-cut allocation patterns such that for \(\mathcal G\)-almost every directed edge \(i\to j\), \(V_{\operatorname{type}(i)}(A_i)\ge V_{\operatorname{type}(i)}(A_j)\); output an exact finite-cut certificate or an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate certificate whose support and cut complexity depend on the role and type description and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), not on the number of replicated agents.

The model it lives in

The population is represented by finitely many complete role-and-valuation types with masses \(\mu_t\), while a measured treeing records globally consistent network incidence. Decision variables are a distribution \(\Lambda\) over finite-cut local allocation patterns and its induced edge transport \(\eta\); the objective is to find an exact or \(\varepsilon\)-feasible envy-free allocation with a bounded normalized cut certificate.

The objection that survived

The opponent's strongest surviving point is that type-level edge couplings \(\eta\) do not by themselves guarantee a globally realizable labelled tree or simultaneous bundle assignment; the mirror must use a graphing or whole-neighborhood allocation measure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2 on trees. It leaves the proposed finite-type version of Theorem 4, Proposition 3, the Austin subroutine, and the paper's non-computational motivations without independent mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(\Lambda\) and \(\eta\) be represented with polynomial-size finite support for piecewise-constant \(v_t\), with dependence on \(\tau\), the role tree, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • What normalized cut bound replaces \(O(n^2)\) for repeated-tree populations, and can exact finite-cut allocations always be purified from the limit?
  • Which consistency and separation conditions on \(\eta\) make the type-level problem equivalent to finite tree realizations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s proportionality result on descendant graphs. The paper already has a divisible resource, but that is not the continuization. The continuization is replacing the list of agents by a distribution over finitely many complete agent/network types, while retaining the cake, local network, and fairness constraints.

A natural regime is a large balanced management hierarchy. Take a rooted \(b\)-ary tree of depth \(d\), with all agents at the same depth having the same valuation density and the same network role. The type of an agent includes her depth, valuation, number of children, and the distribution of types among her neighbours. There are \(n\approx b^d\) agents but only \(\tau=d+1\) types. More generally, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of agents of type \(t\), and let \(\kappa_{ts}\) be the limiting fraction of a type-\(t\) agent’s neighbours that have type \(s\). For a descendant graph, \(\kappa_{ts}\) is determined by the ancestor-descendant structure and the masses of the corresponding subtrees.

To make the continuum precise, let \(V_t(B)=\int_B v_t(x)\,dx\) be the value of a finite-cut cake piece \(B\) to type \(t\). A continuous allocation is not a fractional bundle given to one agent. It is a mass distribution \(\lambda_t\) over ordinary cake pieces: mass \(\mu_t\) of type-\(t\) agents each receives one piece drawn from \(\lambda_t/\mu_t\). The allocation must be realizable as the limit of finite partitions, with the usual per-capita normalization of the divisible resource. The solution also includes edge couplings \(\eta_{ts}\), describing which bundle types meet across type-\(t\), type-\(s\) network edges. Thus individual bundle assignments remain integral in every finite realization; only the multiplicities of identical agents and bundles are represented by mass.

My lead problem is:

HM-Descendant Proportionality. Given a finite rooted role tree, masses \(\mu\), valuation densities \(v_t\), and the induced neighbour laws \(\kappa\), find a realizable family \((\lambda,\eta)\) such that, for \(\lambda_t\)-almost every bundle \(B\), \(V_t(B)\) is at least the average value that this type-\(t\) agent assigns to the bundles of her neighbours: \(V_t(B)\ge \sum_s \kappa_{ts}\int V_t(B')\,d\eta_{ts}(B'\mid B)\). The computational version asks for a finite-cut, finite-support certificate, or an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate one, whose size is controlled by \(\tau\), the valuation description, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), rather than by the population size \(n\).

This mirrors the paper’s Theorem 4, proved in the paper. Its full content is: if \(G\) is an \(n\)-node descendant graph of a rooted tree \(T\) of depth \(d\), then Algorithm \(\AlgTreeLike(G)\) outputs an allocation proportional on \(G\), meaning \(v_i(A_i)\ge \frac{1}{|N(i)|}\sum_{j\in N(i)}v_i(A_j)\) for every agent \(i\), using at most \(n^2\cdot d!\) cuts. The algorithm uses \(f(v)=\left(\frac{d(v)+|T(v)|}{d(v)+1}\right)d!\) slices at vertex \(v\), distributes slices to descendants according to the \(f(v)/d(v)\) rule, and leaves \(d!\) slices for each agent.

The proposed problem keeps exactly the paper’s underlying question: agents have valuation functions, the network determines whose shares enter the fairness comparison, and the output is a cake allocation satisfying network proportionality. What changes is that agents with the same valuation and network role are represented by mass, and the descendant-neighbour averages become type-level edge averages. The paper’s recurrence for \(f(v)\) is already suggestive of this passage: in a large regular hierarchy, repeated descendants can be handled by their masses rather than enumerated individually.

I would expect HM-Descendant Proportionality to be Class A on regular, finitely typed hierarchies. The constraints are linear in allocation measures and edge couplings. For piecewise-constant valuations, the remaining pricing problem is a finite-dimensional vector-measure or configuration problem, approachable by LP, column generation, or convex separation. The main open questions are whether exact solutions admit polynomial-size finite-cut certificates, whether \(\varepsilon\)-solutions can be purified into finite allocations, and how much dependence on the depth \(d\) is unavoidable. The original \(n^2d!\) bound is not itself transferred; the continuous question is whether the \(n\)-dependence can collapse to dependence on the number of roles.

A second, weaker but still credible mirror uses the paper’s tree envy-freeness result.

HM-Tree Envy-Freeness. Given a measured rooted-tree network \((T,\mu,\kappa)\) and valuation types \(v_t\), find a realizable finite-cut allocation and edge coupling such that for every directed type-edge \(t\to s\), \(V_t(B)\ge V_t(B')\) for \(\eta_{ts}\)-almost every pair of bundles \((B,B')\) assigned to the two endpoints. This is the exact mass version of pairwise envy-freeness on the network, not merely a comparison of type-average utilities.

This mirrors Theorem 2, also proved in the paper. Its full content is: for any \(n\)-node tree \(T\) with root \(r\), Algorithm \(\AllocationTree(T,r)\) outputs an allocation that is envy-free on \(T\), meaning \(v_i(A_i)\ge v_i(A_j)\) for every edge \((i,j)\), and uses \(O(n^2)\) cuts. The algorithm first has the root divide the cake into \(n\) equal-valued pieces, then recursively uses Austin moving-knife divisions inside subtrees.

I expect HM-Tree Envy-Freeness to be tractable for role-regular trees such as balanced hierarchies, but less cleanly than the proportionality problem. Pairwise envy requires retaining the edge coupling \(\eta\), rather than only type averages. That is precisely why it is a meaningful computational mirror: the question is whether a large population of repeated tree roles can be handled by a compact transport/LP description while preserving every local comparison.

The authors should recognise both mirrors as their questions. The graph remains a network of agents; the same valuation comparisons are made; the action is still allocating ordinary cake pieces; and the cut bound remains part of the computational objective. The continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\), not the cake or the fairness axiom. The balanced-tree scenario is also a genuine high-multiplicity regime: there may be millions of agents at repeated hierarchy levels, while the number of distinct valuation/network types is logarithmic or even fixed.

The weak point is unavoidable. A literal atomless population cannot each receive a positive-measure, pairwise-disjoint piece of one fixed unit cake. The mirror therefore needs a standard per-capita or replicated-resource interpretation, together with a realizability requirement saying that the mass allocation is a limit of finite cake partitions. If a referee rejects that co-modification and insists on one fixed cake plus exact individual pairwise envy at continuum scale, the envy-free mirror becomes doubtful. The proportionality mirror survives better because its defining condition is already an average over neighbours, and regular descendant networks supply a particularly natural type-level limit.

The paper contains no named hardness result, so these are algorithmic anchors rather than hardness anchors. I would not claim that the mirror covers the whole paper: it covers Theorem 4 emphatically, and Theorem 2 on regular high-multiplicity tree families. The Austin procedure, Proposition 3 by itself, and the paper’s general motivational claims are not separate computational anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirrors is that the network is not merely an attribute of an agent type. It is the incidence structure that determines which individually allocated bundles must be compared. Replacing that structure by \(\mu\), neighbour-type frequencies \(\kappa\), and pairwise couplings \(\eta\) loses precisely the information used by both theorems.

For Theorem 4, the proposed \(\lambda_t\) is not an allocation of the cake. If a positive mass of type-\(t\) agents is assigned the same ordinary piece \(B\), the pieces overlap; if they receive disjoint pieces in one fixed cake, an atomless population has only null pieces, and integrable valuations give every agent value zero. Then proportionality becomes \(0\geq 0\), while the finite-cut objective disappears. Saying that the object is a limit of finite partitions does not fix this: weak limits need not preserve per-agent utilities or the pointwise inequalities
\[ V_i(A_i)\geq \frac{1}{|N(i)|}\sum_{j\in N(i)}V_i(A_j). \]

Per-capita cake supply or replicated cake can prevent that degeneration, but it changes the computational object. The paper allocates one common cake and counts its cuts. With replicated supply, the same cut pattern can be reused on independent copies, and the \(O(n^2d!)\) bound is no longer the theorem’s cut complexity. With a fixed cake and microscopic pieces, a nontrivial limit requires a two-scale allocation or Young measure recording local bundle geometry, not a distribution over ordinary pieces.

The network summaries are also insufficient. \(\kappa_{ts}\) specifies only the marginal types of neighbours. Even \(\eta_{ts}\) specifies only edge marginals. It does not guarantee that the edge couplings can be realized simultaneously by a graph with the required degrees, nor that the particular neighbours of each agent jointly satisfy the average constraint. A valid replacement needs a measure on entire rooted, bundle-labelled neighbourhoods satisfying global mass-transport and partition constraints. That object can retain essentially the whole graph and is not an LP over finitely many agent types.

This problem is especially sharp for descendant graphs. The paper’s construction depends on the vertex-specific quantities \(d(v)\) and \(|T(v)|\), through
\[ f(v)=\left(\frac{d(v)+|T(v)|}{d(v)+1}\right)d!. \]
In a growing balanced hierarchy, either depth grows and the number of relevant roles grows, or depth stays fixed and the root and internal roles have vanishing population mass while remaining neighbours of almost every leaf. A type distribution cannot assign bundles to those zero-mass ancestors, although their bundles still enter the leaves’ proportionality constraints. Retaining them as marked individuals produces a hybrid individual-plus-continuum model, not the proposed continuous society.

Theorem 2 has the same difficulty in a stronger form because envy-freeness is edgewise:
\[ V_i(A_i)\geq V_i(A_j)\qquad\text{for every edge }(i,j). \]
An edge distribution can certify an average property while hiding bad individual edges unless one retains a globally realizable labelled graph. Again, that is a graphing or local-allocation problem, not merely continuization of the population.

This is not an objection that cake is already divisible, nor an objection that the continuous answer might be easy. The objection is that the proposed continuum either ceases to be a cake allocation, weakens the paper’s pointwise network constraints, or reintroduces the entire network and microscopic partition as additional state.

The negative case is nevertheless not airtight. A periodic path, or many repeated copies of a fixed network component, gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime with repeated valuations and local roles. A carefully defined graphing and per-capita resource model could therefore yield a legitimate mirror of Theorem 2. I cannot honestly claim that every such construction is worthless. What can be defeated is the particular finite-type \((\mu,\kappa,\eta)\) mirror offered for Theorem 4, and the claim that it preserves the paper’s allocation and cut-complexity problem without substantial new structure.
