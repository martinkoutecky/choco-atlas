The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, proposition, or other named computational result classifying a problem as hard, tractable, or parameterized.

The closest results are Section 6’s numerical quota searches—for example, the reported best Nice thresholds of \(14/263/80\%\), with error rate \(0.2286\permil\), and Lisbon thresholds of \(17/77.5\%\), with error rate \(0.52118\permil\). Those are finite-case computational findings, not named complexity results to anchor a mirror.

A continuum of state types and some notion of pivotality could be formulated, but it would be an unanchored reformulation. In the paper, population sizes set the target power shares; the coalitions used to calculate Banzhaf power are coalitions of the Council’s 27 states. That makes a population-continuous mirror especially hard to defend as the continuous analogue of a named result here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no anchor, and that is the decisive problem for a mirror of this paper. Its main finding— that no tested quota adjustment brings the Nice or Lisbon rules close to Penrose’s target—is a numerical result for one fixed Council of 27 named states. The paper states no computational complexity result about a problem that could be continued. A quota search is computational work, but its output is not a complexity classification.

The strongest repair would generalize the search: represent councils by a distribution of state types, then ask how to choose quotas to minimize the gap between Banzhaf power and a target distribution. But this changes the setting the paper computes on. Its Banzhaf index treats each finite coalition of states as equally likely. With a continuum of states, an individual state has measure zero, so exact pivotality degenerates; a useful limit would need a new probability model and normalization. Keeping finitely many state types and applying a mass version instead creates a new high-multiplicity weighted-voting problem, not a continuous mirror of the paper’s result.

Continuizing citizens within each state is more natural as a limit, but those citizens are not the Council’s voters. That would study the assumptions behind Penrose’s target, rather than the paper’s computation of state-level Council power. So I would not put this paper forward as a ChoCo mirror: the better candidates either change the modeled society or require a new problem with no complexity result here to anchor it. That makes the case strong against mirroring this paper, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile study of a generalized continuum voting council.
