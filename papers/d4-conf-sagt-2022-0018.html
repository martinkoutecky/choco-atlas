What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper has no named result about whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, or another complexity class, so it cannot anchor a claim that discrete hardness disappears in a continuum. Its strongest computationally usable anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper: for a public project of cost \(C\), in the omnistic model, using the altruism-adjusted Clarke pivot rule with \(c_i=\alpha_i\), let \(N_p\) and \(N_n\) be the pivotal and non-pivotal players. The project is funded exactly when
\[ \sum_{i\in N_p}\left(C-\sum_{j\ne i}b_j\right) +\sum_{i\in N_n}\frac{\alpha_i}{1-\alpha_i} \left(\sum_{j\ne i}b_j-C\right)\ge C. \]
This is a named funding criterion, not a complexity theorem.

A plausible mirror is Omnistic Project Funding over Constituencies. An instance consists of a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), reported per-capita project values \(b_t\ge 0\), known altruism levels \(\alpha_t\in[0,1)\), and project cost \(C\). Each type \(t\) is a large, homogeneous constituency: its members share a value and altruism level, and an authorized representative submits a common report for the group. Its aggregate reported value is \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\). Treat each constituency as a player with valuation \(B_t\), and apply the paper’s omnistic public-project mechanism. Given \(\sum_t B_t\ge C\), the task is to output the payment vector and decide whether its total covers \(C\). In the formula above, substitute \(B_t\) for \(b_i\), and define pivotality by whether removing constituency \(t\)’s reported value takes the total below \(C\).

This is a high-multiplicity regime: there may be millions of residents, but relatively few distinct constituency types. Mass scales each group’s contribution to welfare, while the report captures its shared preferences and altruism. A municipality with homogeneous districts or a cooperative with large, aligned membership could fit the story. The mirror co-modifies the model by treating a constituency as the strategic unit; that is part of this proposal, not an attempt to claim the paper already studies a continuous population.

For this fixed mechanism, the mirror looks tractable: computing the weighted totals, pivotal set, payments, and funding test takes polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the rational input length \(L\). A natural follow-up is to find the least uniform altruism level that makes the project fundable. Proposition 5 reduces that to comparing the pivotal contributions and non-pivotal surplus, then solving a one-variable inequality.

The scope is just the public-project funding result, not the paper’s general characterization of truthful mechanisms, redistribution results, or auction examples. The main weakness is also clear: if the intended population consists of individually strategic residents, their pivotal effects vanish as their mass tends to zero. Constituency-level strategic reporting preserves the paper’s incentive question, but a referee might regard it as a model of representative blocs rather than a continuous version of individual-player mechanism design.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 5 is the only strong anchor, but its content turns on individual pivotality. In the paper, player \(i\) is pivotal when that one player’s report can change whether the project is funded. In a genuinely nonatomic population, an individual has zero mass: changing their report cannot change the aggregate value or the funding decision. The pivotal-player distinction that drives the proposition therefore disappears. The resulting individual truthfulness condition is vacuous, or the finite-player effect survives only at a vanishingly small threshold margin. The altruism payment terms also depend on the scale of the other players’ reports, so a population limit would need an additional normalization that changes the model being mirrored.

The constituency proposal preserves the formula by making each constituency a strategic player with value \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\). That is coherent as a weighted, finite-player mechanism, but it makes a group’s coordinated report the strategic action. Treating the group as one player does not preserve the paper’s individual incentive question. It also makes the result depend on how the population is partitioned into players: splitting or merging constituencies with identical per-capita values and altruism can change who is pivotal and the payment sum, even though the underlying distribution over types is unchanged. The constituency partition is thus extra strategic structure, not information supplied by the continuous society.

The proposed search for the least uniform altruism level has the same limitation. For constituency players, it is a threshold calculation using Proposition 5’s finite-player formula. For nonatomic individuals, the pivotality that gives the threshold its mechanism-design meaning has vanished. The paper has no named complexity result here; its funding proposition already gives a finite-player criterion, and the mirror does not expose a computational problem over a continuous population that the proposition’s model can support.

The strongest opening for the positive case is a setting where organized blocs really are the decision makers—for example, districts whose representatives report on their behalf. That could be a worthwhile weighted-bloc mechanism problem. The negative case is weaker if the authors would regard such collective principals as the intended agents: then the mirror may be sensible, though it is a finite strategic population carrying mass weights rather than a continuum of individually strategic residents.
