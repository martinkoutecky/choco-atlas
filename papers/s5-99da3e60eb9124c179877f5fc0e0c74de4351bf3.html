What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror captures the paper's additive-score, JR-constrained comment selection and its clustered price bound; the experiments and cited prior hardness results are not results proved by this paper's numbered theorems.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact optimization problem admit an algorithm parameterized by the number \(\gamma\) of cohesive population groups?
  • How do approximate masses or approval types inferred from sparse votes affect the JR guarantee and the score bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s constrained comment-selection problem: a platform chooses \(k\) comments to highlight, maximizes an additive score, and requires justified representation (JR). The population can be continuous while the feed remains a set of \(k\) comments; only the society is being continuized.

Anchor: Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For \(k>1\), if users are partitioned into \(\gamma\) cohesive groups, with \(1\leq\gamma<k\), then for every nonnegative additive scoring rule \(f\), the worst-case price of JR is at most \(k/(k-\gamma)\). The bound is tight for some scoring rules, including maximin diverse approval. Here the price is the best score achievable without JR divided by the best score achievable subject to JR. So the theorem says that when the population has a small number of cohesive groups relative to the feed size, imposing JR need cost little score.

The paper does not give a numbered theorem classifying this problem as NP-hard or tractable. It says in §6 that finding an optimal JR set is NP-hard in general, citing Bredereck et al. (2019) and Elkind et al. (2022); that hardness claim is not a numbered result of this paper. Theorem 2 is my named anchor because it is a proved quantitative result about the underlying optimization problem.

Here is a continuous version that preserves that problem. Let the comments be \([m]\), and let the population be nonatomic with total mass \(1\). A type \(t\) is a complete approval ballot \(A_t\subseteq[m]\), with rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). If the score uses user groups, group membership belongs in the type too. Given nonnegative rational comment scores \(w_i\), choose \(S\subseteq[m]\), \(|S|=k\), maximizing \(\sum_{i\in S}w_i\), subject to continuous JR: every measurable cohesive group of mass at least \(1/k\) must have positive mass represented in \(S\). A group is cohesive if almost all its members approve some common comment.

Equivalently, \(S\) satisfies continuous JR exactly when, for every comment \(j\),

\[ \sum_{t:\,j\in A_t,\ A_t\cap S=\varnothing}\mu_t < \frac{1}{k}. \]

If this mass were at least \(1/k\), those users would form a cohesive group around \(j\) with no represented member. Conversely, any unrepresented cohesive group is contained in such a set for one of its commonly approved comments. This gives a precise optimization problem over \(m,\tau,k\), and the bit length of the masses and scores.

A plausible regime is a large online civic discussion or consultation with many thousands of participants, a fixed catalog of comments, and a modest number of recurring approval patterns organized into a few cohesive viewpoint clusters. Then \(n\) can be much larger than the support size \(\tau\), and masses express the share of participants with each ballot. The assumption matters: if nearly every participant has a distinct approval pattern, this is a much weaker high-multiplicity case. Still, the paper’s own collective-response setting makes repeated cohorts of approval patterns credible. The authors should recognize the mirror: it keeps their score-maximizing feed selection and JR constraint intact, replacing voter counts with population masses.

Theorem 2’s covering argument carries over directly: choose one commonly approved comment from each of the \(\gamma\) groups, then use the remaining slots for high-scoring comments. The same bound \(k/(k-\gamma)\) follows. I expect the unrestricted exact optimization problem to have hardness that transfers: any finite approval profile with \(n\) users can be represented by masses \(n_t/n\), and the continuous JR threshold \(1/k\) matches the discrete threshold \(n/k\) after scaling. The output still has to choose \(k\) indivisible comments, so the agenda’s combinatorics remain. The theorem gives a strong score guarantee in the clustered regime; it does not itself give an efficient algorithm for finding the optimum.

That leaves useful follow-up questions: does small \(\gamma\) yield an exact or parameterized algorithm, can the relevant cohesive groups be found efficiently, and what changes when the masses are approximations inferred from sparse votes?

The weakest point is that the paper’s named result is a price bound, not a complexity classification, and its real-world experiments have only about 300 participants per session. The continuous scenario is a plausible extension of the authors’ problem, not a result established by their data. The case survives because the mirror is precise and Theorem 2’s structural argument transfers to masses without changing what is being optimized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 2 is not the kind of named computational result ChoCo asks analysts to mirror. It bounds the *price of imposing JR* by comparing two optimum scores; it gives no complexity classification, running time, or algorithmic approximation guarantee. The paper’s NP-hardness statement appears in §6 as a citation to prior work, not as a numbered result proved here. So Theorem 2 is a weak anchor for this programme’s computational questions.

The best continuous version is nevertheless quite clean. Give each complete approval ballot \(A_t\) a rational mass \(\mu_t\), keep the \(k\)-comment output, and require that for every comment \(j\),
\[ \sum_{t:\,j\in A_t,\;A_t\cap S=\varnothing}\mu_t < \frac{1}{k}. \]
That is continuous JR, and the paper’s clustered proof transfers directly. I don’t see a fundamental modeling defect: the users’ approval patterns are plausible types, and the population mass is meaningful.

The negative case is therefore weak. This mirror reduces to weighted JR-constrained selection of \(k\) comments, so Theorem 2 itself would yield little new work. But rational masses can encode populations whose common denominator is exponentially large in the input bit length; asking for algorithms in support size and bit length is a real high-multiplicity question. Under the programme’s rules, that is support for the mirror, not a reason to reject it. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor’s stronger formulation or sustain the claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
