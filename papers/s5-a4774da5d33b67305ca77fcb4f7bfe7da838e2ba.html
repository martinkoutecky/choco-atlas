The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: the paper has a very natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s strict rule. None of Theorems 1–12 asserts NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), fixed-parameter tractability, or gives a computational formulation. The paper’s remarks that Dodgson’s rule is difficult to compute are uncited prose, not named results in this paper.

The closest anchor is Theorem 10, proved in the paper. Its first assertion says that, for every \(m>k\geq1\), a monotonic scoring rule with scores \(s_1\geq\cdots\geq s_m\) satisfies the \((q,k,m)\)-majority criterion if and only if

\[ q\geq \frac{ s_1-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{m-i+1} }{ s_1-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{m-i+1} +\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_i-s_{k+1} }. \]

Its second assertion says that the same rule satisfies the majority-loser criterion if and only if

\[ s_1-\frac{s_2+\cdots+s_m}{m-1} \leq \frac{s_1+\cdots+s_{m-1}}{m-1}-s_m. \]

I would use the first assertion as the sole candidate anchor.

The continuous problem would be:

Continuous Majority-Power Threshold. Given \(m\), \(k<m\), and a monotonic scoring vector \(s\), let each type be a complete ranking \(\pi\in S_m\), and let \(\mu_\pi\) be its population mass. For \(B\subseteq C\), \(|B|=k\), define

\[ \rho_B(\mu)= \sum_{\pi:\,\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(k)\}=B}\mu_\pi. \]

The scoring rule has score

\[ S_\mu(c)=\sum_{\pi\in S_m}\mu_\pi s_{\operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)} \]

and winner set \(W_s(\mu)=\arg\max_c S_\mu(c)\). Compute the smallest quota

\[ q^*(m,k,s)= \inf\left\{ q: \rho_B(\mu)>q \Longrightarrow W_s(\mu)\subseteq B \text{ for every }\mu\text{ and every }B \right\}. \]

Equivalently, one may maximize \(\rho_B(\mu)\) over continuous societies in which some \(c\notin B\) is a scoring winner. The objective is to find the worst-case population mass that can support \(B\) while an outsider still wins.

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: millions of voters share a relatively small set of complete ballots, with \(m\) fixed or moderate and \(n\gg\) the number of occupied ranking types. Mass means fraction of the electorate; every individual still has an integral ranking. Rational \(\mu\) corresponds exactly to a finite election by taking \(n\mu_\pi\) voters of type \(\pi\).

The expected direction is Class A for this narrow problem. The adversarial problem is a linear program over ranking types, and Theorem 10 supplies the exact answer in closed form. For plurality, Theorem 1 gives the special case \(q^*=k/(k+1)\). The majority-loser clause of Theorem 10 suggests an analogous continuous veto-threshold problem.

The weakness is substantial: the paper already groups voters anonymously, works with voter shares, and proves bounds independent of \(n\). Thus this mirror may look like a reparametrization of the paper’s finite profile simplex rather than a new computational question. It also lacks the programme’s preferred complexity anchor. A more genuinely ChoCo extension would fractionalize Dodgson’s adjacent-swap score via mass transport and ask for its continuous majority-power threshold, but that would be a new conjectural problem rather than a computational result anchored in this paper.

So the positive verdict is: strong model-level plausibility, especially for high-multiplicity electorates; weak admissibility as a ChoCo computational mirror under the stated anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive against the proposed anchor, but not honestly against every possible problem one could build from the paper.

Theorem 10 is not a computational result. It is an axiomatic characterization of when a scoring rule satisfies a quota criterion. The proposed quantity \(q^*(m,k,s)\) is exactly the theorem’s quantity after writing a finite profile as \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/n\). Every rational society \(\mu\) is already a finite election, and the paper quantifies over arbitrary \(n\). Its extremal constructions are rational profiles of precisely this kind. Allowing irrational masses merely takes the closure of the same profile simplex; it does not introduce a new computational object.

This is therefore a collision in substance, not an instance where an outside theorem happens to answer a new question. The paper already works with anonymous groups, voter shares, and bounds independent of \(n\). The proposed continuous problem does not expose a missing algorithmic question: evaluating \(q^*(m,k,s)\) is just evaluating the displayed closed-form expression, while evaluating \(W_s(\mu)\) is aggregating scores over the occupied ranking types. With an explicit type table this takes time linear in the input table; with all \(m!\) rankings listed, the exponential size is already part of the input. There is no column-generation or pricing problem hidden here.

The majority-loser proposal does not rescue the case. The second assertion of Theorem 10 gives its scoring characterization, and Section 3.3 explicitly identifies veto power with the corresponding majority criterion for \(k=m-l\). Its continuous version is the same normalized-profile reformulation.

Changing the type space also does not help. Complete rankings are already the relevant types, and the criterion depends only on aggregate positional scores and top-\(k\) membership. Adding names, prices, budgets, or other individual data would be irrelevant unless one also introduced an intervention problem; that would be a new model, not a mirror of this paper’s theorem. Likewise, asking for an LP over ranking masses would only re-express a quantity that Theorem 10 has already eliminated in closed form.

The strongest possible rescue is the Dodgson direction mentioned by the proponent: define a continuous mass-transfer problem with cost

\[ \min_x \sum_{\pi,\pi'} d_K(\pi,\pi')x_{\pi,\pi'} \]

subject to the post-transfer society making a target candidate a Condorcet winner. This is genuinely meaningful: rankings are legitimate high-multiplicity types, mass is the right quantity, and the cost does not collapse. It could be a worthwhile ChoCo problem.

But it is not anchored by a computational result in this paper. Theorem 8 supplies only sufficient and failure bounds for Dodgson’s quota criterion; it does not formulate an algorithmic problem or establish complexity. The mass-transfer formulation would be a new swap-bribery problem inspired by the definition of Dodgson’s score, not a continuous mirror of a computational theorem here.

Thus the strict negative verdict is strong: the proponent’s Theorem 10 mirror is already the paper’s normalized finite-profile analysis and contributes no computational landscape. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem can be extracted is weaker than that verdict, because the Dodgson-based extension is a real counterexample if the programme permits new algorithmic problems motivated by non-computational results.
