The continuous mirror question

Given \(g\) divisible goods with market values \(m_a>0\), DPR parameters \(K>1\) and \(q\), and a finite support \(T\) of claimant types \(t=(r_{t1},\ldots,r_{tg},ω_t)\) with rational masses \(μ_t>0\), \(∑_tμ_t=1\), and \(∑_tμ_tω_t=1\), set \(\hat u_{ta}=K^{r_{ta}-q-1}m_a\). Choose \(y_{ta}≥0\), the aggregate fraction of good \(a\) allocated to type \(t\), subject to \(∑_t y_{ta}=1\) for each good. Return a feasible allocation maximizing \(\min_{t∈T}\frac{\sum_a\hat u_{ta}y_{ta}}{\mu_t\omega_t\sum_a\hat u_{ta}}\). In a finite realization with \(Nμ_t\) claimants of type \(t\), each receives \(y_{ta}/(Nμ_t)\) of good \(a\), recovering the paper’s normalized egalitarian objective.

The model it lives in

A linear program over aggregate allocations \(y_{ta}\) to type masses, with one resource constraint per good and a max-min objective over types. The finite allocation is recovered by dividing each type’s aggregate share by its claimant count.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the egalitarian DPR allocation LP and Proposition 1’s sparsity guarantee interpreted across types. It leaves aside the proportional-market-value counterexamples, the Nash/Competitive solution, and the robustness simulations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-mass LP’s optimal basic solution always allocate at most τ-1 goods to multiple types, as the paper’s argument suggests?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s egalitarian allocation problem. The paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity class such as P or NP-hardness. Its closest computational anchor is Proposition 1, attributed to Wilson’s unpublished 1998 preprint, for which this paper supplies a proof: *an optimal basic solution of the paper’s egalitarian linear program has at most \(n-1\) split goods.* That is a structural result about the allocation the computation returns, not a complexity classification.

Lead problem: Type-Egalitarian DPR Allocation. Consider a wind-down of a large employee-owned company or cooperative, with many claimants dividing a fixed portfolio of divisible assets. A claimant’s type \(t\) records their rating of every asset and their entitlement; people with the same ratings and entitlement are indistinguishable for this problem. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of claimants of type \(t\), with \(\tau\) supported types among \(N\) claimants. For a small number \(g\) of asset classes, the paper’s finite rating scale makes repeated profiles plausible: the support can be far smaller than the claimant count.

For each type \(t\) and good \(a\), let \(x_{ta}\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of that good assigned to each claimant of type \(t\). With one unit of each good, feasibility requires
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_{ta}=\frac{1}{N}\qquad\text{for every }a. \]
Let \(\hat u_{ta}=K^{r_{ta}-q-1}m_a\) be the paper’s DPR utility, and let \(\omega_t/N\) be each type member’s entitlement, normalized so \(\sum_t\mu_t\omega_t=1\). The question is to find a feasible type-level allocation maximizing the least normalized utility per entitlement:
\[ \max_x\ \min_t \frac{\sum_a \hat u_{ta}x_{ta}} {(\sum_a\hat u_{ta})(\omega_t/N)}. \]
A solution is the exact allocation vector \(x\) and its max-min value. Repeating \(x_t\) for the \(N\mu_t\) members of each type gives a finite allocation with the same fairness criterion. Inputs can use binary-rational masses, entitlements, market values and rational \(K\).

This preserves the paper’s underlying question: allocate the goods to maximize the least normalized utility under its DPR model. The population itself is now represented by a distribution over complete claimant types; fractional allocation variables were already in the paper and are not the continuization. A referee could reasonably recognize this as the same fairness problem for a mass claimant society. I expect this version to be tractable: it is a linear program with size polynomial in \(g\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. For identical members within each type, averaging their allocations preserves feasibility and utility, so the type representation should give an exact compression of the finite LP.

Proposition 1 is the sole named anchor I would use. Restated for this setting, its natural type-level counterpart would bound the number of goods shared across types by \(\tau-1\), by replacing the \(n\) agent constraints in the paper’s basic-solution argument with \(\tau\) type constraints. That does not imply at most \(\tau-1\) goods are shared among actual claimants: members of one type may themselves receive fractions of a good. Establishing the precise support statement is a follow-up question, not something I would claim Proposition 1 already proves.

The mirror covers the paper’s egalitarian DPR allocation and its sparse-solution observation. It does not cover the proportional-market-value counterexamples, the Nash/Competitive solution, or the robustness simulations. The paper’s unnumbered LP formulation is the direct computational basis for the tractability prediction; the proposition supplies a named structural result, not a P-time theorem.

The soft spot is the regime: the paper’s concrete legal examples involve only a few parties, and identical ratings across every asset plus identical entitlements may be a demanding assumption in real disputes. Large cooperative or company wind-downs with a modest set of asset classes and recurring claimant profiles are plausible, but not demonstrated by this paper. The other limitation is that the finite problem is already an LP, so this is a support-size compression result rather than a case where population continuization dissolves hardness. That makes it a modest Class A mirror, not a claim that the paper contains a hidden hardness transition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result about computational complexity: no running-time bound, hardness result, or algorithmic classification. Proposition 1 is the closest anchor, but it is an LP sparsity lemma: a basic optimal allocation shares at most \(n-1\) goods. The paper gives no complexity landscape around that claim.

The proposed high-multiplicity model is coherent, but after a simple rescaling it is the same LP with duplicate agents compressed. If \(x_{ta}\) is each type member’s share and \(y_{ta}=Nx_{ta}\), feasibility becomes \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{ta}=1\), and the objective is \(\min_t \frac{\sum_a u_{ta}y_{ta}}{\omega_t\sum_a u_{ta}}\). Identical members can be averaged without changing feasibility or their utility. So the continuous formulation is an exact compact encoding of the corresponding finite instance. That is a valid dictionary, but it leaves no additional pricing, optimization, or complexity issue: the formulation is already an LP with \(O(g\tau)\) variables.

More seriously, the proposed type-level version of Proposition 1 does not preserve what the paper calls a “split item.” It bounds goods shared across *types*. With one type, that bound is zero; yet if many identical claimants divide even one good, the good is split among those claimants. The type-level result therefore loses the paper’s practical concern about how many assets end up with multiple co-owners. A cohort-as-one-recipient interpretation could restore the bound, but then the problem allocates to collectives and leaves the internal division among their members unresolved.

The best scenario is still plausible: a large cooperative liquidation with many equal-stake shareholders and a modest number of recurring rating profiles. I cannot rule out a mirror the authors would recognize there. But its named anchor reduces either to an immediate LP basis fact over types or to a bound about co-ownership that no longer matches Proposition 1’s operational meaning. The negative case is therefore modest, not airtight: this looks like a legitimate high-multiplicity reformulation, but a thin one, rather than a compelling new computational problem.
