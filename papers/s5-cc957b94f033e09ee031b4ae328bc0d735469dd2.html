The continuous mirror question

Given a finite school set \(S\), rational mass capacities \(q_s\), a finite set of preference types \(T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type ranks \(S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), find \(x_{t,s}\ge 0\) respecting acceptability, \(\sum_s x_{t,s}\le \mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\), maximizing \(M(x)=\sum_{t,s}x_{t,s}\); among maximizers, return one with no feasible positive-mass reassignment that weakly improves every unit and strictly improves a positive mass.

The model it lives in

A finite-type school-allocation model with population masses \(\mu_t\), capacities \(q_s\), and mass variables \(x_{t,s}\). Maximize assigned mass by a transportation LP, then select a Pareto-efficient maximizer by lexicographic or secondary linear optimization; each unit of mass still receives one school or \(\emptyset\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's maximum-cardinality and Pareto-efficiency conclusion for nonstrategic assignments. It leaves the FAM fairness result, equilibrium analysis, stable-matching comparisons, and truthful-agent comparative statics untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity school-allocation mirror, although there is an important qualification: this paper contains no named result asserting NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), FPT, W[1]-hardness, or another complexity classification. The only NP-hardness statement is cited from earlier work on stable matchings with ties, not proved as a numbered result here. Thus the paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor in the strict sense of the assignment.

The closest usable anchor is the paper’s own Theorem 1, proved here:

Every EAM mechanism is maximal and efficient.

Here “maximal” means maximum cardinality in the paper’s terminology: no individually rational matching assigns more students. EAM first lexicographically preserves assignment possibilities for students in a fixed order, thereby obtaining a maximum-cardinality individually rational matching. It then repeatedly applies improving chains and cycles. The theorem says that every resulting mechanism remains maximum-cardinality and is Pareto efficient.

I would mirror that result with the following problem.

Continuous Maximum-Efficient Assignment. An instance consists of a finite school set \(S\), rational capacities \(q_s\), a finite type set \(T\), and a rational mass distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students of type \(t\). A type specifies a complete ranking of \(S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), hence an acceptable set \(Ac(t)\). If the market retains priority information, it is included as a finite priority class in the type; it is not needed for the EAM objective itself.

A solution is a mass-assignment matrix \(x=(x_{t,s})\), where \(x_{t,s}\) is the mass of type \(t\) sent to school \(s\). It must satisfy

\[ x_{t,s}\ge 0,\qquad x_{t,s}=0\ \text{if }s\notin Ac(t), \]

\[ \sum_{s\in S}x_{t,s}\le \mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,s}\le q_s \quad\text{for every }s. \]

The primary objective is to maximize assigned mass,

\[ M(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{s\in S}x_{t,s}. \]

Among assignments achieving maximum \(M(x)\), the output must be Pareto efficient: there must be no feasible reassignment in which every unit of student mass receives a weakly preferred school and a positive-measure set receives a strictly preferred school. Each unit of mass still receives one integral outcome—one school or \(\emptyset\); only the aggregate population is continuous.

This is a credible mirror rather than a relaxation that changes the problem beyond recognition. The regime is a large school district or centralized university-placement market with many students in recurring cohorts: students share the same preference ranking, acceptable schools, and relevant priority attributes. The number of students \(N\) is large while the number of distinct types \(\tau=|T|\) is fixed or moderate. Capacities scale with the population, and \(\mu_t\) records cohort proportions. This is precisely the high-multiplicity interpretation of the paper’s finite assignment problem.

I would expect this continuous problem to be Class A. The maximum-mass part is a transportation or bipartite-flow LP. Pareto efficiency can be imposed by a secondary LP maximizing a strictly preference-increasing utility subject to \(M(x)=M^\star\), or by lexicographic optimization. Its running time is polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|S|\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\) and \(q\). It also has a natural downward bridge: rational masses can be rounded or cleared to obtain high-multiplicity finite instances, while the continuous optimum gives a useful benchmark for large markets.

The paper’s authors should recognize this as their assignment-maximization problem: the objective remains maximum cardinality, individual rationality remains the feasibility condition, and efficiency remains the Pareto refinement. What changes is only that interchangeable students are represented by mass instead of by names.

The weakest point is that Theorem 1 is not actually a complexity theorem, and the exact EAM procedure depends on an ordering of named students. There is no canonical analogue of that order inside a homogeneous continuum. The proposed mirror therefore captures the theorem’s underlying maximum-and-efficiency problem, not every strategic or procedural detail of EAM. The paper’s equilibrium results, especially Theorems 3 and 4, would require a separate model of type-level reporting and are substantially less convincingly continuizable.

So my positive case is real but limited: the paper has a strong, natural Class A continuous assignment problem, anchored most closely by Theorem 1, but it does not provide a qualifying named computational result under the assignment’s strict anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive under the assignment’s own anchor rule: this paper contains no qualifying computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 says that every EAM mechanism is maximal and efficient, but it does not classify the complexity of a problem. The NP-hardness mentioned in the related literature concerns a different stable-matching problem and is neither proved here nor the object of the paper’s results. Thus the proposed mirror is not anchored to a named complexity theorem.

There is also a substantive mismatch. EAM is a mechanism on profiles of named students. Its first stage depends on an enumeration of those students, and its second stage permits arbitrary choices among improving chains and cycles. Theorem 1 asserts that every such procedural choice preserves maximum cardinality and efficiency. The proposed continuous problem instead discards the mechanism, the enumeration, and the reporting environment, and asks for any maximum-mass assignment followed by a Pareto refinement. That is a sensible optimization problem, but it is not the continuous counterpart of “every EAM mechanism is maximal and efficient”; it is the high-multiplicity transportation problem underlying one consequence of that theorem.

A faithful continuization exposes the problem. If the EAM enumeration is part of the type, then every position in the enumeration is effectively an individual-specific type, destroying the finite-type high-multiplicity regime. If the enumeration is suppressed, the EAM rule is no longer defined. Replacing it with an order over preference types produces a new mechanism, not a continuization of the paper’s mechanism. The same issue appears in improving chains: at the aggregate level one can define mass transfers, but which named student is displaced is no longer represented.

The strategic results cannot repair this. In an atomless population, one student’s report has measure zero and cannot affect the aggregate allocation, so the individual reporting game that drives the paper’s equilibrium analysis becomes vacuous. Making an entire type or cohort strategic gives that block positive influence, but changes the players and the game. Keeping named individuals instead abandons the continuum.

That said, this does not establish the stronger universal claim that no worthwhile continuous assignment problem exists. A large school district with recurring preference and priority cohorts is a perfectly credible high-multiplicity regime, and maximum acceptable assignment with a Pareto refinement is a legitimate continuous population model. Its max-flow formulation being straightforward is not, by itself, an objection. The honest negative conclusion is narrower: the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and the proposed mirror is a new high-multiplicity assignment problem rather than a faithful mirror of Theorem 1. The universal “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” case is therefore weak.
