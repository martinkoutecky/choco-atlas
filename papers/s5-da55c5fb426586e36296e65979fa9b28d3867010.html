The atomless service-population model is a coherent extension of costly inclusion. But the proposed anchors are axiomatic existence characterizations, not algorithms, complexity results, or computationally used formulations. The atomless setting also changes individual influence over aggregate cost, so the finite exclusion-pivot payments need not carry over.

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind the anchor rule requires. Its main results characterize when desirable mechanisms exist; they do not classify a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise. The closest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in this paper, but they are axiomatic characterizations rather than computational anchors.

Theorem 1 says that, for a fixed costly-inclusion environment, these are equivalent: some mechanism is production-efficient, strategy-proof, no-deficit, and voluntarily participatory; every exclusion pivot mechanism has those properties; and the cost function satisfies inclusion cost coverage. The latter means every nonempty finite-cost group remains feasible after removing any member, and the sum of its members’ inclusion costs is at least the group’s cost.

Theorem 2 says that some mechanism is production-efficient, strategy-proof, and envy-free if and only if every exclusion pivot mechanism has those properties, if and only if the cost function is convex and symmetric. Both theorems concern mechanism existence, not computational complexity. The paper’s cited Theorem H is also a mechanism characterization, attributed to Holmström (1979), not a complexity result.

The strongest positive extension I can make is to mirror Theorem 1 in a large membership service. Let the population be atomless with total mass \(1\), and let each person’s type be their willingness to pay for an indivisible service. The distribution has support \(T\), with rational type masses \(\mu_t\); the number of people is large while \(|T|=\tau\) is modest. Serving mass \(q\) costs \(C(q)\), independent of which people are served. The allocation is an individual service decision \(x(a)\in\{0,1\}\), with selected mass \(q=\int x(a)\,da\). Thus a type can have a fraction of its mass served even though each person receives an integral bundle.

Here is the proposed Continuous Costly-Inclusion Recognition problem: given a rational piecewise-linear cost curve \(C:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), with \(C(0)=0\), determine whether there is a direct mechanism for all reported type distributions that is production-efficient, strategy-proof, no-deficit, and voluntarily participatory. A solution outputs the mechanism and its allocation and payment rule; a negative answer supplies a cost-curve witness. Writing \(p(a)\) for the charge to person \(a\), efficiency maximizes \(\int v(a)x(a)\,da-C(q)\), no-deficit requires \(\int p(a)\,da\ge C(q)\), and voluntary participation requires \(v(a)x(a)-p(a)\ge0\). This is a natural symmetric-cost limit of the paper’s group-selection problem. The paper’s Proposition 3, also proved here, supports the connection: under symmetry, inclusion cost coverage is equivalent to non-decreasing average costs. In the smooth continuous version, the corresponding condition is that \(C(q)/q\) is non-decreasing, or \(qC'(q)\ge C(q)\).

My lead is this Theorem 1 mirror. For the stated piecewise-linear input, checking the average-cost condition and finding a welfare-maximizing \(q\) look tractable: both can be reduced to checking the finitely many cost breakpoints and type-mass cutoffs. I would expect a Class A result for this restricted model, with a threshold or pivotal-price rule as the mechanism. A related question mirrors Theorem 2: in the same model, does an efficient, strategy-proof, envy-free mechanism exist? The continuous prediction is yes exactly when \(C\) is convex, with symmetry already built into the scalar cost model; the expected algorithmic direction is again tractable. These are proposed continuous questions, not results established by the paper.

The mirror covers only the paper’s symmetric-cost slice. A natural next question is whether a type-dependent cost function \(C(z)\), where \(z_t\) is selected mass of type \(t\), admits an analogous coverage condition. Another is how the atomless mechanism relates to finite pivotal transfers.

The weak point is substantial: in an atomless population, one person’s report does not change aggregate mass or cost. The continuous mechanism therefore relies on individual threshold incentives and marginal prices, while the finite paper’s exclusion pivot payments capture nonzero individual externalities. That difference may change the characterization. More fundamentally, since the paper supplies no computational anchor, even a convincing continuum limit would be a new computational question built from its mechanism results, not a continuous mirror of a complexity result the authors actually proved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper is that it has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize when mechanisms with specified axioms exist for a fixed finite-agent environment. They give no input encoding, algorithm, or complexity bound. So the proposed mirrors are new computational questions built from axiomatic results, not continuous mirrors of computational results in the paper. That is a serious mismatch with ChoCo’s stated aim.

The best version of the Theorem 1 mirror is nevertheless coherent. In a homogeneous service population with mass-dependent cost \(c(q)\), the finite inclusion-cost condition has a natural limit: at smooth points, it becomes \(q c'(q)\ge c(q)\), the condition that average cost is non-decreasing. This is a sensible atomless model, with a plausible mass-transfer interpretation. I cannot honestly reject it by appealing to granularity or saying the finite theorem would no longer hold. My objection is narrower: the paper does not establish this continuous condition, and turning mechanism existence into a decision problem over encoded cost curves is an additional research choice. The proposed input is the cost technology; the society’s distribution is not itself the instance being computed on.

The Theorem 2 mirror has a similar status. With scalar cost \(c(q)\), symmetry is built in, leaving convexity as the substantive condition. That is a reasonable continuum translation, but again it is an axiomatic existence question, not a complexity result from the paper. Restoring the full force of the finite theorem would require costs that depend on the masses of multiple types. That may be a worthwhile model, but the paper supplies no canonical representation of such a cost function or computational task.

There is no honest objection that high multiplicity is nonsensical here. A large service population with repeated willingness-to-pay types is a plausible regime, and type-level mass can represent people while each person still receives an indivisible service. Nor is a simple or positive continuous answer grounds for rejection. The limit conditions may be worth proving and could become useful computational work once a concrete input and output task is chosen.

So the negative case is strong on programme fit but weak as a universal modelling claim. These two anchors do not make the paper a direct source of ChoCo-style complexity results. But the homogeneous-service mirrors are plausible continuous questions, and I cannot substantiate the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
