What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the \(\randdet\), \(\maxavg\) upper and lower bounds in Theorems 3.1 and 3.7; it leaves the other objectives, mechanism classes, centralized bounds, and Euclidean bounds unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it does not have a qualifying ChoCo computational anchor in the strict sense. Its numbered results are distortion bounds, not NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, FPT, or running-time theorems. So the mirror is mathematically credible, but it cannot honestly be presented as a completed continuous-complexity result from this paper.

The best non-qualifying anchor is Theorem 3.7, proved in the paper. It states that, for the \(\maxavg\) objective, every \(\randdet\) mechanism has distortion at least \(5\), even on a line metric. The construction has four candidates, four voters, and two groups of two voters. Using the Bias Tournament of the deterministic in-group rule, one chooses a candidate \(c_1\) with two incoming edges, say from \(c_2\) and \(c_3\). The two groups are arranged so that their representatives are \(c_2\) and \(c_3\). The metric is a line with \(c_2,c_1,c_3\) at \(-1,0,1\), voters at \(-\tfrac12,0,0,\tfrac12\), and \(c_4\) placed according to its position in the auxiliary ordering. The resulting instance has \(\maxavg(c_1)=\tfrac14\), while \(\maxavg(c_2)=\maxavg(c_3)=\tfrac54\). Since the randomized second stage can select only among representatives, its distortion is at least \(5\).

A precise continuous mirror would be:

\[ \textsf{Continuous-}\randdet\textsf{-}\maxavg\textsf{-Mechanism Design}. \]

An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(C\), a finite set of groups \(G\), and a finite universe \(T\) of complete voter types. A type \(t\in T\) includes both a strict ranking \(\pi_t\) of the candidates and a cardinal metric description \(d_t(c)\) for every candidate \(c\); two agents of the same type therefore have identical preferences and identical costs. Each group \(g\) is given a rational distribution \(\mu_g\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_g(t)\) is the fraction of that group having type \(t\). The distributions must be jointly realizable by one metric space. Group masses may also be supplied if the second-stage rule uses group sizes.

For a candidate \(c\), define the group-average cost

\[ A_g(c)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_g(t)d_t(c), \]

and define

\[ \operatorname{MAXAVG}_{\mu}(c)=\max_{g\in G} A_g(c). \]

A deterministic mass-based in-group rule \(F_{\mathrm{in}}\) maps the observable distribution of rankings in each group to a representative \(r_g\in C\). A randomized over-group rule \(F_{\mathrm{out}}\), seeing only the representatives and their observable rankings, returns a probability distribution \(p\) supported on \(R=\{r_g:g\in G\}\). The solution for a given society is the representative tuple \((r_g)_{g\in G}\) together with \(p\), minimizing

\[ \sum_{c\in R}p(c)\operatorname{MAXAVG}_{\mu}(c). \]

The mechanism-design version asks for mass-based rules minimizing the worst-case ratio

\[ \sup_{\mu,d} \frac{\sum_{c}p(c)\operatorname{MAXAVG}_{\mu}(c)} {\min_{c'\in C}\operatorname{MAXAVG}_{\mu}(c')}. \]

This is a genuine population continuization. A high-multiplicity realization has two large constituencies, each containing many copies of two stable voter types, with \(n\gg |T|\): for example, each group can have mass \(\tfrac12\) on each of two repeated types, so \(n=4N\) while \(|T|=4\). The scenario is plausible for large jurisdictions containing repeated demographic or geographic blocs with identical candidate distances. The paper’s finite voters are replaced by masses; candidates, metric costs, groups, and the two-stage representative constraint remain intact. The random output is not the continuization—it is already part of the paper’s model.

I would expect the lower-bound obstruction in Theorem 3.7 to transfer. For any continuous deterministic in-group rule, one can define the same Bias Tournament using half-mass pairwise profiles. A vertex of indegree at least \(2\) again supplies two groups whose representatives are \(c_2\) and \(c_3\). The same four-type line metric then gives ratio \(5\). Thus the likely continuous answer is not that high multiplicity dissolves this obstruction: within the exact \(\randdet\) model, the lower bound should remain \(5\).

The paper also gives the upper-side clue in Theorem 3.1, proved here: if the in-group rule has distortion at most \(\alpha\), then combining it with a randomized over-group rule gives \(\maxavg\) distortion at most \(\alpha+2\). Corollary 3.2 instantiates this with the cited distortion-\(3\) Plurality Matching rule and obtains \(5\). If Plurality Matching has a well-defined anonymous mass-based extension retaining distortion \(3\), the continuous problem would plausibly have optimum distortion exactly \(5\). Establishing that extension would be new work, not a theorem of this paper.

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 3.7’s lower bound already uses only four voters, so the continuum is not what drives the paper’s argument. The mirror mainly tests whether the two-stage metric-distortion problem remains meaningful under high multiplicity; it does not yet reveal a new complexity phenomenon. Moreover, the paper permits arbitrary finite-profile rules, whereas the continuous mirror must impose anonymity and mass-based behaviour. Finally, because the paper contains no named computational-complexity result, this is a credible continuous analogue of a distortion problem, but not a fully compliant ChoCo anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is not that a mass-version is incoherent. It is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Its numbered theorems establish distortion bounds over voting mechanisms and metric instances; none states NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, an approximation algorithm, or a parameterized result. The paper has no computational input-output problem whose population axis can be replaced by a distribution.

Theorem 3.7 is therefore a non-qualifying anchor. Its four-voter construction can certainly be lifted to high multiplicity: replace each voter by \(N\) identical copies, or represent each group by a measure assigning mass \(1/2\) to each of two types. The \(\maxavg\) values and the ratio \(5\) are unchanged. This shows that the high-multiplicity regime is sensible, but it does not produce a new computational object. The lower bound is already a constant-support metric obstruction; \(N\) plays no role in the theorem’s combinatorics, and the paper does not ask one to compute anything from the resulting distribution.

The proponent’s stronger version—optimizing over all mass-based in-group and over-group rules—does not repair this. It changes the problem from analysing a distributed voting mechanism to synthesizing an optimal mechanism. With unrestricted rules, the “algorithm” is a mapping over all possible profiles, not a finite encoded object. With a fixed finite type space, one obtains a new finite mechanism-design problem whose representation, metric-consistency constraints, and rule class must be chosen from scratch. That may be a worthwhile ChoCo project, but it is no longer a continuous mirror of Theorem 3.7; it is an additional research problem invented around it.

Theorem 3.1 has the same defect. Its statement is a conditional inequality: an in-group rule of distortion at most \(\alpha\), followed by random representative selection, has distortion at most \(\alpha+2\). Replacing group averages by expectations gives the immediate mass analogue

\[ A_g(c)=\sum_t \mu_g(t)d_t(c), \qquad \operatorname{MAXAVG}_{\mu}(c)=\max_g A_g(c), \]

but this merely rewrites the proof with sums replaced by weighted sums. Extending Plurality Matching to repeated types might yield a capacitated matching computation, which is a perfectly natural implementation detail. It is not a complexity theorem about the problem studied in the paper, nor does it expose a population-level optimization such as mass transfer, bribery, control, or pricing.

The paper’s other results do not rescue the case. Theorems 3.6, 3.8, 4.6–4.9, 5.1–5.4, and 6.1–6.2 are likewise geometric distortion bounds. Their continuous counterparts would ask whether the same worst-case ratios hold for distributions, not whether a computational problem over those distributions is tractable or hard. A continuous society is present as an admissible analytic input, but there is no computational role for its mass beyond evaluating averages and maxima.

The negative case should not pretend that multiplicity is impossible here. Voters with identical rankings and metric costs can plausibly occur in large constituencies, and the distortion objectives do not depend on individual identities. Nor is this an “already continuous” collision, and high multiplicity would not be a defect. The problem is narrower: after continuization, the paper still offers only a worst-case mechanism-analysis question. Any genuinely computational version requires adding a new mechanism-synthesis, rule-evaluation, or robust-optimization layer.

Thus the strongest defensible verdict is negative for ChoCo, not because the proposed distributions are meaningless, but because neither Theorem 3.7 nor Theorem 3.1 anchors the programme’s computational agenda. The universal claim that no interesting mass-based research could ever be built around this paper is too strong; the proponent is right that a coherent high-multiplicity distortion study could be designed. But it would be a new project, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of the paper’s actual results.
