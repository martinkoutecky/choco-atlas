What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror draws on the individual-immunity characterization in Theorem 3 and the group-responsibility results in Theorems 6 and 7; it leaves the paper’s other characterizations and examples aside. None of these results is computational.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is the paper’s treatment of *how much* responsibility a group assigns. Theorem 6, proved in the paper, says that for a majoritarian criteria-based procedure and a decision \(d\), immunity to majoritarian responsibility voids is equivalent to immunity to individual responsibility voids. Theorem 7 in Appendix C, also proved here, generalizes this: for a group of \(n\) agents and an integer \(m\) such that \(T(c)\le m\) and \(n-T(c)\le m\) for every criterion \(c\), immunity to \(m\)-responsibility voids with respect to \(d\) holds exactly when \(\mathbb R_d\subseteq\mathbb F_d\). Theorem 3, proved in Appendix B, characterizes individual immunity: it holds exactly when every opinion that maximally avoids \(d\)—equivalently, every opinion in \(\mathbb R_d\)—does not support \(d\).

There is a strict qualification: none of these is a computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. They characterize logical properties of finite procedures; they do not establish that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classify its complexity. So the paper has no eligible computational anchor. The results above are the nearest formal footholds for a follow-up, rather than compliant anchors.

That follow-up could be Continuous Majoritarian Responsibility-Void Existence. An instance is a finite criteria set \(C\), a decision set \(D\), a surjective decision function \(f:\{0,1\}^C\to D\) given by its truth table, and a target decision \(d\). A candidate society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ballot types \(O\in\{0,1\}^C\), with \(\mu_O\) the fraction of society casting ballot \(O\). Its group opinion is \(\bar O_\mu(c)=1\) exactly when \(\sum_{O:O(c)=1}\mu_O\ge 1/2\), and its decision is \(f(\bar O_\mu)\).

Use the paper’s set \(\mathbb R_d\) to retain its responsibility criterion: ballots in \(\mathbb R_d\) give the right avoiding value to each criterion that contributes strictly to \(d\), with the other criteria unrestricted. Define the accountable share as \(r_d(\mu)=\sum_{O\notin\mathbb R_d}\mu_O\). The question is whether there exists a society \(\mu\) such that \(f(\bar O_\mu)=d\) and \(r_d(\mu)<1/2\). A yes solution is a rationally encoded distribution witnessing the void; a no answer means every society producing \(d\) has at least half its mass accountable by this lifted criterion.

I would expect this proposed problem to be tractable when \(f\) is given explicitly by its truth table. In this majority model, a society with \(r_d(\mu)<1/2\) has more than half its mass on ballots in \(\mathbb R_d\), forcing the aggregate values on strictly contributing criteria to be the right avoiding values. Thus a witness exists exactly when some \(O\in\mathbb R_d\) satisfies \(f(O)=d\); Theorem 3 supplies that structural test. Scanning the truth table and checking each opinion is polynomial in the explicit input size. This is a consequence of the proposed lift, not a complexity result claimed by the paper.

A plausible regime is a very large public consultation with a fixed, small set of binary policy criteria. Participants share the same available opinions and procedure, and many cast the same complete ballot, so the number \(n\) of participants can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of observed ballot types. The type is the ballot; its mass is the population fraction casting it. Theorem 6 and Theorem 7 give some reason the authors might recognize a question about the population share of responsible participants: their paper already studies responsibility by group size.

The main weakness is that an individual’s unilateral deviation has no effect in an atomless population. This proposal carries responsibility labels forward from the finite model’s \(\mathbb R_d\) characterization instead of deriving them from continuum counterfactuals. That makes it a plausible limit of *responsibility counts*, but the authors or a referee could reasonably say it does not preserve their account of individual responsibility. It also appears to gain little computationally: the global existence question reduces to checking the decision function, and a witness can have support size one. I would make this modest case, while being clear that it does not turn the paper into an anchored computational-complexity paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that none of the cited results is a computational result. Theorem 3 characterizes immunity by a set inclusion; Theorem 6 gives an equivalence for majoritarian procedures; Theorem 7 gives a condition involving group size and thresholds. The paper establishes no algorithm, running time, or complexity classification. These are the nearest formal footholds, as the proponent acknowledges, but they cannot anchor a mirror of the kind ChoCo is tasked with finding.

The strongest possible rescue is the proposed share of responsible voters. A large consultation with many people casting the same ballot is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and the fraction of voters whose ballots lie outside \(\mathbb R_d\) is a natural limit of finite responsibility counts. So “there can be a decision with fewer than half the population responsible” is a coherent follow-up question. Theorem 6 makes that extension especially easy to motivate.

Its conceptual cost is that responsibility in the paper is defined through an individual’s unilateral alternative vote, assessed against possible votes by everyone else. In an atomless society, changing one person’s ballot changes no group opinion. Defining responsibility by membership outside \(\mathbb R_d\) carries the finite labels into the continuum; it does not derive responsibility from the continuum’s own counterfactuals. Treating a positive mass of voters as the unit of deviation would restore an effect on the outcome, but would make the question about coalition responsibility rather than the paper’s individual responsibility.

The proposed existence problem also quantifies over the society instead of taking a distribution over ballot types as input. A stronger computational follow-up could take such a distribution and optimize mass transfers to create or remove a responsibility void. That would introduce intervention costs or budgets and a new optimization problem. The paper supplies neither that problem nor a complexity result for it.

So the negative case is strongest on the programme’s stated criterion: this paper offers no computational result to continuize, and a continuum version of its responsibility concept faces a real semantic choice. It is weaker as a universal claim. The high-multiplicity consultation scenario is plausible, and a limit theory for the responsible share might be worthwhile as a follow-up. The material supports calling the proposed mirror unanchored and conceptually unsettled; it does not prove that no useful continuous population study could grow from the paper.
