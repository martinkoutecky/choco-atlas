The continuous mirror question

Given finite doctor and hospital-position type sets, binary-rational masses, and each type’s strict ranking of acceptable opposite-side types plus an outside option, compute a stable mass flow that is doctor-optimal: for every doctor type and every preference-list prefix, the mass assigned within that prefix is at least as large as in any other stable flow. Stability requires that no mutually acceptable type pair has positive mass on both sides assigned to options they like less than each other. Report if no doctor-optimal stable flow exists.

The model it lives in

Two nonatomic populations are described by finite preference types with masses \(a_i\) and \(b_j\). Variables \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) give the mass matched between types, with row and column sums bounded by their supplies and unmatched mass assigned to outside options. A flow is stable when no pair of types has positive mass on both sides that prefers the pair to its current assignments; doctor-optimality is prefix-wise dominance for every doctor type.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a doctor-optimal stable mass flow always exist for these type-level preferences and rational supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is narrow: a continuous, type-based stable-matching problem anchored to Theorem 2.1, which the paper cites from Gale–Shapley rather than proves. The theorem says that doctor-proposing deferred acceptance always produces a stable matching, produces the doctor-optimal stable matching—each doctor gets their most-preferred stable partner—and gives the same outcome regardless of proposal-processing order. The paper displays the algorithm; since each doctor proposes to each hospital at most once, it runs in polynomial time. This is an algorithmic anchor, though the theorem itself does not state a complexity bound.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Doctor-Optimal Stable Mass Matching. An instance has finite sets of doctor types \(D\) and hospital-position types \(H\); binary-rational masses \(a_i\) and \(b_j\); and, for each type, a strict ranking of acceptable types on the other side, with an outside option. A type is a complete description for this model: its members share the same preferences and other matching-relevant attributes. Normalize doctor mass to \(1\), and let hospital mass be \(1+\varepsilon\) for rational \(\varepsilon>0\) when modeling an unbalanced market.

The decision variables are flows \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), the mass of doctor type \(i\) assigned to hospital type \(j\), with row and column sums bounded by \(a_i\) and \(b_j\). The unmatched mass of each type is the remainder. Call a flow stable if no mutually acceptable pair of types has positive mass on both sides who would strictly prefer that pair to their current assignment. Ask for a stable flow that is doctor-optimal: for every doctor type and every prefix of its preference list, the mass assigned within that prefix is at least as large as in any other stable flow. The output is that flow, or a determination that no flow with this dominance property exists. There is no numeric welfare objective; the solution is a stable assignment with the proposer-optimal property of Theorem 2.1.

A plausible regime is a large residency or hiring market: millions of applicants and positions, but comparatively few recurring preference cohorts, such as specialty, location, and qualification profiles. The mass \(x_{ij}\) represents how much of one cohort matches with another. The expected direction is Class A: a bulk deferred-acceptance procedure might compute the flow in time polynomial in the numbers of types and the input bit length. That is a target, not a result established by this paper. A proof must handle type-level splitting and bit complexity; clearing denominators and running ordinary deferred acceptance on all the resulting clones could take time exponential in the encoded masses.

The main limitation is fidelity. This is an author-recognizable stable-matching extension, but the paper’s random-market analysis ranks named individuals, while this model ranks cohorts. Its central Theorem 3.1—for \(n\) doctors and \(n+1\) hospitals with independent uniform complete preferences, every stable matching gives each hospital expected rank \(\Omega(n/\log n)\)—is an asymptotic probability statement, not a computational result, so I would not use it as an anchor. The normalized rank fraction also tends to zero, making a direct continuum percentile analogue uninformative without further scaling. Theorem 2.1 supports the mass-matching algorithm question, but not a continuous version of the paper’s main rank bound.

So this is one modest positive case, not a mirror of the paper’s central contribution. Its open questions are whether doctor-optimal stable mass flows always exist under cohort-level preferences, whether bulk deferred acceptance achieves polynomial bit complexity in support size and encoding length, and whether any meaningful rank-bound analogue survives when the population imbalance is only one position. If an anchor must explicitly state a complexity classification rather than an algorithmic correctness result, then this paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result to continuize. Theorem 2.1 is the only plausible algorithmic anchor: it states that deferred acceptance returns a stable, doctor-optimal matching independently of proposal order. But it is a cited Gale–Shapley correctness theorem, not a result of this paper, and it says nothing about a complexity landscape. The paper’s own contribution, Theorem 3.1, is an expected-rank bound for random finite markets, not a computational question. Turning the former into the anchor and leaving the latter behind makes the proposed mirror a new stable-matching project more than a continuation of this paper’s contribution.

There is also a mismatch in what counts as a type. In the paper’s random model, preferences rank named counterpart agents. A complete type must include that ranking, and independent uniform rankings give almost every agent a distinct type. The resulting support grows with the population; the aggregate does not compress the market. To obtain repeated types, one must instead make agents rank counterpart *types*. That is a sensible cohort market, but it changes the preference input and replaces individual ranks with mass quantiles.

The best version of the proponent’s idea—large applicant and position cohorts with shared rankings over cohorts—is plausible as a matching model. Yet it asks a new question about stable fractional allocations. With finite type sets, deferred acceptance appears amenable to batching proposals by type pair; the remaining work is to pin down the mass-stability definition and rational arithmetic. Even a useful theorem there would concern the cohort model’s algorithm, not a missing complexity result in this paper. The proponent is right that it is an author-recognizable extension; that alone does not supply the computational anchor the programme is looking for.

The more tempting anchor is the paper’s own Theorem 3.1. Its central regime has \(n\) doctors and \(n+1\) hospitals. On normalizing population mass, the surplus is \(1/n\), which vanishes in the limit. Holding a positive surplus \(\varepsilon\) instead studies a different, macroscopic imbalance. A richer continuum random-preference model might still yield an interesting expected-quantile theorem, but that would be an analytic limit question, not the worst-case or algorithmic question this programme targets. Defining mass ranks and rescaling them cannot supply the missing computational problem.

This is a strong case against treating the paper as a good ChoCo mirror, but it does not prove that no worthwhile continuous matching project exists. A repeated-cohort market may be sensible and worth studying on its own. The negative case is weakest precisely at the universal claim: the paper’s domain makes that extension unusually natural, even though its main theorem and its computational content do not carry over.
