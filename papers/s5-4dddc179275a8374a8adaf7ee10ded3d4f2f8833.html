What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue concerns Theorem 3's participation condition; it does not computationally cover Theorems 1–3 or Table 3.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper has no eligible computational anchor. Its three numbered theorems are classical impossibility results, cited from elsewhere, rather than results about complexity or algorithms:

  • Theorem 1 (Arrow, 1963): no social welfare function satisfies unrestricted domain, independence of irrelevant alternatives, Pareto, and non-dictatorship.
  • Theorem 2 (Gibbard, 1973, and Satterthwaite, 1975): every universal, non-trivial, resolute social choice function is manipulable or dictatorial.
  • Theorem 3 (Moulin, 1988): with more than three candidates and at least 25 voters, no voting rule satisfies both Condorcet consistency and participation.

Table 3 also classifies ten Condorcet extensions by vulnerability to participation paradoxes, but it is an unnumbered classification, not a computational result. None of these results says a problem is hard, tractable, or parameterized tractable.

The closest population-continuous formulation would take complete rankings as types and a distribution \(\mu\) over them. If a group of type \(r\) joins with fraction \(\alpha\), compare the rule’s winner at \(\mu\) with its winner at \((1-\alpha)\mu+\alpha\delta_r\). One could ask whether a resolute rule can be both Condorcet-consistent and guarantee that the joining group does not get a worse outcome. That is a coherent mirror of Theorem 3, and the finite theorem carries over to rational masses representing finite profiles. But it remains an axiomatic existence question, not the computational question this programme requires.

Turning it into a computational task—for example, finding a mass addition that triggers a participation paradox—would be a new question the paper does not state or prove. So I would not present it as an anchored positive case. The paper is about social-choice impossibilities and deliberative democracy, not computational social choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper’s numbered results are axiomatic impossibility theorems, not computational results. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a parameterized problem whose population axis can be continuized. Arrow’s theorem remains an axiomatic question about the profile space. Gibbard–Satterthwaite concerns an individual manipulator, whose mass vanishes in an atomless society; making manipulation consequential requires a positive-mass coalition and a new account of its objective.

Moulin’s Theorem 3 is the best candidate. For a society \(\mu\), adding mass \(\alpha\) of voters with ranking \(r\) gives \(\mu_\alpha=(\mu+\alpha\delta_r)/(1+\alpha)\). But an individual joining an atomless population has \(\alpha=0\), so the participation condition becomes vacuous. Taking \(\alpha>0\) gives a coherent bloc-participation or winner-robustness question, but that is a change from individual participation. To make it computational, one must then fix a rule and choose a task, such as finding the smallest \(\alpha\) that produces a no-show paradox. Theorem 3 itself specifies neither that optimization objective nor an algorithmic problem. Its transfer to rational masses transfers an axiomatic statement, not computational content.

That distinction supports a negative verdict about a *direct* computational mirror. It does not establish the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The paper explicitly discusses groups of like-minded voters, and minimizing the mass needed to trigger a participation paradox is a natural winner-robustness problem. That could be a worthwhile continuous project, even though it extends rather than mirrors the theorem’s individual-level axiom. So the negative case is strong on the absence of computational results, but weak against the best group-mass formulation.
