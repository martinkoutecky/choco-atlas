The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a computational follow-on, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper already makes the population continuous: a type records the characteristics relevant to matching, population measures \(\nu_W,\nu_M\) give types’ masses, and a matching is a joint measure over couple-activity types.

The closest named results are Theorem 1, proved here, which says a stable matching exists; and Theorem 2, also proved here, which says a matching \(\mu\) is stable exactly when it is the weak limit of stable matchings for a sequence of finite matching problems whose population measures converge to \(\nu_W,\nu_M\). These are existence and limit results. Neither gives an algorithm or a complexity classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. So this paper has no named computational result to anchor the required mirror on.

A reasonable adjacent question would be: Stable Mass Matching. Given finite sets of worker and firm types, rational masses, finite activities, and finite preference and feasibility tables, find a joint measure \(\mu\) with the specified population marginals, supported on feasible couple-activity types, such that \(\mu\otimes\mu(I)=0\). The solution is the matching measure itself. Theorem 1 supports existence under the paper’s assumptions, and Theorem 2 supplies the finite-population limit interpretation, but neither establishes the computational complexity of this task.

The weakness is decisive for the requested case: Stable Mass Matching computes in the paper’s existing continuous-population model. It is a plausible computational programme around the paper, but claiming that it mirrors one of the paper’s computational results would invent an anchor the paper does not contain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an existence theorem; Theorem 2 characterizes stable matchings as limits of stable finite matchings. The other named theorems establish existence, representation, or equivalence results. None defines a computational problem and classifies its complexity.

That matters especially here because the paper already makes the population continuous. It takes population measures as data, represents matchings by joint measures, and defines stability through \(\mu\otimes\mu(I)=0\). Theorem 2 already connects that model to finite populations. Turning Theorem 1 into “find a stable matching” or Theorem 2 into “construct approximating finite matchings” would ask new algorithmic questions about an existing continuous model. It would not continuize a discrete result from this paper.

The proposed Stable Mass Matching question is coherent: with finite type and activity tables and rational masses, one could ask for a stable coupling. But its best version still takes the paper’s continuous matching measure and stability definition as given. Its novelty would be computational analysis and finite encoding, not the population continuization ChoCo is meant to assess. Drop those finite restrictions and the paper supplies no finite input encoding or parameters for a complexity question. Keep them and the task is a finite type high multiplicity matching problem. Either way, it is a possible follow-on project, but it does not supply the missing mirror.

Theorem 2 does not provide a stronger computational bridge: its approximating sequences depend on the particular limiting matching, and the theorem gives neither an algorithm nor a uniform approximation guarantee. One could study those questions, but that would again be a new computational agenda, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

This negative case is strongest as a boundary on what counts as a *continuous mirror*, not as a claim that algorithmic work on stable matching is unworthy. The proposed follow-on could be worthwhile in its own right; the paper simply gives no computational result for it to mirror.
