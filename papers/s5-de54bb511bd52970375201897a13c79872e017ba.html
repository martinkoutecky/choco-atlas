The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a qualified one: the paper has a natural population mirror, but it does not contain a qualifying computational anchor under your strict rule. Its results are existential, not stated as membership in \( \mathrm P \), hardness, FPT, or another complexity class.

The best anchor is Theorem 15, proved in this paper. It states:

Every fair-division instance with indivisible mixed manna and additive valuations admits an allocation that is both introspectively envy-free up to one item, \( \IEFo \), and fractionally Pareto efficient, \( \fPO \).

This is stronger than Theorem 1, which proves \( \IEFo+\PO \). I would use Theorem 15 as the sole anchor; Theorem 17 is not suitable because it continuizes the manna, hence the outcome space, rather than the population.

My lead mirror would be Continuous \( \IEFo/\fPO \) Mixed-Manna Allocation.

An instance consists of a finite set of valuation types \(T=[\tau]\), a rational population distribution \( \mu\in\Delta_{\tau-1} \), finitely many indivisible item categories \(J=[m]\), rational per-capita supplies \( \rho_j \), and additive type valuations \(v_t(j)\in\mathbb Q\). A type is a complete valuation and eligibility description; agents of the same type are interchangeable. The regime is \(N\gg\tau\): for example, millions of employees distributed among a few dozen recurring roles, receiving indivisible equipment slots, training places, desirable assignments, and burdensome duties.

Let \( \mathcal B \) be a finite catalogue of integral bundles; the closest version uses \( \mathcal B=2^J \). The decision variable is \(x_{t,B}\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\). It must satisfy

\( \sum_{B\in\mathcal B}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \)

for every type \(t\), and

\( \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni j}x_{t,B}=\rho_j \)

for every item category \(j\). Thus every unit of population receives an integral bundle. Only the aggregate masses \(x_{t,B}\) are fractional; no individual receives a fractional item.

For \( \mu_t>0 \), define the type-\(t\) benchmark

\( M_t(x)=\max\{v_t(B):x_{u,B}>0\text{ for some }u\in T\}. \)

The allocation is continuously \( \IEFo \) if, for every \(t\) and every bundle \(B\) receiving positive mass of type \(t\), there exists \(S\subseteq J\) with \( |S|\le 1 \) such that

\( v_t(B\triangle S)\ge M_t(x). \)

This is exactly the paper’s introspective relaxation: an agent may hypothetically add or remove one item from its own integral bundle, and must thereby eliminate envy toward every bundle present in the population.

It is continuously \( \fPO \) if there is no other feasible mass allocation \(y\) such that every positive-mass type weakly improves its average value,

\( \frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_B y_{t,B}v_t(B)\ge \frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_B x_{t,B}v_t(B), \)

with strict improvement for at least one type.

The problem is: given \( (\mu,\rho,v,\mathcal B) \), find a mass allocation \(x\) satisfying both conditions. A rational \(x\) has an exact finite interpretation: after clearing denominators, \(N\mu_t\) agents have type \(t\), \(N\rho_j\) item slots of category \(j\) exist, and \(Nx_{t,B}\) agents receive bundle \(B\). Conversely, every such finite allocation normalizes to an \(x\). This is a genuine high-multiplicity dictionary, not merely a change from counts to notation.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. It preserves mixed-sign additive valuations, indivisible bundles, introspective one-item fairness, and fractional Pareto comparison. Their proof already uses weighted welfare LPs and dual item prices, so the natural continuous formulation is a configuration allocation with type masses and dual prices. The fact that type-level averages are fractional is forced by the population limit, while individual bundles remain integral.

I would expect the explicit-catalogue version to be a plausible Class A problem: it is a finite configuration system, and \( \fPO \) can be tested by a dominance LP. With a compact bundle description such as \( \mathcal B=2^J \), however, the real question is whether the \( \IEFo \) support condition admits an efficient separation or pricing procedure. That could yield a continuous optimization result, or it could expose a continuum-specific hardness phenomenon. The paper itself does not settle this.

The weakest point is substantial. Theorem 15 is not a computational theorem, and the paper’s KKM argument and augmenting-tree construction are built for finitely many named agents. They do not automatically prove existence or efficient computation for arbitrary \( (\mu,\rho) \). Moreover, replacing individual agents by type masses changes Pareto efficiency from agentwise comparison to type-average comparison. A referee could therefore accept the model as a compelling high-multiplicity analogue while rejecting the claim that the paper has already established it.

So my positive conclusion is: there is a convincing, technically precise population mirror of the paper’s main fairness-efficiency question, and it is worth studying. But under the programme’s formal anchor rule, the paper should receive an amber qualification rather than a fully compliant positive verdict: it contains a strong mirrorable theorem, but no named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor fails before the continuization question begins. Theorem 15 is an existential theorem, not a computational result: it gives no decision problem, search specification, encoding, running time, complexity class, or approximation guarantee. Its KKM step is nonconstructive, and the augmenting-tree procedure only operates after a suitable weight vector and optimal allocations have somehow been supplied. “Given an instance, find an \(\IEFo+\fPO\) allocation” would be a new search problem, not a computational result stated by this paper. Theorem 1 and the appendix theorem have the same defect; Theorem 17 is, correctly, outcome-space rather than population continuization.

There is also a genuine degeneracy if one continuizes only the population, as the programme requires. The paper has \(m\) finite indivisible items. With \(N\) agents and fixed \(m\), at most \(m\) agents receive nonempty bundles, so their population mass tends to zero. The limiting allocation puts essentially all mass on the empty bundle. The positive-mass benchmark \(M_t(x)\) then forgets the exceptional bundles that controlled envy in every finite instance, and the one-item fairness condition becomes vacuous or changes discontinuously. Keeping those exceptional agents as atoms gives a mixed discrete-continuous model, not a society that has itself become continuous.

The proponent avoids this by scaling item supplies with population. That is a coherent new model, but scaling is not forced by this paper: the original problem contains no relation between the number of agents and the number of items. The resulting object has repeated item categories, per-capita capacities, and a new notion of what “one item” means. It is a homogeneous two-sided allocation problem inspired by the paper, not its high-multiplicity relaxation.

The proposed \(\fPO\) condition also breaks the claimed dictionary. The paper’s \(\fPO\) compares every named agent individually. The mirror compares only type averages. Two identical-type agents with values \(0\) and \(10\) could be changed to values \(-1\) and \(12\): the type average improves, but one individual is worse off, so this is not a Pareto improvement in the paper’s sense. A faithful continuum version would need almost-everywhere individual dominance, equivalently a distributional or quantile condition, not average improvement. That repair produces a substantially different problem and invalidates the claimed direct transfer of the paper’s weighted-welfare argument.

Likewise, the proposed \(\IEFo\) constraint depends on which bundles have strictly positive mass. A bundle assigned to one agent in every finite \(N\)-instance can disappear in the limit while still determining finite-agent envy. Thus the proposed formulation has no established two-way high-multiplicity correspondence.

So, under ChoCo’s computational-anchor rule, this paper should receive a negative verdict: there is no named computational result to continuize, and the proposed model is an unproved new allocation problem rather than a mirror of the paper. I would not claim literal impossibility of a worthwhile related project—if the gate were merely “can one formulate a sensible population model?”, the proponent’s construction would be defensible. But it does not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s computational content.
