What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror concerns only the equilibrium existence results in Propositions 1 and 3. Proposition 2’s restriction on equilibrium governments and the remaining analysis have no surviving computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the electorate regime: the paper has six complete preference types, with probabilities \(p(t)\), and studies elections with a large expected number of voters. A large PR electorate concentrated on a small set of ballot types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind needed for an anchored continuous-computational mirror. Its named results concern equilibrium existence and outcomes. Proposition 1, proved in the paper, establishes two-party equilibria in large elections under office-motivated parties. Proposition 2, also proved there, says that in large elections with office-motivated parties, only single-party governments may form in equilibrium. Proposition 3 proves two-party equilibrium existence when parties may also have ideological motives. None gives a complexity classification or an algorithm for computing an equilibrium.

A tempting continuous question would take rational masses \(\mu_t\) on the six rankings and ask for equilibrium vote fractions by type, with government formation determined by the resulting vote shares. That is a different game: an infinitesimal voter cannot change those shares. The paper’s equilibrium arguments rely on voters’ pivotality, including uncertainty about population parity. Removing those effects means Propositions 1 and 3 do not anchor that continuous question.

So the electorate makes a continuous-population model plausible as a new research direction, but I cannot honestly state a continuous problem per computational anchor: this paper supplies none.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to anchor one. Propositions 1 and 3 establish equilibrium existence; Proposition 2 restricts the governments that can arise in equilibrium. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Calling them anchors does not make them computational ones.

The most plausible proposed mirror would give the six voter rankings masses \(\mu_t\), then ask for equilibrium vote fractions by type. But in a nonatomic society, a single voter’s action does not change those fractions or the resulting government. Since voters’ payoffs depend on the government, every action gives the same payoff to an individual given the aggregate. The ordinary nonatomic Nash question is therefore vacuous: arbitrary voting profiles qualify as equilibria. Proposition 1’s and Proposition 3’s two-party equilibrium existence no longer describes a strategic selection, and Proposition 2’s claim about equilibrium governments has no counterpart when arbitrary aggregate vote shares can be equilibria.

That degeneracy comes from the paper’s mechanism, not from a literal choice of tie-breaking or government rule. Its voters matter because a single ballot can affect a pivotal event. The paper specifically relies on rare near-ties and on uncertainty about whether the electorate has even or odd size. A deterministic mass limit erases that individual influence and the integer structure behind it. One could retain finite-population corrections or rescale pivotal events, but then those discrete fluctuations still do the strategic work; the resulting problem is an asymptotic analysis of the finite Poisson game, not a computational problem created by making the population continuous.

Treating each ranking’s entire mass as a strategic bloc would preserve influence, but it changes the players from individual voters to coordinated groups. Keeping a few influential voters as atoms preserves pivotality, but leaves the core strategic mechanism finite. Neither is a better continuous mirror of the paper’s voter equilibrium results.

This does not mean the electorate regime is implausible. Six complete ranking types and a large election are a natural high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s Poisson model is evidence for that regime—not a continuous-treatment collision. The negative case is narrower: a sensible continuous population does not by itself yield a nontrivial equilibrium question here, and the paper offers no named computational result to mirror. A separate project on computing equilibria in finite Poisson games could be worthwhile, but it would not be anchored by a continuous-population version of these propositions. The universal claim that no related scenario could ever be worthwhile is therefore stronger than the evidence supports.
