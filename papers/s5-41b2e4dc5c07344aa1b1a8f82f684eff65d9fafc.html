The continuous mirror question

Given finite teams \(T\), a finite set of complete applicant types \(\Theta\), a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), an integer population scale \(M\), weak team rankings over types, and weak type rankings over teams, compute a rational mass allocation \(x_{it}\ge0\) with \(\sum_i x_{it}=M\mu_t\). Require balancedness, \(\lfloor M/n\rfloor\le\sum_t x_{it}\le\lceil M/n\rceil\), participant-justified EF, and team-justified SD-EF1: for every \(i\ne j\), define \(b_{ij,t}=x_{jt}\) when \(i\succsim_t j\) and \(b_{ij,t}=0\) otherwise, and require some \(z_{ij}\) with \(0\le z_{ij,t}\le b_{ij,t}\), \(\sum_t z_{ij,t}\le1\), and \(x_i\succsim_i^{\mathrm{SD}} b_{ij}-z_{ij}\). The allocation must also be Pareto optimal among mass allocations.

The model it lives in

A many-to-one matching society with finitely many complete types carrying mass \(M\mu_t\). The decision variables are type-to-team masses \(x_{it}\) and removable EF1 masses \(z_{ij,t}\); fractional SD compares team bundles, and the computational objective is to find an allocation satisfying two-sided fairness, balancedness, and Pareto optimality, ideally in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,\log M\).

The objection that survived

The best surviving objection is that team-justified SD-EF1 is defined by deleting one named participant and the paper's algorithm uses individual ordering, so the proposed type-level model may not inherit the theorem or its polynomial bound.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.2's main allocation guarantee and Proposition 4.5's implementation question over high-multiplicity types. It leaves the detailed group-strategyproofness result in Theorem 5.2, the warm-up results, and the quota and outside-option extension in Theorem 6.2 as separate follow-up questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a type-compressed algorithm solve the mass problem in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,\log M\)?
  • Can the paper's eligibility-expansion and lexicographic matching arguments be replaced by anonymous transportation or flow procedures?
  • What is the precise \(M\to\infty\) limit of team-justified SD-EF1, and can a meaningful anonymous analogue of participant group-strategyproofness be defined?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified yes, anchored in Theorem 4.2 and its complexity companion Proposition 4.5. This is not a hardness-dissolution case: the paper contains no numbered hardness theorem of its own. Its NP-hardness statement about the earlier \( \text{team-EF1}+\text{participant-justified EF} \) problem is reported as prior work, so I would not misuse it as an anchor.

Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper, says that the main algorithm returns an allocation satisfying team-justified SD-EF1, participant-justified EF, balancedness, and Pareto optimality, and therefore swap stability. Proposition 4.5, also proved here, says that the algorithm can be implemented in polynomial time. This is the lead result because it simultaneously concerns fairness on both sides, efficiency, and computation.

The natural regime is a large centralized allocation system: for example, a national residency or school assignment process with millions of applicants and finitely many hospitals or schools. Suppose there are \(n\) teams and a large nominal population size \(M\), but only \( \tau \) applicant types, with \(M\gg\tau\). A type is complete: it specifies the applicant’s weak preference over teams and the applicant’s position, including ties, in every team’s ranking. Thus two applicants have the same type only when the problem treats them identically in every respect. A plausible type might combine specialty, examination band, eligibility class, location constraints, and the applicant’s ranking of hospitals. Hospitals use coarse priority bands, so many applicants share the same type.

Let \( \Theta=\{1,\ldots,\tau\} \) be the type set and let \( \mu_t \) be the population fraction of type \(t\), with \( \sum_t\mu_t=1 \). The continuous society has mass \(M\mu_t\) of type \(t\). An allocation is a nonnegative matrix \(x=(x_{it})\), where \(x_{it}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to team \(i\), satisfying \( \sum_i x_{it}=M\mu_t \). This does not give an individual a fractional team: each unit of mass receives one team, while \(x\) records the aggregate assignment.

For each team \(i\), write \(x_i=(x_{it})_{t\in\Theta}\) for its bundle. Team preferences over named applicants become a weak order \( \succsim_i \) over types. Define fractional stochastic dominance by \(q\succsim_i^{\mathrm{SD}}r\) when, for every upper contour set \(U\) of \( \succsim_i \), \(q(U)\ge r(U)\). This is exactly the type-mass analogue of the paper’s SD comparison of bundles.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

Continuous Two-Sided Fair Allocation\(_\infty\). Given \(n\), \(M\), the rational type distribution \(\mu\), participant-type preferences \( \succsim_t \) over teams, and team rankings \( \succsim_i \) over types, find a rational allocation \(x\) satisfying the following.

First, it is balanced in the continuous high-multiplicity sense:
\( \lfloor M/n\rfloor\le \sum_t x_{it}\le \lceil M/n\rceil \) for every team \(i\). In normalized units, the tolerance is \(1/M\), and the pure limit has equal mass \(M/n\) at every team.

Second, it satisfies participant-justified EF. In type form this means that whenever \(x_{it}>0\), \(x_{jt'}>0\), and type \(t\) strictly prefers \(j\) to \(i\), it must be the case that \(t'\succsim_j t\). Otherwise a positive mass of type \(t\) has justified envy toward a positive mass of type \(t'\).

Third, it satisfies team-justified SD-EF1. For teams \(i\neq j\), let \(b_{ij,t}=x_{jt}\) when \(i\succsim_t j\), and \(b_{ij,t}=0\) otherwise. Thus \(b_{ij}\) is the mass in team \(j\)’s bundle whose participants weakly prefer \(i\) to \(j\). The requirement is that there exists removable mass \(z_{ij}\) with \(0\le z_{ij,t}\le b_{ij,t}\) and \( \sum_tz_{ij,t}\le1 \) such that \(x_i\succsim_i^{\mathrm{SD}} b_{ij}-z_{ij}\). The unit \(1\) is one participant in the unnormalised model, or \(1/M\) in normalized population units. This is the honest mass relaxation of “remove at most one participant”; in the strict \(M\to\infty\) limit, EF1 converges to exact team justified EF.

Finally, the allocation must be Pareto optimal. There must be no alternative mass transport in which every moved participant mass goes only to a weakly preferred team, every team weakly SD-prefers its new type bundle, and at least one positive mass of participants or one team is strictly better off. The required output is the allocation matrix \(x\), not an encoding of it.

I expect this problem to be Class A, although proving that is a worthwhile new result rather than something obtained for free from Theorem 4.2. The finite algorithm repeatedly computes lexicographically optimal matchings subject to eligibility constraints. In the mass version, those matchings become transportation or flow problems over types. The SD-EF1 conditions are finitely many linear prefix inequalities, and the removable masses \(z_{ij}\) are continuous variables. The main technical issue is participant-justified EF: its support condition is an implication rather than a single linear inequality. The likely route is a type-level eligibility-expansion algorithm, with each iteration solving a transportation problem and adding a team to a type’s admissible set. Pareto optimality can then be imposed by a final lexicographic or cycle-elimination step.

That is recognisably the authors’ problem. The teams remain discrete, the two-sided preferences remain, the fairness notion remains SD-based, and the output is still an assignment of population units to teams. What changes is only the population representation: repeated indistinguishable applicants are represented by mass, and fractional \(x_{it}\) records many integral assignments at once. In the two limiting cases highlighted by the paper, the correspondence is especially clear. If all participant types are indifferent over teams, the mass algorithm should reduce to a balanced round-robin allocation. If preferences are strict, it should reduce to deferred acceptance with type masses and team capacities.

The same paper’s Theorem 6.2 suggests a natural extension: scale team quotas to mass capacities, add an outside-option type, and ask for stability, extended team-justified SD-EF1, Pareto optimality, and a group-strategyproof participant mechanism. I would not use it as a second anchor, because its proof relies on indexed participants and augmented tie-breaking; an atomless population has no canonical \(p_1,\ldots,p_m\) order. That is an excellent follow-up question, but not a cleanly inherited theorem.

My weakest point is therefore clear: the transition from individual tie-breaking and one-person deletion to anonymous type mass is not cosmetic. A direct type compression of the paper’s algorithm may fail to preserve its group-strategyproofness, and the pure continuum turns EF1 into EF unless the population scale \(M\) is retained. But this does not undermine the main mirror. The core Theorem 4.2 problem—computing a balanced, two-sided fair, Pareto-optimal allocation for a high-multiplicity society—is natural, faithful to the paper, and plausibly exposes exactly the kind of type-level transportation and continuous optimization structure that the ChoCo programme is meant to study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is that the paper’s central guarantee is atomic. Theorem 4.2 is not merely about assigning aggregate mass; it guarantees team-justified SD-EF1, where envy may be removed by deleting one named participant, together with participant-justified EF, balancedness, and Pareto optimality.

A normalized society \(\mu\) contains no canonical “one participant.” If the deletion allowance is \(1/M\), then two populations with the same \(\mu\) but different \(M\) define different problems. If \(M\to\infty\), the EF1 exception disappears and the condition becomes exact team-justified EF. That is a legitimate new limit problem, but it is not the fairness theorem proved in the paper. Conversely, fixing a positive mass allowance \(\varepsilon\) replaces “up to one participant” by a new approximate fairness notion with no basis in the paper. Thus the best repairs either retain an essentially atomic scale or change the paper’s central fairness concept.

The proposed type model does not make the algorithm descend automatically. Proposition 4.5 is polynomial in the expanded number \(m\) of participants and slots. With multiplicities encoded in binary, \(m\) may be exponential in \(\log M\), so the proposition says nothing about a polynomial algorithm in \((n,\tau,\log M)\). The missing compression is substantive: the algorithm lexicographically optimizes an ordered sequence of individual slots and uses an ordered list \(p_1,\ldots,p_m\). Its participant-justified-EF condition is also support-sensitive: a violation occurs whenever two positive masses occupy a forbidden pair of teams and types. Replacing this by a transportation problem requires a new anonymous mechanism and a new proof; it is not a continuous restatement of Proposition 4.5.

The tempting fixes do not preserve the original object. Keeping individual slot and participant order preserves the theorem but destroys high-multiplicity compression. Removing those orders gives a sensible type-level allocation problem, but one whose Pareto and fairness guarantees are not inherited from Theorem 4.2. The resulting project could be worthwhile, but it would be a new theory of anonymous mass matching rather than a continuization established by this paper.

Theorem 6.2 does not rescue the case. With fixed quotas, a continuum population mostly interacts with a finite collection of atomic slots; with quotas scaled by population, the same EF1 and tie-breaking problem returns. Its group-strategyproofness is even more explicitly tied to individual indices and coalitions, for which an atomless type mass has no canonical interpretation.

This is nevertheless a weak negative case. Repeated applicants with common preference profiles and coarse hospital priority bands are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and rational mass allocations can be implemented by cloning types. An exact-limit version is also mathematically natural. I therefore cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proponent has identified a plausible Class A research problem; the defensible criticism is narrower: the paper does not itself provide the compressed continuous algorithm they claim, and their proposed formulation changes or parametrically reintroduces the atomic content of the headline theorem.
