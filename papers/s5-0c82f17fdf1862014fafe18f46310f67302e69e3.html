The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of male and female types, each assigned an ordered cell, a complete preference list, and a binary rational mass μ_p or ν_q summing to one on each side, with lists naming at most two types and ties only on the male side, does there exist a mass matching x_{pq} ≥ 0 on acceptable pairs? Require ∑_q x_{pq} ≤ μ_p and ∑_p x_{pq} ≤ ν_q; require that x_{pq} and x_{p'q'} cannot both be positive when p precedes p' but q follows q'; and require, for every acceptable pair (p,q), that B_p(q)=0 or B_q(p)=0, where B_p(q)=μ_p-∑_{q'}x_{pq'}+∑_{q':q≻_p q'}x_{pq'} and B_q(p)=ν_q-∑_{p'}x_{p'q}+∑_{p':p≻_q p'}x_{p'q}.

The model it lives in

A two-sided ordered-cohort market: each type has a side, an ordered cell, and a complete preference list; its binary rational mass can be split among acceptable opposite-side types. The decision variable is the mass matching x, with capacity, noncrossing-support, and weak-stability constraints.

The objection that survived

The paper's crossing relation uses each person's rank, while the mirror replaces that geometry with an order on cells; the proponent acknowledges the shift but does not establish that the authors would recognize cell-level noncrossing as their problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's NP-completeness result for weak-SSNM existence in SMTI. It leaves the algorithms for maximum weakly stable noncrossing matchings, the other stability notions, and the remaining existence results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can existence be decided in time polynomial in the number of types and the bit length of the masses?
  • Does fractional splitting make the problem tractable, or can the ordered support and stability constraints still encode hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 1, proved here by reduction from 3SAT: deciding whether an instance admits a weak-SSNM is NP-complete, even when every preference list has at most two people and ties occur only in men’s lists. Here weak stability means a pair blocks only when both people strictly prefer each other to their current partners; “strongly” in weak-SSNM refers to requiring that *no* blocking pair exists, even one whose edge would cross the matching. The theorem’s 3SAT source is cited from elsewhere, but this restricted matching result is proved in the paper.

I would mirror it with Continuous Weak-SSNM Existence for Ordered Cohorts. The setting is a large two-sided matching market whose participants and positions are arranged in ordered cells on two parallel lines, with crossing assignments forbidden. For example, a regional placement system could have large cohorts of applicants and posts grouped by location and credential band, with a policy requiring assignments to preserve the order of the two sides. Each type records a side, an ordered cell, and a complete preference list, including ties and acceptability. Members of one type are indistinguishable for the matching problem. There may be millions of participants but only a modest number of such types; each cohort’s mass is a binary rational proportion of its side’s population.

Formally, let \(P\) and \(Q\) be finite sets of male and female types, with masses \(\mu_p\) and \(\nu_q\), each side summing to one. Retain the theorem’s restriction: each list names at most two types, and ties appear only in lists of types in \(P\). A solution is a mass matching \(x_{pq}\ge 0\) on acceptable pairs, with row and column sums at most \(\mu_p\) and \(\nu_q\). Its support must be noncrossing: it cannot contain \((p,q)\) and \((p',q')\) when \(p\)’s cell precedes \(p'\)’s but \(q\)’s cell follows \(q'\)’s.

To define weak stability for split cohorts, count the mass of type \(p\) that would strictly prefer \(q\) to its current assignment:
\[ B_p(q)=\mu_p-\sum_{q'}x_{pq'}+ \sum_{q':\,q\succ_p q'}x_{pq'}. \]
Define \(B_q(p)\) symmetrically. The mass matching is stable if, for every acceptable pair \((p,q)\), at least one of \(B_p(q)\) and \(B_q(p)\) is zero. Thus any positive mass of both types who would strictly benefit can pair off in an arbitrarily small amount and block. The question is: does such a noncrossing, weakly stable mass matching exist? A yes-solution is the rational mass table \(x\); a no-answer certifies that none exists. This preserves the paper’s underlying existence question and its global blocking rule while replacing indivisible participants with divisible cohorts.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: cohort mass grows while the number of distinct preference-and-position types stays much smaller. The authors should recognize the mirror’s core—two-sided preferences, ties, one-to-one capacity, stability, and noncrossing—although representing an ordered cell as a cohort is a deliberate geometric co-modification. It keeps position relevant to crossing while allowing the population at a position to be continuous.

My first expectation would be Class A, tractable, rather than hardness transferring automatically. The reduction in Theorem 1 exploits discrete gadget choices, and divisible cohorts may admit fractional assignments that break those choices. Ordered support and stability still create combinatorial constraints, so tractability is a conjecture, not a consequence of the theorem. The natural follow-up questions are whether existence is polynomial in the number of types and input bit length, whether a fractional matching can always be converted to a discrete stable matching at suitable multiplicities, and how the answer changes as cohort masses vary.

The weakest point is the geometry: if individual rank along each line is essential, collapsing people into ordered cells may erase part of the original problem, while retaining a distinct rank for every person can make the type count grow with the population. There is a second limitation: Theorem 1’s hardness does not itself transfer to the mass problem, because fractional matching changes the available solutions. The case for the mirror survives as a well-posed continuous version of the paper’s existence problem, not as a claim that its NP-hardness survives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational result and a reasonable anchor: the paper proves that deciding whether a weak-SSNM exists is NP-complete. The strongest case against mirroring it is that position is part of an agent’s type. The crossing condition depends on each person’s rank on the line, so agents with identical preferences but different positions are not interchangeable. In the paper’s literal model, that makes the number of types grow with the population; grouping by preference alone loses information needed to determine feasibility.

The proponent’s cohort model is the best reply to that objection, but it changes what “noncrossing” means. If a cohort occupies several distinct positions, its aggregate mass edges do not say which individuals are paired to which positions, so they cannot determine whether assignments cross. If all cohort members instead share one abstract cell, crossing is checked between cells, and the model has replaced individual geometry with an order constraint on categories. That is a coherent capacitated matching problem, but its connection to this paper is less direct. The paper’s motivating applications—such as terminals at distinct locations—make individual position central; large groups sharing a cell and identical preferences are harder to motivate there.

A spatial continuum avoids co-located cohorts, but each location then affects crossing and must be represented in the type. A useful computational formulation would need extra structure, such as a finite partition or piecewise-constant preferences; those choices define new models whose answers may depend on the partition. This leaves no obvious, canonical continuous version of Theorem 1.

That is the strongest negative case, but it is not decisive. An ordered-cohort placement market could plausibly make the proponent’s version a worthwhile problem, and the programme allows co-modifications when population continuization requires them. I would call the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists weak: the geometry makes a faithful high-multiplicity regime difficult to motivate, but it does not rule one out.
