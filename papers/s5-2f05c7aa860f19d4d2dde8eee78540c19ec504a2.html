The continuous mirror question

Given rational per-capita capacities \(c_{jr}\) and a finite support \(T\) of service types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), budgets \(B_t\), demand limits \(D_t\), and base-demand vectors \(a_{tjr}\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal primal-dual solution in time polynomial in \(M,R,|T|,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\). The primal variables \(z_{tj}\geq 0\) define per-type allocations \(x_{tjr}=a_{tjr}z_{tj}\); maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t B_t\log(\sum_j z_{tj})\), subject to \(\sum_j z_{tj}\leq D_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tjr}\leq c_{jr}\). The dual resource prices should support the typewise budget-optimal bundles to \(\varepsilon\) accuracy, yielding the continuous analogue of a non-wasteful, frugal market equilibrium.

The model it lives in

A continuum of price-taking service buyers represented by finitely many complete types \(t\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\). Each type has budget \(B_t\), demand cap \(D_t\), and base demands \(a_{tjr}\); fog resources remain divisible, with per-capita supply \(c_{jr}\). The type-level allocation is \(x_{tjr}=a_{tjr}z_{tj}\), and the weighted convex program uses objective \(\sum_t\mu_t B_t\log(\sum_j z_{tj})\), type demand caps, and mass-weighted resource capacities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.4 and program (27)–(31), including computation of non-wasteful, frugal equilibria; it leaves the fairness results, privacy protocol, and other allocation schemes without a population-continuous claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove a polynomial bit-complexity bound for computing an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal primal-dual solution, and establish the corresponding approximation guarantee for the typewise equilibrium conditions.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a population-continuous version of the paper’s non-wasteful, frugal market-equilibrium computation.

The anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper (its proof is in Appendix B). It says that the optimal solutions to program (27)–(31) are exactly the non-wasteful and frugal market equilibria; that at such an equilibrium every service either spends its full budget or reaches its utility limit; that such an equilibrium always exists; that utilities are unique across these equilibria; and that the program captures all non-wasteful and frugal market equilibria. This is a convex-program characterization, not a formal claim that the problem is in \(P\). It counts as an algorithmic anchor because the paper computes with the program, including in its numerical work and distributed method.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. Call it Continuous Non-wasteful Fog Equilibrium. The instance has \(M\) fog-node resource pools, \(R\) resource kinds, and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) service types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), budget \(B_t\), demand limit \(D_t\), and a full base-demand array \(a_{tjr}>0\). The masses sum to one. Each resource pool has per-service-population capacity \(c_{jr}>0\); this is the supply scaling as the service population grows.

A type-\(t\) service receiving bundle \(x_t\) has utility
\[ U_t(x_t)=\min\left\{\sum_j\min_r\frac{x_{tjr}}{a_{tjr}},D_t\right\}. \]
The requested solution is a price vector \(p\geq0\) and a per-service allocation \(x_t\) for each type. Each type must receive a utility-maximizing bundle affordable under its budget at prices \(p\). Aggregate use must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tjr}\leq c_{jr}\), with \(p_{jr}(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tjr}-c_{jr})=0\). The allocation must be non-wasteful and frugal: resources assigned to a type from a node are proportional to that type’s base-demand vector, no type exceeds its demand limit, and a type buys only from nodes offering its cheapest base-demand bundle.

The paper’s natural candidate characterization is the mass-weighted extension of (27)–(31): choose \(z_{tj}\geq0\) and \(x_{tjr}=a_{tjr}z_{tj}\), maximize
\[ \sum_t\mu_t B_t\log\left(\sum_j z_{tj}\right) \]
subject to \(\sum_jz_{tj}\leq D_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tjr}\leq c_{jr}\). The computational question is to find a price-allocation pair satisfying the equilibrium conditions above; the convex program supplies the proposed route to a solution. For the usual finite-bit algorithmic version, ask for an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal solution to this program, with running time measured in \(M,R,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. I expect this to be tractable (Class A): the support is explicit, the program has size polynomial in those parameters, and the finite-agent theorem’s weighted objective and capacity constraints extend directly to type masses. A formal bit-complexity and approximation analysis for this continuum formulation is still to be proved.

The regime I find plausible is a large managed edge platform serving many tenant workloads, sensor-service deployments, or network-slice instances drawn from a small menu of application and contract profiles. A type records the entire relevant profile: preferences across the fog nodes, resource ratios, budget, and demand cap. Repeated deployments with the same profile contribute mass; locality and SLA differences create distinct types. The number of deployments can be very large while \(\tau\) stays moderate. The paper itself motivates budgets for slices and groups of users or sensors and assumes price-taking buyers in a large market, so this is close to its economics. The mirror keeps its resource goods divisible as in the paper; the continuized object is the service population.

This covers the paper’s finite-demand, selected-equilibrium result in Theorem 3.4. It does not claim a continuous-population result for the privacy protocol or turn the fairness properties into separate axiomatic questions.

My weakest point is the regime: actual fog buyers may be a small number of large service providers, and exact repeated profiles may be less common than repeated workloads or users. The mirror needs many independently budgeted deployments and a resource pool that scales with their population. That is a narrower case than “fog computing” generally, but it seems credible—and the paper’s large-market motivation makes it a scenario its authors could recognize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3.4 is not a complexity theorem in the programme’s strict sense. It characterizes the non-wasteful, frugal equilibria as the optima of a convex program and proves existence and uniqueness of utilities; it gives no running-time or bit-complexity bound. The paper’s algorithmic work does not repair that anchor: the distributed method appears as an algorithm, but its convergence is attributed to standard ADMM rather than stated as a numbered complexity result. If the named-result gate requires a theorem asserting complexity, this anchor does not pass it.

The strongest version of the mirror is still hard to reject on modeling grounds. With rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), \(n_t\) identical buyers of each type reproduce the weighted program: scale resource capacities to \(Nc_{jr}\), then divide the aggregate constraints and objective by \(N\). The type-level allocation can be realized by giving each clone the same per-capita bundle. That is a faithful high-multiplicity formulation, and scaling supply with population is a legitimate co-modification here. A large edge platform with many deployments drawn from a small menu of budget, demand, and preference profiles is plausible enough that I cannot rule it out.

The negative case is that this mirror adds little computational structure. Its masses are fixed input weights; the decision variables are type-level allocations in a polynomial-size convex program, with no new pricing problem or fractional escape from an integrality barrier. The paper’s own characterization already supplies the route, and the remaining finite-bit approximation analysis looks like a direct weighted extension. That makes the mirror seem more like a small high-multiplicity extension than a substantial new complexity landscape.

But the programme explicitly treats high-multiplicity formulations as supporting evidence, and a routine or already tractable answer is not by itself a reason to discard a well-posed mirror. So I cannot honestly defeat the best version of this anchor: my case against is strong only if the strict computational-anchor gate is decisive. If the reader accepts Theorem 3.4’s convex-program characterization as a computational result, the proposed mirror likely survives.
