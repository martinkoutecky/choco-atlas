What the mirror covers

It covers the paper's proposal to aggregate evaluator-feature ratings or simulate a collective response over a stakeholder distribution. It leaves untouched feedback interpretation, RL training, generalization, strategic voting, behavioral effects, representation, and multiple AI systems; the proposed mass-transfer steering formulation is not a result of the paper.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of computing a specified social-choice rule when \(T\) or the type distribution is represented implicitly?
  • What sample size or approximation guarantee lets a finite evaluator panel preserve the \(\mu\)-winner?
  • How should strategic reporting be modeled for massed evaluator types without replacing aggregation by a new equilibrium problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no valid named computational anchor. Although it defines theorem environments in the preamble, it states no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic result. “Arrow’s Theorem,” Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and Moulin’s result are cited external results, not results proved or numbered in this paper. Thus the formal anchor list is empty, and one should not pretend otherwise.

There is nevertheless a credible positive mirror of the paper’s central problem. The strongest candidate is a computational version of its proposals for “RLCHF using evaluator features and aggregated ratings,” together with its discussion of representative populations and strategic feedback.

Call it Continuous RLCHF Winner Steering. Fix a prompt \(x\) and a finite candidate-response set \(\mathcal{Y}=\{y_1,\ldots,y_K\}\). Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete evaluator types. A type includes everything used by the paper’s individual preference model: the evaluator’s feature vector, their ranking or cardinal evaluation of every \(y\in\mathcal{Y}\), and any credibility or weighting parameter used by the collective rule. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the stakeholder population of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

For a Borda-style collective rule, write \(s(t,y)\) for the score assigned to response \(y\) by type \(t\). The collective score is

\[ S_\mu(y)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t s(t,y). \]

Given a target response \(y^\star\), and a cost \(\gamma(t,t')\) for replacing, reweighting, or recruiting a unit of feedback mass of type \(t\) as type \(t'\), choose transfers \(z_{t,t'}\ge 0\) satisfying

\[ \sum_{t'\ne t}z_{t,t'}\le \mu_t. \]

The resulting population is

\[ \mu'_v=\mu_v-\sum_{w\ne v}z_{v,w}+\sum_{u\ne v}z_{u,v}. \]

The problem is to find a transfer \(z\) of minimum cost

\[ \sum_{t,t'}\gamma(t,t')z_{t,t'} \]

such that \(y^\star\) is a winner under the resulting collective feedback:

\[ S_{\mu'}(y^\star)\ge S_{\mu'}(y) \qquad\text{for every }y\in\mathcal{Y}. \]

The solution is the optimal mass-transfer plan and its cost. With no intervention, this reduces to the paper’s ordinary collective-choice problem: choose the response maximizing the aggregate rating.

This is a genuine population mirror, not merely a fractional outcome model. The agents are the continuum; the response remains a discrete alternative. The mass represents fractions of stakeholders or feedback instances, and the action changes the composition of the feedback panel or reported-feedback distribution. In a finite population of size \(N\), \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) gives the corresponding high-multiplicity instance.

A plausible regime is a large deployed assistant serving millions of users, where feedback is collected from recurring cohorts: language community, professional role, domain expertise, accessibility needs, safety priorities, and similar features. For a fixed prompt family and response slate, many users can share the same complete evaluation type even though the total population is enormous. The relevant requirement is not that all users have identical values, but that the number of distinct evaluator types \(\tau=|T|\) is much smaller than the number of agents. This is precisely the regime already presupposed by the paper’s discussion of stakeholder populations, representative sampling, evaluator features, and simulated collective decisions.

The authors would likely recognize this as their problem rather than as a generic voting construction. Section 4 asks how to select a representative subset of stakeholders; Section 6 explicitly proposes aggregating evaluator types into collective ratings or winners; and Section 7.2 identifies strategic feedback as a central concern. The steering formulation makes those questions computational: how much representative-panel manipulation or feedback reweighting is needed to change the collective response?

For an explicitly listed type set and an additive scoring rule, this mirror is likely Class A. The winner constraints are linear, so the problem is an LP with polynomially many variables in \(\tau\) and polynomially many response constraints. If rankings are implicit over all permutations, or if \(\gamma\) is a swap distance, the pricing problem may become the substantive question. Replacing Borda by Kemeny, ranked pairs, or a learned nonlinear social welfare function could instead yield hardness that transfers from the alternative space, or possibly a genuinely continuum-specific obstruction. Approximation questions also arise when \(\mu\) is estimated from samples rather than given exactly.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the paper’s stakeholder aggregation, representative feedback, collective response selection, and strategic-feedback discussion. It does not claim to continuize the neural-network parameter space, the training dynamics, or the response space. Those would be different continuizations.

The weakest point is decisive: this paper does not itself formulate the mass-transfer problem, specify an input model, or prove a computational result. Its finite samples \(f_1,\ldots,f_N\) and juries \(J(x,\mathcal{Y})\) are suggestive, but not yet a continuous society in the programme’s formal sense. Consequently, this is a strong prospective mirror of the paper’s research agenda, but not an anchor-backed mirror of a named theorem. A referee could reasonably accept the problem as a natural computational development of the paper; they could not honestly be told that the paper already contains the complexity result being mirrored.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no valid computational anchor. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary giving an algorithm, complexity classification, or computational impossibility result. Arrow’s theorem, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and Moulin’s result are cited external results, not results established by this paper. On the programme’s standard, there is therefore no paper result whose high-multiplicity relaxation can be mirrored.

The proposed “Continuous RLCHF Winner Steering” does not repair that absence. For fixed \(x\) and finite \(\mathcal Y\), the paper’s actual population analogue is simply

\[ \bar r_\mu(y)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t s(t,y), \]

followed by whatever collective choice rule is selected. The paper never introduces a target response \(y^\star\), a cost \(\gamma(t,t')\), conversion of one evaluator type into another, or any intervention that changes \(\mu\). Those ingredients are imported from bribery. Recruiting an evaluator is not converting an existing evaluator into another type, and strategic reporting is a change of ballot, not a transfer of population mass. Consequently, the proposed LP is a new generic manipulation problem attached to the paper, not a continuization of one of its computational problems.

A more faithful version would replace the finite sample \(f_1,\ldots,f_N\) by an expectation,

\[ \bar r_\mu(x,y)=\int \psi(x,f,y)\,d\mu(f). \]

That is mathematically legitimate, but it exposes the underlying mismatch. If \(T\) is finite, this is just an explicitly given weighted aggregation of scores. If \(f\) ranges over a genuine feature space, the problem becomes one of representing a distribution and integrating a learned model—population-risk estimation or continuous optimization, not high-multiplicity social choice. The paper’s evaluator preferences are also conditional on the prompt and candidate set. A faithful type would need to specify a function such as

\[ (x,\mathcal Y,y)\longmapsto r, \]

along with relevant behavioural and strategic characteristics. Such types are not recurring finite voter types in the proposed regime. Conversely, fixing one prompt and one response slate makes the construction a standard election over a single panel, stripping away the paper’s central issues of training, generalization, feedback interpretation, and inference-time behaviour.

The same problem defeats better alternatives. Representative-panel selection would naturally ask for a sampling or coreset guarantee, such as whether a finite panel preserves the full-population winner with high probability. Strategic feedback would require utilities over model behaviour, reporting actions, and an equilibrium or incentive notion. Neither is specified in the paper, and adding those ingredients creates a new mechanism-design or statistical-learning problem rather than a mirror of a named result. Treating ethical principles as voters does not help either: that continuizes a normative menu, not the population.

One can manufacture a valid high-multiplicity scenario by stipulating a fixed response slate and cohorts whose members have exactly identical complete evaluations. That would be a legitimate continuous voting problem, but its computational content comes from the newly added scoring or manipulation model. The same construction could be attached to any survey, committee, or rating system. It is not enough to establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.

The negative case is therefore strongest as a verdict about this paper: it is a position paper proposing future questions, not a paper containing a computational result to continuize. The universal claim that no future scenario could ever be worthwhile is not mathematically provable, but the proponent has not supplied such a scenario—only a plausible new research problem inspired by the paper.
