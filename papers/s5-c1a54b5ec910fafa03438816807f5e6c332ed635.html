The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed set of \(N\) samples and \(L\) labels, a finite support \(\Theta\) of worker types with masses \(\mu_\theta\), and each type's group and complete update-relevant annotation profile, find matrices \(D_\theta\) minimizing the mass-weighted analogue of the paper's \(D_w\)-update objective: the expected squared fitting loss plus the within-group pairwise \(\operatorname{HSIC}\) term integrated against \(\mu\), plus the mass-weighted ADMM penalty, with group-commonality matrices, group weights, and ADMM states fixed. What convexity condition and algorithmic complexity hold as functions of \(N\), \(L\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a worker-population version of AMCC’s worker-calibration step. Samples and labels remain finite; the population being continuized is the crowd workforce.

The paper’s only numbered computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved here. It states that, when the ADMM penalty and dependence parameters satisfy \(\mu_{\mathrm{ADMM}}/\beta \ge 4L(W-1)\), the worker-individuality update subproblem \(L(D_w)\) is convex; \(L\) is the number of labels and \(W\) the number of workers. The proof uses a Hessian bound and Gershgorin’s theorem. This is a result about one update with the other variables fixed. The paper has no numbered theorem classifying AMCC as polynomial-time or proving hardness.

Call the proposed mirror Continuous Type-Mass AMCC Calibration. Its instance has a fixed set of \(N\) samples and \(L\) labels, \(M\) worker groups, and a finite support \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\) worker types. Type \(\theta\) has mass \(\mu_\theta\), with \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta=1\), a group \(g(\theta)\), and an aggregate annotation matrix \(A_\theta\in\mathbb{Q}^{N\times L}\). Its entries are averages of workers’ signed responses, so they may be fractional even though each individual response is discrete. The instance also gives the fixed group-commonality matrices \(C_m\), group weights \(\lambda_m\), and the fixed ADMM state matrices \(S_\theta,T_\theta\).

The decision is a response matrix \(D_\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}\) for each type. The question is to return a global minimizer of the type-weighted analogue of the paper’s \(D_w\)-update objective:

\[ F(D)= \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\lambda_{g(\theta)}^r \left\|A_\theta-A_\theta(D_\theta+C_{g(\theta)})\right\|_F^2 -\beta\sum_{m=1}^M \int_{\Theta_m}\int_{\Theta_m} \operatorname{HSIC}(D_\theta,D_\phi)\,d\mu(\theta)d\mu(\phi) +\rho\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta \left\|D_\theta-S_\theta+T_\theta\right\|_F^2 , \]

where \(\Theta_m=\{\theta:g(\theta)=m\}\), \(\rho\) is the ADMM penalty, and the HSIC term uses the paper’s fixed kernel representation. This is the update objective: the outer ADMM step handles its auxiliary normalization variables. A solution is the set of matrices \(D_\theta\) attaining the minimum.

A plausible regime is a large annotation platform running repeated projects, with thousands or more workers but a much smaller set of standardized expertise, response-behavior, and group profiles. Workers in a type share the behavior the calibration problem models; their mass is their fraction of the workforce. This is a regime claim, not a claim about all crowdsourcing. AMCC already models shared group behavior alongside worker-specific expertise, so retaining distinct types within each group gives the authors a recognizable analogue of their calibration question. The type-level averages and normalized pairwise term are the natural population-scale versions of their worker sums.

I expect this problem to be Class A, subject to proving the continuous-weighted analogue of Theorem 1’s convexity bound. With finite support, it has \(O(\tau L^2)\) decision variables; if the resulting rational quadratic objective is convex, standard convex optimization gives a polynomial-time route in the input size. The finite theorem motivates that conjecture, but it does not prove it: the mass-weighted pairwise dependence term changes the Hessian bound, and it needs a fresh analysis. The finite-to-continuous dictionary is also concrete: a workforce with \(n_\theta\) workers of each type maps to \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/n\), with type-level annotation matrices formed by aggregation.

The mirror covers the worker-calibration component of AMCC’s consensus model. The paper’s active selection of particular sample-label-worker triplets is a further question: one could ask how to allocate query mass across sample-label-type combinations, with type prices included in the type description. The paper does not give a numbered complexity result for that selection problem, so I would leave it outside this anchor.

The soft spot is that Theorem 1 proves convexity of an update objective under a finite-\(W\) condition; it does not establish polynomial-time solvability of the full alternating AMCC procedure. The continuum extension changes the weighting of worker pairs, and type aggregation may smooth away distinctions that the authors’ individual \(D_w\) matrices capture. This is also a crowd-learning paper rather than a conventional voting paper. The positive case is therefore narrow: it rests on the plausibility of a large, standardized worker population and a well-motivated calibration problem, not on a complexity theorem already established by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror overclaims what Theorem 1 establishes. That theorem gives a sufficient condition for convexity of one \(D_w\) update, with the other variables fixed. It does not classify the complexity of consensus, calibration, or query selection. The proposed continuous problem freezes the commonality matrices, group weights, and ADMM state, then asks for the corresponding update. That is a reasonable new optimization question, but it is not a mirror of a complexity result in the paper.

There is also a mismatch in what the type averages preserve. AMCC fits each worker’s \(D_w\) to that worker’s realized, often sparse annotation matrix \(A_w\). In general,
\[ \frac{1}{n_\theta}\sum_{w\in\theta} \left\|A_w-A_w(D+C)\right\|_F^2 \ne \left\|\bar A_\theta-\bar A_\theta(D+C)\right\|_F^2. \]
The average loses within-type variation; the pairwise HSIC term likewise depends on relationships among individual \(D_w\) matrices. One can preserve those quantities by carrying additional statistics or a distribution of worker behaviors, but then the proposed type matrix is no longer a sufficient representation. If types instead consist of workers with identical realized matrices and update states, aggregation is exact but represents replicated workers, not the paper’s distinctive calibration of varied workers. A model using shared latent response distributions could be worthwhile, but its objective and convexity proof would need to be developed anew.

The strongest alternative is a workforce-level query-allocation problem: allocate query mass across worker types for each sample-label pair, with type-specific costs and response behavior. That would make the workforce the continuous object and could be a meaningful computational problem. But the paper’s triplet rule is a greedy score based on current annotations, local neighbors, and estimated costs; it does not define an allocation objective or a population response model. Supplying those would create a related new problem, rather than a continuous version of a named result here.

So the negative case is strongest against this particular Theorem 1 anchor: it is an algorithmic convexity lemma, and the proposed aggregation does not generally preserve its worker-level objective. The broader claim that *no* worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is much weaker. A large platform with repeated, standardized worker classes could support a sensible continuous workforce model; the paper does not give enough structure to rule that out.
