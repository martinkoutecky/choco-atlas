What the mirror covers

Theorems 1–4 already analyze continuous-population stable and Boston outcomes, information assumptions, and segregation; no separate discrete result is covered by a mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper shows how natural a continuous population is for school choice. Its society is already a unit mass of households with types \((l,c)\), where \(l\) is location and \(c\) is the child’s education cost or priority. A matching is represented by the mass assigned from each type to each school, and the welfare objective is total transportation cost. A large school district’s annual cohort, with many pupils but few schools, is a plausible regime for this model; a finite list of location and priority classes with rational masses would be a natural high-multiplicity approximation. The authors clearly recognize the population-continuum question.

But that is evidence for the programme’s modeling premise, not a qualifying computational anchor. The paper’s named results are proved here, but none asserts a complexity classification or analyzes the running time of an algorithm. The closest is Theorem 1: it gives critical cost cutoffs and the density of the stable allocation under deferred acceptance, then proves that the resulting limit allocation is inefficient. Theorem 2 proves that a Boston equilibrium exists and describes its ring structure and efficiency relative to deferred acceptance. These are substantive results about allocations and equilibria, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. I therefore can’t honestly supply a per-anchor prover question under the requested criteria.

There is also a collision problem for any mirror that simply asks for school matching with a continuous population: that is already the paper’s model. A follow-on asking for computational complexity with a broader class of type distributions could be sensible, but it would be a new question rather than a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. That gap is the case’s weakest point: the paper strongly supports the *plausibility* of continuization, while providing no qualifying computational result from which to build the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has already made the population continuous. Its households form a unit mass, their locations and priority or cost characteristics have a distribution, matchings are densities, and the welfare criterion is aggregate transportation cost. Theorem 1 characterizes the deferred-acceptance allocation in that model; Theorem 2 establishes and describes Boston equilibria. Theorems 3 and 4 continue the same continuum analysis. There is no discrete-population result here for continuization to transform.

Theorem 1 is the clearest example. One could ask for the deferred-acceptance outcome on a finite list of rational-mass types, but that would require grouping households with different locations and priorities. Their exact distances and cutoffs matter to the paper’s result, so grouping them changes the model. The resulting finite-type school-choice problem could be worth studying, but it would be a new high-multiplicity problem, not a computational mirror of Theorem 1. The theorem’s exact continuous version is already on the page.

Theorem 2 offers a more promising follow-on: computing or approximating Boston equilibria from finite type masses might pose a substantial algorithmic question. But it changes the atomless game with measurable strategies into a finite game over type-level actions. Alternatively, one could seek an effective procedure for the paper’s continuum equilibrium; that would computationalize a model whose population is already continuous. Either project might be worthwhile on its own, but neither obtains its motivation from continuizing this paper.

So the proponent is right not to name a qualifying computational anchor. The more fundamental problem is not merely that the paper lacks a complexity theorem: its central results already concern a continuous population. Any computational project must add a new problem or change the model. That is the strongest negative case for a *mirror*. Its weak point is the Boston result: turning the equilibrium characterization into an algorithm could be a useful project. The paper does not establish that it would be uninteresting; it establishes that it would not be a population-continuization mirror.
