The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible anchor: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Section 4 reports simulation observations, and the convergence claim about Airiau et al. is a summary of cited work, not a result proved here. So there is no honest anchor-specific mirror to give.

The strongest positive case is at the level of the paper’s *model*. A natural continuous-population version would represent the electorate at round \(i\) by a finite-support measure \(\mu_i=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_{i,t}\delta_{x_{i,t}}\) over 11-dimensional evaluation vectors. A type is a voter’s current vector; its mass is that fraction of the electorate. Candidate locations, the movement rule, and tie-breaking are fixed. Each round, draw \(q\) voters independently from \(\mu_i\) to form the poll, then move every mass point according to the paper’s 3-pragmatist rule: toward its closest candidate among the poll’s top three. This gives the next measure by pushing \(\mu_i\) through the resulting movement map.

Call the resulting question Finite-Horizon Continuous-Population Poll Winner: given rational initial support points and masses, rational candidate locations, poll size \(q\), horizon \(R\), and target candidate \(c^\star\), compute the exact probability that \(c^\star\) is the plurality winner after \(R\) rounds. The answer is a rational number: each poll outcome has rational probability, and each outcome determines the next measure. This preserves the report’s poll-driven movement and plurality rule while making population mass, rather than individual voters, the state.

The expected direction is tractability when parameterized by the number of candidates \(m\), poll size \(q\), and horizon \(R\): enumerate the \(\binom{q+m-1}{m-1}\) possible poll count vectors at each round and update the \(\tau\) support points. This suggests an algorithm with a parameter-dependent number of branches and polynomial dependence on \(\tau\) and the input bit length, subject to accounting for exact algebraic comparisons arising from unit-vector movements. The unrestricted-horizon complexity and the question of eventual winner stabilization would remain open.

A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated, quantized evaluation profiles and fixed-size public polls, so \(n\gg\tau\). The paper’s survey ratings and simulation make the type choice recognizable to its authors; this mirror covers its iterative plurality model, including the poll-order effects in Section 4.3. The weak point is substantial: the paper establishes neither a complexity problem nor a high-multiplicity regime. Its 1,000 simulated voters may have nearly all distinct evaluation vectors, so the compression benefit is not demonstrated. This is therefore a plausible continuous mirror of the model, but not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its Section 4 gives simulation observations, not theorems about complexity or algorithms. The convergence statement is a summary of Airiau et al.’s work, not a result proved here. Under the requested anchor standard, there is nothing eligible.

The proposed *Finite-Horizon Continuous-Population Poll Winner* is a possible new problem, but it is not a mirror of a result in this paper. It adds an exact probability objective and a stochastic polling process, then asks for the probability of a target winner after \(R\) rounds. Enumerating poll count vectors gives a natural finite-horizon search with \(\binom{q+m-1}{m-1}\) outcomes per round; that is a baseline for the newly defined problem, not a computational question the report poses or answers.

There is also a scaling choice to resolve. The simulations use polls of \(10\%\) of the electorate. If that fraction stays fixed as the population grows, poll proportions converge to the society’s proportions, so the proposed poll randomness disappears. Keeping \(q\) fixed preserves randomness, but changes the polling regime. That alternative can be sensible, as can a population with repeated evaluation profiles, but neither is established by the report’s 1,000-voter sample of 11-dimensional survey evaluations.

A closer continuous version would evolve a measure over evaluation vectors and compute each type’s movement and the resulting plurality masses. For a specified poll sequence, that is an aggregate simulation. To get a richer computational problem—such as finding the least poll bias that changes the eventual winner—one must add an objective and intervention that the paper does not study. That might be worthwhile follow-on work, but it does not rescue an anchor in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed *mirror*: it is unanchored and depends on added modeling choices. It is weaker as a universal claim that no related continuous-population project could be worthwhile. A fixed-size-poll model with many repeated response types is coherent; rejecting every such extension would go beyond what this paper supports.
