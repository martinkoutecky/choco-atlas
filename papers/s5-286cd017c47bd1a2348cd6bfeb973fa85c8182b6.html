The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), per-capita supplies \(q_g\ge 0\) for \(m\) good categories, and nonnegative valuation vectors \(v_1,v_2\) satisfying \(\sum_g q_g v_r(g)=1\) for each \(r\in\{1,2\}\), consider unit population mass with type masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). Choose masses \(x_{r,z}\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^m\), with \(\sum_z x_{1,z}=\alpha\), \(\sum_z x_{2,z}=1-\alpha\), and \(\sum_{r,z}x_{r,z}z_g=q_g\) for each category \(g\). Require EF1 across every pair of supported bundles: for each \((r,z),(s,z')\) with \(x_{r,z},x_{s,z'}>0\) and \(z'\ne 0\), some \(g\) with \(z'_g>0\) satisfies \(v_r\cdot z\ge v_r\cdot(z'-e_g)\); empty bundles impose no envy constraint. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the supremum welfare \(\sum_{r,z}x_{r,z}(v_r\cdot z)\) over feasible allocations. Can one return a feasible allocation with finite rational support and welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/2\) in time polynomial in \(m\) and the binary input length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

Two valuation types carry masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\); each category has per-capita supply \(q_g\). Variables \(x_{r,z}\) assign mass to integral per-agent bundles, subject to exact supply and pairwise EF1 constraints, and maximize utilitarian welfare.

The objection that survived

The finite replication may require a population exponential in the binary input length \(L\), so Approx1 does not itself provide a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m\) and \(L\); the proponent flags the need for direct compression and a bounded encoded support.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's normalized two-type setting for a growing population. It leaves aside Theorems 2–6, which concern fixed three-agent cases, including unnormalized utilities and the improved \(5/3\) ratio, as well as hardness results reported from prior literature.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a factor-\(2\) allocation with polynomial-size rational support be computed in time polynomial in \(m\) and \(L\), without expanding a denominator-scale electorate?
  • Can a direct continuous algorithm improve the factor-\(2\) guarantee, or is that factor tight for this population model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. In the setting developed around the theorem, there are \(n\ge 3\) agents with nonnegative additive valuations, split into two nonempty groups that share two normalized utility functions. Theorem 1 says that \({\sc Approx1}\) returns a complete EF1 allocation whose utilitarian welfare is at least half the maximum welfare of any EF1 allocation. It is the paper’s strongest anchor for a population mirror because it allows \(n\) to grow. The paper’s three-agent results do not make comparably natural large-population anchors. Its related-work section reports hardness results from earlier papers, but the supplied text gives no numbered in-paper hardness result to use as an anchor.

Here is a continuous version of the underlying optimization problem. There is unit mass of agents: mass \(\alpha\) has valuation \(v_1\), and mass \(1-\alpha\) has valuation \(v_2\), where \(0<\alpha<1\). There are \(m\) categories of indivisible goods, with supply \(q_g\) of category \(g\) per unit mass. Assume rational inputs and the normalized condition \(\sum_g q_g v_r(g)=1\) for each type \(r\). A person still receives an integral bundle \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\); only the masses receiving different bundles may be fractional.

Let \(x_{r,z}\) be the mass of type \(r\) receiving bundle \(z\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_z x_{1,z}=\alpha\), \(\sum_z x_{2,z}=1-\alpha\), and \(\sum_{r,z}x_{r,z}z_g=q_g\) for every good category \(g\). Require exact EF1 across every pair of bundle types with positive mass: if a type-\(r\) agent gets \(z\) and another agent gets \(z'\), then \(v_r\cdot z\ge v_r\cdot(z'-e_g)\) for some category \(g\) present in \(z'\). The welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{r,z}x_{r,z}(v_r\cdot z)\).

Call the problem Two-Type Supply-Scaled USW-EF1\(_\infty\), factor 2: given \(\alpha\), the \(q_g\), and the two valuation vectors, return an EF1 allocation \(x\) with finite rational support and \(W(x)\ge \mathrm{OPT}/2\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is the supremum over feasible population allocations. The complexity target is an algorithm polynomial in \(m\) and the binary input length \(L\), with a finite encoded allocation as output.

The regime is a large institution or investment platform distributing recurring, indivisible categories of goods among many faculty and administrative staff, or individual and business investors. Agents within each group share their complete valuation vector; there may be millions of agents but only two types and a moderate number of recurring good categories. Supply scales with population, as needed for agents of positive mass to receive integral bundles. The paper’s item preference order, \(\rho(g)=v_1(g)/v_2(g)\), remains meaningful in this model, so the authors should recognize the same allocation objective and fairness constraint.

There is also a direct finite bridge. For a scale \(N\) clearing the rational denominators, take \(N\alpha\) and \(N(1-\alpha)\) agents and \(Nq_g\) copies of each good category. Give each copy utility \(v_r(g)/N\). Utilities are normalized as in the paper, EF1 is unchanged by the common scaling, and the finite allocation’s total welfare equals \(W(x)\) when \(x\) records the fraction of agents receiving each bundle. Thus the mirror preserves individual integral bundles while making the population the continuous object.

I expect the factor-2 question to be tractable. The two-type structure and the preference ordering used in Theorem 1 survive, and a population algorithm could potentially batch the repeated goods and bundle assignments. The result would cover Theorem 1’s normalized, two-type regime; it would not claim to mirror the paper’s fixed-three-agent guarantees.

The main weakness is computational: expanding rational masses into a finite electorate may require exponentially many agents in \(L\). Theorem 1’s algorithm therefore does not by itself give a polynomial-time algorithm for the stated input. A positive result needs a direct compressed algorithm, together with a bounded, efficiently encoded allocation support. Further questions are whether exact optimization or a better approximation is possible, and whether the finite-instance hardness cited in the related work persists under this replicated-goods regime or dissolves in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only anchor, and I can’t honestly defeat it. The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror needs an extra scaling choice: if the paper’s fixed set of goods stays fixed while the population grows, the goods per person vanish, and the original welfare guarantee stops describing a useful allocation problem. To avoid that, the proponent scales up supplies by category and scales down each copy’s value. That is a real change to the instance family.

But it is a defensible change, and the programme explicitly allows supply to scale with population when that is needed for a meaningful mirror. With that choice, the model retains the paper’s two valuation types, integral bundles, utilitarian objective, and EF1 constraint. The finite replication bridge is also coherent: scaling every valuation by the same factor preserves EF1 comparisons, while total welfare matches the continuous objective. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, not a collision or a distortion I can use to dismiss the question.

One can question whether the result will yield useful compression: the continuous allocation is a distribution over integral bundles, and EF1 must hold across every bundle type in its support. That may leave a difficult configuration problem rather than expose an easy continuous-optimization route. But this is a question about what the algorithms will find, not a reason the mirror is unworthy.

So the negative case is weak. Theorem 1 survives: the paper has a named computational result with a growing population parameter, and the better supply-scaled mirror looks like a problem its authors would recognize. I see no sound basis here for the requested universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
