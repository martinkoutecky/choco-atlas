The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(\Theta\), goods \(G\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\ge0\) with \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta=1\), per-unit-mass budgets \(b_\theta\), and utilities \(u_{\theta j}\ge0\), compute an exact normalized Fisher equilibrium: prices \(p_j\ge0\) and aggregate spendings \(f_{\theta j}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_j f_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta b_\theta\), \(\sum_\theta f_{\theta j}=p_j\), and the Fisher optimality conditions for every positive-mass type; equivalently, solve the paper's convex program with objective \(\sum_j p_j(\log p_j-1)-\sum_{(\theta,j):u_{\theta j}>0}f_{\theta j}\log u_{\theta j}\).

The model it lives in

Types \(\theta\) carry mass \(\mu_\theta\), budget \(b_\theta\), and utility vector \(u_{\theta\cdot}\); decision variables are prices \(p_j\) and aggregate type-good spendings \(f_{\theta j}\), with unit good supply and the Fisher convex-flow objective.

The objection that survived

The paper already accepts arbitrary rational buyer demands, so the mirror adds little beyond applying the algorithm to one node per type; this limits novelty but does not make the finite-buyer model already continuous-population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 6.4 and 6.5 for linear and spending-constraint Fisher markets; it leaves the generic convex-flow framework, quadratic-flow results, and oracle machinery without separate population mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one formalize a two-way dictionary between \(N_\theta\) discrete copies and mass \(\mu_\theta=N_\theta/N\), including the scaling of supplies and budgets, while preserving equilibria and rational encoding size?
  • Can the minimum mass transfer in \(\mu\) needed to force a prescribed price or allocation be computed or approximated under the same flow structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is the Fisher-market part of the paper. The paper contains no named hardness result, so the honest anchors are its tractability theorems. The lead anchor is Theorem 6.4; Theorem 6.5 is a worthwhile, richer extension.

The appropriate regime is a market with many agents but few complete economic types. For example, an advertising market may contain millions of advertisers, while only \(\tau\) types occur: each type specifies a budget, valuations for the available goods, and—where relevant—its spending constraints. If \(N_\theta\) agents have type \(\theta\), the continuous society is \(\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau)\), where \(\mu_\theta=N_\theta/N\). A type is complete in the required sense: agents of the same type have the same budget, utilities, prices and segment parameters.

The population is the continuous object. Goods remain divisible because divisibility is intrinsic to the Fisher-market problem, not because it substitutes for continuizing the population. If type \(\theta\) has per-agent budget \(b_\theta\), its aggregate budget is \(\mu_\theta b_\theta\). Thus the mirror is the exact high-multiplicity version of the authors’ market: summing identical buyers changes only node demands and capacities, not the equilibrium concept.

My lead problem is High-Multiplicity Linear-Fisher Equilibrium\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite type set \(\Theta\), a finite goods set \(G\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\) with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\), rational per-agent budgets \(b_\theta\), and rational utilities \(u_{\theta j}\ge 0\). Each good has one unit of supply. The decision variables are the price \(p_j\ge 0\) of each good and the aggregate spending \(f_{\theta j}\ge 0\) of type \(\theta\) on good \(j\). They must satisfy \(\sum_j f_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta b_\theta\) for every type and \(\sum_\theta f_{\theta j}=p_j\) for every good. The solution must be an exact Fisher equilibrium: every type spends its entire aggregate budget on utility-maximizing goods at the resulting prices. Equivalently, it is an optimal solution of the paper’s convex flow formulation, namely minimizing \(\sum_j p_j(\log p_j-1)-\sum_{\theta,j}f_{\theta j}\log u_{\theta j}\) over the feasible flows, with zero-utility incidences omitted.

This is not a weakened welfare problem. It asks exactly for the equilibrium/optimal flow computed in Section 6.2, but with identical buyers represented by mass rather than by \(N\) separate nodes. A finite market with \(N_\theta\) copies of each type is recovered by taking \(\mu_\theta=N_\theta/N\). Conversely, a continuous solution gives a proportional allocation to every unit of a type’s mass.

The named anchor is Theorem 6.4, proved in this paper. Its full content is: for a linear Fisher-market instance with \(n=|B|+|G|\) buyers and goods and \(m\) positive utility edges, the paper’s algorithm finds an optimal solution in time \(O(n^4+n^2(m+n\log n)\log n)\). The paper also verifies the required strong-polynomiality conditions. The formulation itself is attributed to Shmyrev, but the algorithmic theorem is established here.

After type compression, the theorem gives an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau+|G|\) and the number of positive type-good incidences, independent of the number \(N\) of named agents. I therefore expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable. The further questions it generates are natural continuous-population questions: how does the equilibrium price vector change under perturbations of \(\mu\); what is the minimum mass of agents whose type must change to force a specified price or allocation; and can one compute robust equilibria under uncertainty about \(\mu\)?

The second anchor is Theorem 6.5, also proved here. It concerns Fisher markets with spending-constraint utilities. In the type version, type \(\theta\), good \(j\), and segment \(k\) have utility \(U^k_{\theta j}\), and the segment accepts at most \(L^k_{\theta j}\) dollars per agent. The continuous decision variables are aggregate segment spendings \(f^k_{\theta j}\) and prices \(p_j\). With unit supply, the exact problem is to minimize \(\sum_j p_j(\log p_j-1)-\sum_{\theta,j,k}f^k_{\theta j}\log U^k_{\theta j}\), subject to \(\sum_{j,k}f^k_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta b_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,k}f^k_{\theta j}=p_j\), and \(0\le f^k_{\theta j}\le\mu_\theta L^k_{\theta j}\). A solution is the resulting equilibrium allocation and price vector.

The paper’s Theorem 6.5 states, in full, that for an instance with \(n=|B|+|G|\) buyers and goods and \(m\) spending segments, the algorithm’s running time is bounded by \(O(mn^3+m^2(m+n\log n)\log m)\). The paper again proves strong polynomiality. The underlying convex formulation is due to Devanur, Birnbaum and Xiao, but the strongly polynomial algorithm is proved here.

For the continuous version, replacing \(B\) by \(\Theta\) gives running time polynomial in \(\tau+|G|\) and the total number of type-level segments. This is a particularly convincing mirror because spending constraints are naturally type-level: advertisers in the same industry, households in the same demographic class, or buyers in the same procurement category can share the same diminishing-return schedule. The expected classification is again Class A. It raises the corresponding questions about continuous sensitivity, intervention on type masses, and whether the tractability survives when the number of segments is itself generated implicitly rather than listed explicitly.

The authors should recognize both mirrors as their problem. The objective, feasibility constraints, equilibrium interpretation, and cost-flow network are unchanged. Only the representation of repeated buyers changes: \(N_\theta\) identical budget-and-utility records become one type with mass \(\mu_\theta\), aggregate budget, and aggregate segment capacities. This is precisely a high-multiplicity relaxation, not a change to an easier social objective.

The weakest point is that this is strongest as a continuous collective-allocation mirror, not as a candidate-ranking or winner-control mirror. Moreover, the original paper already uses divisible goods, so an opponent can argue that the apparent continuity comes from the outcome space. That objection does not eliminate the mirror: the paper itself has a finite, named-buyer model, whereas the proposed question treats the society as a distribution over buyer types and seeks complexity depending on \(\tau\), not on the potentially enormous \(N\). Still, if ChoCo were restricted to rank-based elections rather than social allocation and markets, this would be a marginal fit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a collision, not that the market mirror is ill-defined. Fisher markets plainly admit a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many advertisers can share the same utility vector and budget. The problem is that the paper’s formulation has already performed the relevant aggregation.

For Theorem 6.4, given type masses \(\mu_\theta\), define an aggregate buyer node with demand
\[ \bar b_\theta=\mu_\theta b_\theta. \]
The aggregate spending variables \(f_{\theta j}\) and the same utility coefficients \(u_{\theta j}\) form exactly an instance of the paper’s convex-flow formulation, with \(n=\tau+|G|\) and one edge for each positive type–good incidence. The theorem already permits arbitrary rational node demands, so it immediately supplies the claimed algorithm. Expanding a rational mass into named identical buyers and aggregating them back changes nothing in prices, feasibility, or equilibrium.

Thus this is not a new continuous-population computational question. The paper’s algorithm already works on the sufficient statistics of the population; buyer identity has disappeared before the theorem is applied. A question about how equilibrium prices vary with \(\mu\), or about the minimum mass needed to change a price, could be interesting, but it is a new sensitivity or intervention problem, not a mirror of Theorem 6.4.

Theorem 6.5 has the same defect. For a type \(\theta\), every segment capacity simply becomes
\[ \bar L^k_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta L^k_{\theta j}. \]
The transformed network in Section 6.3 sees only these aggregate capacities, demands, and segment utilities. Its nodes and arcs are already type-level objects. The theorem therefore applies unchanged, with the running time polynomial in the number of types, goods, and explicitly listed type-level segments. The spending-constraint extension adds richer data, but no population-dependent structure.

Trying to make either mirror genuinely nontrivial requires adding individual quotas, identity-dependent utilities, fairness constraints, or indivisibilities. Those may be worthwhile models, but they are no longer the Fisher-flow problems proved tractable here. Keeping the paper’s model gives an exact quotient; changing it loses the named theorem.

So the negative case is strong if “worthwhile” means an independent ChoCo contribution: both anchors yield immediate high-multiplicity corollaries of a formulation that is already aggregate. It is not strong if the question is merely whether a legitimate continuous mirror exists. On that narrower question, the proponent is right, and I cannot honestly claim that these market scenarios fail the high-multiplicity test.
