The nearest candidates are Lemma 3.1, proved here, which characterizes 0-1 strongly EFX-orientable graphs by a condition on forests and independent sets, and Theorem 3.6, also proved here, which says every strongly EFX-orientable graph has \(\chi(G)\le3\). Lemma 4.1 proves that every bipartite graph is strongly EFX-orientable. These are substantial structural results, but treating them as complexity anchors would stretch your stated criterion.

The paper’s explicit NP-hardness result is different: deciding whether a graph has an EFX orientation for given valuations is cited to Christodoulou et al. A tempting high-multiplicity setting would be many independent copies of a fixed graphical-allocation gadget, with agents grouped by their roles in the gadget. But EFX then decomposes across copies: each copy retains the same finite graph problem, so taking the population to a continuum adds mass bookkeeping without changing the question the paper answers. I would not hand that to a prover as a continuous mirror.

So my positive assessment is limited: the structural results could inspire a separate continuum study, but this paper offers no named computational result on which to build the requested, prover-ready mirror. The weakest point is the same as the central obstacle: a natural way to scale up the population leaves the paper’s graph-level combinatorics intact, while changing that structure risks changing the authors’ problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest objection is that the paper supplies no qualifying computational result to mirror. Lemma 3.1 characterizes binary strong EFX-orientability; Theorem 3.6 bounds the chromatic number; and Lemma 4.1 gives a sufficient graph condition. They are structural results, not complexity or algorithmic results. The NP-hardness mentioned in the introduction is from earlier work on a different problem—EFX orientation for a graph with given valuations—and the paper says the complexity of recognizing strong EFX-orientability is unknown. So none of the three results the other analyst named gives ChoCo a prover-ready computational question.

The best attempt to repair Lemma 3.1 would be to repeat graph gadgets until their vertices can be treated as types. For disjoint copies, however, EFX decomposes: vertices in one copy value no goods in another. The repeated population therefore leaves the same finite graph question in each component. To make copies interact, the model must preserve which agents are joined by goods. A distribution over agent types alone does not encode those links; a type rich enough to record each vertex’s full neighborhood generally removes the multiplicity one hoped to exploit.

The same issue limits Theorem 3.6 and Lemma 4.1. Their content depends on graph structure—chromatic number, forests, independent sets, and the placement of edges—not on the aggregate distribution of agents’ valuations. A graphon or another continuum network model could preserve that structure, but it would be a new relational model. With atomless edge goods, removing any single good has zero value, so EFX collapses to envy-freeness; preserving meaningful single-good EFX instead requires retaining discrete, individually located goods or introducing a new approximation. Neither move mirrors the paper’s stated structural results as a computational problem over a continuous society.

There is a plausible research direction in continuous network fair division, and a high-multiplicity treatment of the cited NP-hardness might also be worth asking about. But those possibilities do not rescue the proposed anchors: the first changes the model and the question, and the second is not a computational result proved by this paper. The negative case is strong for this manuscript as a ChoCo mirror candidate. The universal claim that no continuous scenario could ever be worthwhile is weaker: a carefully specified continuum network model could be interesting, but this paper does not make that case for us.
