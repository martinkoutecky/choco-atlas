The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest natural population scenario comes from Section 6: let each type be a complete ranking of the candidates, and let \(\mu_t\) be its population fraction. For an agenda \(A\) and candidate \(x\in A\), one could ask whether \(x\) belongs to the split-cycle choice, using pairwise margins
\[ d_\mu(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a]\bigr). \]
A plausible regime is a large electorate with \(N\gg\tau\): many voters, relatively few distinct rankings. This is a genuine continuous-population input, and the paper’s split-cycle rule supplies a concrete object to evaluate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. The proponent’s strongest candidate, Proposition 7, characterizes split cycle axiomatically: it is the finest extended choice function satisfying local rationalizability, crucial defeat, and neutrality. The proposed population model is sensible: give each ranking type rational mass \(\mu_t\), derive pairwise margins, and ask whether \(x\) is selected by split cycle from agenda \(A\). A stronger version could ask for the complexity of evaluating split cycle from a support of size \(\tau\), or of computing a \(\gamma\)-hull from such a population. Those are coherent new computational questions. But they do not mirror Proposition 7 or Theorem 2: they add an algorithmic objective and input model that those results do not address.

So the negative case is strong as a verdict about *this paper’s* continuous mirror, not as a claim that population-based computation for these rules lacks value. The proposed direction may deserve study in its own right; the paper provides no computational anchor for it.
