The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that the full cooperation structure is stable in Qin’s game with Myerson payoffs if and only if every player’s Myerson value under full cooperation is at least their singleton worth, and every pair satisfies \(v(\{i,j\})\ge v(\{i\})+v(\{j\})\). This is a useful exact characterization of a decision problem. With a rational characteristic-function table as input, it also yields a polynomial-time test in the table’s length: compute the Shapley values and check the pair inequalities. That complexity statement is my inference from the proposition and this representation; the paper itself states no complexity result. Strictly, then, the paper has no named result explicitly classifying a problem as in P or hard. Proposition 1 is the nearest anchor I can defend.

A plausible mirror is Mass-Limit Complete-Network Stability. Imagine a large purchasing or production network with many firms in a fixed set \(T\) of standardized roles. A type specifies everything relevant to the game, such as sector, technology, and outside option. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of firms of type \(t\); the number of firms \(n\) is large while the number of represented types \(\tau\) stays small. A coalition’s value depends on its type composition through a rational polynomial \(F(x)\), where \(x_t\) is the fraction of the whole society in the coalition who are type \(t\), and \(F(0)=0\). For a finite replica with \(n\mu_t\) firms of each type, set \(v_n(S)=nF(x(S))\). Firms choose bilateral links as in the paper, and Myerson value determines their payoffs. The decision is whether full cooperation is stable in the first-order population limit.

The limiting test suggested by Proposition 1 is precise. For every represented type \(t\), define its limiting full-cooperation payoff as
\[ \Phi_t(\mu)=\int_0^1 \partial_tF(s\mu)\,ds. \]
The singleton worth tends to \(\partial_tF(0)\). For every pair of represented types \(t,u\), the leading pair-surplus term is governed by \(\partial_t\partial_uF(0)\), including \(t=u\) for two distinct firms of the same type. The mirror problem asks whether
\[ \Phi_t(\mu)\ge \partial_tF(0)\quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \partial_t\partial_uF(0)\ge 0\quad\text{for every }t,u. \]
A yes instance satisfies the limiting stability criterion; a no instance has a violating type or pair as a certificate. With dense polynomial input, these integrals and derivatives can be computed exactly in time polynomial in the input description. I expect this version to be tractable. When the margins are strict, its answer agrees with stability in all sufficiently large finite replicas; equality cases can depend on higher-order terms.

This mirrors only Proposition 1’s stability of full cooperation. It does not cover Proposition 2’s claim about equilibria for arbitrary cooperation structures. The main weakness is that the pairwise condition becomes a vanishing-order effect as the population grows. At zero-curvature boundaries, the first-order mirror cannot decide exact finite-replica stability. The proposal is therefore a plausible high-multiplicity continuation of the paper’s stability question, but it is a modest computational contribution: the paper provides no hardness result whose dissolution the mirror could test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against starts with the anchor itself: Proposition 1 is a characterization of when the complete cooperation structure is stable. The paper does not state or prove a computational result. It gives no computational problem, input encoding, or complexity bound. The proponent’s polynomial test is a reasonable inference from the proposition under an explicit characteristic-function table, but that makes it a newly formulated algorithmic question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed mirror also depends on choices the paper does not motivate: coalition worths are restricted to \(v_n(S)=nF(x(S))\), with \(F\) an explicitly represented polynomial. Under that choice, the limiting conditions reduce to derivatives and an integral of \(F\). A broader class of worth functions would need its own representation—an explicit table, circuit, or oracle—and the computational question could change with that choice. Any resulting complexity landscape would therefore be driven in large part by how \(F\) is encoded, rather than by a computational phenomenon established in the paper.

There is a further modeling strain. Proposition 1 concerns an individual deciding whether to sever a particular link, with payoffs allocated by the finite-player Myerson value. In the proposed smooth large-population model, changing one link affects only a pair of agents; the payoff difference is of order \(1/n\). The Hessian test preserves the sign by looking at that vanishing difference’s leading term. That is a coherent asymptotic refinement, but it is not a stability condition arising directly from an atomless society. A mass-level link or bloc deviation could produce a nonvanishing effect, but it would change the deviation and stability question.

This objection is not that high multiplicity is implausible: many firms in a few standardized roles is a sensible regime. Nor does it rest on the possibility that the continuous answer is easy. The stronger point is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror, while the proposed extension needs both a new input model and a refined notion of stability to retain the paper’s individual-link incentives.

That is a substantial case against this particular anchor, not a proof that no worthwhile mirror can be built. An economist might find the leading-order stability question meaningful, and the authors might recognize it as a natural extension. The negative case is strongest on the paper’s lack of a computational result and weaker on whether the asymptotic refinement is worth studying.
