The continuous mirror question

Given a finite ordered sequence of cohorts with masses \(\alpha_j>0\) summing to \(1\) and profiles \(t^j\in\{0,1\}^d\), positive coefficients \(c_k\), capacity fraction \(\kappa\in(0,1]\), and exact marginal advice \(a_k=\sum_j\alpha_j t^j_k\), what is the optimal worst-case competitive ratio, uniformly over the number of cohorts? On arrival of cohort \(j\), a causal policy observes \((\alpha_j,t^j)\) and admits mass \(x_j\in[0,\alpha_j]\), subject almost surely to \(\sum_jx_j\le\kappa\); its dimension utility is \(U_k=c_k\,\mathbb{E}[\sum_jx_jt^j_k]\), and its objective is \(\min_k U_k\). Compare it with the offline optimum that knows the full ordered sequence and has the same capacity.

The model it lives in

Use finite ordered cohort phases as the instance, with each cohort defined by its arriving mass and binary profile. Decisions admit fractions of cohort mass, the hard capacity is \(\kappa\), and exact advice gives each dimension's total mass \(a_k\); online and offline objectives are the minimum expected dimension utility.

The objection that survived

Chronology is essential, while the proposed \(\tau\) counts profiles rather than order-resolved phases; a finite input model must also encode the ordered path. This limits claims that profile support alone compresses the instance, but finite ordered cohorts remain a recognizable mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the exact-advice binary max-min online guarantees in Theorems 3.4 and 4.6; it leaves the inaccurate-advice, nonbinary-attribute, generalized-mean, and offline-model results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the matching \(O(1/\sqrt d)\) lower bound and \(1/[4(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt d]\) policy guarantee be formalized for split-able cohort mass uniformly in the number of ordered phases?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is an online selection problem over a continuous stream of population mass. It fits the paper’s setting: a large candidate pool, overlapping demographic or skill attributes, known aggregate counts, and arrivals whose order the recruiter cannot control. Candidates with the same binary profile are the same type: the problem assigns them the same utility and uses no individual prices or costs. Letting \(N\) grow while keeping the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles small gives a plausible regime, such as a large recruitment pool with many applicants in each profile group.

Call the lead problem Continuous Exact-Advice Max-Min Selection. An instance has \(d\) dimensions, coefficients \(c_k>0\), a capacity fraction \(\kappa\in(0,1]\), and a path \(t(s)\in\{0,1\}^d\) for \(s\in[0,1]\), where \(s\) measures the population mass arriving so far. The path has \(\tau\) distinct profiles with positive mass. The recruiter knows \(d\), the coefficients, \(\kappa\), and the exact marginal masses
\[ a_k=\int_0^1 t_k(s)\,ds, \]
but sees the profile path only as it arrives. At each point, the recruiter admits a fraction \(x(s)\in[0,1]\) of the arriving mass, using only the path prefix seen so far; total admitted mass must be at most \(\kappa\). For a randomized policy, dimension \(k\)’s utility is
\[ U_k=c_k\,\mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^1 x(s)t_k(s)\,ds\right]. \]
The objective is \(\min_k U_k\). The offline benchmark knows the full path and maximizes the same objective under the same capacity. The policy’s performance is its worst-case ratio to that benchmark, over paths with the same advice. Here \(\tau\) counts distinct profiles, not arrivals; a finite computational version should also report the path encoding size and bit length.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in the paper. It says that, even with exact aggregate marginal advice, there is a family of instances on which no online policy achieves a competitive ratio better than \(O(1/\sqrt d)\). I expect that lower bound to carry over: replace each candidate in the paper’s construction by a cohort of identical profile mass and scale capacity with population size. Replicating each candidate many times makes the population much larger than the profile support while preserving the ambiguity about which dimensions the early decisions need to protect. The continuous question is to construct such a family of mass paths and prove the same lower bound for every causal policy.

The matching algorithmic anchor is Theorem 4.6, also proved in the paper. For every advice-augmented instance with positive offline optimum, its policy guarantees a ratio of at least
\[ \frac{1}{4(2+\sqrt 2)\sqrt d}. \]
The policy uses geometric guesses for the unknown optimum, controlled greedy allocation, and marginal-based protection; online rounding preserves the expected dimension utilities. The continuous question is to give a causal mass-admission rule with this guarantee on every continuous instance. Its updates can be expressed in mass units, with the known marginal masses tracking the remaining opportunities. Together, the two mirrors predict an optimal competitive ratio of \(\Theta(1/\sqrt d)\). The theorem numbering and statements above are from the paper’s [arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.10389v3).

Under ChoCo’s trichotomy, the algorithmic mirror looks Class A-like: it has a constructive online guarantee, and the offline benchmark reduces to a finite LP over the \(\tau\) profile masses. The lower bound is an online information limitation that the continuous version should inherit. It is not a P-versus-NP hardness result. The paper does discuss NP-hardness for deterministic offline max-min selection in §7.1, but that discussion is not a numbered theorem, so I have not used it as an anchor.

The main weakness is that the paper’s results concern an adversarial arrival path, while ChoCo’s usual society input is a distribution over types. A static distribution alone cannot express the information revealed over time: giving the recruiter the full profile masses in advance would change the problem. The mirror therefore has to treat society as a path of arriving type mass, with only marginal totals revealed beforehand. That is a more specialized online model, but it preserves the population as the continuous object. It also has unusually direct support in the paper’s own motivation: its aggregate marginal advice is described as information estimated from large applicant populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed model’s \(\tau\) counts profiles, while the online problem also depends on arrival position. An early cohort and a late cohort with the same profile play different strategic roles. For general streams, a complete type must include its arrival phase, or the instance must retain an order-resolved mass path; profile support alone does not compress the input. That makes the proposed mirror an online fluid-selection problem whose difficulty is encoded in the arrival path, rather than a complexity question over a distribution of voter-like types.

This weakens the proposed \(\tau\)-based framing, but it does not defeat a better mirror. Restrict arrivals to a fixed number of cohort phases and treat a type as a profile-phase pair. Population size can then grow while the number of complete types stays moderate. Theorem 3.4’s construction carries over: replace each candidate by a cohort of mass, scale capacity with total population, and preserve the identical marginal masses and hidden-group ambiguity. Fractional admission still obeys the same averaging argument, so the \(O(1/\sqrt d)\) lower bound survives.

Theorem 4.6 carries over at least as directly. Its policy already chooses fractional admissions and tracks linear utility and remaining marginal mass. On a finite-phase mass stream, sums become mass-weighted sums, and the same budget and protection argument applies. The result would likely be the same competitive guarantee in mass units.

So my best negative argument is about fit and marginal novelty: the two anchors classify an online information problem by \(d\), while the continuous version must retain chronology and appears to inherit both bounds through rescaling. That is a reason to give it lower priority as a ChoCo complexity project. It is not a sound reason to say no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A finite-phase high-multiplicity regime is plausible, and the programme permits a well-posed mirror whose answer simply matches the discrete result. I cannot honestly defeat both anchors; the negative case is weak.
