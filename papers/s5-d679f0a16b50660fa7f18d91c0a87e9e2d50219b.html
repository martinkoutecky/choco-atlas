The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of firm types \(F\) and worker types \(W\), rational masses \(p_f,q_w\), strict rankings over counterpart types, and two explicitly listed rational lotteries over stable flows, compute their least upper and greatest lower bounds under each side's stochastic-dominance order, or report when a requested bound does not exist. A stable flow \(x\) satisfies \(x_{fw}\ge 0\), \(\sum_w x_{fw}\le p_f\), \(\sum_f x_{fw}\le q_w\), and, for each \(f,w\), \(\left(p_f-\sum_{w':\,w'\succeq_f w}x_{fw'}\right)\left(q_w-\sum_{f':\,f'\succeq_w f}x_{f'w}\right)=0\).

The model it lives in

A one-to-one matching market with finitely many firm and worker types, rational type masses, and rankings over counterpart types. The decision variables are stable mass flows \(x_{fw}\ge 0\); each input outcome is a finite lottery over such flows, and the output is the side-wise join or meet as a finite lottery over stable flows.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 2's join and meet computation in a one-to-one type-mass setting; it leaves the decreasing-representation result, the full many-to-many domain, and the paper's other results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the joins and meets for explicit rational lotteries be computed in time polynomial in the number of types and input bit length?
  • Which type-mass stable flows can be represented by lotteries over stable matchings in finite clone markets?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper gives no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or belonging to another complexity class. Its closest computational anchor is Corollary 2, proved in the paper: for two rational random stable matchings, take \(e\) to be the least common multiple of their lottery-weight denominators and expand each lottery into \(e\) equally weighted stable matchings. Pair the entries in those expansions and apply the deterministic stable-matching join or meet term by term. The resulting averages are the random matchings’ join and meet, for either side of the market. This is a constructive computation result, but it gives no running-time bound.

A plausible mirror is Type-Mass Random-Stable Join/Meet. Consider a one-to-one market with finitely many firm types \(F\) and worker types \(W\). Each type is a complete description of agents who share the same ranking over counterpart types and the outside option. Type \(f\) has rational mass \(p_f\), and type \(w\) has rational mass \(q_w\). A deterministic population matching is a flow \(x_{fw}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_w x_{fw}\leq p_f\) and \(\sum_f x_{fw}\leq q_w\). Every unit of mass gets at most one partner; fractional flows mean that a cohort is divided across partners.

Call \(x\) stable if no pair of types has positive mass on both sides who would strictly prefer to match with each other. Precisely, for every \(f,w\),
\[ \left(p_f-\sum_{w':\,w'\succeq_f w}x_{fw'}\right) \left(q_w-\sum_{f':\,f'\succeq_w f}x_{f'w}\right)=0. \]
A random stable population matching is a finite lottery over such stable flows. Its type-pair marginal is \(X_{fw}=\sum_k\lambda_k x^k_{fw}\). Order these outcomes by first-order stochastic dominance within each type: for example, \(X\succeq_F Y\) when, for every firm type \(f\) and every worker-type cutoff \(w\), \(\sum_{w':\,w'\succeq_f w}X_{fw'}\geq\sum_{w':\,w'\succeq_f w}Y_{fw'}\); define the worker-side order analogously.

The problem is: given the rational type masses and two rational finite lotteries over stable mass flows, compute their least upper bound and greatest lower bound under each side’s order. Return each result as an explicit finite lottery over stable flows, or report that the requested bound does not exist. This asks about the underlying matching outcomes, not an encoding.

I would expect this restricted problem to be Class A, if the mass-valued stable outcomes retain the deterministic lattice operations and the lottery-support splitting can be done in time polynomial in the type count and input bit length. The regime could be a large placement market—such as a residency or apprenticeship cohort—in which many applicants and positions share a relatively small number of complete preference and eligibility templates. Here the number of agents is much larger than the number of types. The mirror covers only the paper’s random-stable lattice operations, in a one-to-one subcase; it does not claim to extend the paper’s full many-to-many results.

The authors would likely recognize the question: it preserves their random stable set, dominance order, and join/meet computation while making the population itself type-mass based. The main weakness is also substantial: agents within a type are interchangeable over counterpart types, which induces indifference among named individuals. The paper instead assumes complete, antisymmetric preferences over sets of named agents. This mirror therefore needs a deliberate type-level stability model, and Corollary 2 supplies no complexity guarantee for it. The key follow-up questions are whether the dual lattice survives that aggregation, whether the operations admit a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and bit length \(L\), and how the mass outcomes relate to large finite replications.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has one anchor: Corollary 2. It gives a constructive formula for joins and meets of rational random stable matchings, but it states no complexity result. Its inputs are already-given finite lotteries over already-given stable matchings; it supplies no input encoding, running-time bound, or complexity problem. Algorithms 1 and 2 are also constructive, but the paper gives no complexity analysis for them. So the proposed mirror is not mirroring a computational classification in the sense ChoCo is designed to chart. It would introduce a new question: how hard is it to compute lattice operations after replacing agents with types and masses?

The strongest version of that question would keep the paper’s many-to-many setting: finitely many firm and worker types, rational masses, and a complete type-level description of each agent’s substitutable, LAD choice behavior. A stable outcome would be a mass allocation with a type-level blocking condition, and the task would be to compute joins and meets for explicit rational lotteries over stable allocations.

That formulation faces a basic modeling fork. If a type preserves the paper’s strict preferences over named individuals, its preference description grows with the population and records identities that the type-mass model is meant to compress. If agents instead rank counterpart types, they are indifferent among individuals within a type, and a cohort can split across partners. The proposed blocking condition is a reasonable way to model that situation, but it changes both the preference domain and what counts as a stable matching. In the many-to-many version, type preferences over subsets would also have to become preferences or choice rules over quantities of partner types.

The paper’s lattice proof does not supply the missing bridge. It depends on a finite set of stable matchings, pointwise join and meet operations on those matchings, and a unique finite decreasing representation of each random matching. A continuum of type-level stable flows need not have that representation. A finite-clone construction could help: if every type-level allocation corresponds to a lottery over stable matchings of the cloned market, the mirror becomes an aggregation of the paper’s existing lottery object. If some type-level flows have no such representation, the model needs a new stability and lattice theory before there is a computation problem to analyze. Either way, Corollary 2 does not establish that the proposed operation is the right computational analogue.

This is a case against the anchor, rather than proof that no worthwhile population model exists. Matching cohorts with shared preference templates are plausible, and high-multiplicity matching work supports the regime rather than counting against it. The negative case is therefore weak as a universal claim: its strongest point is that this paper offers an algebraic result about random outcomes, not a computational problem whose continuous-population complexity is waiting to be classified.
