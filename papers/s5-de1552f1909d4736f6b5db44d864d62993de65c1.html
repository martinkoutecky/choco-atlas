The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results are analytical characterizations of potential-maximizing coalition structures and government formation.

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 1 (proved in the paper): when each two-party coalition has value \(C\), while the grand coalition has value below \(C\), every potential-maximizing profile produces either a single bilateral link or the complete three-party link structure. That is a precise equilibrium-structure result, but it says nothing about the complexity of finding such a profile. The paper’s propositions on two- and three-party governments have the same limitation. So none qualifies as an anchor under the requested rule, and there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The possible positive intuition is that one might study many parties grouped by policy position and seat share, with mass representing their share of the chamber and cooperation links as the decision variable. But this paper’s game has exactly three strategically distinct parties; extending its Myerson-value potential and link formation to a continuum would require new modeling choices. The continuous policy line in the paper is continuity of the policy space, not of the population. That extension might be a future research direction, but it is too speculative to present honestly as a mirror of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes potential maximizing link structures in a three player game; it does not classify the complexity of computing them. In the paper’s setting, a direct search has only \(4^3=64\) strategy profiles. The government formation propositions likewise give conditions for outcomes in a fixed three party model, not an algorithmic problem over a growing population.

The strongest rescue would generalize the model to many parties, represented by a distribution over policy positions, seat shares, and other party characteristics, then ask for a potential maximizing government. But a party’s identity matters to the object being optimized: parties choose links with particular other parties, and the Myerson value depends on that link graph. Replacing identical parties by a mass would lose which links form. Treating each type as a bloc would preserve some aggregate weight but change the players’ choices and the resulting game.

An atomless limit poses a further problem. Individual parties and bilateral links have zero mass, while the paper’s coalition structures and surplus allocations are defined for identifiable players and links. Giving the limit a meaningful cooperative game would require new machinery and a new equilibrium problem. That could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new model rather than a continuous version of a named result here.

The policy line is continuous, and seat shares are numerical weights, but neither makes the society continuous: the strategic population remains three parties. One could build a separate model where a continuous electorate generates those shares, but that would add a population layer without producing a computational question from this paper.

I cannot establish the literal universal claim that no conceivable scenario could make a continuous coalition model worthwhile. The stronger, supportable negative is narrower: the proposed many party extension is speculative, and this paper supplies no computational anchor for it. The proponent’s own case concedes that point.
