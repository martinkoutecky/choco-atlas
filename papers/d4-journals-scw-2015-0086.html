What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the standard scoring-rule Maskin-monotonicity question addressed by Proposition 1 and Theorem 1; it leaves the rule-count formula, the Nash-implementation construction, and the generalized scoring-rule results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow. The paper studies exactly the kind of population a continuous mirror could represent: many voters grouped by complete preference type. Let a type be a ranking \(R\in L(A)\), and let \(\mu_R\) be the fraction of society with that ranking. The scoring rule then chooses the alternatives maximizing \(\sum_R \mu_R s_{\sigma(a,R)}\). A monotone change for a candidate \(c\) can be represented by a coupling from old rankings to new ones, supported only on pairs \((R,R')\) with \(L(R,c)\subseteq L(R',c)\).

Strictly, though, the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind the brief requires. Its closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: for a score vector \(s\), \(F_s\) is Maskin-monotonic if and only if \(k^*(s)>m(n-1)/n\), where \(k^*(s)\) is the first index \(k\) with \(s_k>s_{k+1}\). This condition gives a direct recognition test for the finite-voter case, but the theorem does not formulate a computational problem or state a complexity bound. Calling it an “in P” anchor would be my inference, not the paper’s claim.

If that inference is accepted, one precise mirror is Atomless Maskin-Monotonicity Recognition. Given \(m\ge3\) and a rational score vector \(s\), decide whether \(F_s\) preserves every winner under every measurable population change that weakly improves that candidate’s position for each unit of mass. A “no” answer comes with a candidate, rational finite-support societies \(\mu,\nu\), and a coupling between them witnessing that the candidate wins at \(\mu\) and loses at \(\nu\). This is the same scoring rule and the same monotonicity question, with voter proportions replacing voter counts.

The paper’s Proposition 1, proved here, says that when \(m\le n\), no scoring rule is Maskin-monotonic. It implies that the universal atomless version has a degenerate answer: for any \(m\ge3\), choose \(n\ge m\) while avoiding the excluded pair \((3,4)\), partition unit mass into \(n\) equal pieces, and use the finite counterexample. Thus every scoring rule fails the atomless test. Theorem 1 points the same way: its finite-population threshold eventually leaves no possible \(k^*(s)\).

That gives a plausible population mirror of the paper’s standard scoring-rule characterization, but it is a weak ChoCo case. The paper’s contribution is axiomatic, and the continuous recognition question collapses to “no” for every input rather than opening a complexity landscape. A richer question—such as finding the minimum mass that must change rankings to overturn a winner—could be computationally interesting, but this paper supplies no named result anchoring it. My weakest point is therefore the central one: the closest mirror preserves the paper’s subject, but not the programme’s computational focus.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The type model is natural here. A population over rankings, with a monotone change represented by a coupling, preserves the paper’s subject cleanly. The weakness is the proposed anchor: Theorem 1 characterizes an axiom; it does not state a computational result. Its condition can be checked by scanning the score vector and comparing \(k^*(s)\) with \(m(n-1)/n\), but turning that into a recognition problem is an inference by the proponent. The resulting question remains whether a rule satisfies Maskin-monotonicity, an axiomatic property rather than the kind of computational problem ChoCo is charting.

Proposition 1 does not supply a second computational anchor. A unit-mass society can contain equal-mass groups encoding any finite profile, so the universal continuous axiom includes the finite cases covered by that proposition. But the theorem’s \(n\) counts indivisible voters. In a genuine mass-transfer model, a type’s mass can be split into arbitrarily many pieces; the support size \(\tau\) does not play the role of \(n\). Keeping \(n\) as a granularity parameter would restore the finite-voter model. Dropping it leaves an axiom over distributions, not a computational result about a high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 2 and the implementation proposition have the same limitation: they characterize or construct mechanisms, without studying computational complexity.

The strongest upgraded mirror I can see is a robustness problem: given a society where \(a\) wins, find the least mass or swap cost of changes that weakly improve \(a\)’s position for every affected unit but make \(a\) lose. That could become an LP with a ranking-pricing problem, and it fits ChoCo’s computational interests. It also exposes the limit of the negative case: this may be worthwhile work. But it adds an instance society, a perturbation cost, and an optimization objective absent from the paper. It is a new computational problem motivated by the paper’s axiom, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

So the case against rests on scope, not on the population model being implausible: the paper offers a strong axiomatic mirror and no named complexity result for that mirror to carry into ChoCo. I would call the negative case substantial but not airtight, because the robustness extension could be compelling if the authors recognize it as their problem.
