The continuous mirror question

Given a rational \(c\in(0,1)\) and a piecewise-rational valuation distribution \(\mu\) on \([0,1]\), draw \(n\) independent valuations from \(\mu\), apply the paper's random priority order and bluff equilibrium, and let \(P_n\) be the probability that the winner has less than the highest valuation. Compute \(\pi(\mu,c)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}P_n\), exactly or within additive error \(\varepsilon\).

The model it lives in

Valuation \(v\in[0,1]\) is a type, and \(\mu(dv)\) is the mass distribution over types. For each finite approximation, sample bidders from \(\mu\), run the paper's auction with parameter \(c\), and evaluate the resulting inefficient-allocation risk; the target is its large-population limit superior.

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover asymptotic inefficient-allocation probability and the expected round and query rates. They leave the bluff-equilibrium proof, finite-termination result, and expected welfare-loss bound without separate continuous questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does \(P_n\) converge for every rational \(c\in(0,1)\), and can \(\pi(\mu,c)\) be computed or approximated effectively from the recurrence?
  • What are the corresponding exact asymptotic rates for expected rounds and bidder queries?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large-population limit of the auction’s performance, rather than a new allocation rule. Let the society be described by a continuous valuation distribution \(\mu(dv)=f(v)\,dv\) on \([0,1]\), with a piecewise-rational density as its finite input representation. A type is a bidder’s valuation \(v\); \(\mu(A)\) is the fraction of the population with values in \(A\). The object remains indivisible. For each \(n\), draw \(n\) valuations from \(\mu\), apply the paper’s random priority order and bluff strategy, and run the same \(c\)-fraction auction. The continuous questions ask for the limiting performance of these finite populations as \(n\) grows.

This fits a high-volume single-object market in which a large bidder pool follows a stable valuation distribution. The relevant actions remain the paper’s public yes/no responses at each query; the aggregate object is the mass of active bidders and their responses. The output is still one winner. Since an atomless population has no individual winner, the continuous question must be stated through limits of finite-population performance measures.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper: for every \(n\), the probability of an inefficient allocation under the bluff equilibrium is less than \(c\). Its mirror is Inefficiency-Limit. Given \(\mu\) and rational \(c\in(0,1)\), compute
\[ \pi(\mu,c)=\limsup_{n\to\infty} \Pr[\text{the winner’s value is below the highest value in the }n\text{-bidder society}]. \]
A solution is an exact value, or an interval of width at most \(\varepsilon\) containing it. The theorem immediately gives \(\pi(\mu,c)\le c\). The new question is the exact asymptotic risk profile, not whether that bound holds. I consider this the strongest mirror because it keeps the paper’s central objective—avoiding inefficient allocation—while making the population distribution the object being studied.

Two further anchors support the paper’s explicit efficiency–running-time tradeoff. Theorem 5.4, proved here, says that for \(c\le \tfrac12\) and \(n\ge2\), the expected number of rounds is at most
\[ e^{(1-c)/c^2}\left(\log_{1/(1-c)} n+1\right). \]
Its mirror, Round-Scale, takes \(\mu,c\) and asks for
\[ \rho(\mu,c)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E[R_n]}{\ln n}, \]
where \(R_n\) is the number of rounds. Theorem 5.7, also proved here, says that for \(n\ge2\), the expected number of queries is at most
\[ e^{(1-c)/c^2}\left(\frac2c+\frac12\right)(n+1). \]
Its mirror, Query-Scale, asks for
\[ \kappa(\mu,c)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E[Q_n]}{n}, \]
where \(Q_n\) counts individual bidder queries. In both problems, the instance is the encoded distribution and \(c\), and a solution is an exact asymptotic rate or an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate interval. The theorems already imply finite upper bounds on these rates. I would expect the exact profiles to be tractable through the paper’s recurrences and a limit analysis; the corresponding design question is to choose \(c\) to meet an asymptotic inefficiency target at minimum query cost.

These mirrors cover the paper’s equilibrium performance and its round, query, and inefficiency results. They do not turn fractional outcomes or the prior alone into the continuous object: the population measure is the object, and the auction is evaluated along finite populations approaching it. The paper proves these anchors itself; it does not give an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result for an algorithmic problem. These are operational query-complexity results, so the positive case should not oversell them as a new complexity classification.

The weakest point is that this is an asymptotic performance problem, not the kind of instance-solving complexity landscape ChoCo chiefly seeks. Also, if a bidder’s exact value is their complete type, a smooth \(\mu\) has infinitely many possible types and does not give the finite-support, many-agents-per-type regime in the programme’s standard model. The proposed mirror is therefore strongest as a continuous-population limit of the paper’s own performance analysis; whether the authors would accept that limit as their problem’s continuous form is the real point to test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection applies to all three anchors: the paper analyzes a fixed auction’s performance, not the complexity of solving a computational social-choice problem on a given society. Theorem 6.1 bounds an equilibrium allocation’s error probability; Theorems 5.4 and 5.7 bound the auction’s expected rounds and queries. Those are real and useful results, but taking their limits as \(n\to\infty\) asks for asymptotic mechanism-performance analysis. It does not ask what can be computed from a continuous population, or how the complexity of a social-choice problem changes when that population is represented by masses.

For Inefficiency-Limit, the proposed quantity still compares the winner with the highest-valued bidder in a finite random sample. In an atomless population, no individual has positive mass, and the essential supremum of values need not be an attained value belonging to a winner. Keep the finite samples and the quantity is well-defined, but the object being studied is then a limit of finite-auction order statistics, not an auction run on the continuous society. Moreover, the paper’s recurrence for inefficiency depends on \(c\) and \(n\), not on the valuation distribution. Even the stronger design question—choose \(c\) to meet an asymptotic risk target at minimum query cost—looks like optimizing a parameter of this particular mechanism, rather than a complexity question whose input is the society.

For Round-Scale and Query-Scale, the mismatch is sharper. “Running time” here means rounds and bidder queries in an interactive auction; it is not the computation needed to find an outcome from an instance. A mass society has no individual bidders to query one by one, so the proposed rates preserve the finite-\(n\) protocol and then normalize its costs. The paper’s recurrences already describe those costs, and their transition probabilities do not depend on \(\mu\). Thus a continuous density is not doing computational work in either mirror. Changing to aggregate mass updates would give a different process whose relation to the paper’s expected query counts needs to be established.

There is a reasonable stronger scenario: a large auction with a finite set of valuation cohorts, each containing many indistinguishable bidders. That makes high multiplicity plausible and gives positive mass to each type. But the paper assumes continuously distributed private values, so exact realized values are generally all distinct; priority order and individual yes/no responses remain part of the mechanism. A cohort version would need to specify how the mass process selects its single winner and handles ties, then establish that its efficiency and query results mirror this auction. It may be a worthwhile new model, but it does not rescue these particular asymptotic anchors without that work.

So the negative case is substantial against the proponent’s three proposals: they turn finite-auction performance bounds into population-size limits, without yielding the computational-complexity landscape ChoCo targets. It is weaker against the literal universal claim that no continuous mirror could be worthwhile. A high-multiplicity cohort auction is plausible, and I cannot rule out a version its authors would recognize. The paper gives less support for that mirror than the proponent claims, but the absolute no-mirror conclusion is not airtight.
