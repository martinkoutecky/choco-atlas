I can’t make a compliant anchor-based case: the paper contains no named result about computational complexity or algorithms. Its closest results are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in the paper. Proposition 1 characterizes Nash equilibria in the three-voter model: for \(0\le\lambda<1\), the unique equilibrium proposals are \((Q_1,Q_2,Q_3)\); at \(\lambda=1\), there are multiple equilibria; and for \(\lambda>1\), the unique equilibrium is \((Q_5,Q_5,Q_5)\). Proposition 2 gives the corresponding five-voter result: proposals \((Q_1,Q_2,Q_3,Q_4,Q_5)\) for \(0\le\lambda<1\), multiple equilibria at \(\lambda=1\), and \((Q_5,Q_5,Q_5,Q_5,Q_5)\) for \(\lambda>1\). These are equilibrium characterizations for a fixed small game, not results that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The numbered Results 1–4 are empirical findings, not computational results.

There is a plausible research direction: model a large workforce with types defined by productivity, union eligibility, and preference parameter, and masses representing population shares. Type-contingent wage proposals would determine employment mass; the wage could be selected from the median proposal with probability \(q\), or from a randomly sampled worker’s proposal with probability \(1-q\). One could ask how to compute equilibrium proposals and resulting employment. But that would be a new computational question, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. In particular, the continuum limit needs care: an individual’s proposal has no effect on the median of a nonatomic population, which changes the accountability mechanism central to the paper.

So the positive case is limited to a potentially interesting continuum extension of the paper’s wage-bargaining model. There is no eligible anchor from which to state the requested prover-ready mirror problem or predict its complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither of the proponent’s anchors is a computational result. Proposition 1 characterizes equilibria in a three-voter game, and Proposition 2 gives the corresponding characterization for five voters. Both use five fixed productivity levels and a common preference parameter. Neither poses a computational problem over an input family or establishes an algorithmic or complexity result. Results 1–4 are empirical findings. Turning one of the propositions into “compute equilibrium for a distribution of worker types” would create a new question, rather than mirror a computational result in the paper.

The best version of that new question would give a large workforce masses of types defined by productivity, union eligibility, and preferences, then ask for equilibrium wage proposals under a weighted-median rule and a random-dictator treatment. But with a genuinely nonatomic population, an individual deviation changes neither the median nor the distribution of a randomly selected worker’s proposal. In the \(q=1\) treatment, an individual cannot affect the median; when \(q<1\), the individual’s chance of being the selected proposer is zero. The accountability mechanism that motivates the paper’s comparison therefore disappears from the continuum game. Treating each type as a single strategic player could restore influence, but it makes each type a coordinated bloc; identical agents do not become a coalition simply by sharing a type.

One could instead keep a finite voting group or dictator while making the workforce continuous. That may be a sensible labor-economics extension, but it places the strategic power in a small set of decision-makers and adds an institution the paper does not model. The continuous population then supplies the employment consequences of their decisions, rather than furnishing a continuous-population mirror of Propositions 1 or 2. Keeping three or five voters, as those propositions do, does not solve the problem: neither result scales the strategic population.

So the negative case is strong on fit with ChoCo’s programme, though it cannot rule out every worthwhile adjacent study. The obstacle is not that a continuous answer would be easy or already known. It is that the paper offers no computational result to mirror, and the most direct continuum version removes the individual influence on outcomes that its central treatment is designed to study.
