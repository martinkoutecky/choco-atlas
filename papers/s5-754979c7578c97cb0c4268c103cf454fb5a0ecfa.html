The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible computational anchors in this paper. Theorem 2, for example, is proved here and says that a rule satisfies composition, dummy, splitting-proofness of museums, splitting-proofness of consortia, and consortia consistency if and only if it is the proportional-proportional rule. The other numbered theorems likewise characterize allocation rules by axioms. None asserts a complexity bound, hardness result, or algorithmic formulation that the paper computes with. So there is no valid one-per-anchor prover question under your criteria.

The strongest natural continuous-population object is still easy to state. Imagine a large museum-pass market with a fixed set of museums, consortia, and pass prices. A type \(t\) records which pass a visitor buys and the nonempty subset \(S_t\) of eligible museums they visit; if prices vary by visitor, include the price in the type. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For a named rule such as proportional-proportional, the question would be: given the support of \(\mu\), the prices, and the consortium structure, compute the per-capita allocation vector \(r\) by weighting each type’s per-visitor allocation by \(\mu_t\). Multiplying \(r\) by the number of visitors gives total allocations. Large visitor cohorts with relatively few pass-and-visit patterns make this a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors would likely recognize it as their allocation problem over population masses.

That candidate calculation is directly obtained by replacing sums over pass holders with weighted sums over types, so it is polynomial in the explicit support and input bit length. It is a sensible population mirror, but the paper does not name or study this computational problem, and its characterization theorems do not anchor it. The missing computational result is the decisive weakness in the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror. Its main theorems characterize allocation rules by axioms; they do not analyze an algorithmic problem, its complexity, or an optimization formulation. That is decisive under the requested anchoring standard: there is no named computational result here from which to pose a continuous prover question.

The proposed allocation calculation is still a coherent high-multiplicity model. A type can record which pass a visitor buys and which museums they visit; a rational mass over those types gives per-capita revenue and allocations by weighting each type’s contribution. Repeated visitor patterns are plausible, and the paper’s composition axiom supports treating those visitors as interchangeable. So lack of multiplicity, identity dependence, or a meaningful mass interpretation would be poor objections.

But this calculation evaluates an already specified rule. A stronger computational proposal would need to add a task the paper does not study, such as choosing among rules under a stated objective. Translating the paper’s characterizations themselves into continuous form would remain an axiomatic project, not the computational programme’s target. The negative case is therefore strong on fit to the programme, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could be asked: the population mirror is sensible; it simply is not anchored in a computational result of this paper.
