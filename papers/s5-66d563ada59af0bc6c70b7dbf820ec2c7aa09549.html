The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and each type's weak preference order over acceptable types on the other side, choose rational masses \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) on mutually acceptable pairs, subject to \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\nu_b\). For every acceptable pair \((a,b)\), define \(p_{ab}=\mu_a-\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(q_{ab}=\nu_b-\sum_{a'}x_{a'b}+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}\), and require \(p_{ab}=0\) or \(q_{ab}=0\). Find a stable flow whose total matched mass is at least \(2/3\) of the maximum stable mass, in time polynomial in the number of positive-mass types \(\tau\), the type-level preference description, and mass bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A mass flow \(x_{ab}\) matches portions of type cohorts, with each individual receiving at most one integral partner. Stability requires that for every acceptable type pair, at least one side has zero mass that is unmatched or strictly prefers the other type to its current assignment; the objective is to maximize matched mass, approximately.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's \(3/2\)-approximation for one-to-one stable matching with ties; it leaves the paper's unnumbered many-to-many extension aside. Nearby work studies ordinally stable fractional assignments for a fixed set of named agents ([Chen, Roy, and Sorge](https://arxiv.org/html/2011.12259v1)), while work on similar-agent groups gives parameterized results for discrete \(\mathrm{Max\ SMTI}\) ([Meeks and Rastegari](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109)); these are relevant neighbors, but do not restate this type-mass problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(3/2\)-approximation be computed directly in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), without expanding rational masses into individual agents?
  • What is the exact complexity of maximizing stable mass for inputs with small type support?
  • What precise correspondence holds between rational type-mass flows and stable matchings in finite replicated markets?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a narrow positive case around Theorem 1, the paper’s one numbered computational result worth anchoring on. Proved here, it says that GS Modified returns a stable matching with no dangerous alternating paths, hence a \(3/2\)-approximation to a largest stable matching, in \(O(m)\) time, where \(m\) is the total length of the preference lists. In approximation terms, its matching has at least \(2/3\) the cardinality of an optimum stable matching. The theorem’s key object is still the paper’s problem—maximum-cardinality stable matching with ties—not a formulation or encoding.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large centralized matching market, such as a hiring platform, where many candidates and positions fall into a modest number of cohorts. A type on each side specifies a complete preference order over the other side’s types, including acceptable types and ties; members of a type share that order and are indifferent among members of any one counterpart type. Each agent still receives at most one partner. The regime is credible when the population is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of positive-mass types; it is not a claim that every small matching market has a useful continuum limit.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: \(3/2\)-Approximate Maximum Stable Mass Matching. The input consists of type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and the type-level preference orders just described. For every mutually acceptable type pair \((a,b)\), choose a rational mass \(x_{ab}\ge 0\), subject to
\[ \sum_b x_{ab}\le \mu_a,\qquad \sum_a x_{ab}\le \nu_b. \]
The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{(a,b)}x_{ab}\), among stable assignments.

Stability has a direct mass interpretation. Let \(r_a=\mu_a-\sum_b x_{ab}\) and \(s_b=\nu_b-\sum_a x_{ab}\) be unmatched mass. For an acceptable pair \((a,b)\), define
\[ p_{ab}=r_a+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'},\qquad q_{ab}=s_b+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}. \]
These are the masses of type-\(a\) and type-\(b\) agents who are single or strictly prefer the other type to their current assignment. Require \(p_{ab}=0\) or \(q_{ab}=0\) for every acceptable pair: if both are positive, some positive mass on each side could form blocking pairs. A solution is such a stable rational flow with total matched mass at least \(2/3\) of the maximum stable mass. The computational question is whether this can be found in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the type-level preference description, and the mass bit length \(L\).

This is genuinely a population mirror: \(x_{ab}\) records what fraction of each cohort is matched across the type pair. Fractional values distribute assignments across agents in a continuum; they do not give any individual a fractional partner. Replicating the rational masses into sufficiently many identical agents recovers a finite stable-marriage instance of the paper’s kind.

For this approximation question I expect Class A. GS Modified achieves its guarantee through proposal and acceptance steps over preference lists, so an aggregate version that moves rational masses between type pairs seems plausible. The paper’s \(O(m)\) bound, however, does not establish the desired compressed runtime: expanding masses into individual agents could take time depending on their denominator. The continuous algorithm would need to show that the dangerous-path argument and proposal state can be handled using the type support and rational masses directly.

The weakest point is that the paper’s theorem is proved for named individuals and does not analyze this mass-level stability condition. The authors might view cohort preferences and fractional assignment flows as a substantive extension. The case survives because the mirror preserves their central question—how large a stable matching can be—and the same blocking logic, while making the high-multiplicity assumption explicit. Its scope is Theorem 1’s \(3/2\)-approximation for one-to-one stable matching with ties; the paper’s unnumbered many-to-many extension is not a second anchor.

Two follow-on questions are whether the exact maximum stable mass remains hard for small-support inputs, and whether stable mass assignments admit a precise two-way correspondence with limits of stable matchings in replicated finite markets. The introduction reports discrete NP-hardness of the exact maximum-cardinality problem, citing Manlove et al.; that is useful context, but it is not a numbered result of this paper and is not my anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the theorem is built for an explicit graph of named people. Its proof tracks individual proposals, partners, and dangerous paths. In real matching markets, applicants and employers may rank one another idiosyncratically; grouping them into a few types can require treating everyone in a cohort as interchangeable and indifferent among members of a counterpart cohort. If exact type refinement makes the support large, the hoped-for compression disappears.

That pressure does not defeat the better mirror. A centralized market with standardized applicant cohorts and employer categories can plausibly use rankings over categories, with indifference within each category. For type masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\), let \(x_{ab}\) be matched mass, subject to the usual capacity constraints. For each acceptable pair \((a,b)\), let \(p_{ab}\) and \(q_{ab}\) be the masses on each side that are unmatched or strictly prefer the other type to their current assignment. Requiring \(p_{ab}=0\) or \(q_{ab}=0\) gives a well-defined weak-stability condition; maximizing \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\) asks for the largest stable mass. With rational masses, a common-denominator replication realizes the flow as an ordinary matching of clones. The population limit therefore keeps both the objective and the blocking logic meaningful.

The proof’s proposal-by-proposal algorithm may not aggregate into a polynomial-time algorithm in support size and bit length. That is the computational question a prover would need to answer; it is not a reason the question lacks value. Related work on fractional matchings studies ordinal stability and optimization over fractional assignments of a finite set of agents, so it is close, but it does not itself answer the succinct, type-mass population question ([Chen, Roy, and Sorge](https://arxiv.org/html/2011.12259v1)). Work on stable matching with groups of similar agents likewise makes the type regime more credible; it supports the mirror rather than colliding with it ([Cseh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109)).

So I cannot honestly defeat Theorem 1’s anchor. The mirror is narrower than the paper’s arbitrary-person setting, and its value depends on finding an algorithm that works directly on the compressed population. Those are real limitations, but they do not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
