What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 1’s finite nonexistence claim by asking whether efficiency and strong envy-freeness can coexist over availability types with mass; the paper gives no computational result beyond that claim.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result. Its one named theorem is a combinatorial impossibility result, so it is the strongest honest anchor for a feasibility mirror, though not an NP-hardness or algorithmic classification.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for every integer \(q\ge 2\), there are \(q\)-sport assignment problems with no assignment that is both efficient and strongly envy-free; consequently, there is no assignment that is both fair and strongly envy-free. This is a genuine assignment-feasibility result, and the paper proves it here rather than citing it.

The plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large recurring sports group whose members fall into a small number \(\tau\) of weekday-availability patterns. For example, many members might share each of a few schedules. A type is an availability set \(S\subseteq [m]\), and \(\mu_S\) is that type’s fraction of the group. Members of a type are interchangeable for the paper’s model: they care only about which days they play, not who their partners are. The number of people can be very large while \(\tau\) remains small.

My lead mirror is Continuous \(q\)-Sport Efficient and Strongly Envy-Free Feasibility. An instance gives \(q\), \(m\), and a finite support of availability types with positive rational masses \(\mu_S\) summing to \(1\). Each type consists of an atomless population of that mass. An allocation assigns every person an integral schedule \(B\subseteq S\); equivalently, it can be represented by masses \(\lambda_{S,B}\) of type \(S\) receiving schedule \(B\). On each day, the scheduled people must be partitionable into matches of exactly \(q\) people. Efficiency means maximizing the total mass of assigned person-days, \(\sum_{S,B}\lambda_{S,B}|B|\). Strong envy-freeness means that whenever \(|S|>|T|\), every person of type \(S\) receives at least as many games as every person of type \(T\). The problem asks for an allocation satisfying both properties, or a report that none exists.

In this atomless model, the answer is always yes: assign every person of type \(S\) to every day in \(S\), so \(\lambda_{S,S}=\mu_S\). This maximizes assigned person-days, and a person with more available days then gets more games. Each positive-mass day cohort can be partitioned into \(q\)-person matches; with no court-capacity constraint in the paper, the fixed match size does not limit the mass scheduled. The problem is therefore tractable, with a compact solution computable in \(O(\tau m)\) time. The answer is a result of the continuous model, not something Theorem 1 itself proves.

This is a recognizable mirror of the paper’s underlying assignment problem: it keeps availability types, integral individual schedules, the objective, the fairness comparison, and matches of size \(q\). The continuous change is what happens to the finite divisibility constraint. For instance, in the paper’s \(q=4\) illustration, residual players arise because a finite number of available people need not divide evenly into fours. Under high multiplicity, that rounding residue becomes negligible; in the atomless model, it vanishes. Further questions include how fast finite instances approach this limit, and whether per-day court capacities or individual quotas restore an impossibility.

My weakest point is also the reason the mirror is tractable: without a court limit, the continuous population can use all available person-days, so the paper’s impossibility disappears in a particularly simple way. Adding scaled court capacities might yield a richer problem, but those capacities are absent from the paper and would need to be defended as part of the mirror. And because Theorem 1 is an impossibility theorem rather than a complexity result, this case supports a narrow continuous feasibility question, not a broader complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only named anchor is Theorem 1, and it proves an impossibility for finite assignments; the paper gives no complexity result or algorithmic classification to mirror. So the case depends on whether continuous feasibility remains a substantive computational problem.

For the proposed mirror, let each type be an availability set \(S\). Assign every person of type \(S\) to every day in \(S\). This maximizes assigned person-days, and anyone with more available days receives more games, so the assignment is efficient and strongly envy-free. With an atomless population and no court limit, any day’s mass can be grouped into \(q\)-person matches: the match constraint leaves no indivisibility remainder. This works for every support and every \(q\). The finite theorem’s obstruction—leftover people who cannot fill a match—has disappeared, along with the feasibility question’s computational content.

Trying to preserve that obstruction by requiring the number of assigned people on each day to be divisible by \(q\) requires a finite population scale \(N\): one must test whether \(N d_j\) is divisible by \(q\), where \(d_j\) is the assigned mass. The same distribution can have different answers at different scales—for example, a day with mass \(1/3\) gives one person when \(N=3\), but two when \(N=6\), for \(q=2\). Supplying \(N\) makes this a high-multiplicity discrete problem, rather than a problem about the continuous society alone.

Per-capita court capacities or individual quotas could restore scarcity and support a separate continuous allocation problem. But capacities are absent here, and the theorem’s formal model explicitly omits quotas. Those extensions do not give Theorem 1 a nontrivial continuous mirror. The negative case is strongest against that sole anchor; it cannot rule out a worthwhile quota-based extension, which the paper’s footnote makes plausible, but such a project would study constraints the theorem does not address.
