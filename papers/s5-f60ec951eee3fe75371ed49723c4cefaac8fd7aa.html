The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: assignment mechanisms with many applicants and few shared preference types have a natural continuous-population analogue of this paper’s local-sufficiency question. But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor that mirror under your criterion.

The closest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in this paper. Theorem 1 says that, in any setting, if a mechanism is \(r\)-locally partially strategyproof, then it is \(r^2\)-partially strategyproof. Theorem 2 says that, for any setting with at least four objects and any \(\varepsilon>0\), there are an \(r\in(0,1)\) and a mechanism that is \(r\)-locally partially strategyproof but not \((r^{2-\varepsilon})\)-partially strategyproof. These establish an incentive guarantee and the tightness of its exponent. They do not classify a computational problem, give an algorithm, or prove hardness. The paper says local constraints can reduce the burden in automated mechanism design, but does not analyze that computation. Corollary 1 and the cited Facts 1 and 2 are also incentive implications, not complexity results.

A plausible population model would be a large school or hospital assignment market. A type is a strict ranking of the available objects; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants with ranking \(t\); object supplies are scaled per capita. A feasible direct rule gives an applicant a lottery based on their report and the distribution of the other applicants. The continuous question would be: if truthful reporting beats every adjacent-swap report for all utilities in \(\mathrm{URBI}(r)\), does truthful reporting beat every report for all utilities in \(\mathrm{URBI}(r^2)\)? A solution would prove the implication for every feasible rule or give a counterexample. This keeps the paper’s assignment and incentive question intact while making the population the continuous object.

The setting is credible where, say, a large applicant pool has many people but relatively few distinct rankings. The paper’s definitions and Theorem 1 make this a plausible extension: the incentive comparison is about one applicant’s report with the rest of the society fixed, which can be represented by a distribution of other applicants. Theorem 2 also makes tightness a natural follow-up, but its finite-setting counterexamples do not by themselves establish tightness under meaningful scarce-capacity constraints in a continuum market.

That is the soft spot: this is a continuous analogue of the paper’s mathematical question, not an anchor-backed computational mirror of a named complexity result. Its expected direction is the \(r^2\) implication, not a tractability or hardness classification; turning it into a computational problem would require specifying what representation of a mechanism is input and what task is to be computed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 concern incentive guarantees for an already specified mechanism; neither classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithm. The paper’s remark that local constraints can reduce the burden in automated mechanism design is motivation, not a computational result: it specifies no mechanism representation, input, or computational task. Under ChoCo’s scope, that is a decisive gap.

The school or hospital market proposed for Theorem 1 is a coherent population model. With a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, per-capita capacities, and an allocation rule that gives an individual a report-dependent lottery while holding the aggregate fixed, local and global incentive comparisons still make sense. But this model does not turn Theorem 1 into a computational question. The theorem is about what follows from incentive constraints for any given rule; adding \(\mu\) as a parameter supplies no task of computing or optimizing that rule. The continuum also needs a precise representation: an unrestricted rule over a continuum of population distributions is not a finite input. Choosing a finite-dimensional policy class and an objective could produce a computational project, but that would define a new mechanism-synthesis problem, not mirror a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 2’s tightness claim has the same problem. One could ask whether the exponent remains tight among anonymous continuum assignment rules with binding per-capita capacities. That would be a legitimate mathematical extension, and the paper’s finite counterexample does not settle it. But it would still be a question about the strength of an incentive guarantee, not about computational complexity. Adding an algorithmic objective would require further choices the paper does not make.

So the negative case is not that large assignment markets lack sensible types or that individual incentives become vacuous. They can be modeled coherently, and high multiplicity would support that modeling choice. The weakness is specific: neither proposed anchor identifies a computational problem for the continuous population model. A computational mechanism-design project could grow from the paper’s motivation, but this paper does not provide a result for that project to mirror.
