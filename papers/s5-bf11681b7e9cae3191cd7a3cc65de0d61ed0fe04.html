The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying named computational result in this paper, so I can’t build the required anchored case for a continuous mirror. The only numbered result is Proposition 1, proved elsewhere (Part II, §3.1): for every Broomean problem \(B=(E,N,a,s)\), absolute and comparative fairness can both be respected exactly when the weighted proportional allocation \(P(B)\) gives each person no more than their claim amount; when that condition holds, \(P(B)\) meets both requirements. This is a characterization of when two fairness requirements are compatible, not a complexity result about computing an allocation.

The closest positive lead is the absolute priority rule, which the paper presents as a procedure for choosing allocations but does not establish in a numbered result as a polynomial time algorithm or other complexity classification. A plausible follow-up would model a large pool of creditors by finitely many claim types \(t=(a_t,s_t)\), where \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population share, and ask for the per-person allocation prescribed by the continuous extension of the absolute priority rule from a per-person estate. Standardized consumer debt is a plausible regime: many creditors, relatively few distinct claim profiles. The rule’s repeated capping of types whose weighted proportional shares exceed their claims suggests a tractable computation in the number of types.

That is a possible computational question based on the paper’s allocation rule, but it is not an anchor the paper actually supplies. The article’s contribution is chiefly normative and conceptual, and its divisible allocation concerns outcome continuity while the population remains finite. So the positive case has a plausible high multiplicity setting, but no named computational result to mirror as requested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes when absolute and comparative fairness can be satisfied together; it does not classify the complexity of computing an allocation. The absolute priority rule is an allocation procedure, but the paper presents it as a normative rule, not as a complexity result. So neither gives the programme the kind of computational question its mirrors are meant to sharpen.

The strongest version of the proposed extension is sensible: a large pool of creditors with repeated claim types \((a_t,s_t)\), mass \(\mu_t\), and a per-capita estate, with the rule returning a per-person allocation for each type. That preserves the treatment of individual claims rather than letting measure-zero individuals disappear. But it asks how to compute a rule on a new population model; the article does not pose that task or make a computational claim about it. A study of manipulation or robustness would go further, but would need to add an objective and operations absent from the paper. Those could be worthwhile new questions, but they would not be mirrors of its results.

I would not reject the creditor model because the answer might be easy: a well-posed question with a dull answer can still be worthwhile. The case against is narrower. This paper’s results are normative and conceptual, and its stated contribution is a fairness rule, not a computational-complexity landscape. The proponent’s example shows that a high-multiplicity version of fair division can make sense; it does not supply an anchored continuous mirror of this paper. The universal claim that no worthwhile computational extension exists is hard to establish, and this is where the negative case is weakest.
