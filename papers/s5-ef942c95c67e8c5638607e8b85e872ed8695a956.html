The continuous mirror question

Given a finite metric space \((V,d)\), eligible occupied sites \(A\subseteq V\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), and a positive integer \(k\), define \(\rho_\mu(a)=\min\{r:\sum_{b\in A:\,d(a,b)\le r}\mu_b\ge 1/k\}\). Is there a set \(S\subseteq A\), \(|S|\le k\), such that \(d(a,S)\le\rho_\mu(a)\) for every \(a\in A\)?

The model it lives in

Each occupied location is a type, its rational mass \(\mu_a\) is its population share, and each type has the same metric distances and facility eligibility. Choose at most \(k\) occupied sites to minimize the maximum ratio \(d(a,S)/\rho_\mu(a)\); the anchor asks whether this optimum is at most \(1\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3’s 1-fairness feasibility and Theorem 1’s 2-fairness guarantee; it leaves the empirical comparisons and Steiner-center direction outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does weighted 1-fair feasibility admit a sharper complexity classification on fixed-dimensional Euclidean support metrics?
  • Can weighted fairness optimization improve on the factor-two guarantee for Euclidean support metrics?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a weighted population of demand locations. A type is a location at the planning resolution: everyone of that type has the same distances to other types and is eligible for a facility at the same site. Its mass \(\mu_a\) is its fraction of the population. For example, a county might plan for hundreds of thousands of residents represented by a few thousand census-block locations, with \(k=100\) facilities. The facilities remain discrete choices from occupied sites; only the population becomes a distribution. That is close to the paper’s model, which already treats a population as points in a metric space and allows points to coincide.

For a finite metric space \((V,d)\), let \(A\subseteq V\) be the occupied, facility-eligible sites, with positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\). Define the mass neighborhood radius of site \(a\) by
\[ \rho_\mu(a)=\min\left\{r:\sum_{b\in A:\,d(a,b)\le r}\mu_b\ge \frac1k\right\}. \]
This replaces the paper’s “at least \(n/k\) people” threshold with “at least \(1/k\) of the population.” It preserves the density-dependent fairness objective: minimize the maximum ratio of travel distance to neighborhood radius.

My lead is Mass-Weighted 1-Fair \(k\)-Center Feasibility. Given \((V,d), A,\mu\), and \(k\), is there a set \(S\subseteq A\), \(|S|\le k\), such that \(d(a,S)\le\rho_\mu(a)\) for every \(a\in A\)? A solution is the facility set \(S\). Equivalently, this asks whether the weighted version of the paper’s \(\alpha\) objective can achieve \(\alpha\le1\), with the paper’s zero-radius convention.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: “The problem of determining whether 1-fairness can be achieved on a given instance is NP-complete.” The paper establishes membership in NP and proves NP-hardness by reduction from Dominating Set, using a construction with leaves and disjoint 4-cycles to constrain the center choices. The weighted question has the same hardness: give each point in a discrete instance mass \(1/n\). Then \(\rho_\mu(a)\) equals the paper’s neighborhood radius, since a ball has mass at least \(1/k\) exactly when it contains at least \(\lceil n/k\rceil\) points. Thus this mirror is Class B: hardness transfers. With an explicit finite metric or graph metric, the weighted problem is also in NP: a proposed \(S\) can be checked using exact rational mass sums.

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime rather than a different fairness objective. For instance, residents represented by the same block location are indistinguishable for the travel-distance and neighborhood calculations. A rational distribution also has an exact finite-population interpretation: clearing the mass denominators gives integer counts at the support sites. Conversely, any finite population gives rational proportions. The distribution is therefore a compact high-multiplicity representation, potentially far shorter than listing every resident.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 1, also proved in the paper: “2FairKCenter is 2-fair in every metric space.” The authors note that its algorithm is essentially the density-net algorithm of Chan, Dinitz, and Gupta [3], and include a self-contained proof. Its weighted mirror is Mass-Weighted 2-Fair \(k\)-Center Construction: given \((V,d), A,\mu,k\), output \(S\subseteq A\), \(|S|\le k\), such that \(d(a,S)\le2\rho_\mu(a)\) for every \(a\in A\). A weighted greedy version chooses a remaining site \(s\) with minimum \(\rho_\mu(s)\), then removes each remaining site \(a\) with \(d(a,s)\le\rho_\mu(a)+\rho_\mu(s)\). The selected sites’ neighborhood balls are pairwise disjoint and each has mass at least \(1/k\), so at most \(k\) are selected. Each removed site is within \(2\rho_\mu(a)\) of a selected site. This gives a polynomial-time algorithm in the support size and input bit length, and it remains tractable even when clearing denominators would produce an enormous explicit population.

These anchors support different conclusions: exact 1-fair feasibility is hard, while a useful constant-fairness guarantee is tractable. The mirror covers those two theoretical results and the associated fair \(k\)-center objective; it does not claim to continue the paper’s empirical comparisons or its Steiner-center variant. Further questions include exact optimization of \(\alpha\) for weighted populations, sharper guarantees in Euclidean or restricted metrics, and how complexity changes with \(k\), support size, or geometric structure.

The weakest point is the geographic aggregation. Treating a whole block as one location makes its residents identical for travel purposes, which can obscure within-block variation and change the set of eligible sites. That is a real modeling choice, but not a retreat from the paper’s question: at a declared planning resolution, it preserves the paper’s density-based neighborhood and facility-placement problem. The mass formulation is exactly the high-multiplicity version of that model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper as a ChoCo target is about fit: its agents do not express preferences or make a collective choice. The problem is to place facilities in a metric space while bounding the worst individual’s travel distance relative to a local density threshold. A continuous geographic demand measure is a natural operations-research model, but it is not obviously the programme’s central problem of computation over a society of preference types.

That objection weakens under the best version of the mirror. At a declared planning resolution, locations are valid types: residents at one site have the same distances to every facility and contribute identically to the fairness calculation. A county represented by block locations and rational population shares is plausible, and the paper’s allowance for coincident points makes this interpretation especially natural. Aggregating locations can alter neighborhood radii, but that is a modeling tradeoff, not a fundamental failure of the mirror.

I cannot honestly defeat the Theorem 3 anchor. Assign mass \(1/n\) to each point in a discrete instance. A ball then has mass at least \(1/k\) exactly when it contains at least \(\lceil n/k\rceil\) points, so the weighted 1-fairness question reproduces the theorem’s question. The Dominating Set reduction therefore carries over. Restricting attention to Euclidean geography might change the complexity question, but it does not displace the valid general-metric mirror.

Nor can I defeat the best version of the Theorem 1 anchor. The weighted greedy proof works: each selected neighborhood ball has mass at least \(1/k\); the balls are pairwise disjoint; hence there are at most \(k\) selected centers. The same removal rule gives the factor-two guarantee. This is a direct, useful high-multiplicity formulation, even if the proof requires little new machinery.

The honest negative case is therefore weak. The paper may sit at the edge of ChoCo’s subject matter, and its weighted extensions look mathematically immediate. But neither point defeats the anchors: the first is a plausible population model, and the second is not a reason to reject a well-posed mirror. I do not see a sound argument that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
