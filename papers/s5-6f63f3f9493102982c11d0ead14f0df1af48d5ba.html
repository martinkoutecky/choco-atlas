The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and employer type sets with rational masses summing to one on each side, a compatibility graph, and strict rankings of counterpart types, does there exist a popular measurable one-to-one matching \(M\) with minimum matched mass \(q(M)\) among all popular matchings, for which a positive mass of agents can be paired with mutually preferred partners? To specify strict rankings within counterpart types, give each type a normalized coordinate in \([0,1]\) and use that shared coordinate as the within-type priority order; matchings are measure-preserving partial bijections, and popularity compares \(M\) with every such matching by integrating each agent’s \(+1\), \(-1\), or \(0\) vote.

The model it lives in

A two-sided society with finite support over applicant and employer types, each carrying rational mass. Outcomes are measurable, measure-preserving partial matchings between individuals; popularity integrates individual head-to-head votes over the society, the objective is matched mass \(q(M)\), and instability requires a positive mass of mutually blocking pairs.

The objection that survived

Even with a canonical priority coordinate, voter comparisons depend on the full matching map, and the case does not show that this admits a finite type-level optimization or oracle representation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s hardness result for minimum-size popular matchings that are unstable. It leaves the paper’s other results, including Theorem 4’s algorithm and its other hardness results, outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether a minimum-mass popular matching with positive-mass blocking exists, as a function of \(\tau\) and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s minimum-size popular matching that is not stable result. It keeps the paper’s central objects: agents on both sides are voters, alternatives are matchings, popularity is a head-to-head condition, and instability means a blocking pair. Only the population is continuized.

A credible regime is a large, recurring two-sided placement market: many candidates and one-position employers, with relatively few standardized profiles on each side. For example, candidates might share preference rankings over employer categories, while employers use common rankings based on credential categories and a public priority score. Each type includes the full preference and priority rules used by the problem. The number of people can be enormous while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is modest. This is an instance regime, not a claim that every matching market is naturally high-multiplicity.

Here is a precise version. An instance has finite sets of applicant types \(I\) and employer types \(J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\), and a compatibility graph on \(I\cup J\). The masses sum to one. Agents of each type occupy an atomless interval of that mass. Each type has a strict ranking of compatible counterpart types; within a counterpart type, everyone uses the same public priority ordering of its members. This gives every agent of a type the same complete preference order, including the tie-break. A matching \(M\) is a measurable partial bijection between the two populations: each matched person gets exactly one partner. Its matched mass \(q(M)\) is the measure of matched applicants.

For two matchings \(M,N\), let \(\Delta(M,N)\) be the integral over all agents of \(+1\) if they prefer their assignment in \(M\), \(-1\) if they prefer their assignment in \(N\), and \(0\) if they abstain. Call \(M\) popular when \(\Delta(M,N)\ge 0\) for every measurable matching \(N\). Call it unstable when a positive mass of agents on the two sides can be paired so that every pair mutually prefers each other to their current assignments. The decision problem is:

Continuum Minimum-Mass Popular Instability. Given the masses, compatibility graph and finite type rankings, does there exist a popular matching \(M\) that is unstable and attains the minimum of \(q(N)\) over all popular matchings \(N\)?

The matching remains one-to-one at the level of each person. Type-edge totals may be fractional because a type’s mass can be divided among counterpart types; they are useful summaries, but they do not by themselves specify a matching. The assignment itself must retain enough information to compare the same voters across alternatives. Thus the question continuizes the population, not the outcome space.

The anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper. It states that, given a bipartite graph with strict preference lists, deciding whether it has a minimum-cardinality popular matching that is unstable is NP-complete. The paper proves this result; it is not merely citing it. My continuous problem replaces cardinality by matched mass, and a blocking edge by a positive-mass blocking opportunity. I would expect hardness to transfer: the reduction’s SAT choices and consistency constraints live in the growing family of preference gadgets, hence in the type support, rather than in a large count of repeated voters. That is a reason to investigate this mirror, not a proof. Splitting mass could create mixed gadget states and invalidate the reduction.

The main unresolved questions are whether the continuous problem is polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, and whether its popular matchings admit a tractable optimization or separation formulation. A discrete-to-continuous dictionary would also clarify whether a continuous solution can help with large finite markets.

The weakest point is that popularity depends on which individual gets which partner in each alternative, so an aggregate flow matrix is insufficient. The common priority ordering makes the type model finite to describe, but it is stylized; and using positive-mass blocking is the atomless market version of the paper’s single blocking edge, not a literal copy. Still, the model preserves the paper’s voting and matching question while making population mass the decision scale. That is a concrete, defensible mirror of Theorem 9, even though the hardness transfer remains open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 9 is the only anchor, and its hardness comes from a graph whose vertices have distinct roles in the reduction. Their neighborhoods and strict rankings over named partners encode the clauses and consistency constraints. Under ChoCo’s complete-type definition, those roles are generally distinct types, so the natural encoding has \(\tau\) growing with the whole reduction. That leaves a continuous instance, but gives no repeated population types whose multiplicity is the source of the paper’s computational question.

The stronger proposed mirror groups agents by category and gives each category a shared ranking, with public priorities resolving ties among people. That creates a tension at the center of this result. If assignments and votes retain those individual priorities, a type’s mass and category-level matching flows do not specify who receives which partner, or how the same voters compare two matchings. The model must carry identity-level assignment information, which undercuts its finite type description. If instead people within a category are interchangeable, the finite model is cleaner, but within-category ties replace the paper’s strict rankings and its individual blocking edges. Positive-mass blocking is a sensible definition for that market, yet it is a different popularity problem; Theorem 9 supplies little evidence about its structure.

This is a deeper issue than whether the SAT reduction survives fractional assignments. Such a change in the answer could itself be a valuable result. The issue is that the paper’s anchor depends on named-agent comparisons, while the high-multiplicity model needs a principled way to forget those identities. The proposed model preserves the comparisons by adding priorities, then needs those priorities to be represented in the continuum. A type-level version avoids that burden by changing what counts as a comparison and a block.

So the strongest case against is that Theorem 9 does not establish a compelling continuous mirror without substantial new modeling work: the identity-preserving version resists population compression, and the compressed version studies a coarser matching market. I would not call that a decisive objection. A category-based popular-matching problem could still be worth studying on its own, and the paper’s subject makes it plausible that its authors would recognize the connection. The negative case is therefore limited: it weakens this anchor’s fit to the programme, but it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror.
