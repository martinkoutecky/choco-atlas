What the mirror covers

Theorem 5, Theorem 6, and Theorem 7 have plausible axiomatic mass analogues; the paper supplies no computational result for a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for an axiomatic population mirror, not the computational mirror ChoCo is asking for. A plausible regime is a civic platform with many participants but relatively few distinct complete rankings: take \(T=X!\) and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of participants with ranking \(t\). Theorem 5’s impossibility survives on a society supported by just two rankings, each with positive mass: Pareto Efficiency excludes every proposal below \(a\), while Weak Position Unanimity excludes \(a\), which is ranked first by everyone. Theorem 5, proved in this paper, says that for \(m\ge3\), no DSF can satisfy both axioms. That is a credible population-continuum analogue, but its conclusion is about normative requirements, not computational complexity.

The other named theorems are also axiomatic: Theorem 6 rules out Position Unanimity for neutral profile-index-based DSFs when \(m\ge3\); Theorem 7 rules out simultaneously satisfying Anonymity, Neutrality, Clone Consistency, and Position Unanimity when \(m\ge3\). None is a named result about tractability, hardness, or an algorithm. The paper explicitly says it did not discuss algorithmic matters. So there are no qualifying computational anchors, and I cannot give one continuous computational problem per anchor or assign the requested tractability class. Computing a divisiveness measure from a rational distribution might be a new research question, but it would not be anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The civic-platform setting is a strong high-multiplicity regime: many participants can share one of a relatively small number of complete rankings. And the proposed mass versions of Theorems 5–7 are sensible. That still does not give ChoCo a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. Every named theorem is an incompatibility among normative axioms; none states an algorithmic upper bound, lower bound, or complexity classification. The paper explicitly leaves algorithmic matters for later, after a satisfactory measure has been identified. Its closing observation that computation may be demanding, and that sampling could help, is a research suggestion, not a computational result.

Theorem 5 survives a direct mass reformulation. Put positive mass on each of the two rankings in its construction: both place \(a\) first, and reverse the order of the remaining proposals. Pareto Efficiency then rules out those lower proposals, while Weak Position Unanimity rules out \(a\). This is a good continuous *axiomatic* question, but its answer is still about whether requirements can coexist. Theorem 6 likewise invites a mass-profile version of its Position Unanimity impossibility for neutral profile-index DSFs. Theorem 7 invites one using positive masses on \(abc\) and \(cba\), with the same anonymity, neutrality, clone, and position requirements. Each is a legitimate analogue; none asks how efficiently to compute anything.

One could instead study algorithms for calculating a particular divisiveness measure from rational type masses. That would be a new computational project, not a mirror of these theorems. The paper defines a broad design space: a measure may depend on an arbitrary scoring function and distribution over coalition splits, an arbitrary voting rule, or an arbitrary profile index. To compute a continuous score-based measure, one would also need to choose what a random split of a mass distribution means. Those choices may lead to worthwhile questions, but the paper does not select one or establish a computational claim about it. A hardness or tractability result for such a choice would be a follow-on result, not the continuous counterpart of Theorem 5, 6, or 7.

So the negative case is strong on ChoCo’s stated gate: this paper supplies no computational result to mirror, even though its population has a natural continuous representation and its axiomatic results can be transported there. The case is weaker as the universal claim that no scenario could make related computational work worthwhile. A civic platform with many users and few rankings could motivate such work. But that would be a new algorithmic study built around a chosen divisiveness measure, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
