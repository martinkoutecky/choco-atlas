Why no mirror

Proposition 3 is a named computational result, but it constructs stability against deviations by any coalition, including a single agent. The proposed mirror instead tests only positive-mass coalitions and uses mass-level contracting, so it changes the strategic solution concept the result concerns. The resulting atomless game is a plausible research question, but this case does not establish it as a continuous analogue of Proposition 3 that the authors would recognize as their problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that for the consensus rule, if the truthful winner is the default \(a^\star\) and \(a^\star\) is not welfare-maximizing, Algorithm 1 constructs an IR-SNE whose outcome maximizes social welfare. The constructed coalition is
\[ S=\bigcup_{t<a^\star}D_t\ \cup\ \{i:\arg\max_a U_i(a)\ne\arg\max_a u_i(a)\}, \qquad D_t=\{i:u_i(t)>U_i(t+1)\}, \]
with alternatives ordered by social welfare. The proposition states a running time of \(O(nm)\). It is proved here, with the full proof in Appendix B.2.

This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result. The paper mentions hardness results for manipulation and bribery, but does not give them as numbered results of its own. So Proposition 3 is the appropriate anchor under the requested standard.

My lead mirror is Continuous Consensus IR-SNE Construction. An instance has a finite alternative set \(A\), a default \(a^\star\), and a finite support \(T\) of utility types. Each type \(t\) has a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a nonnegative rational utility vector \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{A}\). The type is the complete utility vector relevant to this paper’s model; agents of the same type have the same contract possibilities. Let
\[ W_\mu(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_t(a). \]
Restrict to instances where the truthful consensus outcome is \(a^\star\) and \(a^\star\notin\arg\max_a W_\mu(a)\).

Represent the population by an atomless mass space with \(\mu_t\) mass of type \(t\). Each agent has a per-capita, outcome-contingent net transfer \(q_i(a)\); transfers balance at every alternative, so \(\int q_i(a)\,d i=0\). Utilities are \(u_t(a)+q_i(a)\). Consensus selects a non-default alternative only when almost all population mass ranks it first; otherwise it selects \(a^\star\). A coalition may consist of any measurable submass, change its members’ contracts and votes, and leave outsiders to vote truthfully under their updated utilities, as in the paper. A deviation is individually rational if coalition members weakly gain and a positive mass strictly gains.

The problem asks for a finite, exact description of transfers, reports, and a measurable coalition that forms an IR-SNE from the truthful null-contract state and selects an alternative in \(\arg\max_a W_\mu(a)\). The returned construction may be uniform within each input type, but it must be stable against deviations by arbitrary measurable submasses. That last requirement matters: merely running the finite algorithm once per type would not establish equilibrium in the continuous model.

The regime I believe in is a large autonomous fleet using one of a small menu of network-wide coordination plans. Agents are vehicles or operators with the same utility vector because they share relevant attributes such as operating region, vehicle class, schedule, and safety constraints. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the fleet in type \(t\); the fleet could be very large while the number of distinct types \(\tau=|T|\) remains small. Outcome-contingent credits or transfers redistribute the gains from choosing a plan. A default plan is the fallback when the fleet fails to agree. This preserves the paper’s collective alternatives, transfers, consensus rule, and equilibrium question; only the population becomes the continuous object.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The natural construction replaces sums over agents in Algorithm 1 with mass-weighted sums over types. For rational input, the target is an exact algorithm in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), where \(L\) is the input bit length; its output can be a compact table of per-type transfers. The paper’s \(O(nm)\) bound does not itself prove that bound: the continuous analogue must show that the construction remains an IR-SNE against arbitrary measurable coalitions, then bound its arithmetic and bit complexity.

The scope is Proposition 3’s consensus construction. I would not stretch this mirror to the paper’s general-AMR discussion or its other stability criteria. The authors should recognize the question: their motivation includes large-scale multi-agent systems, and the mirror retains the same consensus rule, welfare objective, transfers, and IR-SNE notion. It treats transfers as per-capita amounts balanced across population mass, a natural normalization when welfare is measured per capita.

The weakest point is that Proposition 3 is already an \(O(nm)\) algorithm on the paper’s explicit-agent input. This mirror therefore does not show discrete hardness dissolving in a continuum; its plausible gain is compression from the number of agents to the number of utility types. Also, consensus remains knife-edge: any positive mass that disagrees forces the default. The almost-everywhere interpretation makes zero-mass exceptions irrelevant, but it should be stated openly as the continuum version of unanimity.

The further questions are whether the mass-weighted construction is stable against every measurable subcoalition, what exact correspondence holds between rational mass instances and finite high-multiplicity elections, and how robust the equilibrium is when a small positive mass disagrees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is the right anchor: the paper gives a named construction of an IR-SNE, with a stated \(O(nm)\) running time. The serious objection is whether its game has a continuous-population version that still asks the same stability question.

The input cannot be just a distribution of utility vectors. Deviations change agents’ outgoing contracts, and the paper says recipient identity can matter for coalition formation; Appendix A.2 even gives a model where making recipients anonymous can change whether IR-SNE exists. If types record only utilities and transfers become type-level flows, that contract choice disappears. If types also record the named counterpart relationships that deviations use, the support may grow with the population, defeating the proposed compression. A fleet with a centralized, anonymous transfer pool is a plausible setting, but it changes a feature the paper treats as consequential.

There is also a sharper problem with atomlessness. Finite strong Nash rules out deviations by any nonempty coalition, including a single agent. The proposed mirror instead requires strict gains for positive mass. In an atomless model, a singleton can change an outgoing contract without changing any population share or the consensus outcome. If the individual payoff change still counts, that null coalition can destabilize the outcome while remaining invisible to aggregate budget balance. If null changes are ignored, individual utilities and contract participation are defined only up to measure-zero exceptions, so the paper’s coalition test has been replaced. Requiring positive-mass deviations gives a coherent possible model, but it changes the stability notion that Proposition 3 constructs.

The algorithmic gain alone is modest: Proposition 3 already runs in linear time in the explicit utility table, and its steps appear amenable to weighted aggregation over repeated utility vectors. That makes a type-compressed construction plausible. It does not resolve the harder question of whether the construction is stable against all allowed deviations once agents may split into arbitrary submasses and alter identity-specific contracts. The substantive research burden falls on repairing that mismatch, rather than on an evident new complexity landscape.

I would not claim that no useful continuous model can be devised. A system with genuinely collective, type-level contracting and stability defined against positive-mass coalitions could be worthwhile. My case against this anchor is that it would be a new coalition game whose relevance to the paper’s individual-contract IR-SNE is uncertain. That is the weak point: if the authors accept mass-level contracting and mass-level stability as their problem, this mirror may survive.
