The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s worker population. One caveat is central: the paper gives named query-budget results, but no theorem classifying an optimization problem as polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard. The anchors below are therefore resource-complexity results, not standard \(P\)-versus-\(NP\) results.

A plausible regime is a large, anonymous microtask platform with many fleeting workers but relatively few reliability classes. A worker type \(t\) specifies the worker’s task-independent correctness probability \(p_t\), along with any other relevant parameters such as batch capacity and response cost. The population has rational type masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); its collective quality is \(q=\sum_t\mu_t(2p_t-1)^2\). The scheduler does not observe a worker’s type, and each worker completes one batch and leaves. This fits the paper’s assumptions closely: it already models anonymous workers arriving from a reliability distribution and asks how to assign tasks and infer answers. The mirror replaces random draws from that distribution with large type pools in fixed proportions.

Here is the lead problem, High-Multiplicity Adaptive Crowdsourcing Budget. An instance has \(M\) distinct binary tasks with unknown truths \(\theta\in\{\pm1\}^M\), a maximum batch size \(r\), a target average error \(\epsilon\), and a class of finite-support rational worker populations with quality \(q\) and positive average bias. A policy assigns each arriving worker a subset of at most \(r\) tasks, using earlier responses if it chooses; a decoder outputs an estimate \(\hat\theta\). Each response costs one query. The question is to find a policy and decoder minimizing \(D=Q/M\), the number of queries per task, subject to
\[ \sup_{\theta,\mu}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \Pr_{\mu,\pi}\!\left[\hat\theta_i\ne\theta_i\right]\le\epsilon. \]
Here \(\mu\) ranges over the allowed type-mass populations, and the workers’ types are hidden from the policy. A solution is the policy, decoder, and their query budget. For the continuous-population version, worker-pool sizes grow with the task population while the number of types stays moderate; the finite task labels and each worker’s integral batch remain as in the paper.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2.7, proved in this paper’s Section 3.5. In the paper’s notation, for every constant \(C<1\), there is a constant \(C'>0\) such that, whenever \(q\le C\),
\[ \min_{\tau,\hat\theta}\ \max_{\theta\in\{\pm1\}^m,\;F\in\mathcal F_q} \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \Pr\!\left[\theta_i\ne\hat\theta_i\right] \ \ge\ \frac12 e^{-C'dq}. \]
The minimum ranges over adaptive assignment schemes using at most \(md\) queries and estimators based on the responses; the maximum ranges over all task truths and worker distributions with collective quality \(q\). Thus even the best adaptive scheme cannot make worst-case average error smaller than this bound. In the mass-population mirror, the corresponding question is whether the same lower bound, up to a finite-pool correction, holds for fixed type proportions. I expect the budget value to be tractable to constant factors: this is an information limit on the query budget, not evidence of NP-hardness.

A useful constructive companion is Corollary 2.3, also proved here. It states that, with a non-adaptive assignment, worker degree at least \(1+1/q\), and the paper’s iterative inference algorithm, \((32/q)\log(2/\epsilon)\) queries per task suffice to achieve error at most \(\epsilon\), for \(\epsilon\le1/2\) and all sufficiently large \(m\). Its mass-population mirror asks for a non-adaptive schedule and decoder attaining that guarantee when workers come from large pools with the specified type proportions. I would also expect this budget-approximation problem to be tractable: the paper supplies the finite-instance algorithm, and the continuous version would need to establish its guarantee for fixed masses, including the effect of sampling workers without replacement. Together, Theorem 2.7 and Corollary 2.3 suggest the same order of budget, \(\Theta(q^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon))\), in the suitable batch-capacity regime.

The authors would likely recognize these as their allocation-and-inference problem: the mirror keeps the binary tasks, response model, anonymous workers, per-response cost, and adaptive versus non-adaptive assignment. The population story is credible for a large labor market with stable reliability classes and many workers per class. The paper itself notes that sampling with or without replacement becomes hard to distinguish when the crowd is large, which supports this fixed-pool interpretation.

The weak point is also substantial. The paper already represents worker quality by a distribution \(F\), so changing that distribution into rational type masses may look like a restatement of its existing model. And its results concern statistical query complexity, not the computational complexity of finding an optimal policy. The fixed-mass model makes a distinct question about the high-multiplicity limit, but the paper does not establish a new \(P\), hardness, or parameterized-complexity result for it. That limits the case’s value as a ChoCo complexity contribution, even though the population mirror itself is plausible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection comes before the choice of mirror: the paper has no numbered result classifying the computational complexity of a problem. Theorem 2.7 lower-bounds the number of responses needed to estimate the task truths; Corollary 2.3 gives a sufficient response budget for the paper’s algorithm. Those are meaningful query-complexity results, but they say how much data is needed, not how hard it is to compute an optimal allocation or inference procedure. The paper reports near-linear runtime for its particular algorithm, without analyzing the complexity of finding an optimal policy. That leaves no direct ChoCo complexity result for a continuous mirror to extend.

The proposed worker masses also closely reproduce the paper’s existing model. For finite support, \(\mu=\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{p_t}\) is simply a worker-quality distribution \(F\). Theorem 2.7 already takes its worst case over distributions \(F\) with fixed collective quality \(q\), including such distributions; Corollary 2.3’s guarantee depends on \(q\), not the support size or the individual masses. When workers arrive independently from \(F\), describing the law as type masses adds no new allocation choice. The paper itself motivates this law by noting that sampling from a sufficiently large crowd resembles sampling with replacement.

A genuinely fixed-pool mirror would need to specify each type’s finite supply relative to the task population. With abundant pools, it approaches the paper’s distributional model. With supplies small enough for depletion to matter, the worker mix changes over time, and the proposed instance needs additional supply and arrival parameters; the iid lower bound and random-graph guarantee do not settle that model. A finite-pool limit theorem could be useful, but it would establish a sampling correction to existing query bounds, not a new complexity landscape.

The best alternative is to add task classes, such as known difficulty levels, and allocate response mass across them under an average-error target. That is a plausible crowdsourcing extension. The paper assumes equal task difficulty, though, and its results provide no computational anchor for the heterogeneous version. Its natural allocation formulation would chiefly concern response budgets across classes, rather than the continuous complexity of the paper’s stated results.

So the negative case is strongest against these anchors as ChoCo contributions: they are statistical resource bounds, and the worker-distribution mirror largely restates a distribution already present in the paper. It cannot establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous model exists. In particular, if the programme counts query complexity as computational complexity, or values a proved fixed-pool limit, the proponent’s case remains plausible.
