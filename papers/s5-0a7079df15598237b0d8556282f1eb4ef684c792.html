The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its only numbered result is Lemma 1, proved in Appendix A: if users satisfy \(P_{fa}+P_{md}<1\) and \(P_f+P_m<1\), then their prior and posterior beliefs obey \(y'_{ij}(h)>y_{ij}>y'_{ij}(l)\). That is a structural inequality about when inferred reports reflect judgments; it does not classify a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized.

One could model user types by their judgment and reporting behavior, represent peer links by type-to-type mixing, and study how much mass must change behavior to flip the service rating. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 1 is the paper’s only numbered result, and it is not a complexity theorem. That is the proponent’s strongest point. But it does not establish that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror: the paper’s central contribution is a computational rating procedure, and the lemma supplies a structural condition that governs how that procedure interprets reports.

A better question anchored in Lemma 1 and the rating rule in (3) is: given a distribution over user types, what is the minimum mass-transfer cost for changing user behaviors so that the DPC’s filters and peer predictions produce the opposite service rating? Types would include the judgment and reporting behavior, belief reports, scoring history, and peer category needed to determine a user’s treatment. A type-to-type peer-matching distribution captures the paper’s random peer selection. Then the question is a mass-manipulation or winner-robustness problem over the paper’s own mechanism. Lemma 1 identifies which types’ inferred reports track their judgments; the rating rule determines when the resulting reports change the outcome.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many users evaluating the same service with a small number of behavior and peer categories, such as a large platform cohort with shared prior histories. In that regime, replacing users with masses preserves what the rating procedure uses. Exact social-network neighborhoods and long individual trust histories could instead make the type support approach the population size; that weakens those instances, but does not rule out the cohort regime.

The proponent is right that this robustness problem is a new formulation, not a complexity result already proved in the paper. It is also the proponent’s own proposed mass-to-flip question, which they dismiss as unanchored. The honest limitation is that the paper offers a less direct anchor than a paper with a stated bribery, control, or robustness theorem—and the details of peer matching and accumulated trust may make the resulting type space large. If the eligibility rule requires an existing numbered complexity claim, this paper fails that rule. But that establishes the absence of a ready-made anchor, not the absence of a worthwhile continuous computational question.
