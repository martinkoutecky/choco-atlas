The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of indivisible good categories \(G\), valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\), and compactly represented normalized monotone submodular valuations \(v_t\), does there exist an allocation of masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) to integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\) such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), each category's total assigned mass equals its supply, every pair of positively assigned bundles satisfies EF1 under the relevant type's valuation, and no feasible reassignment preserving type masses and supplies weakly benefits almost every recipient while strictly benefiting positive mass?

The model it lives in

A population of valuation types carrying rational mass; decision variables \(x_{t,S}\) assign mass to indivisible bundles, with category supplies scaled per capita. The question is existence of an EF1 and Pareto optimal assignment under mass-preserving, supply-preserving reassignments.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's existence decision for EF1 and PO allocations; it leaves aside Theorems 1 and 2's two-agent nonexistence examples and Theorem 4's identical-valuation existence result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can type-level mixing create an EF1 and PO allocation when the corresponding three-agent instance has none?
  • Is the continuous existence problem NP-hard already for three types with coverage valuations?
  • Are there useful algorithms when the number of categories or valuation types is bounded?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that, given an instance with three agents, any number of indivisible goods, and monotone submodular valuations, it is NP-hard to decide whether there is an allocation that is both EF1 and Pareto optimal. The proof reduces from Balanced Vertex Cover, whose NP-completeness is cited from Conitzer and Sandholm [CS06, Lemma 1]. The paper’s reduction uses weighted coverage valuations for two agents and an additive valuation for the third.

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large public agency distributing bundles of scarce, nonfungible benefits—say, devices, training places, and service entitlements—to residents. Many residents may share exactly the same valuation over benefit categories, with diminishing returns because several categories can address the same need. The number of residents is enormous, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct valuation types is small. The theorem’s three agent roles suggest a particularly direct regime: three large cohorts, each with one shared valuation, while the number \(m\) of benefit categories can grow with the underlying combinatorial instance.

I would ask the following as the continuous mirror of Theorem 3. Call it Cont-Exist-EF1+PO. An instance has a finite set \(G\) of indivisible good categories, a finite set \(T\) of types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational per-capita supply \(b_g\in[0,1]\) for each \(g\in G\). Each type \(t\) has a normalized monotone submodular valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), given compactly—for the theorem’s coverage case, by a coverage description. Each person receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\); \(b_g\) is the mass of people who can receive a copy of category \(g\), not permission to divide an individual good. An allocation is a collection of masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}=b_g \quad\text{for each }g\in G. \]
Thus \(x_{t,S}\) is the fraction of the whole population of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\). It describes assignments to residents, not lotteries for individual residents.

Call such an allocation EF1 if, for every two bundles \(S,R\) assigned positive mass to types \(t,u\), respectively, either \(R=\varnothing\) or there is some \(g\in R\) with
\[ v_t(S)\ge v_t(R\setminus\{g\}). \]
Call it Pareto optimal if there is no feasible reassignment that preserves the mass of each type and every good’s supply, leaves almost every resident weakly better off, and makes a positive mass of residents strictly better off. More explicitly, such a reassignment may match mass previously assigned \((t,S)\) to new bundles \(R\); it is a Pareto improvement if every positive-mass match satisfies \(v_t(R)\ge v_t(S)\), with strict inequality on positive total mass. Cont-Exist-EF1+PO asks whether an allocation satisfying both conditions exists. Its solutions assign each resident an integral bundle, while the aggregate assignments and supplies are measured in mass.

My prior is that this problem remains hard, with hardness driven by the number of good categories and the graph structure encoded in their valuations—not by population multiplicity. That is the Class B prediction: the continuum may remove granularity in how many residents receive each bundle, but it does not remove the item-level combinatorics or the individual EF1 and Pareto requirements. A positive result would therefore need to show that mixing bundles across a cohort does not defeat the reduction’s constraints. Theorem 3 itself does not prove that continuous hardness claim.

This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ underlying problem: types are complete descriptions of residents’ relevant valuations, goods remain indivisible for every recipient, and both fairness and efficiency are judged at the individual level. Scaling the stock of each category with the population is the needed co-modification to keep the allocation meaningful as population grows. The mirror covers Theorem 3’s decision question; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s two-agent nonexistence examples or its result for identical valuations. Further questions include whether the theorem’s reduction survives type-level mixtures, whether a hardness result holds already with three types and coverage valuations, and whether the continuous problem admits useful algorithms when \(m\) or \(\tau\) is small.

The soft spot is exactly that mixture issue. In the paper, each of the three agents gets one bundle; here a cohort can split across many bundles. That extra flexibility may create an EF1 and Pareto-optimal continuous allocation even when the finite three-agent instance has none. So the model is credible as a continuous version of the authors’ problem, but transferring their hardness requires a new argument, not just replication of the instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the only computational anchor here; Theorems 1 and 2 are nonexistence results, and Theorem 4 is an existence characterization. The strongest case against a mirror is that Theorem 3’s reduction depends on three agents each receiving one bundle. Agent \(a_1\)’s bundle encodes a vertex cover, while \(a_3\)’s bundle supplies the size comparison. In a cohort model, each type can split across bundles. Coverage is evaluated on each person’s bundle, so the mass assigned to a cohort does not itself encode one vertex set. The proof’s two Pareto-optimal patterns and its Balanced Vertex Cover argument therefore do not carry over.

Requiring every member of a type to receive the same bundle would restore that reduction’s structure, but it would be an extra uniform-treatment constraint. The natural high-multiplicity version lets identical residents receive different bundles. The proponent’s formulation does that, keeps bundles integral, and scales category supplies with population. A large agency serving recurring groups of residents with overlapping needs is a credible setting; the resulting question retains individual EF1 comparisons and population-level Pareto improvements. This is a substantial mirror, even though the paper’s reduction says nothing yet about its complexity.

There is adjacent economic work on atomless populations with indivisible commodities, including core and equilibrium questions, but that does not answer this computational EF1-plus-PO problem ([Inoue](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304406805000182)). It cannot support a novelty-collision objection.

So the negative case is weak at its decisive point. The cohort split defeats the *transfer of Theorem 3’s proof*, but that gap is not a reason to reject the continuous question; the problem is well-posed, and the scenario is plausible. I cannot honestly defend the claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The sound negative conclusion is narrower: Theorem 3 supplies a strong candidate for study, but no continuous hardness result follows from it.
