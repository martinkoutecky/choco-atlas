The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous problem to. There is no theorem stating that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying an algorithmic task, and the paper gives no input model or running-time analysis.

The closest results are mathematical, not complexity results. Theorem 8.8, proved here, identifies efficient symmetric linear value maps with \(\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\): each coordinate is the map’s egalitarian weight at one coalition size, and the ordinary egalitarian Shapley family is the diagonal. Theorem 8.10, also proved here, says the best-fit parameter is the weighted mean of those coordinates. Theorem 8.12 says the fit \(R^2\) is that mean squared divided by their weighted second moment, or equivalently one minus their relative weighted variance. These are explicit structural formulas, but they do not establish the complexity of computing a problem.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large cooperative enterprise with many interchangeable participants in a few role types, where a coalition’s worth depends on the mass drawn from each type. But turning that story into a mirror requires defining a continuum TU game, its Shapley analogue, and a canonical norm on solution maps. The paper defines none of those. A problem asking for the best-fit egalitarian parameter in such a model could be interesting, but it would be a new extension rather than a continuous version of a named computational result here.

That is the soft spot in the positive case, and it is decisive under the requested anchor rule: the paper studies geometric analysis of finite-player solution concepts, not the computational landscape of a population-as-continuum problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 3.2 is an axiomatic characterization; Theorems 5.1, 6.2, 8.8, 8.10, and 8.12 establish invariance, projection, and structural identities. The propositions give closed-form calculations. None defines a computational problem with an input model or classifies its complexity. The paper studies the geometry of solution maps, not algorithms for changing a society. Under ChoCo’s scope, there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous mirror.

The strongest candidate anchors in the proponent’s discussion do not change that. Theorem 8.8 parametrizes finite-player maps by \(n-1\) coalition-size coordinates. Theorems 8.10 and 8.12 express a projection and its fit using weighted means and variances. These are mathematical descriptions, not complexity results about a society supplied as data. Calling \(n\) a population parameter does not make the coalition strata a distribution over voter or agent types.

A better proposed extension might model a large enterprise with repeated role types, make coalition worth depend on the mass of each type, then define a continuous value and project it onto an egalitarian family. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity cooperative-game model. But it would need to choose the continuous game class, the value concept, the representation of inputs, and an inner product for the solution maps. The paper supplies none of these choices. Making them would launch a new model and a new computational question; it would not continuize one of this paper’s computational results. Its Harsanyi inner product and stratification are defined on the finite Boolean lattice of coalitions, and the paper proves no computational claim about them.

So the negative case is strong as a case against a ChoCo mirror of this paper: none of the proposed anchors survives the programme’s computational criterion. It is weaker as the absolute claim that no worthwhile continuous extension could exist. A continuous cooperative-game geometry may be worth studying, but this paper gives no computational anchor for it, and the proponent did not establish that such an extension would be a mirror the authors would recognize as their problem.
