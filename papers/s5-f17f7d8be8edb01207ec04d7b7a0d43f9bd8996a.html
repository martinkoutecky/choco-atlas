The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant-type and seat-site sets \(P,Q\), rational masses \(\mu_p,\nu_q\) summing to \(1\), ordinal rankings of \(Q\) by each \(p\in P\), and a feasible predicted transport plan \(\widehat X\), is there an ordinal mechanism running in \(\operatorname{poly}(|P|,|Q|,L)\) that returns a feasible plan \(X\) such that for every consistent line realization \(d\) of the type sites and every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), \(C_\alpha(X,d)\le \min\{C_\alpha(\widehat X,d),3\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d)\}\)? Here \(C_\alpha(X,d)\) is the maximum cost of a subflow \(Y\le X\) of total mass \(\alpha\), and \(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d)=\min_Z C_\alpha(Z,d)\) over feasible plans \(Z\).

The model it lives in

Applicant types and seat-site types each occupy distinct line sites, with rational mass at each site and shared type-level preferences and distances. A transport plan assigns applicant mass to seat-site mass; the predicted plan is feasible, and performance is measured by the cost of the highest-cost \(\alpha\) mass. This is a capacitated extension of the paper’s no-collocation matching model.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s prediction-aware guarantee and Theorem 1’s prediction-free factor-\(3\) guarantee. It leaves other prediction formats and matching on general metrics aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a mass-level reserved-outlier construction and uncrossing argument establish the all-\(\alpha\) guarantee with rational outputs and running time polynomial in the type supports and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large matching market with a small number of repeated applicant and seat types. For example, many students could come from a few residential catchments, with many seats at each school site. Let an applicant type be a complete ranking of the seat types; all members of that type have the same preferences and, in a given line realization, the same distances to the seat types. Let a seat type represent interchangeable seats at one site. The mechanism sees rational masses \(\mu_p\) of applicant types and \(\nu_q\) of seat types, both totaling \(1\), plus their ordinal rankings. It does not see the coordinates. A consistent realization assigns distinct line coordinates to the types that induce the reported rankings.

The regime is \(N\) actual applicants and seats much larger than the numbers of types \(\tau_A,\tau_B\): for instance, millions of assignments but dozens of catchment and school-site types. A matching becomes a transport plan \(X=(x_{pq})\), where \(x_{pq}\) is the mass of type \(p\) assigned to seat type \(q\), with row sums \(\mu\) and column sums \(\nu\). A predicted matching becomes a feasible plan \(\widehat X\). For \(0<\alpha\le1\), define \(C_\alpha(X,d)\) as the cost of the largest \(\alpha\) mass under \(X\):
\[ C_\alpha(X,d)=\max\left\{\sum_{p,q}y_{pq}d(p,q):0\le y_{pq}\le x_{pq},\ \sum_{p,q}y_{pq}=\alpha\right\}. \]
This is the mass version of top-\(k\) cost: with \(N\) equal-mass agents and \(\alpha=k/N\), it is the discrete top-\(k\) cost divided by \(N\). The action is the assignment flow; the objective is its worst-case distortion over line realizations consistent with the ordinal input.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for every instance \(\sigma\), predicted perfect matching \(\widehat M\), and \(k\in[n]\), Algorithm 2 returns a matching \(M\) whose distortion satisfies
\[ \operatorname{distortion}_k(M,\sigma)\le \min\{\operatorname{distortion}_k(\widehat M,\sigma),3\}. \]
The prediction is therefore matched whenever it is better, while the output retains the factor-\(3\) guarantee. The paper proves the pointwise cost bounds underlying this statement, not just the bound after taking the supremum over metrics.

Its continuous problem is: given rational type masses, ordinal rankings, and a feasible predicted transport plan \(\widehat X\), does there exist a polynomial-time ordinal mechanism, running in \(\operatorname{poly}(\tau_A,\tau_B,L)\), that returns one feasible plan \(X\) such that for every consistent line realization \(d\) and every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\),
\[ C_\alpha(X,d)\le \min\{C_\alpha(\widehat X,d),\,3\,\mathrm{OPT}_\alpha(d)\}, \qquad \mathrm{OPT}_\alpha(d)=\min_{Y\text{ feasible}} C_\alpha(Y,d)? \]
A solution is the mechanism and its guarantee for all input instances, not a flow chosen with knowledge of the hidden coordinates. This is a direct continuous-population version of the paper’s prediction question.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 1, also proved in this paper. It says that for every instance \(\sigma\), every \(k\in[n]\), and every matching returned by Algorithm 1, the matching’s distortion is at most \(3\). Its mirror removes the predicted plan: given the same type masses and rankings, find a polynomial-time ordinal mechanism producing a feasible assignment flow \(X\) with
\[ C_\alpha(X,d)\le 3\,\mathrm{OPT}_\alpha(d) \]
for every consistent line realization and every \(\alpha\). The flow version of Algorithm 1 reserves applicant mass equal to the mass of the outlier seat types, assigns that reserved mass to them, and assortatively matches the remaining mass using the recovered orders. I expect both mirrors to be tractable, in Class A: the paper’s order recovery and uncrossing arguments look suited to mass transfers in one dimension. Extending those arguments to prove the guarantees and the stated support-size running time is the actual question; the discrete theorems do not establish it automatically.

The paper’s results supporting this case are algorithmic distortion guarantees. It has no named NP-hardness result to anchor a question about discrete hardness dissolving. The mirror would instead ask whether its best-of-both-worlds mechanism survives when the applicant and seat populations are measures and the mechanism runs on type support rather than individual count. Further questions include whether finite instances can be rounded while preserving the two guarantees up to additive error, whether the factor \(3\) remains tight for mass mechanisms, and whether the method extends to other fairness objectives.

The main weakness is a real model change. The paper explicitly disallows collocation, while a positive-mass type at one line coordinate represents many co-located agents or seats. That idealization fits repeated school seats or catchment cohorts, and can be viewed as a limit of distinct locations clustered tightly enough to preserve rankings. Still, the exact mass-type model would need the authors’ acceptance as a natural capacitated, high-multiplicity version of their problem. It is not enough to call the transport plan “continuous”: the case rests on the population being represented by masses, with type-level costs shared and the finite market recoverable as a high-multiplicity approximation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can make a real objection to the proposed finite-support model, but I cannot honestly make the universal negative case airtight. Theorem 2 is a named algorithmic guarantee, and Theorem 1 is another; saying the paper has no computational result to mirror would be wrong.

The difficulty is what counts as a type here. A type complete enough for the paper’s cost objective must fix an agent’s distances to the items. With two distinct item locations on a line, those distances determine the agent’s location. Since the paper excludes collocation, such a type cannot contain multiple agents. Grouping by rankings instead gives repeated types, but members can have different distances. Then a flow between ranking types does not say which members bear which costs, so it does not determine the top-\(\alpha\) cost. The prediction flow does not supply that missing link. This defeats the proponent’s proposed model as a direct mirror of the stated problem.

The strongest repair is a capacitated site model: many applicants share a location type, many seats share school sites, and the prediction is a transport plan. That is a plausible school-assignment problem, but it permits collocation and replaces individual seats with capacities. It therefore changes a defining assumption of this paper. The programme allows such co-modifications, though, and the authors could reasonably recognize this as their problem. So this objection does not defeat the repaired mirror; it identifies the point where the negative case is weakest.

Theorem 1 falls with the same type issue, and in the repaired model it is a special case of Theorem 2. The proposed pair of anchors is therefore one substantive question, not two independent reasons to pursue the paper. A capacitated version also looks close to a mass-level rewrite of the paper’s sorting and uncrossing arguments. That makes it a weak candidate for uncovering a new complexity landscape, but the programme explicitly treats the high-multiplicity dictionary as a deliverable, so apparent ease alone cannot rule it out.

My best case against is that the faithful finite-support mirror lacks well-defined type-level costs, while the natural repair changes the matching model. I cannot show that the repaired question is unworthy: repeated applicants and seats at sites are a convincing large-market regime, and the authors may accept it. The negative verdict is therefore weak.
