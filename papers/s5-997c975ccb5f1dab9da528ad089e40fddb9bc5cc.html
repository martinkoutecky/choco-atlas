The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is substantively positive but formally limited: this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, yet it has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s rules.

The paper’s strongest named result is Theorem 3.1, proved here. It states that, for every \(\delta>0\), the expected proportion of firms and workers whose utilities differ by at most \(\delta\) across all stable matchings converges to \(1\) as the market grows. Formally, for firms,
\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{|F\setminus A_F(\delta;U,V)|}{n}\right]\to 1, \]
where \(A_F(\delta;U,V)\) is the set of firms whose utility range between the firm-optimal and worker-optimal stable matchings exceeds \(\delta\). The theorem is proved in this paper, using Pittel’s result in the pure-private-value case and the cited random-biclique theorem of Dawande et al. in the common-value case.

The natural mirror would be the following.

Take a large centralized labour or residency market with finitely many firm types \(A\) and worker types \(B\). A type is a complete preference-and-utility template: two firms of type \(a\) have identical utilities \(u_{ab}\) for every worker type \(b\), and two workers of type \(b\) have identical utilities \(v_{ab}\) for every firm type \(a\). The society is given by rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=\sum_b\nu_b=1\). These could represent, for example, large annual cohorts of workers with the same qualification profile and hospitals or employers in a small number of standardized institutional classes.

A continuous matching is a mass matrix \(\pi=(\pi_{ab})\), where \(\pi_{ab}\) is the mass of actual type-\(a\)/type-\(b\) matches, with row sums \(\mu\) and column sums \(\nu\). This is not an outcome-space lottery: every infinitesimal agent receives one integral partner; \(\pi\) only records the aggregate mass of such matches.

Call \(\pi\) stable if there are no types \(a,b\) such that a positive mass of type-\(a\) firms currently receive partners they rank below \(b\), and a positive mass of type-\(b\) workers currently receive partners they rank below \(a\). Let \(\mathcal S(\mu,\nu)\) be the set of stable mass matchings. For a type \(a\), define its per-capita utility in \(\pi\) by
\[ U_a(\pi)=\frac{1}{\mu_a}\sum_b \pi_{ab}u_{ab}, \]
and analogously define \(V_b(\pi)\). Its stable-core utility range is
\[ \Delta_A(a)=\max_{\pi\in\mathcal S}U_a(\pi) -\min_{\pi\in\mathcal S}U_a(\pi), \]
with the analogous definition for workers.

The continuous problem is:

Given \((A,B,\mu,\nu,u,v)\) and rational thresholds \(\delta,\varepsilon>0\), compute
\[ > \Phi_\delta= > \sum_{a:\Delta_A(a)>\delta}\mu_a+ > \sum_{b:\Delta_B(b)>\delta}\nu_b, > \]
or decide whether \(\Phi_\delta\le\varepsilon\).

This is a direct continuous version of the paper’s question: how much population has a materially different utility across stable outcomes? The action variable is the stable mass matching \(\pi\); the objective is the mass exposed to a utility difference exceeding \(\delta\).

I would expect this problem to be Class A in the finite-type regime. Aggregate deferred acceptance should compute the firm-optimal and worker-optimal mass matchings using type-level proposals and capacity arithmetic, without expanding a mass \(p/q\) into \(p\) individual agents. The remaining challenge is to prove that the boundary splitting and utility-range computation remain polynomial in the number of types and the input bit length. That would be a genuine high-multiplicity computational result, although it is not proved by Lee.

The scenario is plausible, but only after changing the paper’s random environment in a principled way. In Lee’s model, pair-specific private utilities are independently drawn, so almost every agent has a distinct full preference vector. If that vector is part of the type, then the number of types grows nearly linearly with the market, destroying high multiplicity. The mirror therefore uses repeated preference templates—standardized hospital classes and recurring worker cohorts—rather than preserving independent idiosyncratic values for every named pair. The matching, stability, and utility-range question remain recognizably the authors’ problem; the iid private-value assumption does not.

Corollary 3.2 gives a related interpretation: for any fixed cost of misrepresenting preferences, the expected proportion of agents with an incentive to manipulate converges to zero. Theorem 5.1 extends this conclusion to four incomplete-information structures. A manipulation mirror is possible, but weaker: in an atomless market, one individual has zero mass and cannot change the outcome. One must therefore model manipulation by a positive-mass cohort or type block, which risks changing the strategic question. I would not make that the lead mirror.

The decisive weakness is formal. Theorem 3.1, Corollary 3.2, and Theorem 5.1 are asymptotic economic results, not named results asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic formulation with a complexity guarantee. Theorem 4.1 is explicitly cited from Dawande et al. and is a random-biclique asymptotic theorem, not a computational anchor. Thus the paper supplies a compelling subject-matter basis for a continuous stable-matching problem, and existing continuum matching work supports its plausibility, but it does not satisfy ChoCo’s anchor requirement. Under a strict reading, the correct verdict is: strong natural mirror, no qualifying computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is that Lee’s paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 3.1, Corollary 3.2, and Theorem 5.1 are probabilistic asymptotic statements: they establish convergence of proportions in a sequence of random markets. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or even a computational decision problem. The proposed \(\Phi_\delta\) is therefore not a continuization of a computational result in the paper; it is a new aggregate stable-matching problem motivated by the paper.

The mismatch is substantive in Theorem 3.1. Lee’s object is a sequence of pair-specific random matrices \(U,V\). The quantity
\[ \Delta(f;u,v)=u_{\mu^F}(f)-u_{\mu^W}(f) \]
tracks the same named firm across all stable matchings. With continuously distributed private values, almost every firm has a distinct utility vector. If that vector is part of the type, the number of types grows with the market, so the high-multiplicity regime disappears.

The proponent’s finite-type replacement removes precisely that structure. A mass matrix \(\pi\) records how much type \(a\) is matched with type \(b\), but not which members of type \(a\) receive which partners. Thus
\[ U_a(\pi)=\frac{1}{\mu_a}\sum_b\pi_{ab}u_{ab} \]
is a type-average payoff, not the utility of the same agent across stable matchings. It can be unchanged while different portions of the type receive very different partners. Retaining individual labels would restore Lee’s statistic, but then identity becomes relevant to the problem and the agents can no longer be quotiented into indistinguishable types. Replacing the statistic by worst-case, best-case, or quantile payoffs would be a legitimate new model, not Lee’s theorem in continuous form.

One can try to preserve the random environment by taking an atomless distribution over complete utility vectors. That is mathematically possible, but it produces an infinite-dimensional type space and an input consisting of a distribution over functions or random fields. It is no longer the finite-type, high-multiplicity computational setting for which ChoCo’s LP and pricing machinery is designed. Conversely, restricting the environment to finitely many recurring templates yields a sensible matching model, but eliminates the independent pair-specific randomness driving Lee’s theorem and removes its \(n\to\infty\) phenomenon.

Corollary 3.2 has an even sharper degeneration. Its manipulation is unilateral: one named agent misreports while the others remain truthful. In an atomless society, one agent has measure zero and cannot change the aggregate stable matching, so the gain from manipulation is identically zero. A nontrivial version must let a positive-mass type or cohort coordinate its report. That is collective manipulation, not the individual incentive problem studied in the paper, and requires new assumptions about coordination and costs.

Theorem 5.1 does not repair this. Its information structures condition on what a particular firm knows about the realized preferences of other named agents. In a continuum, that agent again has no aggregate influence. A positive-mass Bayesian type could be studied, but this would be a new distributional game rather than a continuous version of Lee’s individual incomplete-information result. Theorem 4.1 also cannot serve as an anchor: it is an externally cited random-biclique theorem, not a computational result of this paper.

There is a real weakness in claiming an absolute impossibility: standardized hospital classes and recurring worker cohorts do form a plausible high-multiplicity matching market, and aggregate deferred acceptance may be worth studying independently. But that would be a new computational matching project. Lee’s own results either lose their agent-level identity when passed to masses, or retain their random utility structure only through an unwieldy infinite-dimensional type space. Under ChoCo’s criteria, that is not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
