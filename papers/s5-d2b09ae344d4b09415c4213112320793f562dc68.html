The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that Nonemptiness, Anonymity, Unanimity, Continuity, and Objective Expected Consistency characterize the Bentham solution. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that replacing Objective Expected Consistency with Minimum Consistency characterizes the Rawls solution. Proposition 1 proves that Nonemptiness, Anonymity, Unanimity, and Subjective Expected Consistency cannot all hold. These are axiomatic results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. So I cannot honestly give a continuous problem *per computational anchor*.

The strongest positive-side construction I can offer is an unanchored candidate based on the Bentham choice problem in Theorem 1. Let the alternatives be a finite set \(S\), and let the society have \(\tau\) utility types. Each type \(t\) is a complete utility function \(u_t:S\to\mathbb{Q}\), shared by a fraction \(\mu_t>0\) of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Given the types, their utilities, and their binary-rational masses, return all alternatives in
\[ \arg\max_{s\in S}\sum_t \mu_t u_t(s). \]
The decision is which alternative to choose; the objective is aggregate utility. A plausible regime is a large population of residents choosing among public policies, where many residents fall into a relatively small number of shared utility profiles, so the population size is much larger than \(\tau\). The model preserves the paper’s cardinal utilities and their interpersonal comparability.

This is a natural population version of Bentham’s rule, but its computation is immediate: evaluate the weighted sum for each alternative, using \(O(m\tau)\) rational operations for \(m=|S|\). That tractability is an inference from the proposed formulation, not a result of the paper. A Rawls version would maximize \(\min_{t:\mu_t>0}u_t(s)\), but it would likewise be a straightforward computation and would not supply a missing hardness anchor.

The plausible connection is to the paper’s underlying social choice object: its Bentham solution can be applied to a distribution over repeated utility types. This candidate covers that choice rule only. It does not continue the paper’s axiomatic characterization, its consistency conditions, or its impossibility result. The weakest point is therefore central: the authors could recognize this as a population-based computational question about the Bentham criterion, but not as the continuous form of the paper’s main research problem. The paper asks which axioms characterize choice rules, not how to compute them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no computational anchors to defeat in this paper. Proposition 1 is an impossibility result about axioms; Theorems 1 and 2 are axiomatic characterizations of the Bentham and Rawls correspondences. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s “Continuity” axiom concerns how a choice correspondence behaves under small changes to a utility profile; it does not make the population continuous.

The proposed distribution over utility types is a coherent high-multiplicity model: a large population could share a small number of cardinal utility profiles. But applying Bentham’s rule to it—choosing \(\arg\max_{s\in S}\sum_t\mu_tu_t(s)\)—does not continue Theorem 1’s result. It evaluates a rule the paper already defines. The theorem’s subject is whether axioms characterize a correspondence over utility profiles. The proposed computation neither tests nor preserves that characterization. The same distinction applies to Rawls in Theorem 2. Proposition 1’s subjective probabilities are also not population shares: they describe agents’ differing beliefs about which utility profile will occur, not how much population has each type.

A stronger proposed mirror might ask for the least costly transfer of mass between utility types that makes a target alternative win under Bentham or Rawls. That could be a sensible new ChoCo problem, but the paper supplies no transfers, costs, feasible changes, or target-selection task. Adding those would create the computational problem rather than continue one in the paper. Alternatively, one could characterize welfare correspondences over distributions of utility types, but that remains an axiomatic question rather than the computational programme’s target.

So the case against a *paper-derived computational mirror* is strong: there is no computational result here to continue, and the suggested Bentham evaluation is a new task built around a defined rule. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could be built from these ideas is weaker. A population-based welfare intervention problem could be worth studying; the honest objection is that it would be a new research direction, not a mirror of this paper’s results.
