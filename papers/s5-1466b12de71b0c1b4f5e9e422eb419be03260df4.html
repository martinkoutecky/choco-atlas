The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are about whether a social choice correspondence can be Nash implemented, not the complexity of deciding or computing anything. For example, Proposition 2, proved in the paper, says that under Condition D and A1, an SCC satisfying Condition \(\mu^{**}_2\) is partially-honest implementable; Proposition 4, also proved there, gives the corresponding sufficiency result under A2 and Condition \(\mu^{**}_\circ\). Those are mechanism-existence results, not results that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The corollaries likewise give sufficient conditions for implementability. So there is no honest anchor from which to state the requested continuous problem.

The most plausible positive lead I can construct is a continuum of replicated two-person bargaining committees. A type would specify the two members’ preferences and, under A1, the committee’s one-partially-honest information regime; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of committees of type \(t\). The designer would choose one mechanism to use across the committees, and the feasibility question would be whether it implements the relevant SCC in every committee. That has a concrete mass and decision variable, and the paper’s mechanism results could motivate studying it.

But the continuum here sits *outside* the paper’s two-agent game. Each committee still has exactly two strategic agents, and the committees’ mass shares do not change their Nash equilibria or the implementation conditions. It is a replicated batch of the original problems, not a continuization of the population that plays them. The paper’s divisible-goods examples concern continuous outcomes, which the programme explicitly treats as out of scope. I therefore would not present the replicated-committee construction as a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s central point is decisive: this paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 2 and 4 give sufficient conditions for existence of an implementing mechanism; the corollaries do likewise under extra assumptions. They characterize which social choice correspondences are implementable, but provide no complexity classification or algorithmic problem with an encoded population as input. There is therefore no qualifying result here to anchor the requested computational mirror.

The replicated-committee idea does not supply one. Each committee still has exactly two strategic agents, and the paper’s assumptions concern whether one or both of those agents are partially honest. A distribution over committee types only batches instances of the same two-agent question. If committees are independent, their masses do not change the implementation conditions. If a common mechanism links their reports or outcomes, that is a new, population-wide implementation model, not a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s two-agent game.

The strongest repair would be to make the members themselves a continuum and study implementation from aggregate reports. But that changes the strategic model the paper characterizes. In a standard anonymous nonatomic model, one person’s report has zero effect on the aggregate, so the Nash incentives that drive partial-honesty implementation may disappear. Avoiding that problem would require further modeling choices—such as blocs with positive influence—that again move away from the paper’s fixed two-agent results. Divisible goods and bargaining examples do not bridge the gap: they make outcomes continuous while leaving the society at two agents.

This is a strong case against a *mirror of this paper* within ChoCo’s computational-population scope, not a proof that a new computational theory of many-agent implementation could never be worthwhile. Such a theory may be interesting, but it would need its own model and computational questions; this paper does not furnish the result to continuize.
