What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the top-cycle and robust-dominant-set characterizations as distributional axioms; it leaves the auxiliary structural lemmas, strategyproofness variants, and proof constructions without computational counterparts.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a qualified one: the paper has a very natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s rules.

The closest anchor is the paper’s Theorem \(\texttt{thm:TC}\), proved in the paper. It states that the top cycle is the only pairwise social choice correspondence satisfying strategyproofness, set non-imposition, and homogeneity. The related Theorem \(\texttt{thm:F-SP}\), also proved here, states that a pairwise SCC satisfying non-imposition, homogeneity, and neutrality is strategyproof if and only if it is a robust dominant set rule. Neither theorem is an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, approximation, or other computational-complexity result; the paper contains no named result of that kind. Thus these cannot formally serve as ChoCo anchors.

The natural mirror of Theorem \(\texttt{thm:TC}\) would be the following.

Let \(A\) be a finite set of alternatives and \(T=\mathcal R(A)\) the set of complete rankings. A society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\). Define the normalized majority margin

\[ g_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \bigl(\mathbf 1[x\succ_t y]-\mathbf 1[y\succ_t x]\bigr). \]

Let \(x\succeq_\mu y\) iff \(g_\mu(x,y)\ge 0\), and let \(\operatorname{TC}(\mu)\) be the alternatives that reach every alternative in the directed graph of \(\succeq_\mu\). The continuous problem is:

Given \(A\), a rational type distribution \(\mu\), and optionally an alternative \(x\), compute \(\operatorname{TC}(\mu)\), or decide whether \(x\in\operatorname{TC}(\mu)\).

This is a genuine high-multiplicity population model. The many agents might be voters in a national election, party members, or respondents drawn from a small number of repeated ballot types. Their complete rankings, rather than their names, determine their type; typically \(n\gg |\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\), with \(|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\le m!\). Rational \(\mu\) is exactly the normalized profile of a finite election whose voter multiplicities realize those proportions.

The action variable is the returned choice set, or, for a strategic version, a mass transfer

\[ \mu'=\mu-\varepsilon e_t+\varepsilon e_{t'} \]

in which an \(\varepsilon\)-mass block of type \(t\) changes its reported ranking to \(t'\). A non-vacuous continuum version of strategyproofness would prohibit

\[ \operatorname{TC}(\mu')\succ_t^F \operatorname{TC}(\mu), \]

where \(\succ_t^F\) is Fishburn’s extension. This mass-block formulation is necessary because an individual atom in an atomless population has zero influence.

The expected computational classification of continuous top-cycle winner determination is Class A: construct the weighted majority graph and compute reachability, in polynomial time in the explicit support size, \(m\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\). The authors would recognize the model as their problem: it preserves pairwise information, majority ties, the top-cycle definition, and the strategyproofness setting. The paper’s homogeneity assumption makes the normalization especially natural.

The weakness is substantial. For rational distributions, this is essentially a normalized restatement of the paper’s variable-electorate model, not a new complexity phenomenon. Moreover, individual strategyproofness disappears in the literal continuum limit, while the mass-block repair changes the notion toward group strategyproofness. The paper’s Theorem \(\texttt{thm:TC}\) would therefore provide a strong axiomatic motivation, but not a compliant computational anchor.

So the best positive claim is: the paper supports a sensible and mathematically clean continuous-population winner-determination problem, probably tractable, with natural extensions to manipulation by positive-mass coalitions, perturbation robustness, and tie-boundary analysis. But under the programme’s anchor requirement, the correct verdict is that this is a promising near-case rather than a qualifying continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at ChoCo’s entry gate: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor.

Theorem \(\texttt{thm:TC}\) and Theorem \(\texttt{thm:F-SP}\) are axiomatic characterizations of social choice correspondences. They do not define a computational problem, specify an input representation, or assert a complexity, approximation, parameterized, or algorithmic result. The sentence that the top cycle “can be easily computed” is not a named computational result. Thus there is no paper result whose computational landscape can be continuized.

The proposed mirror of \(\texttt{thm:TC}\) is nevertheless mathematically legitimate. A distribution \(\mu\) over rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity society, and the authors would recognize it. But it is only a new winner-determination exercise:

\[ g_\mu(x,y)=\sum_t\mu_t \bigl(\mathbf 1[x\succ_t y]-\mathbf 1[y\succ_t x]\bigr), \]

followed by reachability in the resulting graph. With explicit support, this takes polynomial time in the support size and \(m\); with the full type space, it is polynomial in \(\tau=m!\), which is simply the input-size measure. The continuous simplex contributes no optimization problem, pricing problem, or computational phenomenon. The top-cycle output is constant on the finitely many cells cut out by the hyperplanes \(g_\mu(x,y)=0\).

This is not the mistaken objection that high-multiplicity work is already done, nor is “the answer is unchanged” being used as a reason to reject the mirror. The point is narrower: the paper itself supplies no computational question here. Winner determination is an invented companion problem, not a continuous version of one of the paper’s results.

The strategic version in the positive case is weaker still. A unilateral report change by one agent has zero effect on an atomless \(\mu\), so strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Replacing it by a positive-mass deviation,

\[ \mu'=\mu-\varepsilon e_t+\varepsilon e_{t'}, \]

is a reasonable repair, but it is a coalition or mass-intervention notion rather than the paper’s individual strategyproofness. The paper itself treats group-strategyproofness as a separate strengthening. This repaired problem could be studied, but it is a new manipulation problem with a newly chosen intervention model, not a computational mirror of \(\texttt{thm:TC}\).

The same problem defeats the proposed mirror of \(\texttt{thm:F-SP}\). On normalized distributions, homogeneity is largely built into the representation: replacing a population by \(k\) copies leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Pairwiseness becomes dependence on \(g_\mu\), neutrality is relabelling, and non-imposition is witnessed by the same McGarvey-style profiles. The resulting statement is an axiomatic theorem over a simplex, not a complexity result. Testing whether an arbitrary correspondence over that continuum satisfies these axioms is not a standard finite computational problem; restricting to an explicitly encoded rule simply produces a different problem.

One might improve the proposal by adding a mass-perturbation cost, asking for the least \(\varepsilon\) that changes top-cycle membership, or by enriching types with locations, utilities, or identities. The first creates a new robustness or bribery problem absent from the paper. The second either gets quotiented away because the paper uses only rankings, or changes the model so fundamentally that it is no longer a mirror of these theorems. Making alternatives themselves continuous likewise abandons the finite-alternative top-cycle setting.

So the strongest honest verdict is asymmetric: a continuous population version of the top cycle exists and is sensible, but this paper offers no worthwhile ChoCo mirror in the programme’s computational sense. The literal universal claim that no interesting distributional question can be formulated is too strong; the claim that this paper supplies no qualifying computational mirror is very strong.
