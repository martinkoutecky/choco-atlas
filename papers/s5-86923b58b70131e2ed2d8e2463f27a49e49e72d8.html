Why no mirror

The proposed population-weighted SDR objective is coherent and plausibly tractable under its Euclidean and logistic restrictions. But Proposition 3.6 characterizes an optimizer without asserting that it can be computed, and the paper’s numerical work computes its iterative barycenter scheme rather than this optimizer. The continuous question therefore lacks an anchor that satisfies the computational-result requirement.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this specified Euclidean logistic objective be optimized to additive accuracy \(\varepsilon\) in time polynomial in dimension, support size, input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • Does tractability extend to the paper’s Wasserstein or other nonlinear opinion metrics?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow optimization mirror for choosing the common social discount rate. Its anchor is Proposition 3.6, proved in Appendix B: under the proposition’s smoothness assumption, a proposal maximizing the product of the agents’ acceptance probabilities is a Fréchet barycenter of their anchor opinions, with weights determined by their acceptance functions. The paper computes barycenters in its numerical work and describes numerical methods for them in Appendix E.2. This is a structural optimization result, though it gives no complexity bound.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large climate-policy consultation represented by many residents or stakeholder members sharing calibrated discount-rate models and the same willingness-to-deviate parameters. A type \(t\) is the pair \((\theta_t,\alpha_t)\): the parameters \(\theta_t\) of that type’s allowable discount curve and its acceptance sensitivity \(\alpha_t\). Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population. There might be millions of participants but only hundreds of distinct calibration-and-response types. This is a scenario for the instance regime, not a claim that every climate negotiation has low type diversity.

Here is the corresponding problem. Let allowable discount curves be parameterized by \(\theta\in[0,1]^d\), with the Euclidean parameter metric, a choice the paper permits for its curve space. The input is a finite list of types \((\theta_t,\alpha_t,\mu_t)\), with rational parameters, \(\alpha_t\ge 0\), \(\mu_t>0\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), plus an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\). Use the paper’s illustrative acceptance function
\[ \phi_t(s)=\frac{1}{2(1+\exp(\alpha_t s))}. \]
The Continuous SDR Agreement Problem asks for a rational proposal \(\hat\theta\in[0,1]^d\) such that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\log\phi_t\!\left(\|\hat\theta-\theta_t\|^2\right) \;\ge\; \max_{\theta\in[0,1]^d} \sum_t \mu_t\log\phi_t\!\left(\|\theta-\theta_t\|^2\right)-\varepsilon. \]
The output curve is the common SDR proposal. The objective is average log acceptance propensity, the per-capita form of the paper’s product objective: for discrete counts \(N_t\), maximizing \(\prod_t\phi_t^{N_t}\) has the same maximizer as maximizing this weighted sum with \(\mu_t=N_t/N\). It therefore gives a precise high-multiplicity version of the proposition’s proposal-selection problem.

I expect this specified version to be tractable for additive approximation: maximizing the objective is equivalent to minimizing a weighted sum of \(\log(1+\exp(\alpha_t\|\theta-\theta_t\|^2))\), a convex objective over a box. Convex optimization is the likely route to an algorithm polynomial in the dimension, support size \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), under a standard rational approximation model. That is a proposed complexity question and expectation, not a result established by this paper. Natural follow-ups are whether tractability survives for the paper’s Wasserstein and other nonlinear opinion metrics, and what happens when acceptance depends on a social network rather than being independent across agents.

This mirror covers only the static proposal-selection result in Proposition 3.6 and the paper’s common-SDR application. It does not continuize the paper’s time evolution, clustering, or network dynamics. The opinion itself already lies in a continuous parameter space; that alone is outcome-space continuity, not the mirror. The population distribution over types is the continuization here.

The weak point is the anchor: Proposition 3.6 characterizes an optimizer but does not establish that the underlying problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. If “anchor” strictly requires a named complexity-class result, this paper has no qualifying anchor, so the case above is only a conditional positive case under the allowance for a named structural optimization result that the paper computes with.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational complexity result to mirror. Proposition 3.6 characterizes a proposal that maximizes acceptance probability; it gives no algorithmic bound or classification. The evolutionary scheme and its clustering extension are simulation procedures, not complexity results. The paper also keeps the population finite: its opinions lie in continuous metric spaces, but its agents are indexed individually. That distinction matters here—the continuous opinion space is not a continuous society.

The proponent’s climate-consultation example is nevertheless a plausible high-multiplicity regime. If many participants share the same discount-curve parameters and acceptance behavior, types with rational masses are a sensible model. For the stated logistic acceptance function, maximizing the finite population’s product of acceptance probabilities is equivalent to maximizing the mass-weighted sum of log acceptance probabilities. That makes the proposed optimization problem coherent, and I cannot rule it out by claiming that mass loses needed individual information: for this static objective, it does not.

But that is a newly specified computational problem, rather than a computational question posed or classified in the paper. The authors provide no input encoding for a population distribution, no output-accuracy requirement, and no complexity measure for the proposal-selection task. The proposed mirror supplies those choices, along with a parameter box, metric, acceptance function, and notion of approximation. A more general mirror could use other opinion spaces or ask for an accepted fraction instead of a per-capita log acceptance rate, but those choices would define different computational problems. The latter criterion, in particular, is a natural population-level reformulation, but it is not the probability that every member accepts—the consensus event in the finite product formulation.

That gap is more pronounced for the paper’s dynamic scheme. Its agents have individual behavioral parameters and changing network neighborhoods. A type can include those features, as the programme requires, but then the mirror needs a model for how a continuum population carries and updates its interaction structure. The paper does not pose that model or a computational task over it. Clustering agents into representative groups is a useful simulation device; it does not by itself specify a continuous-population algorithmic problem.

So the best negative case is a scope objection: there is no named computational result here whose complexity landscape the continuous mirror would complete. The proposed SDR optimization may still be a worthwhile new question, and the high-multiplicity scenario is credible. I cannot honestly make the universal claim airtight: if the programme accepts a named structural optimization proposition as an anchor, the proponent’s mirror could survive. My case is strongest only under the stricter reading that a mirror must be anchored in a computational result of the paper.
