The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution μ over complete voter types, where each type specifies a ranking, utility function, and lazy-voter attitude, and where the support has a single-crossing voter order or a common single-peaked candidate axis, decide whether arbitrarily large integers \(N\) with \(Nμ_t\in\mathbb{Z}\) admit a pure Nash equilibrium in the replicated election under \(R\in\{R_C,R_V\}\), using \(\epsilon_N=1/(2m^2N^2)\); return one coordinatewise limit of the normalized type-by-ballot masses along such a sequence.

The model it lives in

The society is the distribution μ over complete types, with support size τ. Replicate each type \(t\) exactly \(Nμ_t\) times; the decision variables are normalized ballot masses \(x_{t,b}^{(N)}\) for \(b\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}\), and the output is a limit \(x_{t,b}\) of these masses along replications that have a pure Nash equilibrium.

The objection that survived

The paper proves algorithms only for explicit finite electorates; it does not establish that the algorithms or equilibrium characterization transfer to rational type masses and their limiting profiles.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers lazy voters with randomized tie-breaking under the single-crossing result of Theorem 2 and the single-peaked result of Theorem 3. It leaves truth-biased voters and lexicographic tie-breaking aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the equilibrium existence conditions be decided from rational type masses in time polynomial in \(m\), τ, and the input bit length?
  • Which limiting ballot-mass profiles and winner sets can occur in the single-crossing and single-peaked cases?

The case FOR (proponent)

The case I believe is a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s lazy-voter equilibrium problem. Its population is described by type masses, while equilibrium is defined through larger and larger finite replications of those masses. This gives a continuous object to compute—limiting ballot masses—while retaining the one-voter deviations that make the paper’s Nash question meaningful.

Let \(T\) be the positive-mass support. A type \(t\) specifies a complete ranking \(\succ_t\), utility function \(u_t\), and lazy-voter attitude. The input gives rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\), with \(\tau=|T|\). For each integer \(N\) such that every \(N\mu_t\) is integral, form an election with \(N\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), using the paper’s lazy-voter utilities and, for example, \(\epsilon_N=1/(2m^2N^2)\). Require all types to be tie-consistent, and promise either a single-crossing order of the replicated voters or a common single-peaked candidate axis.

For each such \(N\), a ballot profile has normalized type-by-ballot masses
\[ x^{(N)}_{t,b}=\frac{|\{i:\operatorname{type}(i)=t,\ b_i=b\}|}{N}, \qquad b\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}. \]
They sum to \(\mu_t\) for each type, and candidate \(c\)’s normalized score is \(\sum_t x^{(N)}_{t,c}\). Define a limiting equilibrium mass profile \(x\) as the coordinatewise limit of profiles from pure Nash equilibria along some sequence \(N_j\to\infty\). My named problems are Limit-(L,\(R\))-ExistNE: does an unbounded sequence of these replications have a PNE, and return one limiting profile \(x\)? And Limit-(L,\(R\))-TieNE(\(c^\star\)): does such a sequence have PNEs in which \(c^\star\) belongs to a non-singleton winning set in every election, and return a limiting profile? Here \(R\in\{R_C,R_V\}\), as in the anchors below.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for lazy voters and either random-candidate or random-voter tie-breaking, both ExistNE and TieNE admit polynomial-time algorithms when the utilities are tie-consistent and the preference profile is single-crossing in the voter order. The paper uses an equilibrium characterization from Elkind et al. [15]; Theorem 2’s algorithmic result is proved here. Its single-crossing structure is especially apt for the limit problem: voter groups that support different members of a tied set must form consecutive blocks along the voter-mass order.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. It establishes the same polynomial-time results for lazy voters and either randomized tie-breaking rule when utilities are tie-consistent and preferences are single-peaked with respect to a common candidate order. In the limit model, this corresponds to societies whose voters’ rankings share a candidate axis; the paper’s dynamic program can be tested with weighted masses in place of voter counts.

For both limit problems, I expect Class A, tractable. The reason is more specific than “the finite problem is in P.” The paper’s Theorem 1, attributed to Elkind et al. [15], characterizes when the lazy-voter game has an equilibrium. In sufficiently large replications, its case requiring each candidate to be ranked first by at most one voter disappears: every positive-mass type is repeated many times. The remaining existence cases have direct mass certificates. Either all types share one favorite, or there is a set \(X\) of \(k\ge2\) candidates and a rational assignment \(y_{t,c}\) of each type’s mass to candidates in \(X\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{c\in X}y_{t,c}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t y_{t,c}=\frac1k. \]
Whenever \(y_{t,c}>0\), type \(t\) must rank \(c\) first within \(X\) and satisfy the paper’s strong-balance utility inequality
\[ \frac1k\sum_{d\in X}u_t(d)\ \ge\ \max_{d\in X\setminus\{c\}}u_t(d). \]
Such a certificate yields an equilibrium in all sufficiently divisible replications; for TieNE, require \(c^\star\in X\). The paper’s single-crossing graph and single-peaked dynamic program suggest ways to find these certificates from weighted type support, with work polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

The plausible regime is a large electorate with many voters but relatively few recurring complete preference-and-utility types: for example, voters ordered by a salient ideological or socioeconomic dimension, with preferences single-crossing, or voters evaluating candidates along a shared policy axis, with preferences single-peaked. The type definition includes every feature the game uses, including utilities and tie attitude; mass means population share. The finite ballot choices and tie-breaking rule remain those of the paper.

The scope is deliberately narrow: lazy voters, randomized tie-breaking, and the paper’s tie-consistent single-crossing or single-peaked settings. This paper does not give a numbered hardness theorem of its own. Its cited NP-hardness results for unrestricted preferences come from Elkind et al. [15]; they motivate the structured cases but are not my anchors. I am not extending the claim to truth-biased voters or lexicographic tie-breaking.

The weakest point is that a limiting equilibrium of replications is a new asymptotic question; the paper computes equilibria in a specified finite election. Also, its polynomial bounds are stated for explicit voter profiles. A bit-complexity result for rational masses must show that the algorithms really process type multiplicities in compressed form. The graph and dynamic-programming proofs make that look plausible, but Theorems 2 and 3 do not establish it. That gap is a concrete part of the proposed mirror: prove compressed algorithms for the two limit problems, characterize their possible limiting winner sets, and then ask what survives when tie-consistency is relaxed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s equilibrium notion depends on a voter having a nonzero effect on the result. In a genuinely atomless electorate, one voter has zero mass: changing their ballot cannot change the score vector or the tie-breaking lottery. Lazy voters therefore abstain for the \(\epsilon\) bonus, and the strategic content of the finite game disappears. If \(\epsilon\) is scaled down with the electorate, it disappears too. The limit is not the paper’s equilibrium problem.

The proposed finite-replication limit avoids that collapse, but it keeps the pivotal one-voter deviations by retaining elections of size \(N\) and asking what happens as \(N\) grows. The limiting mass profile alone does not describe those deviations: they depend on score changes of \(1/N\), exact ties, and the finite-\(N\) tie-breaking incentives. That makes the proposal an asymptotic question about a sequence of atomic games, rather than a native continuum equilibrium model. It is a legitimate question, but the case against it is that continuization has compressed the input without removing the discrete strategic mechanism that drives the paper’s results.

That objection does not defeat either anchor. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are explicit computational results, and both have plausible high-multiplicity versions. A type-level order can preserve the single-crossing promise; a candidate axis preserves the single-peaked promise. The algorithms’ counts and voter comparisons appear amenable to weighted arithmetic on rational masses. In the lazy-voter characterization, sufficiently large replications eliminate the “at most one first-place voter per candidate” case, leaving balanced-set conditions that can be stated directly in masses. Exact balance may make the resulting question restrictive, but that alone is not a reason to dismiss it. I would specify the mass certificate so each type can contribute only to its unique favorite in the candidate set; strict rankings do not permit arbitrary reassignment across that set.

So the best negative case is about fit and added value: these results already give polynomial algorithms for the structured finite games, and the likely mirror may mainly compress voter counts while retaining finite-game pivotality. That is weaker than showing no worthwhile mirror exists. I cannot honestly defeat the stronger versions of Theorems 2 and 3 on the material given; both remain credible anchors for the programme.
