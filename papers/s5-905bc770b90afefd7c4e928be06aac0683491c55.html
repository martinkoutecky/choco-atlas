The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), an unmatched option \(U\), a finite set of student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), strict preferences over schools, school priorities over types, and per-capita capacities \(q_s\), compute a mass-valued student-proposing deferred-acceptance allocation \(y\). After school \(e\) closes, keep every assignment \(y_{t,s}\) for \(s\ne e\) fixed and let \(d_t=y_{t,e}\). Compute an assignment \(x_{t,s}\) of each displaced mass \(d_t\) to an acceptable remaining school or \(U\), with capacity additions \(\Delta_s=\max\{0,\sum_t(y_{t,s}+x_{t,s})-q_s\}\), such that the resulting allocation is stable and student-optimal among stable continuations preserving all non-displaced offers.

The model it lives in

A fluid school-choice market whose types record student preferences and school priority classes, with each type carrying mass. The decision variables are displaced-mass assignments \(x_{t,s}\) and the induced capacity additions \(\Delta_s\); the criterion is stability and student-optimality, with capacity expanded only enough to accommodate the assignment.

The objection that survived

The paper ranks individual students strictly, while the low-support mirror needs agents in a type to share priority classes; the mass model therefore requires class-level priority and stability semantics that Theorem 4.4 does not establish.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the resource-reduction recovery result in Theorem 4.4. It leaves the subtractive and additive error results, affected-group counts, and simulations outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the threshold rule from Stable Expansion give a student-optimal stable continuation for this mass model?
  • Can the continuation be computed in time polynomial in the number of schools \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is Continuous Stable Expansion for the paper’s resource-reduction result. It asks what a large matching system should do when a school closes after offers have gone out, while preserving the assignments already offered to everyone else.

The anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper. It says that Stable Expansion produces the student-optimal stable match subject to keeping all offers to non-displaced students. The theorem relies on Algorithm 1: for each student displaced by the closure, scan their preferences and assign them to the first school that ranks them above its best rejected non-displaced applicant; leave them unmatched if no such school exists. The paper does not state a formal complexity classification, but this is a constructive result: Algorithm 1 makes one pass over each displaced student’s preference list, so the finite-instance computation is polynomial in the explicit input size.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. A cohort has schools \(S\), an unmatched option \(U\), and a finite support \(T\) of student types. Each type \(t\) has a mass \(\mu_t\), a strict preference order over schools, and a position in each school’s priority order. The masses are rational and sum to one. Schools have per-capita capacities \(q_s\). The instance also gives, or asks the solver to compute, the error-free student-proposing DA allocation \(y\). A school \(e\) then closes. The displaced mass of type \(t\) is \(d_t=y_{t,e}\); all assignments to other schools are preserved as offers.

The task, Continuous Stable Expansion, is to compute a mass assignment \(x_{t,s}\) of the displaced population to the remaining schools or \(U\), together with the resulting capacity additions. It must assign all \(d_t\) units, respect each type’s preference list, preserve every non-displaced assignment, and be stable under the types’ preferences and schools’ priorities. Capacity may grow only as needed to accommodate the resulting assignment: if \(z_s\) is the school’s final assigned mass, its final capacity is \(\max(q_s,z_s)\). Among stable continuations satisfying offer preservation, the output should be student-optimal for the displaced mass. The mass version of the paper’s algorithm gives a concrete candidate: for each displaced type, scan its preference list and choose its highest-ranked school that ranks the type above the best rejected non-displaced type in the original DA run. If no such school exists, assign that mass to \(U\). Since members of a type share the relevant preferences and priorities, the rule acts on the type’s mass rather than on named students.

The natural regime is a large annual school-admission or public-housing match: tens of thousands of applicants, but a much smaller set of recurring preference templates and priority categories. A type records everything relevant to matching, including preferences, priorities, and whether its mass was assigned to the closing school. The resulting type-level assignment is fractional, while each person still receives one school. Per-capita capacities and fractional masses are the natural scaling of the paper’s capacity-expansion model, so that co-modification belongs in the mirror. The authors already study school closures, offer preservation, stability, and student-optimal recovery; this keeps their problem intact while making the population the continuous object.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The paper supplies a structural rule rather than a large optimization formulation: compare each affected type with school-specific rejection thresholds, then assign its mass to its best eligible school. In a finite-support version, that suggests computation polynomial in the number of schools \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the bit length \(L\) of the rational masses and capacities, even when the implied headcount is enormous. Computing the original DA allocation for typed masses is also a natural part of that question. The support-sensitive bound is a prediction for the continuous problem, not a theorem claimed by this paper.

This mirror covers Theorem 4.4’s resource-reduction recovery problem. It does not claim to cover the paper’s subtractive or additive error scenarios, nor its expected affected-group counts or empirical simulations. The paper has no comparable named hardness result to anchor a second mirror; Theorem 5.1’s envy guarantee is not a complexity result, so I would not use it as a filler anchor.

The weakest point is whether a realistic market really has few complete types. The paper assumes strict rankings of students, and an individualized lottery tie-break can make priority profiles nearly unique; including those profiles in the type definition may push \(\tau\) toward the population size and erase the high-multiplicity advantage. The case is strongest for systems where many applicants share preference templates and finite priority classes, and where tie-breaking is itself defined at the class or mass level. That is a genuine restriction on the regime. It does not make the mirror a different problem: it identifies when the paper’s recovery problem has a useful high-multiplicity version.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.4 is not a complexity result. It proves that Stable Expansion returns a student-optimal stable match under the paper’s constraints. Algorithm 1 gives a direct finite-instance procedure, but the theorem says nothing about a computational classification. The proponent’s support-sensitive claim would therefore be a new result about a typed version of the problem, rather than a continuous mirror of a complexity result in this paper.

There is also a mismatch between the proposed type space and the paper’s priority model. Schools strictly rank individual students, and the simulations generate school rankings from individual student-school scores. A complete type must include a student’s position in each school’s ranking. Those positions distinguish students, so the paper’s own model generally has \(\tau\) close to the population size; writing the roster as rational masses does not compress it. Modeling priority tiers instead would change how the paper’s strict priorities and blocking pairs work. Modeling lottery tie-breaks as continuous priority variables would require a different input representation from the paper’s finite-support, rational-mass model.

The strongest rescue is a market with repeated preference templates and finite, shared priority classes. A mass version of the closure problem is plausible there. But it removes the individualized priorities central to the paper’s model, and the paper itself gives no computational theorem for that revised setting. That weakens the case that this particular paper supplies a compelling ChoCo anchor.

This negative case is limited. Finite priority classes are plausible in some admissions systems, and the authors might recognize the resulting mass model as their problem. So I cannot rule out every worthwhile mirror. The case against is that the proposed anchor is not a complexity theorem, while preserving the paper’s strict-priority model defeats the low-support premise the mirror needs.
