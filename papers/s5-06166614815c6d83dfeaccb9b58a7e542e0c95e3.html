Why no mirror

In the proposed replica model, each branch has its own stock and price vector, so the masses merely aggregate independent applications of those theorems. Sharing stock or prices across branches would create a different market that neither result anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror I see is a population of many independent local markets. Think of a large chain of branches, each with its own copy of the same local stock and two procurement departments. A department’s feasible bundles are described by a matroid; across branches, the same few pairs of matroids recur.

A type \(q\) records a branch’s two matroids and item set \(S_q\). Its mass \(\mu_q\) is the fraction of branches of that type, with \(\sum_q\mu_q=1\). Each branch has two buyers, so the corresponding buyer types \((q,1)\) and \((q,2)\) each have mass \(\mu_q/2\). In a large chain there can be many buyers but few distinct types. Stock scales with the number of branches: each branch has its own copy of \(S_q\). This lets each branch’s realized allocation remain integral even though its mass-weighted aggregate allocation can be fractional.

Here is the common continuous question. For each type \(q\), the input gives \(S_q\) and two matroids \(M_{q1},M_{q2}\), accessed by standard matroid oracles, as well as the rational masses \(\mu_q\). Write \(r_{qi}\) for buyer \(i\)’s rank valuation. Find one item-price vector \(p_q:S_q\to\mathbb{Q}\) for each type. In every branch of type \(q\), either buyer may arrive first and choose any utility-maximizing subset of the remaining stock; the second buyer does the same. Let

\[ W_q^\star=\max\{r_{q1}(A)+r_{q2}(B): A,B\subseteq S_q,\ A\cap B=\emptyset\}. \]

The prices must make every such sequence of choices attain \(W_q^\star\), whichever buyer arrives first and however ties are broken. Thus every branch is welfare-optimal, and the population’s aggregate welfare is \(\sum_q\mu_qW_q^\star\). The solution is the family of prices \((p_q)_q\), not a central rule for coordinating buyers’ choices.

My first anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that if \(M_1\) is a partition matroid whose classes have size at most \(2\), with upper bound \(1\) on each class, and \(M_2\) is arbitrary, then both the base-price and rank-valuation pricing conjectures hold, and a suitable price vector can be computed in polynomial time. In the base formulation, every cheapest basis of either matroid leaves a basis for the other. In the rank-valuation formulation used above, every first buyer’s utility-maximizing bundle, followed by any utility-maximizing bundle of the second buyer from what remains, gives total rank equal to the maximum over all disjoint allocations, in either arrival order.

Replica-Safe Pricing (partition case) is the continuous version under that same per-branch restriction: every supported type must have its first buyer’s matroid of this partition form, while the second can be arbitrary. The output is a price vector for each supported type satisfying the common condition above. The branch masses aggregate the outcomes but do not change the price condition. I expect this problem to be tractable: apply Theorem 1 independently to each supported type. The runtime is polynomial in the total ground-set sizes and matroid-oracle calls across the \(\tau\) types, plus the time to read their binary-rational masses of total bit length \(L\). The paper’s price construction uses finite, polynomial-size prices.

My second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It says that when both matroids are strongly base orderable, the same two pricing conjectures hold; moreover, a suitable price vector is computable in polynomial time when the required basis-to-basis bijections can themselves be computed in polynomial time. The underlying guarantee is again that every utility-maximizing first bundle and every subsequent utility-maximizing bundle yield welfare-optimal disjoint allocations, for either arrival order.

Replica-Safe Pricing (strongly base-orderable case) asks the same continuous question, but requires both matroids of each supported type to be strongly base orderable, with the polynomial-time basis-bijection condition. I again expect it to be tractable, by running Theorem 2’s construction separately for each type. The mass representation creates no additional pricing difficulty.

These are close to the paper’s problem: the item prices, matroid rank valuations, sequential arrival, arbitrary tie-breaking, and welfare objective are all preserved. The population regime is a chain of many small markets with standardized local structures; buyers within a branch share their role and valuation structure, and only \(\tau\) branch types occur. That makes the aggregation plausible where many branches repeat, while keeping the paper’s local two-buyer mechanism intact. Both anchors are the paper’s own proved theorems; the statements and numbering appear in the [paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.08759).

The mirror covers Theorems 1 and 2, not the paper’s full program. It does not claim a result for one shared inventory serving all buyers, a single market with many sequential buyers, or general gross-substitutes valuations. Those lead to natural further questions: do type-specific prices still work when types must share a common price vector, and can any analogous guarantee survive with three or more buyers per branch?

My weakest point is that this is a replicated-market population: each branch has private stock, so the continuous result is obtained by applying the paper’s algorithm type by type. A referee could reasonably view it as a straightforward aggregation of solved instances rather than a new continuous computational landscape. I still think it is an honest mirror of the paper’s exact pricing question in a high-multiplicity regime; I would not use it to claim that the paper establishes anything for a large, connected market with shared inventory.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The missing-complexity-result objection does not apply: Theorems 1 and 2 give named polynomial-time algorithms. The stronger objection is that the proposed population does not enter either theorem’s pricing condition.

For the partition-matroid anchor, each type \(q\) is a complete two-buyer market with its own stock and its own price vector \(p_q\). The conditions therefore separate by type: each branch must satisfy Theorem 1 on its own. The mass \(\mu_q\) only weights the sum of branch welfare; it does not change which prices work. Repeating a branch many times changes the reported aggregate, but creates no population-level pricing or allocation decision. This is a distribution over market instances, rather than a high-multiplicity version of one market.

The same problem affects the strongly base-orderable anchor. Its algorithm and oracle assumptions concern a pair of matroids on one ground set. Applying it to each branch leaves the price choice and welfare guarantee independent across branches; the basis-bijection condition does not involve the masses. Thus both anchors establish the same point: batching solved two-buyer markets is easy to describe, but it does not make society continuous in the sense ChoCo is meant to study.

A stronger version could require one common price vector across branches, perhaps chosen to maximize mass-weighted welfare over different market types. That would make the distribution matter. But it asks for a shared-price problem across a collection of markets, not the paper’s guarantee for a two-buyer market. Pool the stock instead, and buyers from different types now interact in a long sequential market; the two-buyer results no longer anchor the question. Keeping infinitesimal buyers while retaining the paper’s indivisible purchases also requires a further model of how goods and individual choices scale.

So the case against these two anchors is strong: their proposed continuization aggregates independent instances rather than continuizing the interacting market studied in the paper. The universal claim is harder. A shared-price, mass-weighted variant might be worthwhile, and I cannot honestly rule it out as a research question. My negative case is therefore strongest against the proponent’s mirrors, not airtight against every possible continuous market-pricing model.
