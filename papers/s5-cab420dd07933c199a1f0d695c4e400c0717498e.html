The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric binary friendship matrix \(A\), and \(k\), compute a mass allocation \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tj}=1/k\). For \(U_t(j;x)=\sum_s A_{ts}x_{sj}\), require \(U_t(\ell;x)\le U_t(j;x)\) for every type \(t\), every occupied group \(j\) with \(x_{tj}>0\), and every group \(\ell\); also require that no coalition of mass \(1/k\), formed from masses \(y_{tj}\le x_{tj}\), gives each participating type \(t\) utility \(\sum_s A_{ts}\sum_j y_{sj}>kU_t(j;x)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type block graph with rational population masses \(\mu\) and symmetric type-level friendship matrix \(A\); decision variables \(x_{tj}\) allocate each type's mass among \(k\) groups of mass \(1/k\). The task is feasibility and output, with utility given by type-level friend mass and fairness measured by envy and mass-based blocking coalitions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.2's simultaneous fairness and computation theme on block graphs, with exact balance, exact envy-freeness, and a \u((k,0)\)-core guarantee. It leaves the arbitrary-graph guarantees of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2, the \(k=2\) results, and the difficulty of finding balanced minimum cuts unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite block populations with integral group sizes, can the continuous allocation be rounded while retaining explicit envy and core guarantees?
  • Which succinct representations of heterogeneous interaction kernels yield computable individual envy and blocking conditions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is the paper’s simultaneous envy and core guarantee, in a high-multiplicity regime where friendship is determined by cohort. Imagine a large recurring banquet or orientation event: there are many people but only a fixed number of cohorts, such as department-by-year groups, and any two cohorts are either all friends or have no friendship ties. As the population grows, the number \(n\) of people grows while the number \(\tau\) of cohorts stays fixed or moderate. This is a structured instance regime, not a claim about arbitrary social networks.

My lead is Continuous Block-Graph Fair Partition. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric binary matrix \(A\) specifying which type pairs are friends, and a number \(k\) of groups. A diagonal entry \(A_{tt}=1\) means distinct members of type \(t\) are friends. The decision variable \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to group \(j\). A solution must satisfy
\[ x_{tj}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^k x_{tj}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{tj}=\frac1k. \]
Thus every unit-mass individual still belongs to one group; a type’s mass can be divided among groups.

Normalize utility by population size. The utility of a type-\(t\) person in group \(j\) is
\[ U_t(j;x)=\sum_{s\in T} A_{ts}x_{sj}. \]
Require exact envy-freeness: for every \(t,j\) with \(x_{tj}>0\), and every group \(\ell\), \(U_t(\ell;x)\le U_t(j;x)\). Require also the \((k,0)\)-core condition: there is no blocking coalition described by masses \(y_{tj}\) with \(0\le y_{tj}\le x_{tj}\), total mass \(\sum_{t,j}y_{tj}=1/k\), and
\[ \sum_s A_{ts}\sum_j y_{sj} > k\,U_t(j;x) \]
for every \((t,j)\) with \(y_{tj}>0\). The task is to output such an \(x\); as in the paper, this is a feasibility problem, with no welfare objective added.

This problem has a direct solution. Set \(x_{tj}=\mu_t/k\). Every group then has the same type composition, so envy is zero. If \(d_t=\sum_s A_{ts}\mu_s\) is the total mass of type-\(t\)’s friends, their utility in any group is \(d_t/k\). A coalition cannot give a type-\(t\) member more than \(d_t\), their total friend mass, so it cannot strictly exceed \(k(d_t/k)\). The output is computable in time proportional to its \(\tau k\) entries, with rational bit lengths polynomial in the input length. This is a Class A mirror, though a simple one.

The paper’s anchor is Theorem 1.2, proved here. It states that for every \(\varepsilon\in[6\sqrt{k\ln k/n},1]\), there exists an efficiently computable \(\varepsilon\)-balanced partition that is
\[ O\!\left(\max\!\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{k}},k\right\}\ln\frac{\Delta k}{\varepsilon}\right) \]
approximately envy-free and in the
\[ \left(k+\sqrt{k},\ O\!\left(k^{3/2}\ln\frac{\Delta k}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\text{-core}. \]
The algorithmic existence argument is developed in this paper using the Lovász Local Lemma. My mirror keeps the underlying question—assign agents to balanced groups so that no one envies and no same-sized coalition can profitably deviate—and asks what becomes of it when the population is represented by mass. The factor \(k\) in the mirror is on the same scale as the theorem’s \(k+\sqrt{k}\); the disappearing additive term reflects the change to normalized, divisible mass. The exact mirror guarantee above is a direct result for this model, not a limit theorem claimed by Theorem 1.2.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime: the graph remains undirected, utilities still count friends in one’s group, and the two fairness notions are unchanged. The co-modification is that type mass may be allocated fractionally across groups. That is the natural continuum analogue of assigning many interchangeable cohort members, and block graphs arise by blowing up each type into many discrete agents. It also gives a clear discrete-to-continuous dictionary: discrete type counts become proportions, and group assignments become mass allocations.

I would not claim this mirrors the paper’s results on arbitrary graphs or the computational difficulty of finding a minimum balanced cut. Nor is there a numbered hardness result in the paper to anchor a continuum-hardness question: the NP-hardness of balanced min-cut is mentioned in §5.1 with an outside citation, rather than given as a numbered complexity theorem here. Theorem 1.2 is the strongest single anchor for this mirror because it addresses both fairness criteria and computation.

The weakest point is also the source of the easy answer: cohort-level friendship and divisible type mass allow every group to receive the same composition. That erases much of the network heterogeneity and rounding difficulty that motivates the finite-graph algorithm. A referee might regard it as too special if the intended application has person-specific friendships. The case survives for genuinely cohort-structured settings, where many agents share the same social role and friendships are modeled at that level. Natural next questions are whether the same guarantees extend from block graphs to graphon interaction kernels, and how quickly they degrade when assignments have finite granularity and cohorts cannot be split exactly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has a legitimate high-multiplicity regime in mind: a dense block graph with many interchangeable members in each cohort. The trouble is that, in precisely this regime, the paper’s fairness problem largely disappears. Set \(x_{tj}=\mu_t/k\), so every group has the same type composition. Each type then has the same utility in every group, giving exact envy-freeness. If \(d_t=\sum_s A_{ts}\mu_s\), any coalition can give a type-\(t\) member utility at most \(d_t\), while the member’s current utility multiplied by \(k\) is already \(d_t\). Thus the partition is in the \((k,0)\)-core. This works for any nonnegative type-level interaction matrix; it does not use the paper’s balancing argument, graph structure, or LLL algorithm.

That is more than a faster answer to Theorem 1.2. Its central finite-graph difficulty is assigning indivisible vertices so that their different neighborhoods are simultaneously served while group sizes stay balanced. Splitting each cohort’s mass evenly removes that joint assignment problem. The envy and core guarantees become consequences of identical group compositions and a degree bound. The proposed mirror is therefore a real, coherent problem, but a thin one: its computational content does not resemble what makes the paper’s result interesting.

The strongest way to preserve that content would be a graphon model with heterogeneous interaction profiles \(W(u,v)\), where agents’ utilities depend on their locations \(u\) and the group’s distribution over \(v\). But this creates a sharp modeling tradeoff. With a finite-step or finite-rank kernel, one can balance the finitely many interaction features across groups in an atomless population; the same equal-utility and \(k\)-core argument then applies. With a genuinely heterogeneous kernel, the fairness conditions concern every \(u\) and the input is an infinite-dimensional function. A finite computational problem needs a succinct representation and a way to approximate it that preserves the individual envy and blocking conditions. The paper supplies no natural representation of that kind.

Sparse social networks make the gap sharper: under the usual population normalization, bounded-degree utilities vanish in the limit. Rescaling utility to retain local neighborhood effects may be reasonable, but it changes the model again and needs its own justification. Thus the plausible routes either smooth away the assignment challenge or require a new computational framework for heterogeneous kernels.

Theorem 1.2 is a genuine computational anchor, so the case against does not rest on a lack of a result to mirror. And the block-graph scenario is not impossible. My negative case is that the proposed finite-type mirror earns its guarantees by removing the paper’s main difficulty, while the richer graphon version has no evident finite-input formulation that preserves it. I cannot rule out a carefully chosen, succinct graphon model being worthwhile; that is where this case is weakest.
