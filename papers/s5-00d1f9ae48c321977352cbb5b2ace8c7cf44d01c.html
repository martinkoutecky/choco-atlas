The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\) classes, ordered tasks \(t=1,\ldots,T\), \(\tau\) worker cohorts with rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), and report fractions \(p_{r,t,k}\) for each cohort, task, and class, suppose each cohort shares a one-coin reliability parameter with a \(\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\) prior. Run the paper’s one-pass rule with \(\hat y_t\in\arg\max_{k\in[K]}\sum_r\mu_r\hat q_{r,t-1}p_{r,t,k}\), fractional agreement mass \(C_{r,t}=\mu_r\sum_{s=1}^t p_{r,s,\hat y_s}\), and update \(\hat q_{r,t}=(\lambda C_{r,t}+\alpha-1)/(\lambda t\mu_r+\alpha+\beta-2)\), where \(\lambda\) scales population mass to effective worker count. Determine whether this mass-valued estimator has the convergence guarantee of Theorem 1 under corresponding truth-recovery assumptions.

The model it lives in

A society of worker mass partitioned into \(\tau\) reliability cohorts, each with a common one-coin parameter and Beta prior; at task \(t\), each cohort supplies a mass fraction \(p_{r,t,k}\) reporting class \(k\). The decision is the weighted-majority task label, and each cohort’s reliability estimate is updated from its fractional mass agreeing with that label, with \(\lambda\) converting mass to effective counts.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 minimizes a log posterior although Equation 7 gives its maximizer; correcting this to a maximizer makes the target the current estimate itself, so the stated rate does not establish convergence to true worker quality.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the one-pass worker-quality convergence claim in Theorem 1 for shared-reliability cohorts; it leaves the two-pass variant, empirical accuracy comparisons, and other reported results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the fractional recurrence satisfy a meaningful \(o(1/\sqrt{t})\) convergence or error bound under explicit truth-identification assumptions?
  • Was Theorem 1 intended to converge to a fixed true reliability, or to an optimizer of the posterior objective? The printed minimizer conflicts with Equation 7, and changing it to a maximizer makes the target equal to the estimate.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: online label aggregation by a large population of workers grouped into a small number of genuine reliability cohorts. Imagine a crowdsourcing service with millions of recurring annotators, but only a modest number \(\tau\) of qualification tiers. Workers in a cohort have the same task assignment policy and the same one-coin accuracy parameter; their individual reports vary, but the problem treats them alike. The instance records each cohort’s fraction of mass voting for each class on each task. Thus the worker population is continuous while the classes and task sequence remain discrete.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Appendix A. It defines \(f_t(W)\) as the joint posterior density of worker qualities at time \(t\), and \(L_t(W)=\log f_t(W)\) using the paper’s displayed expression. It then asserts that the quality estimates \(\hat W\) from Equation 7 converge to \(W_t^*=\arg\min_W L_t(W)\) at rate \(o(1/\sqrt{t})\). The analysis is under the paper’s stated assumption that most workers are honest enough for Equation 5 to estimate task truths accurately; the theorem’s formal statement itself does not repeat that assumption. This is a convergence guarantee, not a result classifying the problem as polynomial-time or hard. The paper’s complexity comparisons are in an unnumbered table, so I would not present them as a numbered anchor.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Cohort One-Pass Label Aggregation. An instance has \(K\) classes, \(T\) tasks arriving in order, and \(\tau\) worker cohorts with rational masses \(\mu_r>0\), where \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). For each cohort \(r\), task \(t\), and class \(k\), the input gives \(p_{r,t,k}\), the fraction of that cohort’s mass reporting \(k\), with \(\sum_k p_{r,t,k}=1\). Each cohort has an unknown common reliability \(q_r\), a Beta prior with parameters \(\alpha,\beta\), and the same one-coin interpretation as in the paper. A scale \(\lambda\) converts normalized mass into effective worker count.

At each task, estimate its true class by the paper’s weighted vote with sums replaced by mass integrals:
\[ \hat y_t\in\arg\max_{k\in[K]}\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\hat q_{r,t-1}p_{r,t,k}. \]
Then update cohort \(r\)’s quality using its mass of reports agreeing with the estimated truth,
\[ C_{r,t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mu_r p_{r,s,\hat y_s}, \qquad \hat q_{r,t}= \frac{\lambda C_{r,t}+\alpha-1} {\lambda t\mu_r+\alpha+\beta-2}. \]
The question is to produce the task-label and cohort-quality sequence in one pass, using only the cohort sufficient statistics, and characterize when the quality estimates converge at the rate asserted by Theorem 1 under the corresponding honest-majority and identifiability conditions. A solution is an online algorithm and a proof of its guarantees. This is the theorem’s underlying inference problem, not a continuous outcome space or an axiomatic question.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: the per-task vote and quality update depend on \(\tau\) cohort summaries, so the natural algorithm takes \(O(\tau K T)\) arithmetic operations and stores \(O(\tau+T)\) values. The mass-valued reports are a direct high-multiplicity form of the paper’s repeated worker labels, and the authors would likely recognize the task as their quality-estimation problem under an exchangeable-cohort regime. It mirrors the convergence claim, not the paper’s empirical accuracy comparisons or every variant of its model.

The main weakness is that the continuous limit can make the statistical question much easier. If cohort reports are exact population fractions, finite-worker sampling noise disappears; the paper’s finite-population convergence behavior may then collapse or change. Also, as printed, Theorem 1 calls \(W_t^*\) a *minimizer* of a log posterior, although Equation 7 is the Beta posterior mode, normally a maximizer. The theorem’s proof appears to use the mode interpretation. A prover should resolve that wording before claiming the rate transfers. Further questions include how the guarantee changes with sparse task assignment, whether it survives cohort-specific confusion channels, and which finite-worker error bounds remain meaningful when reports are represented by exact masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a poor anchor for a computational mirror. As stated, it calls \(W_t^*=\arg\min_W L_t(W)\), even though \(L_t\) is a log posterior and Equation 7 gives its maximizer. Correct “min” to “max” and \(W_t^*\) is the same posterior mode as \(\hat W_t\), so the claimed rate measures distance from the estimate to itself. The theorem also compares against a target that changes with \(t\); it does not establish convergence to a worker’s fixed true quality. A continuous version that copies this statement would inherit little substantive content.

The proposed cohort model changes the estimator. The paper maintains a separate posterior for each worker. A cohort’s report fractions and one pooled quality estimate do not generally reproduce its weighted vote: workers’ estimated qualities can differ because their observed histories differ, and those histories can be related to their current reports. A stronger mirror could preserve that information by tracking mass over posterior states, such as prior cohort and cumulative agreement count, and splitting each state’s mass by the current reported class. That would be a sensible continuous model, but its update is a weighted replay of the paper’s recurrence. Its work is linear in the supplied state support and tasks; it introduces no evident separation or optimization problem. Exact population fractions also remove the sampling variation that gives the paper’s worker-quality analysis much of its statistical interest.

So the strongest case against is that the named anchor is not a sound convergence guarantee, and the repaired population version appears to be routine streaming arithmetic rather than a new computational problem. Label aggregation also estimates an exogenous truth from noisy reports; its connection to collective choice is weaker than a model where agents’ preferences determine a social outcome.

That case does not establish the universal negative the prompt asks for. A population of recurring annotators with shared parameters is plausible, and tracking mass over posterior states is a well-posed high-multiplicity formulation. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, a straightforward tractable mirror can still be worth studying. The negative case is therefore strongest against the value of this particular anchor, and weak against the existence of any worthwhile mirror.
