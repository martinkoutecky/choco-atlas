The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or giving an algorithm.

The closest candidate is Theorem 4.3. It says that for prime \(p\), closed and covering preferences, and a continuous measure \(\mu\) for which no favourable division exists with \(2p-2\) tiles, there are at least \(\binom{2p-1}{p-1}2^{2-p}\) favourable divisions with \(2p-1\) tiles. Here “favourable” means envy-free, equidistributing \(\mu\), giving each player at most three tiles, with at most one player receiving three. That is a topological existence-and-count result, not a computational result. Theorem 2.3’s example with exactly two envy-free divisions is likewise not a complexity result.

There is also a scope mismatch: the paper’s players remain finite in number. The cake and measure are continuous, but that continuizes the resource, not the population. A continuous-population model of agents assigned mass across boxes could be formulated, but it would be a new question without a named computational result here to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that this paper proves topological existence and counting results, not a complexity classification. That rules out claiming the paper already contains an algorithmic theorem. It does not rule out a worthwhile computational continuation: Theorem 4.3 specifies a concrete feasibility question—whether an envy-free, measure-equidistributing division with the stated bundle restrictions exists—and gives a sharp piece-count threshold to investigate.

A natural high-multiplicity version has a finite set \(T\) of complete preference types, with rational population masses \(\mu_t\). Scale the resource supply per capita as the population grows. Represent an allocation by masses \(x_{t,B}\), where \(B\) is an allowed bundle pattern: each mass unit receives an integral bundle, while \(x_{t,B}\) records what fraction of type \(t\) receives it. Impose the type-level envy and equipartition conditions, using finite rational encodings of preferences and the measure. Then ask whether a favorable allocation exists, or how many cuts are needed to guarantee one. This keeps the population continuous and turns the paper’s feasibility conditions into an algorithmic problem. Its complexity could hinge on pricing over bundle configurations, which is precisely the kind of question the programme is meant to expose.

Theorem 2.3 is a weaker anchor: raw counts can change when identical agents are represented by divisible masses. A sensible version would count distinct combinatorial allocation patterns after identifying swaps among same-type agents, rather than count every possible mass flow as a separate division. That needs careful modeling, but it does not undermine the Theorem 4.3 question.

So the paper offers no direct complexity result to transfer, and its proof does not settle these algorithmic questions. The stronger conclusion that there is no worthwhile mirror does not follow: the population-type model gives a concrete, high-multiplicity computational problem anchored in the paper’s central favorable-division result.
