The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case anchored in this paper’s named results: it contains no named computational result of the kind the task requires. Proposition 4.5 characterizes a fixed point, and Theorem 4.11 proves equilibrium existence under a finite-improvement assumption. Propositions 5.5 and 5.7 concern consensus convergence. None gives a complexity classification or an algorithm with a running-time result. The algorithm in Section 3.3 describes the dynamics; it does not analyze their computational complexity. So there are no eligible anchors to quote and restate.

The strongest positive indication is that a high-multiplicity scenario is imaginable: a large organization with many interchangeable members, grouped by opinion, role, learning rate, and mobility terms. A mass-based model could track how member types are distributed across coalitions and ask whether any type can profitably move. That is recognizably related to the paper’s exit–join incentives and within-coalition consensus.

But that would be a proposed research direction, not a result of this paper. Its Aumann–Drèze payoffs use finite-player Shapley values, and its interaction matrices are indexed by specific coalitions. A continuum version would need to define the corresponding payoff allocation and consensus model. Theorem 4.11 does not settle that version: its existence argument relies on the finiteness of \(N\) and \(\Pi(N)\). Since there is no eligible computational anchor, I would not present a continuous problem as though it were the mirror of one of the paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no anchors to defeat: the proponent explicitly raises no eligible continuous question. The paper’s named results establish equilibrium existence under a finite-improvement assumption (Theorem 4.11), characterize fixed points (Proposition 4.5), and analyze consensus, stability, and polarization. None gives a complexity classification, reduction, or running-time bound. Algorithm 3.3 describes the dynamics; it does not analyze the cost of computing them. So the paper offers no computational result for a continuous mirror to preserve or re-examine.

The strongest alternative is to turn Theorem 4.11 into an equilibrium-finding problem for a large population: group interchangeable agents by opinion, role, learning rate, and mobility terms, then ask how to compute a stable allocation of their mass across coalitions. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not reject it on the ground that agents have different types. But the theorem proves existence using a finite set of partitions and an assumed finite-improvement property; it says nothing about finding an equilibrium efficiently. A continuum version of its existence claim would be an analytic question, while an algorithmic equilibrium-search problem would need a new formalization of coalition allocation and payoff.

That leaves a firm case against calling this paper a worthwhile *mirror* for the programme: its results are about dynamics and equilibrium structure, not computational questions. It does not support the requested exercise of anchoring continuous questions on named computational results.

The broader claim—that no scenario could yield worthwhile computational work—is weaker and cannot honestly be made airtight. A carefully specified high-multiplicity equilibrium-search problem may be worth studying. But that would be a new direction prompted by the paper’s subject matter, rather than a continuization of a computational result the paper actually establishes.
