What the mirror covers

The proposed analogues cover only Theorems 3.5 and 3.6 over mass distributions; they leave the empirical failure rates, described procedures, and other claims about Borda variations without a continuous result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The population regime is a strong fit for continuization. A voter type can be a complete or partial ranking of the candidates, and \(\mu_t\) can be the fraction of voters submitting that ballot. The paper already aggregates ballots into preference profiles; its 2021 Queens primary, for example, had 195,096 voters and a compact set of ballot types. That makes \(n\gg\tau\) plausible, and percentages are a natural way to express the electorate.

The closest candidate mirror is ABC Condorcet-Loser Feasibility. An instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported partial rankings. A ranking of \(k\) candidates among \(m\) gives rank \(r\) the score \(m-r+1\), and each unranked candidate \((m-k+1)/2\). Ask whether some candidate \(a\) is an ABC winner and loses every pairwise comparison: a ranked candidate is preferred to an unranked one, while a ballot ranking neither candidate contributes to neither side. A solution is such a candidate, or a “no” answer. Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper, says ABC can never elect a Condorcet loser and therefore can never elect a majority loser. Its completion argument extends to rational mass: split each partial type’s mass across the two reverse completions, and the resulting complete-ballot Borda society preserves the relevant score and pairwise comparisons. The continuous feasibility problem therefore has no “yes” instances: it is tractable, but only in this degenerate sense. A related question suggested by the paper is how much ballot mass must change to alter a winner under a specified truncation or compromise strategy.

A second possible mirror is MBC Majority-Loser Feasibility, with the same kind of mass distribution, but MBC scores ranked positions \(k,k-1,\ldots,1\) and unranked candidates zero. Ask whether an MBC winner can receive a majority of last-place votes under the paper’s convention for partial ballots. Theorem 3.5, also proved here, says this can never happen. Its completion proof likewise carries over by weighting each ballot type by its mass. This too is an always-“no” decision problem.

These are the paper’s nearest formal results, but they are not qualifying computational anchors under the stated rule: they prove properties of voting rules, rather than asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The algorithms discussed in Section 4 and the failure rates in Section 6 are not numbered theorems or propositions. So the paper has no named computational result on which I can base a compliant positive case. The plausible continuous mirrors above show that its subject matter fits a high-multiplicity society model, but their global feasibility questions inherit immediate impossibility results rather than opening a complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no named computational result to continue. Theorem 3.5 and Theorem 3.6 are rule-level impossibility claims; Section 4 describes procedures used to analyze elections, but gives no complexity classification or algorithmic guarantee. The article’s main results concern which failures a voting rule can exhibit and how often they appeared in a dataset. That makes it a poor anchor for ChoCo’s programme of classifying computational problems.

I would not dispute the fit of the population model. Ballot rankings are natural voter types, and the paper’s preference profiles already aggregate many voters with identical ballots. But that fit does not make the two proposed feasibility questions computational mirrors. Both completion arguments extend naturally to rational masses: split partial-ballot mass among the completions, and scale complete-ballot mass as needed. That gives continuous versions of the same rule properties. Checking a particular supported profile for the named failure, however, only requires calculating scores and pairwise comparisons, in \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations. This is a new per-profile decision question, not a complexity result the paper established.

The strongest improvement is to ask for the minimum fraction of voters who must truncate or compromise their ballots to change the winner. That connects more directly to the paper’s empirical procedures and its discussion of coordination. For those specified actions, each supported ballot type has only a small menu of altered ballots; score constraints are linear, so the mass-minimization question is a direct LP. This could be useful as a new robustness measure, but its value would come from adding an optimization objective to the paper, not from continuing either theorem. A more general bribery question would need a cost model the paper does not provide.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed theorem anchors, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A minimum-mass manipulation study is plausible, and the natural high-multiplicity regime supports it. The honest objection is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror—not that its subject cannot support a new continuous computational question.
