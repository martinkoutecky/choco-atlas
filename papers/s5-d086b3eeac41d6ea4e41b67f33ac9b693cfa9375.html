The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a rational row-stochastic confidence matrix \(Q\), true opinions \(\hat x_t\in[0,1]\), positive costs \(c_t\), a threshold \(x^*\), and a specified recurrent class \(K\), find the minimum per-capita intervention cost and an attaining intervention \((\delta_t)_{t\in K}\), subject to \(0\le\delta_t\le1-\hat x_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in K}\pi_t^{(K)}(\hat x_t+\delta_t)\ge x^*\), where \(\pi^{(K)}\) is the normalized stationary distribution of \(Q\) on \(K\).

The model it lives in

A continuum population consists of cohorts \(t\in T\) with mass \(\mu_t\); members of each cohort share a true opinion, cost, and confidence profile, and update according to \(Q\) on cohort averages. Raising cohort \(t\)'s opinion by \(\delta_t\) costs \(\mu_t c_t\delta_t\) per capita. The recurrent-class consensus is the stationary-weighted average, so the optimal intervention is the sort-and-fill rule ordered by \(\pi_t^{(K)}/(\mu_t c_t)\).

The objection that survived

The mirror requires agents to share confidence profiles as well as opinions and costs, and the paper does not establish how important such repeated-profile regimes are to its applications.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the minimum-cost threshold intervention for one recurrent class. It leaves the recurrent-class selection problem and the transient-state MILP formulation untreated.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest formal computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper’s Appendix A. It gives an exact optimum for the minimum-payment problem on a recurrent class. Order its agents so that \(\pi_1^{(k)}/c_1 \ge \cdots \ge \pi_{n_k}^{(k)}/c_{n_k}\), and let \(s\) be the first index for which raising agents \(1,\ldots,s\) to opinion \(1\), while leaving the rest at their true opinions, brings the stationary-weighted consensus to at least \(x^*\). The theorem says the optimum fully raises agents before \(s\), leaves agents after \(s\) unchanged, and partially raises agent \(s\): \(p_j=c_j(1-\hat x_j)\) for \(j<s\), \(p_j=0\) for \(j>s\), and \(p_s=\frac{c_s}{\pi_s^{(k)}}\left(x^*-\sum_{j<s}\pi_j^{(k)}-\sum_{j=s}^{n_k}\pi_j^{(k)}\hat x_j\right)\). It is an exact sort-and-fill algorithm, although the paper does not label it with a complexity class.

My lead mirror is Continuous Consensus-Tipping Campaign. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\); a rational row-stochastic confidence matrix \(Q\) over those types; rational true opinions \(\hat x_t\in[0,1]\); positive per-unit opinion-change costs \(c_t\); a threshold \(x^*\); and a target closed recurrent class \(K\) of \(Q\). Each type is a cohort of interchangeable agents: its members share the same true opinion, cost, and confidence profile, and their updates depend on the mean opinions of the cohorts according to \(Q\). Thus \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population in cohort \(t\), and the regime is many agents but relatively few such types—for example, a large organization with a finite set of campaign-relevant roles and interaction patterns.

The campaign chooses an opinion increase \(\delta_t\in[0,1-\hat x_t]\) for each type in \(K\). More generally, it may distribute different increases among members of a type; because the dynamics depend on the type mean and the cost is linear, only their average increase \(\delta_t\) affects this problem. The initial cohort mean is \(\hat x_t+\delta_t\), and the intervention costs \(\sum_{t\in K}\mu_t c_t\delta_t\) per member of the whole population. The question is to find the least such cost that makes the asymptotic consensus in \(K\) at least \(x^*\); a solution is the vector \(\delta\) and its minimum cost. If \(\pi^{(K)}\) is the normalized stationary distribution of \(Q\) on \(K\), the constraint is \(\sum_{t\in K}\pi_t^{(K)}(\hat x_t+\delta_t)\ge x^*\).

This preserves the core of Theorem 1: one-time paid shifts, linear costs, stationary influence, and a threshold on the eventual consensus. The continuous version sorts types by \(\pi_t^{(K)}/(\mu_t c_t)\), then fills the highest-ratio types to their opinion cap, uses at most one partially raised type, and leaves the rest unchanged. For singleton types of mass \(1/n\), the ordering agrees with the paper’s \(\pi_i^{(k)}/c_i\); the total-cost scale is simply per-capita. With rational input, the stationary vector and the sort-and-fill solution can be computed in polynomial time in the type count and input bit length. I expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable.

The paper also describes the recurrent-only whole-population problem as a knapsack over recurrent classes, and says the transient-state MILP is NP-hard. Those claims appear in prose, not as numbered complexity theorems in the supplied text, so I would not present either as a formal hardness anchor. Theorem 1 is the one named result I would anchor on; it is proved here and gives the exact optimization rule.

The mirror covers the recurrent-class tipping problem, not the paper’s entire transient-state model. Natural next questions include selecting among several recurrent classes under a per-capita budget, and extending the intervention problem to transient cohorts using their hitting probabilities. The weakest point is the cohort-mixing assumption: it gives the continuous population a small, stable set of confidence types, while the paper’s finite graph permits highly individualized neighborhoods. That restricts the mirror to genuine repeated-role regimes. It also means the gain here is a population-scale formulation and an efficient algorithm for this subproblem, not a newly exposed hardness boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 1’s mirror is that “same role” is too weak a notion of type for this paper. Agents must also share their confidence profile: who they listen to, and with what weights. Those profiles determine stationary influence, so grouping people who merely have the same opinion and price can erase the very structure that governs the result. In a generic directed network, those full profiles may be unique, leaving no high-multiplicity population. A type-level matrix \(Q\) instead assumes cohort-homogeneous mixing, which is a substantial restriction on the paper’s network model.

That objection narrows the mirror but does not defeat its better version. A large organization could contain repeated cohorts with the same opinions, costs, and confidence profiles. For a cohort of mass \(\mu_t\), shifting its mean opinion by \(\delta_t\) costs \(\mu_t c_t\delta_t\) per capita, while consensus depends on the stationary weights \(\pi_t\). The resulting problem preserves Theorem 1’s central structure and sorts types by \(\pi_t/(\mu_t c_t)\). It is also the exact limit of networks formed by cloning those cohort members.

So I cannot make a convincing universal case against this anchor. Its weakness is that the mirror covers a restricted, block-homogeneous network regime; the paper does not establish that this regime is important to its authors’ intended applications. But that is a question of scope and author recognition, not a fundamental failure of continuization. Theorem 1 survives the strongest objection I can support.
