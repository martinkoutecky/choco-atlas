The continuous mirror question

Given supported applicant types \(\mathcal M\) and position types \(\mathcal W\), with rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) each summing to \(1\), and weak rankings of mutually acceptable opposite-side types, choose matching masses \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\nu_b\). Define unmatched masses \(u_a=\mu_a-\sum_b x_{ab}\) and \(v_b=\nu_b-\sum_a x_{ab}\); for each acceptable pair \((a,b)\), require \(u_a+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}=0\) or \(v_b+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}=0\). Compute the maximum \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), and classify its complexity in the treewidth \(t\) of the type graph, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided market with finitely many supported applicant and position types, rational mass for each type, and weak rankings over mutually acceptable types on the other side. Variables \(x_{ab}\) give matched mass between types; row and column sums respect type masses, and stability requires that for each acceptable pair at least one side has zero mass that would strictly prefer the other type. Maximize total matched mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror uses Theorem 1's max-SMT hardness component. It leaves the other problems in Theorem 1, Theorem 2's XP algorithms, and the rotation-digraph results in Theorems 3 and 4 unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of continuous max-SMT in type-graph treewidth \(t\), support size \(\tau\), and bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a Continuous Max-SMT problem for a large two-sided matching market. It keeps the paper’s max-SMT question—find a weakly stable matching that matches as many agents as possible—and changes the population representation.

A type is a complete description of an applicant or a position, including its preferences over the other side’s types. Let \(\mathcal M\) and \(\mathcal W\) be the supported types, with rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), each side’s masses summing to \(1\). Preferences are weak rankings of acceptable opposite-side types; mutual acceptability is required. A variable \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) is the mass of type \(a\) matched to type \(b\), with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\nu_b\). The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\). The solution is the optimal value and a matching flow attaining it.

Stability has a direct population meaning. Let \(u_a=\mu_a-\sum_bx_{ab}\) and \(v_b=\nu_b-\sum_ax_{ab}\) be unmatched masses. For an acceptable pair \((a,b)\), the masses of each type that would strictly prefer the other type to their current assignment are

\[ B^M_{ab}=u_a+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}, \qquad B^W_{ab}=v_b+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}. \]

Require \(B^M_{ab}=0\) or \(B^W_{ab}=0\) for every acceptable pair. If both were positive, equal positive submasses could leave their assignments and match each other. Ties do not count as strict improvement. Although the flow can split a type’s mass, it can be realized by partitioning that population into subgroups, each of whose members receives one partner or remains unmatched.

The scenario is a large annual hiring or residency market: many applicants and positions, but relatively few complete types defined by skills, location, job conditions, and preferences over counterpart categories. The population might number in the millions while the support has dozens or hundreds of types. The model asks only about population continuity; it does not continuize outcomes, time, or axioms.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that SESM, BSM, max-SMT, and min-SMT are all W[1]-hard parameterized by the treewidth \(tw\) of the primal graph; moreover, unless ETH fails, none can be solved in time \(f(tw)n^{o(tw)}\) for any function \(f\) of \(tw\). I use its max-SMT component: maximizing the cardinality of a weakly stable matching with ties is W[1]-hard in that parameter, with the stated ETH-based running-time lower bound. The paper proves Theorem 1 by reductions from Multicolored Clique; that source problem’s hardness is cited from earlier work.

For Continuous Max-SMT, the corresponding parameter would be the treewidth \(t\) of a type primal graph: one vertex per supported type, with an edge for each mutually acceptable pair. The precise question for a prover is: given the rational type masses and weak preference rankings, compute the maximum mass of a weakly stable matching flow, with running time stated in \(t\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\). The analogue might be FPT in \(t\), polynomial in \((\tau,L)\), or hard. My expectation is Class C: the stability requirement still has a disjunction for every acceptable type pair, and those interactions can retain combinatorial structure even when mass is divisible. The theorem itself does not establish this; adapting its reduction to the flow model is an open part of the mirror.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem at population scale: it preserves stable marriage, weak stability, and the maximum-matching objective. The high-multiplicity regime also makes type-level indifference natural: positions or applicants within a type are indistinguishable to the other side.

The main weakness is that Theorem 1’s primal graph has individual agents as vertices, while this mirror’s graph has types as vertices. Replicating a type can change treewidth, and the paper’s reduction may use many distinct preference types. Also, divisible mass can permit stable flows that no discrete matching realizes. So the mirror is credible, but its complexity result cannot be read off from Theorem 1.

This case covers only max-SMT’s component of Theorem 1; it makes no claim about the other three problems or the paper’s rotation-digraph results. It also raises a useful dictionary question: for rational masses that are integer multiples of \(1/N\), does the continuous optimum equal the largest discrete max-SMT matching after scaling by \(N\), or can fractional allocation strictly improve it? That gap is central to determining whether hardness transfers or the continuum changes the problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 1 proves hardness for a different parameterized instance class. Its primal graph has a vertex for every named agent; the proposed mirror replaces those vertices by supported types and asks about the treewidth \(t\) of the type graph, alongside support size \(\tau\). The reduction from Multicolored Clique establishes no hardness result for societies with small \(\tau\), or for that quotient graph. So the theorem does not yet anchor the proposed type level complexity question.

There is also a real modeling change: applicants of one type and positions of another must rank categories, tying together all individuals within a category. That is plausible for large hiring or residency markets, and the proposed blocking masses give a coherent population version of weak stability. The changes don’t defeat the mirror; they mean its value has to come from the new type based question, not from transferring Theorem 1.

With rational masses, any rational flow can be scaled to integer counts using a common denominator for the masses and flow. It then corresponds to a matching among cloned agents with preferences tied by category. That makes the mirror a high multiplicity restriction of max-SMT, rather than a phenomenon unique to a continuum. Under ChoCo’s stated criteria, though, that is evidence the regime is meaningful, not an objection to studying it.

There is close computational prior art on fractional stable matchings, including work on ordinal stability with ties and maximizing the number of fully matched agents. But that work allows fractional assignments among finite named agents; it does not answer the type mass population problem, so it is not a collision. [Chen, Roy, and Sorge, “Fractional Matchings under Preferences: Stability and Optimality”](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/13).

My honest negative case is therefore weak. Theorem 1 alone gives little support for a low support parameterized result, and the mirror may prove to be a clone restricted reformulation. But neither point shows that the question is not worth asking. The proponent’s mirror survives the available objections; its actual complexity remains open.
