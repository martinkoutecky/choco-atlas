Lemma 15

Every anchor argued

  • Lemma 15 — survived: The opponent argues that ordinary individual nonatomic play loses price influence, but the proposed shared-authority cohort model preserves the paper’s two strategic buyers in a plausible purchasing-cooperative setting.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) divisible goods of unit supply and two cohort types \(r\in\{1,2\}\), with rational masses \(\lambda_r>0\) summing to \(1\), common linear utilities \(u_r\), and per-person budgets \(b_r\), each cohort’s shared authority submits one nonzero utility report. Its aggregate budget is \(M_r=\lambda_r b_r\), and its payoff is its total true utility under the paper’s Fisher-market rules and equilibrium-allocation payoff selection. Describe all pure Nash equilibrium report pairs, with their prices and induced allocations.

The model it lives in

A Fisher market with two strategic cohort agents, each representing mass \(\lambda_r\) of members with the same utility vector and per-person budget. Each cohort submits one common report, has aggregate budget \(M_r=\lambda_r b_r\), and receives an aggregate allocation of the divisible goods; market clearing, deviations, and payoffs follow the paper’s rules.

The objection that survived

The mirror requires every cohort to submit one joint report through a shared authority; ordinary member-by-member nonatomic Nash behavior would remove individual price influence. The proponent acknowledges this limitation, so it bounds the mirror to coordinated purchasing cohorts rather than defeating that stated scenario.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 15’s complete equilibrium characterization for two strategic cohorts; it does not extend the characterization to three or more independently reporting cohorts or cover the paper’s other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does an exact polyhedral characterization extend to \(\tau\ge3\) strategic cohorts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: a continuous high-multiplicity version of the paper’s two-buyer equilibrium characterization. The paper gives no named P, NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result. Its useful computational anchor is Lemma 15, proved here: for a two-buyer market, after ordering goods so that \(u_{1j}/u_{2j}\ge u_{1,j+1}/u_{2,j+1}\), the Nash equilibrium strategy profiles are exactly
\[ \mathcal S_{NE}=\{(\alpha,\alpha):\alpha\in\mathcal B\},\qquad \mathcal B=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal B_k\ \cup\ \bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathcal B'_k. \]
Here, writing \(M_1,M_2\) for the buyers’ budgets,
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal B_k=\{\alpha\ge0:\;& \sum_{i<k}\alpha_i<M_1,\quad \sum_{i>k}\alpha_i<M_2,\quad \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i=M_1+M_2,\\ &u_{1j}\alpha_i-u_{1i}\alpha_j\le0,\quad u_{2i}\alpha_j-u_{2j}\alpha_i\le0 \quad\text{for all }i\le k,\ j\ge k\}, \end{aligned} \]
and
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal B'_k=\{\alpha\ge0:\;& \sum_{i\le k}\alpha_i=M_1,\quad \sum_{i>k}\alpha_i=M_2,\\ &u_{1j}\alpha_i-u_{1i}\alpha_j\le0,\quad u_{2i}\alpha_j-u_{2j}\alpha_i\le0 \quad\text{for all }i\le k,\ j\ge k+1\}. \end{aligned} \]
The \(\alpha_i\) can be read as equilibrium prices. Thus the lemma describes *all* two-buyer equilibria as a union of \(2n-1\) explicitly specified sets, rather than merely giving necessary conditions.

Here is the continuous problem I think that result can honestly anchor. Take \(n\) divisible goods, two buyer types \(r\in\{1,2\}\), and rational masses \(\lambda_r>0\) summing to one. Members of type \(r\) share a complete template \((u_r,b_r)\): the same linear utility vector and per-person budget. A type-level purchasing authority submits one common nonzero utility report for its whole cohort. The cohort’s aggregate budget is \(M_r=\lambda_r b_r\); its aggregate allocation is \(x_{rj}\), with \(\sum_r x_{rj}=1\) for each good. Its total true utility is \(\sum_j u_{rj}x_{rj}\), equivalently its members’ common per-person utility multiplied by \(\lambda_r\). Market prices and allocations follow the paper’s Fisher-market rules, including its equilibrium-allocation payoff selection. Two-Cohort Fisher-Game Equilibrium Enumeration asks: given the goods, rational type masses, budgets, and utilities, describe *all* pure Nash equilibrium report pairs, with their prices and induced allocations. A solution is an exact description of the equilibrium set, not just one equilibrium. Normalize total budget to one and the paper’s \(\mathcal B_k,\mathcal B'_k\) description applies with the cohort aggregate budgets \(M_r\).

The regime is many buyers in only two templates: for example, large purchasing cooperatives whose members have the same preferences and budgets and whose common authority submits the report. If the population has \(N\) members, \(N\) can grow while the support stays at \(\tau=2\). Aggregating identical members gives exactly the two-buyer Fisher market with budgets \(M_1,M_2\); the utility reports, price manipulation, market clearing, and Nash deviations remain central to the question. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their two-buyer game at cohort scale.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: Lemma 15 supplies a polynomial-size exact description—\(2n-1\) sets with \(O(n^2)\) linear constraints apiece. That supports a polynomial-time construction in \(n\) and the rational input bit length, although the paper does not state a complexity bound. This case covers Lemma 15’s two-buyer characterization only; it does not extend the result to arbitrary numbers of strategic buyers. Natural next questions are whether the polyhedral description extends to \(\tau\ge3\) strategic cohorts, and what happens when cohorts may only partially coordinate.

The weak point is also the central modeling choice: a type acts as a coordinated bloc. That is plausible for a purchasing consortium, but it does not follow from high multiplicity alone. Under ordinary individual Nash behavior in an atomless population, one person has zero influence on market prices; the price-manipulation mechanism in this paper can disappear. So I regard the cohort model as a credible, limited continuous mirror of the two-buyer result, not as the standard nonatomic Nash version of the paper’s game.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 15 is the paper’s strongest possible anchor, though it is a structural characterization of equilibria rather than a complexity theorem. It characterizes the equilibrium set for two strategic buyers as a union of polyhedral sets. The proposed two-cohort mirror keeps exactly those two strategic players: each cohort’s authority submits one report, and its mass changes its aggregate budget to \(M_r=\lambda_r b_r\).

That makes the model coherent as a game between two purchasing organizations, but it leaves no high-multiplicity population in the strategic problem. Once budgets are aggregated, the number of people in each cohort disappears; the input is still the same two-buyer Fisher game, with different budget values. Indistinguishability within a type justifies grouping data. It does not make the members one strategic actor. A shared authority that forces everyone in a cohort to report together adds a coordination assumption, so the result describes a game between blocs rather than the paper’s buyers as individuals.

Keeping individual buyers instead gives a different limit. As the population grows, each person’s budget share shrinks, and a unilateral deviation has vanishing influence on prices; in an atomless model, it has none. A person can still choose a report that affects their own demand, but their best achievable bundle at fixed prices is one maximizing their true utility subject to their budget. The resulting market outcomes are therefore Fisher equilibria for the true utilities, whose computation is established in the paper’s cited market-equilibrium literature. The price manipulation that makes the paper’s strategic characterization distinctive has disappeared. Allowing a whole positive-mass cohort to deviate together restores price influence, but that is the bloc model again.

So the negative case is strongest against a faithful continuization: individual strategic behavior loses the price-manipulation mechanism; preserving it requires treating each cohort as a single player, leaving population multiplicity outside the game. I cannot honestly make the universal claim airtight. A large purchasing consortium or oligopolistic firm might be a sensible bloc, and the paper itself discusses oligopolies. That leaves a plausible, narrow two-cohort interpretation—but it is a continuous description of two strategic organizations, not a continuization of the population’s strategic behavior.
