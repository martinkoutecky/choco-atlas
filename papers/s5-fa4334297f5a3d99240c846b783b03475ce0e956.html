The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational mass vector \(\mu\), per-capita supply \(s\), capacities \(\lambda_t\), and marginal values \(u_t(1)\ge\cdots\ge u_t(\lambda_t)\), run the alternative ascending auction for each admissible \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) bidders of type \(t\) and \(Ns\) indivisible units; compute the limit of normalized allocations and clinching payments, and determine whether it equals the mass welfare optimum and the normalized multi-unit Vickrey outcome.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity Ausubel auction with bidder types \(t\), population mass \(\mu_t\), per-capita supply \(s\), integral bundles \(q\in\{0,\ldots,\lambda_t\}\), mass variables \(z_{t,q}\), welfare objective \(\sum_{t,q}z_{t,q}U_t(q)\), and normalized clinching or Vickrey payments defined through the admissible finite-\(N\) limit.

The objection that survived

In an atomless population, individual bidders cannot create positive-mass clinches, and normalized clinching payments or equilibrium selection may depend on integer scaling and tie conventions that the distribution \(\mu\) alone does not encode.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the private-values implementation and Vickrey-equivalence results of THEOREM 1, while leaving the iterated-dominance uniqueness result and the interdependent-values results of THEOREMS 2–4 unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which rational inputs \(\mu\) and \(s\) do normalized allocations and payments converge uniquely under the specified tie-breaking rule?
  • Can the limiting allocation and payment vector be computed from weighted marginal-value breakpoints in polynomial time?
  • Does sincere bidding remain an equilibrium in the nonatomic limit, and how large is the resulting equilibrium set?
  • What finite-\(N\) convergence rate relates the continuous outcome to the discrete Ausubel auction?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s private-values results, led by Theorem 1. This paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result, so I cannot offer a hardness anchor. Its named anchors are equilibrium and implementation theorems; the computational question is whether their aggregate, high-multiplicity outcomes can be computed and whether the theorems survive the population limit.

The natural regime is a Treasury-bill auction with many small investment funds. There are \(N\) bidders but only \(\tau\) portfolio-demand types, with \(\tau\) fixed or at least \(\tau\ll N\). A type completely specifies a bidder’s capacity and marginal-value schedule: \(t=(\lambda_t,u_t)\), where \(u_t(1)\ge\cdots\ge u_t(\lambda_t)\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), so \(N\mu_t\) bidders have type \(t\). The supply must scale with the population: the auction offers \(Ns\) homogeneous objects, with \(s\) the supply per unit population. This is the economically meaningful large-market regime, rather than sending the number of bidders to infinity while keeping supply fixed.

The goods remain indivisible. What becomes fractional is only the mass of identical bidders receiving each integral bundle. Let \(z_{t,q}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) bidders receiving exactly \(q\) objects. Thus

\[ \sum_{q=0}^{\lambda_t} z_{t,q}=\mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{q=0}^{\lambda_t}qz_{t,q}=s. \]

The welfare objective is

\[ \max \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{q=0}^{\lambda_t} z_{t,q}U_t(q), \qquad U_t(q)=\sum_{r=1}^{q}u_t(r). \]

The action variable is a monotone demand schedule \(x_t(p)\) for each type: every member of type \(t\) submits the same demand at price \(p\). Under sincere bidding,

\[ x_t(p)\in\arg\max_{0\le q\le\lambda_t}\{U_t(q)-pq\}. \]

The continuous problem should not simply replace the paper’s individual clinching formula by an informal “type bidder.” A precise definition is to take, for every admissible \(N\), the finite auction with \(N\mu_t\) bidders of each type and \(Ns\) objects, run the paper’s alternative ascending auction, normalize allocations and payments by \(N\), and ask for the limit as \(N\to\infty\). A fixed proportional tie-breaking rule handles equal marginal values.

I would call this problem High-Multiplicity Ausubel Outcome. Given \(T\), \(\mu\), \(s\), the type valuation schedules, the price range, and the information rule, compute the limiting type demand schedules, the mass allocation \(z\), and the normalized clinching payments. The required certificate is that the output is the welfare-maximizing allocation and agrees with the normalized multi-unit Vickrey outcome.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, in the alternative ascending-bid auction with private values, sincere bidding by all bidders is an ex post perfect equilibrium and yields the efficient outcome of the Vickrey auction. With no bid information, sincere bidding is weakly dominant for every bidder after every history. With full or aggregate bid information, sincere bidding need not be dominant. The theorem is therefore not merely about welfare maximization: it links the dynamic demand-and-clinching mechanism, equilibrium behavior, and the Vickrey benchmark.

The high-multiplicity question is whether the same equivalence holds after replacing the \(N\) named bidders by the distribution \(\mu\): does the limiting sincere type-demand process produce the limiting Vickrey allocation and payments? This is recognisably the authors’ problem. It preserves homogeneous goods, demand curves, clinching, quasilinear values, the Vickrey comparison, and the efficiency claim. It does not replace the auction by a generic divisible-resource allocation problem.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. With finite \(T\), bounded capacities, and rational data, the welfare problem has only \(\sum_t(\lambda_t+1)\) mass variables. Its solution is a weighted marginal-value ordering, or equivalently a small linear program. Aggregate demand has only finitely many breakpoints, and the limiting clearing price and type-level payments can be computed from weighted quantiles of those breakpoints. The interesting research work is proving that this aggregate computation exactly represents the normalized ascending auction, including ties and payments, not discovering a hidden combinatorial obstruction.

A secondary anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in this paper. Under private values, incomplete information, and the full-support assumption—that every weakly decreasing marginal-value function in the relevant finite value domain has positive probability—sincere bidding by all bidders is the unique outcome of iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies in the alternative ascending auction.

This suggests Mass-IEWD Ausubel. Its input is the same \((T,\mu,s)\), together with a finite support of possible valuation types and a positive mass for every type in that support. The solution must output the unique surviving type-symmetric demand policy under iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies, together with its limiting allocation and payments. The expected direction is again Class A, but with a sharper question: does the uniqueness result commute with the high-multiplicity limit? The theorem’s backward induction over price rounds suggests that it should, provided the finite type support and tie-breaking rule are encoded explicitly.

I would not stretch this case to Theorems 3 and 4. Their interdependent-values results depend on private signals, order statistics, affiliated beliefs, and the paper’s “turning back the clock” rule. A continuum of bidders makes rivals’ empirical signal distribution effectively deterministic, which can erase the winner’s-curse phenomenon unless one adds a latent random population state. That could be a worthwhile second programme, but it is a materially less secure mirror of this paper.

The weakest point is the clinching limit. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass and cannot be pivotal. The literal expression

\[ C_i(p)=\max\left\{0,M-\sum_{j\ne i}x_j(p)\right\} \]

therefore degenerates if transferred directly to a continuum. The proposed finite-replica definition is meant to solve exactly that problem, but it leaves open whether normalized clinching payments and equilibrium reasoning converge cleanly. If they do not, the continuous object may retain the Vickrey allocation while losing the strategic content of the Ausubel mechanism.

That weakness does not destroy the positive case. It identifies the right research question: whether the paper’s central dynamic-equilibrium/Vickrey equivalence survives high multiplicity. The mirror is concrete, economically plausible, population-continuous rather than merely outcome-continuous, and likely tractable. It also generates nontrivial follow-ups about convergence rates, tie-handling, finite-\(N\) approximation, and whether zero individual pivotality creates genuinely continuum-specific equilibria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror cannot preserve the paper’s central object—individual strategic pivotality—while making the population genuinely continuous.

First, this is not a computational paper. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are equilibrium and implementation results; they state no complexity bound, algorithm, or computational optimization problem. The proposed LP is therefore not the high-multiplicity version of a computational result in the paper. It is a new welfare-clearing problem, justified by an equilibrium theorem that does not survive the limit in its original form.

For Theorem 1, clinching is fundamentally a marginal-bidder notion. In the finite auction,

\[ C_i(p)=\max\left\{0,M-\sum_{j\ne i}x_j(p)\right\}. \]

After normalization, removing one bidder changes aggregate demand by \(O(1/N)\), which disappears in a nonatomic population. An individual cannot create a positive-mass clinch. If one defines the limit directly, the dynamic mechanism collapses into supply-demand clearing followed by rationing. The weighted marginal-value LP computes an efficient allocation, but it no longer computes the paper’s dynamic equilibrium/Vickrey equivalence.

The proposed finite-replica repair does not produce an intrinsic continuous mechanism. Vickrey payments depend precisely on the \(1/N\)-scale externality that the population measure forgets. For example, take unit-demand bidders with values \(10\) and \(0\), limiting high-value mass \(1/2\), and per-capita supply \(1/2\). With \(N=2k\), there are \(k\) high bidders and \(k\) units; every winning high bidder pays \(0\). With \(N=2k+1\), there are \(k+1\) high bidders and \(k\) units; every winner pays \(10\). Both sequences converge to the same continuous society, but normalized total payments converge respectively to \(0\) and \(5\). Proportional tie-breaking does not resolve this externality problem; one must specify a rounding and finite-size convention that is additional data absent from \(\mu\).

Theorem 2 fails even more directly. Its proof relies on the full-support assumption making every bidder’s report matter with positive probability. In the continuum, aggregate demand is deterministic and a unilateral bidder has measure zero. Her report cannot change the clearing price or rivals’ aggregate behaviour, so iterated weak dominance cannot uniquely select sincere bidding. If one instead lets a positive-mass type deviate jointly, that is a coalition or a type-level strategic player, not the individual game studied in Theorem 2. If one retains finite \(N\) to preserve pivotality, one has retained the discrete auction rather than obtained a continuous one.

The allocation-only version is mathematically sensible, but it is merely weighted marginal-value sorting with a small LP. The mechanism, equilibrium selection, and Vickrey externalities—the paper’s actual contribution—have been removed. The strategic version is not intrinsic to the continuous society; the aggregate version is generic and already computationally trivial.

The negative case is not a proof that no asymptotic auction study could ever be useful. A finite-\(N\) convergence or finite-size robustness analysis could be worthwhile. But that is an asymptotic study of the discrete Ausubel auction, not a continuous-population computational mirror. On the anchors actually offered, the continuous model either loses the theorem or ceases to be a genuinely new computational problem.
