The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a high multiplicity version of the paper’s NSW-optimal stable matching problem. The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: for an input with \(n\) men and \(n\) women and strict preference lists, SNSW-Alg runs in \(O(n^4\log n)\) time. The preceding Theorem 2, also proved here, identifies what it computes: a stable matching maximizing Nash social welfare under the paper’s rank utilities \(n-r\). Together, these results establish a polynomial-time algorithm for choosing the fairest NSW outcome from the stable matchings.

A plausible regime is a large centralized assignment of candidates to individual one-person positions: for example, a recurring placement market with hundreds of thousands of candidates and slots, but far fewer distinct preference classes. A candidate’s type could specify their relevant qualifications and ranking of position classes; a slot’s type could specify its role and ranking of candidate classes. Agents in a type are interchangeable for the market’s purposes. The population on each side has total mass one, distributed over its types. This scenario makes multiplicity meaningful: the input is a distribution over a modest number of classes, rather than a list of uniquely described people.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Type-NSW Stable Mass Matching. An instance consists of finite supported type sets \(T_A,T_B\), rational masses \(\mu_t,\nu_u\) summing to one on each side, and a strict ranking of \(T_B\) for every \(t\in T_A\) and of \(T_A\) for every \(u\in T_B\). Write \(\tau_A=|T_A|\) and \(\tau_B=|T_B|\). Give a type its paper-style rank utility: \(U_t(u)=\tau_B-\operatorname{rank}_t(u)\) and \(V_u(t)=\tau_A-\operatorname{rank}_u(t)\), with rank zero for the most preferred type.

A solution is a nonnegative mass matrix \(x=(x_{tu})\) whose row sums are \(\mu_t\) and column sums are \(\nu_u\). It represents the mass of each type paired with each opposite-side type; each person still receives one partner, while a cohort may split across partner types. Define \(A_{t,u}(x)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}x_{tv}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned below \(u\), and \(B_{u,t}(x)=\sum_{s:\,t\succ_u s}x_{su}\). Require \(A_{t,u}(x)=0\) or \(B_{u,t}(x)=0\) for every pair \((t,u)\). This says no positive mass on both sides can leave their current, worse-ranked partners to match one another. Among such stable couplings, maximize

\[ \exp\!\left(\frac12\sum_{t,u}x_{tu}\bigl(\log U_t(u)+\log V_u(t)\bigr)\right). \]

The expression is the population-weighted geometric mean of agents’ utilities. In the special case where each agent is its own type, \(\tau_A=\tau_B=n\), and each mass is \(1/n\), the type rankings and utilities recover the paper’s rank-based objective. The requested output is an optimal stable coupling \(x\), represented exactly.

I would expect tractability to be the first hypothesis to test. The paper’s key step is to turn the product objective into an additive log objective and optimize over the structure of stable matchings using rotations and a maximum-weight closed subset. The mass objective remains additive after taking logs, so a mass-valued version of that structural approach could plausibly lead to a weighted closure or flow formulation. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 3: the theorem’s rotation-poset argument concerns finite matchings, and the mass-stability condition above is disjunctive. The core questions are whether stable mass couplings always exist, whether they have a compact rotation-like representation, and whether the optimum and its bit complexity can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau_A,\tau_B\), and the input bit length \(L\). One should also ask how this optimum relates to the optima of large finite replications.

This mirror covers the paper’s main theoretical result: optimizing NSW subject to stability. It does not mirror the paper’s empirical comparisons, and I would not use the cited NP-hardness of sex-equal stable marriage as an anchor: that is a different objective and is not a numbered result proved in this paper.

The weakest point is the type-level co-modification. The paper assumes strict rankings of individual agents; cohorts with shared rankings over types make members of the same opposite-side type interchangeable. That changes the preference model and its stability notion. It is defensible in the stated market regime, where classes capture all distinctions the mechanism uses, but it means the authors might regard this as a close extension of their problem rather than its literal continuous limit. The case survives because the decision, objective, and stability incentive are preserved, and the model makes the population itself the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are genuine computational anchors: the paper proves that NSW maximization over stable matchings has a polynomial-time algorithm. The strongest objection is to whether the proposed mass problem is a continuous version of that result.

In the paper, an agent’s utility depends on their rank among named individuals on the other side. A mass \(x_{tu}\) between two classes does not say which individuals are paired, and so it does not determine those rank utilities. Keeping the exact preferences would require tracking the positions of individuals within each class; that can bring the population-sized input back. The proposed model avoids this by ranking classes and assigning each class a single utility. But \(U_t(u)=\tau_B-\operatorname{rank}_t(u)\) ranks the class list, rather than taking a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s \(n-r\). It can also change when one class is split into two indistinguishable subclasses.

A better formulation could use rank percentiles derived from counterpart masses, with an explicit convention for ties within a class. That avoids dependence on the number of class labels and is a plausible continuous assignment problem. It still changes the paper’s strict individual rankings, rank utilities, and rotation-based representation. A prover would be studying NSW optimization for stable mass matching under a newly specified preference and stability model. That could be worthwhile, but the paper’s theorem does not establish that it is the same computational question.

This objection weakens if the target market naturally has interchangeable classes—such as recurring cohorts matched to classes of positions—and treats preferences and utility at the class level. In that setting, a mass matching problem is sensible, and the authors might recognize it as a meaningful extension. I can reject the proponent’s stated formula as a faithful mirror of the theorem; I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case is therefore weak at its universal claim.
