Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium proposals under sophisticated voting; Theorem 2 gives the probability and conditions for implementing the Condorcet winner. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem. Computing the proposal thresholds from an encoded \(F\) is another possible task. Each requires choosing an input encoding and computational output for a model the paper leaves analytically specified. Those choices could support a new complexity study, but they do not supply a computational result from this paper to mirror.

A finite high-multiplicity version of VRP could also be worth studying. The paper itself explains why it changes the problem: a finite electorate makes the median uncertain and allows proposals and votes to convey information, changing the equilibrium analysis. That could form a separate finite-to-continuous research project; it does not rescue either proposed anchor. High-multiplicity work would support that regime’s plausibility, rather than count against it.

The negative case has a clear limit: large-population settings such as direct democracy are plausible, and computational questions about VRP may be worthwhile.
