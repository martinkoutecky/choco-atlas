Why no mirror

Proposition 6 proves a stochastic-stability claim for a family of weighted majority games; it does not assert that a computational problem is solved or hard. The proposed two-type limit is a statable evolutionary asymptotic question, but it cannot supply the missing computational result required for a mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed limit concerns only Proposition 6's regime, where small players can win on their own; it leaves the basic-process results, the three-player analysis, and Proposition 7's complementary regime unmodeled.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large two-class membership organization with transferable surplus and weighted voting: for example, many members with full voting weight and many with associate weight. Within each class, members have identical voting and bargaining rights. If class counts grow with a fixed proportion, the population is large while the number of types stays at two. That is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the problem in this paper.

The best result to build on is Proposition 6, proved in this paper. It says that in a constant-sum homogeneous weighted majority game represented by \([q;a,\ldots,a,1,\ldots,1]\), if the \(n-p\) small players can form a winning coalition on their own—that is, \(n-p\ge q\)—then \(z=(a/q,\ldots,a/q,1/q,\ldots,1/q)\) is the process’s unique stochastically stable state. The process here is the paper’s demand-adjustment process with its upward-biased mutations.

Here is a precise limit question for that result. Take rational parameters \(a\ge2\), \(0<\alpha<1\), and \(Q>0\), with \(1-\alpha\ge Q\). Consider admissible sequences of the paper’s constant-sum games with \(p_n/n\to\alpha\), \(q_n/n\to Q\), and \(n-p_n\ge q_n\). Choose demand grids that contain the stable vector and whose step \(\delta_n\) satisfies \(n\delta_n\to0\). For each finite game, first take the stationary distribution’s zero-mutation limit, as in the paper. Then scale each player’s demand \(x_i\) to \(n x_i\), and record the empirical distribution of those scaled demands separately for the two types.

Call the resulting problem Two-Type Continuum Stability Limit: given \((a,\alpha,Q)\) and the promise that such an admissible sequence exists, characterize all weak subsequential limits of those type-by-type demand distributions. A solution is the limiting law over pairs of within-type demand distributions. The answer predicted by Proposition 6 is the point mass on \((\delta_{a/Q},\delta_{1/Q})\), where each \(\delta_r\) is a unit mass at \(r\).

The corresponding continuum society has masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\), with voting weights \(a\) and \(1\). A coalition containing masses \(u\) and \(v\) of the two types wins when \(au+v\ge Q\). A type’s demand is a per-member claim, and a coalition can satisfy its members when their integrated claims do not exceed the coalition’s value, \(1\) if it wins and \(0\) otherwise. At the proposed limit, claims are \(a/Q\) and \(1/Q\), so a coalition with exactly quota weight makes total claims \(1\). This preserves the paper’s underlying question—what demands survive the greedy adjustment and upward mutations—while treating population composition as mass.

This mirror covers only Proposition 6’s two-type regime. It does not cover the paper’s general convergence results or its three-player analysis. It also turns Proposition 6’s selection result into a limit-computation question; this restricted family may have a simple closed-form answer, rather than a substantial complexity classification.

My weakest point is that the paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the requested kind: its propositions characterize convergence and stochastic stability, not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. So Proposition 6 is the strongest *candidate* anchor, but not a qualifying computational anchor under the stated rule.

There is also a modeling gap at the continuum level. One player’s demand update has zero effect on aggregate claims when that player has zero mass. The limit question above avoids defining a new mean-field adjustment process by taking the limit of the paper’s finite processes. That is a defensible high-multiplicity mirror, but an author or referee could reasonably ask for a standalone continuum dynamic with a principled notion of mutation and stability. The next questions would be whether the limit is robust to the order of the mutation and population limits, and whether the complementary regime in Proposition 7 has a similarly well-defined mass-level selection.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 6 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and its population regime is credible: a large organization with many members of each of two voting types is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. The paper’s own two-type analysis supports that judgment. I would not argue that grouping members by type distorts the model, or that prior high-multiplicity work is a collision.

The difficulty is that Proposition 6 is a stochastic-stability result, not a computational result. It does not ask for an algorithm or classify the complexity of a problem. The proposed “Two-Type Continuum Stability Limit” asks for weak limits of stable states along sequences of finite games. That may be a worthwhile asymptotic question, but it is an evolutionary-game-theory limit question, not the computational mirror ChoCo is trying to chart. The paper supplies no named computational result for that mirror to extend.

There is also a real gap between the finite process and a standalone continuum one. In the finite model, a mutation changes one player’s demand and may prompt other players to adjust. In a nonatomic society, changing one agent’s demand changes no aggregate mass or coalition claim. A state recorded only as a type-level demand distribution cannot see that mutation. Making a positive mass mutate at once would give a defined aggregate process, but it would change the perturbation and the stability question. Taking the zero-mutation limit in every finite game before taking population size to infinity, as the proponent does, avoids that problem; it yields a legitimate sequence limit, but not a continuum adjustment process.

The constant-sum assumption also becomes singular at the proposed limit. For the finite homogeneous games, the total weight is \(ap+(n-p)=2q-1\). Under the proposed scaling this tends to \(a\alpha+(1-\alpha)=2Q\). A divisible population with that total weight has coalitions of exactly half the total weight. At quota \(Q\), such a coalition and its complement are both winning under a weak threshold, and both losing under a strict threshold. Either choice breaks the finite games’ constant-sum property. Assigning half the worth at a tie could restore constant-sum, but changes the coalition values at precisely the binding coalitions relevant to the demand process. That is a possible new model, not a limit that preserves Proposition 6’s assumptions.

So the strongest negative case is that this anchor either remains a limit theorem about finite stochastic processes, outside the programme’s computational remit, or requires new definitions of mutation and coalition worth before it becomes a standalone continuum process. I would not claim this proves there is no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario: the two-type population is plausible, and the paper itself is evidence for that regime. The case against is strongest against treating Proposition 6 as a computational-complexity mirror; it is weaker against studying its asymptotic or evolutionary extensions on their own.
