The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result: it proves no problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified by computational complexity. Its central result, Theorem 1, says that if hospital choice functions are observably substitutable and the cumulative offer mechanism is strategy-proof, then its outcome is weakly Pareto efficient among individually rational allocations. Corollary 1 gives conditions—observable substitutability, observable size-monotonicity, and non-manipulability via contractual terms—that ensure this efficiency. Those are mechanism-property results, not computational-complexity anchors.

A continuous matching-with-contracts model could take mass over complete doctor types and ask whether a continuum cumulative offer mechanism preserves the strategy-proofness and welfare relationship. But that would mirror Theorem 1’s welfare claim, not a named computational result of the paper. Example 1’s finite counterexample to group strategy-proofness is likewise not a complexity result. Under the requested anchor standard, there is no qualifying result on which to base a continuous-mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest reason against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 relate strategy-proofness, choice conditions, and weak Pareto efficiency; Claim 3 supplies a finite counterexample to group strategy-proofness. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper provides no input-size model, algorithmic bound, or separation problem. A continuous question about these theorems may be worthwhile in matching theory, but it would be an axiomatic question, outside this programme’s computational scope.

The best version of the proposed Theorem 1 mirror is easy to imagine: model students or doctors as masses of preference types, scale institutional capacities with population, and define a continuum cumulative-offer mechanism. One could then ask whether an appropriate mass-coalition notion of strategy-proofness implies a suitable Pareto-efficiency property. I would grant that this could be a sensible economic model; high multiplicity is not the weak point. But it still asks about incentives and welfare, not computational complexity. Asking how to compute its outcome would be a separate project, requiring a representation of the hospital choice rules and an algorithmic problem the paper does not specify.

Claim 3’s counterexample does not supply a stronger anchor. It constructs a group manipulation among four named doctors under a specially designed hospital choice function. In a nonatomic population, individual deviations have zero mass, so a faithful strategic notion would need to focus on positive-mass coalitions. That is a reasonable way to repair the model, and the finite example could motivate it. But the resulting question would again be whether coalitions can manipulate, not the complexity of a computational problem established in the paper.

Nor does extending the cumulative-offer computation itself rescue the mirror. A continuous matching algorithm, or an algorithm for finding profitable mass deviations, could be a valuable new result. But it would need its own input representation, choice-function restrictions, and computational question. Those additions might make a good research project; they do not turn this paper’s welfare and incentive claims into computational anchors. So the negative case is strong on fit to ChoCo, though it cannot rule out useful computational work on continuum matching more broadly.
