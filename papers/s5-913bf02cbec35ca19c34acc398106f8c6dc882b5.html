The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is only a nearby possibility, not a genuine mirror I can defend. The paper’s main results in §§5.1–5.3 characterize which finite electorate sizes admit unbiased, Shapley–Shubik-fair, or Banzhaf-fair rules. But they do not state a named complexity result about computing such a rule or deciding a computational problem. The supplied text also omits theorem numbers, so I cannot quote the numbered computational anchor your brief requires. I would not relabel an existence characterization as an NP-hardness or algorithmic result.

A plausible setting is a large electorate with a small number of voter types, where each type has many interchangeable members and its mass is the fraction of the electorate it represents. One could ask whether a monotone, neutral, resolute rule can give every voter equal power. That is a sensible high-multiplicity question, but the paper’s theorems do not settle it: their rules may distinguish voters by name, while type-level indistinguishability would restrict the allowed rules.

The continuous version has a more basic gap. Shapley–Shubik fairness uses a uniformly random ordering of finitely many voters; Banzhaf fairness uses a uniformly random subset. For a population continuum, neither has a canonical counterpart, and an individual’s mass is zero. A rule over type-level vote masses therefore needs a new definition of influence before it yields a prover-ready problem. That would be an axiomatic extension with newly chosen fairness semantics, not a continuous version of a named computational result in this paper.

So my strongest honest conclusion is that the paper has no qualifying computational anchor for this task. Its finite existence classifications are interesting background for a future typed, high-multiplicity theory, but I cannot give a continuous problem per anchor without inventing the central notion that the paper studies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s three main theorems classify which finite electorate sizes admit unbiased, Shapley–Shubik-fair, or Banzhaf-fair rules. They do not analyze an algorithmic problem: there is no input describing a rule to optimize, no complexity claim, and no representation of rules whose size or running time is studied. The coalition-count propositions are identities used to prove those existence results, not computational results. That leaves no qualifying computational anchor for ChoCo.

The strongest nearby mirror would take a finite set of voter types, with mass \(\mu_t\) for each type, and ask whether a deterministic rule on type-level vote masses can be neutral, resolute, and fair. But this runs into a basic obstruction before fairness enters. Write \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) for the mass of type \(t\) voting for candidate \(1\), and let \(F(x)\) be the winner. Neutrality requires \(F(\mu-x)=1-F(x)\). At the feasible profile \(x=\mu/2\), the profile is its own complement, so neutrality requires \(F(x)=1-F(x)\), impossible for a resolute rule. The finite rules in the paper can get around the analogous tie by distinguishing among named voters; a type-mass rule has collapsed those distinct coalitions to the same point.

That also defeats the proposed readings of the Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf results. Their indices measure whether an individual voter can swing an outcome. In an atomless population, changing one individual changes no type mass, so that individual’s swing probability is zero for an aggregate rule. Rescaling a marginal effect or defining a new type-level power index might produce a research question, but it would choose new fairness semantics; it would not continue the paper’s indices. Preserving names as types avoids aggregation only by making the support grow with the electorate, while using a continuum of individual labels leaves each individual with zero mass.

There are plausible finite-bloc models, but they shift the object: treating each bloc as a player makes the question about power among blocs, while treating bloc members as voters returns to the zero-mass and balanced-profile problems. Randomized resolution could also escape the contradiction, but it changes the paper’s central requirement and still needs a new influence definition. Those may be worthwhile projects in their own right. They do not supply a computational continuous mirror of this paper. The negative case is strongest on that distinction; it cannot rule out a valuable axiomatic theory of continuous voting power.
