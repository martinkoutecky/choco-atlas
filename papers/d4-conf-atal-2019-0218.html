What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns only Theorem 3.1’s convergence-time bound; it leaves Theorems 3.3–3.4 and the simulation comparisons alone.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: this paper offers a mirror of algorithmic convergence in a large anonymous population, not a continuous version of all its game-theoretic claims. Its best anchor is the convergence-time theorem.

The natural regime is a large wireless network whose interchangeable devices share the same payoff, collision penalty, firmware, courtesy rule, and monitoring arrangement. Those shared features make them one complete type, so the society is \(\mu_{t_0}=1\) even as the number of devices grows. Take \(N=KR\), with fixed congestion \(K>1\) and \(R\) growing. Then devices greatly outnumber channels, while the per-capita channel supply stays fixed: each context has \(R/N=1/K\) channels per person, across \(K\) contexts. In normalized units, each device has mass \(1/N\); its actual allocation remains an indivisible channel, while the population and successful allocations are represented by fractions. Scaling the channel supply with the population is part of this mirror.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper using convergence analysis based on Cigler and Faltings [13]. It states that in a repeated allocation game with \(N\) agents and \(R\) resources, the expected number of steps before Algorithm 1 converges to a correlated equilibrium is bounded by
\[ O\!\left(N\left(\log\left\lceil\frac NR\right\rceil+1\right)(\log N+R)\right). \]
For the proposed regime \(N=KR\) with fixed \(K\), this gives an \(O_K(R^2)\) expected convergence bound. This is a named algorithmic result, though it is an expected-time bound for a learning process, not a formal claim that some decision problem lies in P.

I would pose its continuous counterpart as CA3NONY Mass-Limit Convergence. Its instance is the homogeneous society above, with \(K\), the common backoff probability \(p\), the collision payoff \(\zeta\), and the quota and monitoring rules from the paper. For each \(R\), instantiate the exact finite game with \(N=KR\) agents and \(R\) indivisible resources, initialize strategies as in Algorithm 1, and let \(\tau_R\) be the random number of steps until the process reaches an efficient correlated equilibrium. The question is to determine the high-multiplicity asymptotic of \(\mathbb E[\tau_R]\): in particular, whether
\[ M(K,p)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E[\tau_R]}{R^2} \]
exists, and, if so, to compute it; otherwise, characterize the corresponding limsup and its scaling. A solution would give the limit or limsup with a proof, or an algorithm that approximates it to any requested precision. This states the question about the underlying convergence process; it does not treat the encoding or state representation as the problem.

I expect this to be tractable for the homogeneous, fixed-\(K\) mirror. The finite theorem already gives a polynomial expected-time upper bound in this regime, and the paper’s Markov-chain analysis suggests there may be useful aggregate states for sharper asymptotics. That is a reason to investigate the mirror, not evidence that the exact limit follows from Theorem 3.1. Useful next questions include whether the scaled hitting-time law converges, whether the \(R^2\) scale is tight, and how the answer changes when there are a few complete agent types with different relevant parameters.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it preserves the same repeated anti-coordination task, context cycle, courtesy updates, monitoring, quotas, and efficient-equilibrium target, while making population mass the object whose asymptotics are studied. The cited decentralized-learning work [13] supports the regime’s plausibility. The mirror asks for the quantitative behavior of this particular process, rather than for existence or structure of a mean-field equilibrium.

The weakest point is that Theorem 3.1 already covers arbitrary finite \(N\) and \(R\). Taking its high-multiplicity limit may therefore yield a useful analysis of their result without opening a substantially new complexity question. Also, collisions and learned resource assignments can depend on microscopic correlations that a mass distribution alone may not capture. The proposed question keeps the finite collision process in its approximants to avoid silently replacing it with a different congestion model, but the authors would still need to decide whether that asymptotic hitting-time problem is a continuous-population problem they want to own.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is real: Theorem 3.1 gives an expected convergence-time bound, so “there is no named computational result” is not a sound objection. The problem is that CA3NONY Mass-Limit Convergence keeps the finite stochastic game in every instance and asks what happens along the sequence \(N=KR\). Its society is always the single type \(\mu=\delta_{t_0}\); the proposed limit is a sharper asymptotic analysis of the algorithm, not a computational problem whose input is a continuous society. The theorem already gives an \(O_K(R^2)\) bound in that regime. Finding a limiting constant would improve that analysis, but it would not by itself chart a complexity landscape or give an algorithm for a problem specified by a type distribution.

The stronger version is more tempting: give agents several complete types, scale resource supply per capita, and compute an optimal courtesy or quota policy subject to convergence and incentive constraints. That is a plausible continuous-population mechanism-design problem. But the paper fixes the learning rule and assumes common payoffs and a common backoff probability; optimizing policies for heterogeneous types introduces a new problem rather than continuizing one of its results. The nearby equilibrium result, Theorem 3.3, has the same limitation: a continuum model could study a tagged agent’s incentive to deviate, but that is an analytic mean-field equilibrium question unless one adds a new computational task.

There is also a modeling obstacle to the direct limit. With \(N=KR\), each resource still faces only \(O(1)\) agents on average, and collisions depend on integer occupancy. A smooth mass per resource tends to zero while those collision events remain consequential. A faithful limit would therefore need to retain a distribution over local occupancy and strategy states. That may be possible and useful, but its main object is a stochastic limit over local configurations, not the kind of mass-based computational problem the programme is designed to classify.

The negative case is not airtight. A large wireless network with fixed congestion is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors might well value a rigorous limit law or sharper convergence-time estimate. That is the surviving weakness: if stochastic convergence asymptotics count as a worthwhile computational mirror, the proponent’s anchor stands. The stronger case against is that the paper offers a plausible analytic follow-on, but no clear continuous computational problem anchored in its results.
