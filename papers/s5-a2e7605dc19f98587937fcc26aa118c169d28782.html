The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), integer weights \(a_t\), and rational quota fraction \(q\), consider every integer \(N\) for which \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nq\) are integers. Form the weighted voting game with \(N\mu_t\) players of type \(t\), each of weight \(a_t\), and quota \(Nq\). If \(\eta_t^{(N)}\) is the common nucleolus payoff of type \(t\), determine whether \(u_t^{(N)}=N\eta_t^{(N)}\) converges along admissible \(N\to\infty\); compute the limit if it exists, and otherwise determine nonconvergence.

The model it lives in

The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over weight types \(T\). At each admissible population size \(N\), type \(t\) has \(N\mu_t\) members of weight \(a_t\), and the quota is \(Nq\). The finite game's nucleolus lexicographically minimizes coalition deficits; the continuous question studies the limit of its type-level per-capita payoffs \(u_t^{(N)}\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_t^{(N)}=1\). Coalition profiles have masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), but their finite-game multiplicities remain part of the limit being studied.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 for weighted voting games. It leaves the \(k\)-vector extension, Theorem 5's general framework, and other nucleolus results discussed in the paper aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the type-level per-capita nucleolus vectors converge for every rational instance?
  • Can convergence be decided and the limit computed from \((T,\mu,a,q)\), with complexity stated in \(\tau\), \(W\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • What role do the asymptotic multiplicities of coalitions with each type-mass profile play in determining the limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest admissible anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for a weighted voting game with integer player weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) and quota \(q\), an algorithm computes the game’s nucleolus in time polynomial in \(n\) and \(W=\max_i w_i\). Because weights are binary encoded, this is a pseudopolynomial result. The paper’s discussion of NP-hardness points to earlier work [5]; it is not a numbered result of this paper, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large cooperative or shareholder body with many members but only a few voting-weight classes. A member’s type is their voting weight, since that is the full description the weighted voting game uses. For instance, there might be millions of members spread across a handful of share classes. Here the population size \(N\) is much larger than the support size \(\tau\).

I would pose the mirror as the mass-limit nucleolus convergence problem. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of types, positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an integer vote weight \(a_t\) for each type, and a rational quota fraction \(q\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t\ge q\). For each population size \(N\) that makes every \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nq\) integral, form the finite weighted voting game with \(N\mu_t\) members of type \(t\), each of weight \(a_t\), and quota \(Nq\).

In this sequence, ask whether the type-level, per-capita nucleolus payoffs converge, and compute the limit if they do. Specifically, if \(\eta_i^{(N)}\) is the nucleolus payoff of member \(i\), symmetry makes it equal for all members of a type; define \(u_t^{(N)}=N\eta_i^{(N)}\). The continuous society has mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\), and the limiting payoff vector \(u\) must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t=1\). A coalition is represented by its type masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\); it wins if \(\sum_t a_tx_t\ge q\), and receives \(\sum_t x_tu_t\). The objective is inherited from the paper: the finite games choose the payoff allocation whose coalition deficits are lexicographically minimal, and the continuous question asks for the limit of those allocations. A complete answer should also determine when the sequence fails to converge; its subsequential limits are a natural follow-up.

This is a genuine continuous-population question about the paper’s underlying problem. It keeps the weighted voting rule, the grand-coalition payoff, and the nucleolus objective, while representing the society by type masses. It does not simply declare fractional coalitions to have a new, multiplicity-free nucleolus: the limit is defined from the paper’s finite concept, so whether it has a useful direct characterization is part of the research question.

My expectation is Class A in the regime of few types and bounded weights: type symmetry and the paper’s dynamic-programming approach suggest that a type-compressed method could compute the limit in terms of \(\tau\), \(W\), and the input bit length \(L\). This is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 1: the theorem’s running time is polynomial in \(N\) and \(W\), and says nothing by itself about convergence or an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau,W,L\). Follow-up questions include whether the limit always exists, how quickly the finite payoffs approach it, and whether it admits a direct characterization using the fractional coalition profiles \(x\).

The weakest point is exactly that gap. Nucleolus comparisons depend on how many coalitions attain each deficit, and those multiplicities can behave subtly as the electorate grows. The paper’s algorithm computes each finite instance; it does not establish a continuous limit or compress its computation to a mass description. That makes this a credible extension question, but not a result one can claim the paper already settles.

The scope here is Theorem 1 and the WVG nucleolus only. It does not attempt to mirror the paper’s \(k\)-vector extension or its general separation-oracle framework.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational result, and there is a credible high-multiplicity setting for it: a large voting body with many members in a few weight classes. A member’s weight is a complete type for this game. So the strongest case against cannot rest on the absence of an anchor or a plausible population regime.

The real difficulty is that the nucleolus orders a multiset of coalition deficits. In a cloned population with \(N\mu_t\) members of each type, a type-count vector \(k\) represents \(\prod_t \binom{N\mu_t}{k_t}\) distinct coalitions. Those coalitions have the same deficit under a type-symmetric payoff, but their multiplicity still enters the nucleolus objective. As \(N\) grows, these counts can grow exponentially. A mass profile \(x\) records the coalition’s type masses, but does not itself say how to weight that profile in a lexicographic comparison. A direct continuum nucleolus would therefore need an additional rule for ordering or measuring its continuum of coalition profiles; different choices need not match the finite nucleolus.

The proposed clone-limit is the strongest repair: it specifies how those multiplicities arise and asks a precise convergence question. But that makes the target a limit under a particular replication scheme, whose behavior may depend on the asymptotics of the labeled subset counts, rather than a nucleolus defined directly on the mass distribution. That is a substantive concern about what the mirror studies, not a claim that the limit is uninteresting or that the finite theorem settles it. The specified rational scaling also makes the replication scheme reasonably canonical, so this concern does not defeat the mirror outright.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the prompt asks for. The coalition-counting issue is the best objection, but the proponent’s limit construction answers much of it, and the paper’s algorithmic anchor and high-multiplicity regime both survive. The negative case is therefore weak: it cautions against presenting the result as an intrinsic continuum nucleolus, but it does not establish that the clone-limit question is not worth the programme’s time.
