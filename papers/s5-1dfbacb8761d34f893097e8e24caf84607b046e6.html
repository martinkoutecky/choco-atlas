What the mirror covers

The proposed limit concerns Theorem 2's Rolodex payoffs; it does not cover Theorem 1's queue-dependent Stole–Zwiebel payoffs or the paper's other implications.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the Rolodex result, Theorem 2, which the paper proves in Appendix B. It says that for \(n\) workers with positive gains from trade, the Rolodex game has a unique no-delay subgame-perfect equilibrium in the limit \(q\to0\). The firm’s payoff satisfies
\[ \tilde\pi_j=\tilde\pi_{j-1}+\frac{F(j)-\tilde\pi_{j-1}-jw}{1+j}, \qquad \tilde\pi_0=F(0), \]
and worker \(i\)’s payoff is
\[ \tilde w_n^i=w+\frac{F(n)-\tilde\pi_{n-1}-nw}{1+n}. \]
The result is proved here, and the authors show that these payoffs equal the Shapley values.

A plausible mirror would be a large firm with many interchangeable workers: every worker has the same preferences, bargaining role, and outside option per unit of worker mass. The society has mass \(1\) and one type, so \(\tau=1\) while the number of workers grows. To scale the model, let \(f:[0,1]\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) be an increasing, concave, rational piecewise-linear production function with \(f(0)=0\). Approximate the population by \(n\) workers of mass \(1/n\), set output from hiring \(j\) workers to \(F_n(j)=f(j/n)\), and give each worker outside option \(w/n\). Each finite approximation uses the paper’s Rolodex protocol.

The continuous problem is: given \(f\) and \(w\), compute the limiting firm payoff \(P=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde\pi_n\) and the common wage density \(u=\lim_{n\to\infty}n\tilde w_n^i\). The recurrence in Theorem 2 yields
\[ P=\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx-\frac w2, \qquad u=f(1)-P. \]
Assume \(f(1)-\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx-\frac w2>0\), the limiting positive-gains condition. For piecewise-linear \(f\), the pair can be computed exactly by integrating its segments, in time polynomial in the input description. The expected direction is tractability. This question covers the Rolodex equilibrium allocation; I would not stretch it to cover the Stole–Zwiebel result, where queue position affects wages and a high-multiplicity encoding would need to track that position.

The main weakness is also decisive for ChoCo’s anchor rule: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 2 characterizes equilibrium payoffs; it does not assert a problem is in P or establish a hardness result. The proposed limit computation is a concrete, plausible high-multiplicity extension, but it is not a mirror of a qualifying computational anchor. It also defines the continuum outcome through finite-population limits rather than proving an equilibrium for a bargaining game with a continuum of workers. That makes it a useful follow-up question, and a weak case that this paper supplies the required computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The Rolodex theorem is the only serious anchor, and it fails ChoCo’s stated gate. Theorem 2 characterizes equilibrium payoffs; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper gives no decision or search problem with a complexity result, algorithmic bounds, or hardness result to carry into a continuous society. That distinction matters here: a useful equilibrium theorem is not, by itself, a computational-complexity anchor.

The proposed mirror is nevertheless well motivated as an economic limit model. A unit mass of interchangeable workers, a production function \(f\), and outside options scaled per unit of mass give a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Taking the limit in the theorem’s recurrence yields \(xP'(x)=f(x)-P(x)-xw\), and hence the proposed aggregate payoff. That is a coherent question about a continuum bargaining equilibrium. It changes the paper’s subject into a limit analysis, though; it does not continue a computational question the paper posed. The analysis sits in the continuum-equilibrium tradition that ChoCo treats as neighboring machinery, rather than in the missing complexity landscape the programme is meant to map.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s criterion, but its weakness is clear: the population model itself is convincing. I can reject it as a computational mirror of this paper; I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous extension exists if the scope broadens to economic limit models.
