The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result: no theorem or proposition classifies an underlying problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that the gradual uniform rule is the unique rule satisfying efficiency, individual rationality, strategy-proofness, and position-wise envy-freeness. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity result. Proposition 1, also proved here, says that the uniform reallocation rule is not position-wise envy-free. Neither qualifies as an anchor under your criterion.

There is still a plausible continuous-population computation suggested by the paper, though it cannot serve as a compliant anchor. Imagine a large housing or utility cooperative with many households but a small number of standardized preference-and-endowment types. A type \(t\) has a common single-peaked preference with peak \(p_t\), and a common endowment \(e_t\); \(\mu_t\) is its share of the population. For binary-rational masses summing to one, let \(\bar E=\sum_t\mu_t e_t\). The question is to compute an individually assigned amount \(x_t\) for each type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=\bar E\), according to the gradual uniform rule:

\[ x_t= \begin{cases} \min\{p_t,\max\{e_t,\lambda\}\}, & \sum_t\mu_t p_t\ge \bar E,\\ \max\{p_t,\min\{e_t,\lambda\}\}, & \sum_t\mu_t p_t\le \bar E, \end{cases} \]

where \(\lambda\) is chosen to satisfy feasibility. A solution is \(\lambda\) and the resulting vector of type assignments. With finite support of size \(\tau\), the threshold can be found by sorting the breakpoints \(e_t,p_t\) and solving a linear equation on the appropriate interval, using time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. Rational masses also correspond exactly to a finite population with repeated identical types.

This mirrors the paper’s allocation rule: it preserves the single-peaked preferences, individual endowments, reallocation, and the rule’s type-level treatment of agents. It changes the population representation to a distribution and asks for the allocation, rather than continuizing the commodity further. The paper’s Theorem 1 is relevant motivation for why that rule matters, but it does not establish the computational claim.

The weakest point is central: computation is not the paper’s contribution. Its main result is axiomatic, while its good is already perfectly divisible. The proposed mirror covers only computing the gradual uniform allocation, not the uniqueness characterization or the paper’s fairness analysis. A continuous computation problem is credible here, but the paper supplies no qualifying named computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is a counterexample to a fairness property; Theorem 1 characterizes the gradual uniform rule by axioms. A continuous version of that characterization would still be an axiomatic result, outside ChoCo’s computational focus.

The proponent’s strongest candidate is coherent. In a large cooperative, let each type \(t\) have a common peak \(p_t\) and endowment \(e_t\), with population share \(\mu_t\). One can ask for the gradual-uniform allocation \(x_t\), satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=\sum_t\mu_t e_t\). That truly continuizes the population; the fact that the good is already divisible does not make it an outcome-space mirror. Nor is repeated preference-and-endowment types an implausible regime.

But this candidate computes a rule the paper already states explicitly. For finite support, its threshold \(\lambda\) can be found by sorting the \(e_t\) and \(p_t\) breakpoints and solving the weighted feasibility equation between them. That may be useful implementation work, but it does not turn the paper’s contribution into a computational problem with a complexity result to mirror. Making the computation more substantive would require adding a new objective or constraints—such as trading costs or restricted transfers—that the paper does not study. Mirroring the uniqueness theorem over distributions would remain axiomatic.

I would not argue that this candidate fails because it is easy, or because the commodity is continuous, or because high multiplicity is already studied. The narrower negative case is stronger: there is no eligible computational anchor here. Its limit is that a credible implementation question exists, and the paper could inspire new computational problems. So the claim that no worthwhile question could arise in any scenario is too broad to establish from this paper; what the paper does not support is a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of its actual results.
