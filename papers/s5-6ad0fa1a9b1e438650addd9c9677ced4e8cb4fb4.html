The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), baseline score vectors \(p_t\), and a fixed target pairing \(\bar t\) in which paired types have equal mass, find the minimum cost of choosing one revised score vector \(z_t\) for each type, with cost \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{u\in N(t)}|z_t(u)-p_t(u)|\), such that every nonmatching acceptable pair \(t,u\) satisfies \(z_t(u)\le z_t(\bar t)\) or \(z_u(t)\le z_u(\bar u)\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over interchangeable participant types, each assigned uniformly to a partner type by a fixed mass pairing. Each type receives one revised preference-score vector, and the objective is the mass-weighted \(\ell_1\) change; stability requires that no nonmatching acceptable pair of types strictly prefers each other to their assigned partners.

The objection that survived

The uniform-within-type repair restricts preference changes to whole cohorts, while Theorem 12 permits individualized changes; this confines the mirror to a cohort-level intervention regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 12’s vertex-cover hardness and conditional approximation lower bound in the cohort-level mass model; it leaves the bipartite algorithm, general-graph approximation, and the deletion and preference-extension results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the bipartite polynomial-time method of Theorem 13 extend to the type-mass formulation?
  • What discrete-to-continuous guarantees hold when members of a type may receive different preference revisions?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous \(\ell_1\)-MinPM. A plausible regime is a large placement or peer-pairing service: many participants share each profile, including the same acceptable partner profiles, preference values, and compensation costs. The service has a proposed pairing plan and can adjust participants’ preference values to make that plan stable. The type graph records which profile classes can pair.

For a precise version, let \(T\) be the finite set of participant types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Refine types by their role in the proposed plan, so every member of a type \(t\) is assigned to the same partner type \(\bar t\), or is unmatched; paired types \(t,s\) satisfy \(\bar t=s\), \(\bar s=t\), and \(\mu_t=\mu_s\). A type also specifies its acceptable partner types \(N(t)\) and baseline scores \(p_t(u)\). Assume the plan is maximal, as in the paper’s problem.

A solution may change different members’ scores by different amounts. Represent those choices for type \(t\) by a measure \(\pi_t\) over modified score vectors \(z\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{N(t)}\), of total mass \(\mu_t\). Its cost is
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\int \sum_{u\in N(t)} |z_u-p_t(u)|\,d\pi_t(z). \]
For an acceptable pair of types \(t,u\), let \(W_{t\to u}=\mu_t\) if \(t\) is unmatched, and otherwise let
\[ W_{t\to u}=\pi_t\bigl(\{z:z_u>z_{\bar t}\}\bigr). \]
The solution is stable when, for every acceptable pair \(t,u\), at least one of \(W_{t\to u}\) and \(W_{u\to t}\) is zero. Thus no positive mass on both sides strictly prefers the other type to its assigned partner. Each person still receives one partner; the masses record how many people have each type and each modified score vector. A decision version asks whether a stable modification of cost at most a given rational budget exists.

The anchor is Theorem 12, proved in this paper. It says that \(\ell_1\)-min-pm—given a maximal \(q\)-matching \(M\) and preference matrix \(p\), find minimum-cost preferences \(p'\) making \(M\) weakly stable—is NP-hard even when every capacity is one. It also says that, assuming the Unique Games Conjecture, no \(2-\varepsilon\) approximation exists for any \(\varepsilon>0\). The paper proves the reduction from vertex cover; its approximation lower bound uses the cited Khot–Regev result.

The reduction gives a particularly clean high-multiplicity lift. For each vertex \(v\) of a vertex-cover instance, take two types \(v'\) and \(v''\), paired with one another in the target plan. Type \(v'\) has score \(0\) for its target partner and score \(1\) for each neighbor type \(u'\) corresponding to an edge \(uv\); \(v''\) has only \(v'\) as an acceptable partner. Give each of these \(2n\) types mass \(1/(2n)\), realizable by \(K\) identical participants per type for any \(K\). For each graph edge, stability requires one endpoint cohort to have zero mass strictly preferring across that edge. Since that cohort’s baseline scores are \(0\) for its target partner and \(1\) for the neighbor, enforcing the required weak comparison costs at least one per-person unit across the whole cohort. The cohorts that pay this cost form a vertex cover. Conversely, raising the target-partner score to \(1\) for every cohort in a vertex cover makes the plan stable. The optimum is therefore exactly the minimum vertex-cover size divided by \(2n\). The same construction preserves the approximation ratio.

I expect hardness transfers (Class B): the hard combinatorics remain in the graph on profile types even when the population is arbitrarily larger than the support. This mirror covers Theorem 12 alone; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s deletion or preference-extension results. It also suggests concrete follow-up questions: does the paper’s bipartite polynomial-time result extend to this mass model, and what bounds in support size or type-graph structure make the general problem tractable?

The weakest point is the scenario’s need for a meaningful type graph: members of a profile class must be interchangeable with members of the partner classes they can reach. The clone construction has many people per type, but preserves an arbitrary graph on those types; its hardness comes from that support-level structure, not from population multiplicity. That makes this a narrow, author-recognizable mirror of inverse preference modification, rather than evidence that every matching market has a useful continuum limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed model may weaken the paper’s exact stability requirement. Theorem 12 asks that *no blocking edge* remain. But a condition on mass distributions can miss blockers concentrated in vanishingly small groups: in a sequence of \(K\)-person cohorts, one agent on each side may still form a blocking pair, while their masses tend to zero and disappear from the limiting distribution. The mirror therefore needs a clear stability semantics and a discrete-to-continuous dictionary.

That concern does not defeat the stronger version of the mirror. Require preference changes to be uniform within each type, and interpret types as large, interchangeable cohorts with shared compatibility. Then the target matching is a mass coupling between types, and the vertex-cover construction lifts directly: each type has mass \(1/(2n)\), paired cohorts have equal mass, and making a cohort stop strictly preferring a neighbor costs its mass. The optimum is the minimum vertex-cover size divided by \(2n\). Stability is exact in this regime because every member of a cohort has the same relevant preferences. This is a plausible high-multiplicity market, and the resulting hardness is a meaningful Class B result under the programme’s criteria.

So the exactness gap is a real issue for the proponent’s unrestricted measure-valued formulation, but it does not dispose of the cohort-level mirror. I cannot honestly defeat Theorem 12’s anchor: the negative case is weak, and the proposed mirror appears worth asking about.
