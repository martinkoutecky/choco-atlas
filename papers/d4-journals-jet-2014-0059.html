What the mirror covers

None of the paper's results yields a computational mirror; Theorems 1–3 and the other named propositions concern limits, solution-set inclusions, nonemptiness, or equilibrium structure.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible population model here. A buyer type could be \((v,d)\), recording valuations and demand capacity; a seller type could be \((r,q)\), recording reservation prices and inventories. In a large wholesale market with many repeated buyer and supplier profiles, mass records the share of each type. A continuous assignment would send trade mass between buyer and seller types, subject to their per capita capacities. The paper’s replication setup is direct evidence that this high-multiplicity regime makes sense to its authors.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its closest results are:

  • Theorem 1, proved here: if a payoff belongs to the Core of every \(\rho\)-fold replicated market, for all \(\rho\ge1\), then it is a competitive-equilibrium payoff of the original market.
  • Theorem 2, proved here: a payoff duplicated across both copies belongs to the Set-wise stable set of the two-fold replicated market if and only if the original payoff is a competitive-equilibrium payoff.
  • Theorem 3, proved here: for every positive integer \(\rho\), there is a market with a payoff outside competitive equilibrium whose replicated payoff belongs to the Core of the \(\rho\)-fold market.

These are limit and solution-set theorems, not results about an algorithm, hardness, or parameterized complexity. The assignment LP in the appendix is used in proofs; the paper does not give a named complexity result for computing or deciding anything from it.

So the honest positive case is limited: the market admits a plausible type-mass continuation, and replication supports its high-multiplicity interpretation. But there is no per-anchor computational question I can responsibly hand to a prover as a mirror of this paper’s named results. The gap is the requested one: the paper studies what happens to cooperative solution sets as markets grow, but does not chart the computational complexity of those problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest premise is sound: this market has a credible high-multiplicity regime. In a large wholesale market, many buyers can share a valuation-demand type \((v,d)\), and many sellers can share a reservation-price-inventory type \((r,q)\). The paper’s replication construction supports that interpretation. A mass model with per-capita capacities and type-level average trades would be a reasonable continuization. I cannot make a serious objection based on agents being individuated or on fractional trades.

The problem is that none of the three theorems the proponent names is a computational result. Theorem 1 is a limit statement about the Core as \(\rho\) grows; Theorem 2 compares Set-wise stability and competitive-equilibrium payoffs in a two-fold replica; Theorem 3 gives a non-equilibrium Core payoff for some market at each replica count. They establish facts about solution sets. They do not pose an algorithmic input-output problem or classify its complexity. That is the strongest negative ground available here: the continuous population model is plausible, but the paper offers no named computational result for a continuous mirror to inherit.

Theorem 1 is the best candidate. One could define a continuum market over the same buyer and seller types and ask whether its Core coincides with competitive equilibrium. But that is a solution-set or limit question, and the paper’s central contribution already concerns precisely that kind of convergence. To make it computational, one would need to add a new task—such as deciding membership in the continuum Core from a finite-support rational input. That may be a worthwhile project, but it is a new complexity question, not a mirror of Theorem 1.

The same gap applies to Theorems 2 and 3. A continuum version of Theorem 2 would compare two solution sets; an algorithmic version would require defining a computational task for Set-wise stability, whose blocking condition quantifies over distributions and assignments. Theorem 3’s quantifiers also range over a different market for each \(\rho\), rather than following one fixed society into a population limit. One can devise computational questions around either result, but the results themselves provide no complexity claim to mirror.

So the negative case is narrow: the market model and its continuous population are convincing, but that does not make this paper a strong ChoCo mirror. Its named results are about the economic behavior of solution concepts as markets are replicated, not about computational complexity. I cannot honestly argue that no useful continuous computational question could ever be asked about this model; I can argue that answering one would be a new research direction, rather than continuizing a computational result in the paper.
