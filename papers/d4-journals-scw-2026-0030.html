What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror would concern the paper’s fairness-constrained implementation characterizations; it leaves the Pareto-efficiency and double-implementation results outside that proposal.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large public-school district. Many families may share the same complete, state-contingent preferences and other relevant characteristics, so a type is that full description and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of families of type \(t\). The district could have \(N\gg\tau\), with many families but relatively few distinct types. The planner’s goal would be a social choice correspondence over school-assignment outcomes; the mechanism would give families opportunities to reach those outcomes through their messages. That is a recognizable population mirror of the paper’s fairness concern, and the authors could plausibly recognize it.

A precise prospective question is: given finite \(X\), states \(\Omega\), complete preference types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), and a correspondence \(F:\Omega\to 2^X\), does there exist a full-information mechanism for the district that anonymously implements \(F\)? A solution would specify the mechanism and show that at each state every outcome in \(F(\omega)\) is attainable at an equilibrium where everyone has the same opportunity set, while every such equilibrium outcome belongs to \(F(\omega)\). The corresponding no-envy version would require each type to choose the realized outcome from every type’s opportunity set. This would cover the paper’s fairness-constrained implementation results, not its Pareto-efficiency discussion.

But the paper provides no qualifying named computational result to anchor that question. The nearest is Theorem 1, proved here: no-envy implementability implies a no-envy-consistent profile of opportunity sets, and such consistency is sufficient when the SCC is no-veto and \(n\ge3\); anonymously, implementability implies anonymous consistency, which is sufficient when the SCC is unanimous and \(n\ge3\). Theorem 2, also proved here, says that in rational environments no-envy consistency, anonymous consistency, and common Maskin independence are equivalent. These are implementation characterizations, not results about a problem being in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The cited *Computational implementation* paper is elsewhere and cannot serve as an anchor for this paper.

There is also a substantive modeling obstacle. If the continuous mechanism depends only on aggregate message masses, one person’s deviation changes no mass, so the paper’s individual Nash deviations lose their force. Treating a whole type’s mass as one deviating player would restore influence, but would change the strategic problem. My expectation for the proposed existence question is therefore unresolved: type-level consistency conditions may be tractable to check, but that would not by itself settle mechanism existence under a faithful continuum equilibrium notion. The useful follow-up questions are whether the relevant conditions depend on type masses or only on support, and whether a continuum implementation concept preserves the paper’s individual deviation logic. The positive case is a credible scenario for future work; it does not meet the programme’s anchor requirement on this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is at the level of the anchors. Theorem 1 gives necessary and almost sufficient conditions for anonymous and no-envy implementation; Theorem 2 relates those conditions to common Maskin independence in rational environments. Neither classifies a computational problem: the paper gives no complexity result for deciding implementability, searching for a mechanism, or optimizing over mechanisms. Turning one of those questions into a decision problem would require choices about how states, the correspondence, and mechanisms are represented. That could be a research project, but it would be a new computational framing of an axiomatic paper.

The strongest proposed mirror would take a large district with finitely many complete family types and rational type masses, then ask whether a correspondence is anonymously or no-envy implementable. The district is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; the difficulty is that the paper’s implementation conditions are about individual deviations. In an aggregate continuum mechanism, one family’s deviation leaves the type-mass distribution unchanged, so it cannot affect the outcome through the aggregate. Treating an entire type’s mass as one player restores influence by replacing unilateral individual deviations with bloc deviations. Keeping individuals’ deviations instead requires the mechanism to respond to particular people, information that a finite-support distribution over types does not encode. These are changes to the strategic object, not merely a more natural unit of population.

Theorem 2 is an especially weak anchor for a population mirror. For an explicitly listed finite environment, common Maskin independence can be checked from preferences and the correspondence. Replacing repeated agents by rational masses does not make those conditions about masses: the relevant lower-contour intersections depend on which types are present, not how much mass each type holds. A continuum version could preserve pointwise fairness, but then it still needs to track individual exceptions. If it instead imposes fairness only almost everywhere, a zero-mass or vanishing-mass group can lose the protection the paper’s no-envy condition gives each individual. Either choice leaves a potentially interesting theorem about implementation, but not a population-weighted computational problem anchored in this result.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo mirror. The negative case is not airtight: one could study a new continuum game or a type-bloc implementation concept, and the school-district setting makes high multiplicity plausible. But those projects would need to change the paper’s unilateral-deviation primitive or retain identity-level information that the proposed continuous society omits. The paper offers no named computational result to carry across that gap.
