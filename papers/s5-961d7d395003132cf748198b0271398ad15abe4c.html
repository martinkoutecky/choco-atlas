The continuous mirror question

Given a rational type-mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\), and a rational-coefficient polynomial comparison kernel \(f(x,y)\) of fixed degree on \(P_\mu\times P_\mu\), where \(P_\mu=\prod_a[0,\mu_a]\), compute for each type \(a\) the value \(b_a=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\big[f(z+\epsilon e_a,z)-f(z,z+\epsilon e_a)\big]\right|_{\epsilon=0}\), with \(z=\mu/2\), and return the weak ranking induced by \((b_a)_a\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic society has \(\tau\) complete agent types with masses \(\mu_a\). A coalition is represented by a type-mass vector \(x\in P_\mu\), and \(f(x,y)\) compares two coalition compositions. Type \(a\)’s attribution is the directional first-order effect of adding its mass at the midpoint \(z=\mu/2\); rank types by these effects.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It gives a formula for computing the semivalue of the comparison pseudo-game \(F\) induced by \(f\): for every player \(i\) and probability vector \(p\), \(\psi_i^p(F)=\sum_{S,T\subseteq X\setminus\{i\}}\left[(p_{|S|}+p_{|T|})(f(S\cup\{i\},T)-f(T,S\cup\{i\}))+\frac{p_{|S|}-p_{|T|}}{2}(f(T,S)+f(S\cup\{i\},T\cup\{i\}))\right]\). For Banzhaf, the second term vanishes, leaving \(\beta_i(F)=2^{-(|X|-2)}\sum_{S,T\subseteq X\setminus\{i\}}(f(S\cup\{i\},T)-f(T,S\cup\{i\}))\). The paper uses this formula to calculate examples directly from comparisons. It does not establish a complexity classification; this is a computational identity and my sole anchor.

A plausible setting is a large school district or university where educators compare the performance of student groups. A type records every student trait that the comparisons use—for example, skill profile and program—and students of one type are interchangeable for this task. The district might have thousands of students but only dozens of positive-mass types. Let \(\mu_a\) be the population fraction of type \(a\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\). A coalition is represented by its type-mass vector \(x\), where \(0\leq x_a\leq\mu_a\). The function \(f(x,y)\) gives the comparison between groups with compositions \(x\) and \(y\). This follows the paper’s group-assessment motivation while treating a student’s importance as a type-level quantity; students of the same type receive the same attribution.

I would ask the authors to study Type Banzhaf Comparison Ranking. An instance consists of a rational mass vector \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types and a rational, fixed-degree polynomial \(f\) on pairs of feasible coalition-mass vectors. Let \(z=\mu/2\). For each type \(a\), compute \(b_a=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left[f(z+\epsilon e_a,z)-f(z,z+\epsilon e_a)\right]\right|_{\epsilon=0}\), and return the exact values and their weak ranking. Here \(e_a\) is the unit vector for type \(a\). The first comparison adds an infinitesimal amount of type \(a\) to one group; the second records the reverse comparison with that addition on the other side.

This is a high-multiplicity limit of the anchored Banzhaf calculation. In a finite society of \(N\) students whose comparisons depend only on group composition, Theorem 4 averages the relevant comparisons over two coalitions excluding the tagged student. As \(N\) grows, those random coalitions’ type masses concentrate at \(\mu/2\); adding one type-\(a\) student changes mass by \(1/N\). For differentiable \(f\), the per-person values, normalized by \(N2^{-N}\), converge to \(b_a\). Thus the continuous question preserves the paper’s comparison-based attribution and player ranking, with ties among genuinely interchangeable students.

For the stated representation of \(f\), this problem is tractable: differentiate the polynomial and evaluate its gradient at \((\mu/2,\mu/2)\), using time polynomial in its encoding size and \(\tau\). That is a tractability result for this mirror, not a claim made by the paper. It also raises natural follow-up questions: how fast do finite-society rankings converge, what attribution makes sense for nonsmooth comparisons, and how can sparse or incomplete observed comparisons specify \(f\)?

The weak point is the smoothness and type-composition restriction. The paper allows arbitrary comparisons between finite coalitions; this mirror only represents comparisons that vary smoothly with group composition. The limit also concentrates Banzhaf’s random coalitions at half the population, so it captures a local, per-person attribution. Still, the paper’s own group-assessment scenario makes type-level comparison a credible instance regime, and Theorem 4 supplies a precise computational quantity whose continuous analogue is meaningful.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the only anchor, and it is an identity for computing semivalues—not a complexity result. It gives a weighted sum of comparison values, but says nothing about a computational problem’s complexity, an algorithmic bottleneck, or a parameterized regime. With comparisons listed explicitly, the sum is just aggregation over the input; with comparisons represented by a formula or oracle, the difficulty depends on that representation. So this paper offers little of the complexity landscape the continuization programme is meant to chart.

The proposed Type Banzhaf Comparison Ranking does have a recognizable limit behind it. If comparisons depend smoothly only on type composition, uniformly sampled coalitions concentrate at \(z=\mu/2\), and the normalized Banzhaf value tends to a directional derivative there. But that conclusion relies on giving the continuous model a smooth kernel \(f\). The paper’s input is comparative information about finite coalitions, often incomplete; it supplies no values at fractional compositions and no principle selecting a kernel between them. Different continuous extensions can agree on every observed comparison and have different derivatives. With a fixed degree polynomial supplied as input, the proposed problem is derivative evaluation; with a less restricted kernel, its computational content depends on an encoding the paper does not motivate.

A better mirror could preserve more of the semivalue calculation: extend comparisons to a kernel over type-mass vectors, then average type-level marginal effects over a range of coalition sizes, as a Shapley-style limit would. That is a coherent model, but it still needs the extra kernel and a specified input format. Under a succinct smooth representation, the question becomes computation on that representation; under sparse observed comparisons, the continuous values are underdetermined. Neither gives a complexity question grounded in Theorem 4’s result.

The negative case is not airtight. The paper’s student-group example could support a high-multiplicity regime if students are interchangeable by a sufficiently complete type and group performance is modeled from type composition. Then type-level marginal effects might be useful. But that would require a substantive model of group performance and a new computational question; the paper’s theorem alone neither supplies the continuous object nor identifies a hard computational task for it.
