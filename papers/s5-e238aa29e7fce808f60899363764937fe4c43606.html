The continuous mirror question

Let Τ be a finite set of expert types with masses μ_t, and let each type t have a finite report alphabet R_t. On each task, ω is drawn from an unknown Bernoulli prior; conditional on ω, agents of type t independently draw signals from a type-specific law and report their posterior beliefs. In the nonatomic population, the observed profile is q with q_{t,r}=μ_t Pr(r_i=r | ω). Given T IID task samples (q,ω), determine the minimax sample complexity for returning ĥf such that, with probability at least 1-δ, E_{(q,ω)}[(ĥf(q)-Pr(ω=1|q))^2]≤ε, uniformly over Bayes-compatible signal laws.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with finitely many expert types of masses μ_t; conditionally independent signals within and across types; finite posterior-report alphabets R_t; exact per-task report masses q_{t,r}; and the squared-loss Bayes prediction Pr(ω=1|q) learned from IID profile-outcome samples.

The objection that survived

The exact conditional-independence limit often makes each profile state-identifying or leaves only the prior, so the finite-\(n\) lower-bound phenomenon may vanish; the proponent flags this degeneracy but does not establish when a nontrivial limit remains.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the conditional-independence learning question of Theorem 5.2 at the exact profile limit. Theorem 4.1 appears only as an interchangeable-type extension, not as a transfer of its arbitrary-correlation lower bound; the informative-expert refinements and multi-outcome results are left open.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the minimax sample complexity as a function of ε, δ, the number of types, and their report alphabets?
  • When the state-conditioned population profiles are close, does a lower bound analogous to Theorem 5.2 persist?

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is a statistical high-multiplicity mirror. This paper has no named theorem about runtime complexity, \(P\), or NP-hardness: its named complexity results are about sample complexity. So it supplies no anchor for a strict runtime-complexity classification. The mirror below keeps the paper’s learning question and continuizes its population of experts.

Imagine a large forecasting platform with \(N\) experts but only \(\tau\) distinct forecasting types: people using the same information pipeline, signal model, and reporting rule. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s fraction of the population. On each prediction task, let \(q_{t,a}\) be the fraction of the whole population that has type \(t\) and reports \(a\). Thus \(q_{t,a}\geq 0\) and \(\sum_a q_{t,a}=\mu_t\). The profile \(q\) lies in a product of simplices and is the continuous population input. Assume that experts within a type are interchangeable, so their report histogram contains the relevant information. Forecasting tasks remain discrete IID samples; it is the expert population that becomes continuous.

My lead mirror is High-Multiplicity Bayesian Forecast Learning. An instance gives the type masses \(\mu\), finite report alphabets \(R_t\), and sample access to an unknown law \(P\) over \((q,\omega)\), with binary outcome \(\omega\). From \(T\) IID samples, return a measurable aggregator \(\hat f(q)\in[0,1]\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over the samples,

\[ \mathbb E_P[(\hat f(q)-f_P^*(q))^2]\leq\epsilon, \qquad f_P^*(q)=P(\omega=1\mid q). \]

The question is the minimum sample complexity as a function of \(\epsilon,\delta,\tau\), the report alphabets, and any stated structure on \(P\).

The anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. For \(n\geq 2\) experts, each with \(m\) possible signals, and arbitrary joint distributions over signals and a binary event, the sample complexity of learning an \(\epsilon\)-optimal Bayesian aggregator is at most \(O((m^n+\log(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2)\) and at least \(\Omega((m^{n-2}+\log(1/\delta))/\epsilon)\). The lower bound reflects the cost of arbitrary conditional correlations among experts.

That theorem motivates the mirror, though its lower bound does not automatically transfer: the mirror assumes within-type interchangeability. For fixed \(\tau\) and finite report alphabets, an \(N\)-person histogram has only \(\prod_t {N_t+|R_t|-1\choose |R_t|-1}\) possible values, rather than \(m^N\) ordered report profiles. This suggests that the exponential dependence on population size may give way to dependence on the dimension of the type-profile simplices. In the literal continuous profile space, however, unrestricted \(P\) can induce an arbitrary posterior function \(f_P^*\). I expect this version to be hard for continuum-specific statistical reasons: without assumptions such as finite profile support or smoothness of \(f_P^*\), there is no finite distribution-free sample bound. That is a meaningful boundary question, and it asks whether a regularity assumption produces useful rates depending on the simplex dimension.

A second, more tractable mirror is Conditionally Independent Continuum Aggregation. It uses the same type masses and report profiles, with signals independent conditional on the event. For a finite population of size \(N\), write \(\nu\) for its normalized measure of reports. If \(p=P(\omega=1)\), the paper’s conditional-independence characterization gives the aggregator

\[ F_{p,N}(\nu) = \sigma\!\left( \operatorname{logit}(p) + N\int\bigl(\operatorname{logit}(r)-\operatorname{logit}(p)\bigr)\,d\nu(t,r) \right), \]

where \(\sigma\) is the logistic function. For an empirical report measure, this is the finite-expert posterior expressed through the population profile. The problem is to learn an \(\epsilon\)-optimal predictor for such profiles from IID profile-outcome samples, uniformly over the conditional signal laws. A natural question is whether the sample bound remains independent of \(N\) as the profile becomes continuous.

Its anchor is Theorem 5.2, also proved in the paper. For conditionally independent expert signals and \(n\geq2\), the sample complexity is at most \(O(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/(\epsilon\delta)))\) and at least \(\Omega(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\), with no dependence on \(n\). The theorem’s proof uses a constant-pseudo-dimension aggregator family. That gives a credible tractability expectation for the mirror: the continuum profile enters through an aggregate log-odds statistic, while the learned family remains low-dimensional. This is a statistical expectation for the stated mirror, not a runtime theorem in the paper. Further questions include whether the bound survives the \(N\to\infty\) limit, how type-specific restrictions change it, and whether the lower bound persists when a continuum profile nearly identifies the event.

These are plausible extensions of the authors’ problem because they retain the binary event, truthful posterior reports, Bayesian benchmark, squared loss, and learning from historical report-outcome samples. A forecasting platform with millions of forecasters and relatively few recurring information pipelines is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The mirror covers the general-distribution and conditional-independence sample-complexity results; it does not purport to mirror every extension in the paper.

The weak point is that the paper’s \(n\) counts experts observed on one task, whereas continuization replaces an ordered report vector with a type-level mass profile. The histogram is sufficient only under anonymity within types; name-dependent aggregation would not survive that change. Also, an exact continuum profile can make state inference degenerate when conditional report distributions differ. The authors might therefore recognize the conditional-independence version as a natural limit of their model while treating the unrestricted histogram problem as a new statistical question. That still makes a defensible mirror, with the strongest positive case in the conditional-independence regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s model changes the input in a consequential way. The paper gives the principal a sample of a fixed joint law on an *ordered* report vector and the outcome. The proposed mirror replaces that vector with a histogram \(q\), then lets the histogram range over a continuous simplex. That quotient is valid only when reports from experts of the same type are interchangeable in the full joint law. For the arbitrary distributions in Theorem 4.1, correlations can depend on which expert produced which report. Preserve those distinctions by refining the types, and the number of types can grow with the population, undoing the proposed high-multiplicity gain. Impose interchangeability, and you remove the asymmetric correlation structure that drives the theorem’s lower bound.

The stronger histogram version still faces a fork. With finite \(N\) and finite report alphabets, \(q\) lies on a finite lattice: the problem is posterior learning over anonymous count profiles. That is coherent, but its domain size grows with \(N\). In the exact continuous-profile version, unrestricted \(P\) can place arbitrary mass on an uncountable set of profiles and give them arbitrary posterior values. There is then no distribution-free sample guarantee without assumptions such as finite support or smoothness. Those assumptions lead toward ordinary regression on a simplex; they supply the learnability structure, rather than the high-multiplicity relaxation. So Theorem 4.1 motivates the question but does not establish a useful continuous complexity landscape.

Theorem 5.2 has a sharper obstruction. Under its conditional-independence premise, suppose each type has a fixed report law \(\lambda_{t,\omega}\) in state \(\omega\). In an exact continuum, the observed report measure for each type is \(\lambda_{t,\omega}\). If the type-wise measures differ between the two states, that profile identifies \(\omega\), so \(f^*(q)=\omega\) and prediction is perfect. If they are the same, the profile carries no state information and the optimal prediction is just the prior. The finite-population aggregation problem therefore collapses in the exact limit.

A more careful limit could scale each expert’s information down as \(N\) grows, preserving finite aggregate evidence. But the limiting aggregator then depends on a scalar aggregate log-odds statistic and an unknown prior. That is a coherent statistical limit; it does not recover a new high-multiplicity algorithmic challenge from Theorem 5.2. If instead common latent shocks make the continuum profile random within a state, the problem may remain nontrivial, but it is now learning a posterior over random measures. With no restrictions, it returns to unrestricted regression; with a finite-dimensional latent model, that model supplies the tractability.

There is also a scope concern: these theorems bound the number of samples needed, not runtime or the complexity of a social-choice decision problem. If ChoCo includes statistical learnability, that is not a disqualifier by itself, but it makes the case for this paper depend on the extra modeling choices above.

I would reject these two anchors as strong evidence for ChoCo’s programme. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists: an exchangeable model with a well-motivated common-shock structure, or a carefully scaled weak-signal limit, could be worth studying. But neither is established by the proposed formulation, and the paper’s theorems do not by themselves make those extensions compelling.
