The continuous mirror question

Given a finite defeasible theory \(T\), a finite literal universe \(U\), and rational choice types \(C_1,\ldots,C_\tau\subseteq U\) with masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), determine whether there is a proposal \(P\subseteq U\) and a superiority relation under which every literal in \(P\) is defeasibly derivable, such that \(\sum_{i:C_i\subseteq P}\mu_i>1/2\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over complete choice types, each type being a consistent set of literals with a fixed compatibility judgment. The decision variable is a rational proposal \(P\), witnessed by a superiority relation; the objective is to exceed majority mass under weak compatibility.

What the mirror covers

The selected mirror covers Theorem 4's WPDP existence and majority result. Theorem 1 also has a separate population-threshold extension; Theorems 2–3 and Proposition 1 are not addressed by these mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a valid reduction establish NP-hardness of discrete WPDP, supporting the predicted Class B transfer through finite-profile masses?
  • How does exact or parameterized complexity depend on support size \(\tau\), literal-universe size \(|U|\), and input bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Winning Proposal Definition Problem in a large membership organization with a shared policy background: for example, a consultation with many members, a finite agenda, and recurring bundles of positions. Let a type be a consistent set \(C\) of literals in the paper’s sense. Members of the same type make the same compatibility judgment about any proposal. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) observed types, with binary rational masses summing to \(1\); \(\mu_C\) is the fraction of members of type \(C\). A population of millions with a few hundred recurring types is plausible here. The agenda and proposals stay discrete: the continuous object is the population.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that WPDP is NP-hard for the majority Social Choice Function: given the background defeasible theory and agents’ choices, determine a rational proposal that satisfies majority. Its continuous mirror is Mass-Majority Rational Proposal. An instance consists of a finite defeasible theory \(T\), a finite literal universe \(U\), a distribution \(\mu\) over rational choice types \(C_1,\ldots,C_\tau\subseteq U\), and a compatibility mode. Ask whether there is a rational proposal \(P\subseteq U\) that receives more than half the population’s mass. For the weak-compatibility version, that mass is
\[ \sum_{i:C_i\subseteq P}\mu_i. \]
A yes solution is \(P\), together with a superiority relation under which every literal in \(P\) is defeasibly derivable. This asks the same underlying question as WPDP—whether some rational proposal wins—while replacing voter counts with population fractions. The paper’s setup already has common background rules, proposals, compatibility, and majority; I expect its authors would recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime.

I expect hardness to transfer if Theorem 4’s lower bound is valid: a listed finite profile becomes the distribution assigning each choice its frequency divided by the number of voters. This would be Class B, with the important qualification that the theorem does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is bounded or otherwise small. The mirror also raises natural follow-ups: does the answer depend on strong versus weak compatibility, and can the maximum support mass be computed or approximated when no proposal wins?

A second, narrower mirror uses Theorem 1, also proved in this paper, which states that the Rationality Detection Problem is NP-complete. RDP asks, given \(T\) and one choice \(C\), whether some revision of the superiority relation makes every literal in \(C\) defeasibly provable. Its population version, Rationality Coverage, takes \(T\), a distribution over consistent choice types, and a rational threshold \(q\); it asks whether at least mass \(q\) has individually rational choices. Each type is assessed under its own permitted superiority-relation revision, as in the paper’s individual rationality definition. At support size one and \(q=1\), this is exactly RDP. I therefore expect the hardness to persist even with one type: it is inherited from the original reasoning problem, not caused by population multiplicity. This is a useful population-level question—what share of a membership’s stated positions can be rationalized against its common background—but it is a less compelling mirror than proposal search because aggregation adds little to the underlying computation.

The main weakness is in the anchors’ proofs as printed. Theorem 4’s argument that there are exponentially many proposals but each can be checked in polynomial time does not establish NP-hardness; that observation is closer to an upper-bound argument than a hardness reduction. Theorem 1 invokes the NP-complete Preference Revision Problem of [11], but the stated reduction direction alone does not establish NP-hardness unless the singleton-choice equivalence is made explicit. So the continuous questions are well-motivated mirrors of the paper’s models, but the predicted hardness transfer should be treated as conditional on repairing or supplying those reductions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to the lead anchor is that Theorem 4’s proof does not establish NP-hardness. It argues that there are exponentially many proposals and that a proposed one can be checked in polynomial time; that supports an exponential search procedure, not a hardness reduction. So the paper supplies no established hardness result there to transfer.

That does not defeat the underlying mirror. The stronger version is to ask for
\[ \max_{P\ \text{rational}}\ \sum_{i:C_i\subseteq P}\mu_i \]
under weak compatibility, and whether that maximum exceeds \(1/2\). This makes the population mass consequential while keeping the proposal and logical background of the paper. A large consultation with recurring policy bundles is a plausible setting, and a finite electorate maps directly to rational masses. Adding proposer eligibility or other relevant member attributes to the type would preserve that interpretation. The model is well-posed and looks recognizable to the authors; the weakness of Theorem 4 limits what can be claimed about its complexity, but does not make the question unworthwhile.

The Theorem 1 mirror is thinner. Its coverage version tests each supported choice independently and sums the masses of the rational ones. At support size one it is exactly RDP; with more types, the population enters only in that final weighted sum. It is a reasonable organizational audit, but the continuous population creates no coupling in the reasoning or collective decision. That makes it a weak fit for the programme’s most compelling computational questions, though not a false or incoherent mirror.

I can’t honestly defeat every anchor. Theorem 4’s stated hardness is unsupported, and RDP coverage is mostly a weighted batch of individual tests. But the mass-majority proposal problem survives those objections as a plausible, author-recognizable continuous question. The negative case is therefore weak precisely where it needs to be strongest: there is a worthwhile WPDP mirror even if the paper has not proved the complexity claim used to motivate it.
