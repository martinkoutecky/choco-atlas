The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: hedonic coalition formation has a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but this paper supplies no named computational-complexity result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. It proves bounds on stability and welfare; it does not classify a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar. The integer program in Section 3 was used to discover bounds and find examples, but it is not a named computational result. So I would not present any theorem here as a qualifying anchor.

The most promising regime is a large community or workforce where many people share one of a small number of exact, symmetric compatibility profiles. A type is an agent’s complete profile of pairwise utilities; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with type \(t\). This is plausible when the population is large and the number of distinct profiles is much smaller. It also preserves the paper’s central object: a partition into coalitions, with agents evaluating a coalition through their pairwise utilities. It does not make the authors’ particular motivating story decisive; the test is whether they recognize the typed, high-multiplicity coalition-formation problem as theirs.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper, but it is a structural theorem rather than a computational one. It says that in any symmetric \(\alpha\)-hedonic game, if a coalition structure is \(k\)-improvement core stable against coalitions of sizes \(1,\ldots,q\), then for every \(m\ge q+1\) it is \((m,f(q,m))\)-core stable, where
\[ f(q,m)=\max\!\left(1,\, k\left( \left\lfloor\frac{m-1}{q-1}\right\rfloor\frac{\alpha_m}{\alpha_q} +\mathbf{1}_{(m-1)\nmid(q-1)} \frac{\alpha_m}{\alpha_{(m-1)\bmod(q-1)+1}} \right)\right). \]
For the narrower ASHG case, Corollary 6, also proved here, gives the bound \(k\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{m-2}{q-1}\right\rfloor\right)\). Corollary 3 proves that in fractional hedonic games every \(q\)-size core-stable coalition structure is \(q/(q-1)\)-improvement stable. These are the closest results to mirror, but none is an eligible computational anchor under the stated criterion.

As an exploratory continuous analogue of Corollary 6—not a result anchored by the paper—I would ask the following. In a symmetric additively separable hedonic game, represent an agent’s current coalition assignment by a source stratum \(a\), with mass \(\mu_a\), underlying utility type \(t(a)\), and current utility \(b_a\). Let \(w_{tt'}=w_{t't}\) be the pairwise utility matrix. For a proposed deviating coalition, let \(y_a\in[0,\mu_a]\) be the mass drawn from each stratum. Its utility for agents in stratum \(a\) is
\[ U_a(y)=\sum_{a'} y_{a'}w_{t(a),t(a')}. \]
Given rational thresholds \(\rho,\sigma,\gamma\), ask whether there is a vector \(y\) with \(\sum_a y_a=\sigma\) such that \(U_a(y)>\gamma b_a\) for every stratum with \(y_a>0\). Such a vector is a certificate; “no” means no \(\sigma\)-mass deviation improves every participating agent by that factor. Define \(\rho\)-size stability by the absence of such a deviation for every mass at most \(\rho\). Scaling Corollary 6 with \(q/n\to\rho\) and \(m/n\to\sigma\) suggests the bound \(k(1+\sigma/\rho)\), though that continuous statement is not proved in the paper.

For a fixed number of source strata, the deviation question can be checked by enumerating possible supports and solving linear feasibility problems. That suggests fixed-parameter tractability in the number of strata; whether it admits an efficient algorithm when that number grows, or whether worst-case bound computation is hard, is open in this proposed mirror. It would be a new computational question motivated by the paper’s theorem, not a complexity result the authors already establish.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s \(q\) and \(m\) count agents, while the continuous model replaces them with mass thresholds and allows types to split across coalitions. In fractional hedonic games, arbitrary divisibility may make small-mass stability collapse toward full stability, so the ASHG version is the stronger candidate. Thus the paper supports a credible typed-population setting and a precise follow-on question, but it is not itself a strong computational anchor for continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The first problem is the anchor. Theorem 1, Corollary 6, and Corollary 3 prove bounds relating stability notions; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The integer program in Section 3 generates examples to help discover those bounds. It is not an algorithm for a problem the paper studies. Under ChoCo’s stated criteria, these results do not supply a computational anchor.

Corollary 3 also has a particularly weak continuous mirror. In a natural continuum version of fractional hedonic games, a type’s utility in a deviating coalition of masses \(y\) is
\[ U_t(y)=\frac{\sum_{t'} y_{t'}w_{tt'}}{\sum_{t'}y_{t'}}. \]
Scaling every mass by the same positive factor leaves every utility unchanged. So any blocking coalition can be scaled down to any positive mass cap \(\rho\), while preserving its improvement factor. Stability against all deviations of mass at most \(\rho\) therefore collapses to stability against deviations of any mass. The \(q\)-size relaxation that Corollary 3 relates to improvement stability has lost its scale parameter.

Corollary 6 has a more credible rescue. In a large workforce with finitely many roles and role-based symmetric interaction weights, normalize utilities by population size and represent a deviating coalition by a mass vector. The ASHG bound then has a sensible large-population limit: for \(q/n\to\rho\) and \(m/n\to\sigma\), its factor approaches \(k(1+\lfloor\sigma/\rho\rfloor)\) away from integer boundaries. That is a coherent continuous *structural* question. But it still translates a theorem about bounds, rather than a computational result. The proposed blocker test is a new problem: for each chosen support of participating strata, it is linear feasibility, giving a generic \(2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(L)\) support-enumeration algorithm. A complexity study beyond that could be worthwhile, but the paper neither asks that question nor provides a result to mirror.

The high-multiplicity regime itself is plausible for finite-role ASHGs, so I would not reject it on the ground that agents are inherently unique. The weakness is that the proposed data also include each stratum’s current utility. Those strata depend on the coalition structure; representing arbitrary outcomes may require many distinct baseline utilities, or even a continuum of them. A finite-support blocker instance handles a supplied, compressed outcome, while a continuous version of the paper’s universal bound would range over all such outcomes. Making that range finite is an additional modelling choice.

So the strongest negative verdict is that this paper is a poor ChoCo target: its named results are not computational, and its clearest fractional mirror degenerates. The ASHG mass model is the serious exception. It is mathematically sensible and might motivate a separate computational project, which is why the broader claim that *no* scenario could yield worthwhile work is weaker than the case against this paper as a direct mirror.
