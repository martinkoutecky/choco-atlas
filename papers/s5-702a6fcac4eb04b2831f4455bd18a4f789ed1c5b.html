The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous-population version of the Borda consensus target in Theorem 26, proved in this paper. The theorem considers a connected undirected graph \(G=([m],E)\) of agents, each starting with a Borda vector \(b_i(0)\). At each step, an edge \(\{i,i'\}\) is selected with probability \(P_{ii'}>0\), and its endpoints both replace their vectors by their average. Write \(\bar b=\frac1m\sum_i b_i(0)\), order the objects so \(\bar b^1\le\cdots\le\bar b^n\), and let \(T\) be the first time every agent’s vector has that same ordering. The theorem defines \(r^j=\min\{\bar b^{j+1}-\bar b^j,\bar b^j-\bar b^{j-1}\}\) and \(d^j=\max_i b_i^j(0)-\min_i b_i^j(0)\). With \(W=\sum_{\{i,i'\}\in E}P_{ii'}\bigl(I-\frac12(e_i-e_{i'})(e_i-e_{i'})^\top\bigr)\), it proves \(P(T>t)\le 4m\lambda_2(W)^t\sum_{j=1}^n(d^j/r^j)^2\), where \(\lambda_2(W)\) is the second-largest eigenvalue. The proof is in this paper, using a contraction bound from Boyd et al. The printed definition of \(r^j\) needs endpoint conventions for \(j=1,n\), and the bound presupposes positive score gaps.

This is an algorithmic convergence result, not a conventional P/NP or parameterized-complexity classification. I find no named hardness or complexity-class result in the paper to anchor a stronger claim. The reason to use Theorem 26 is that it explicitly identifies the target as the ordering of the society’s average Borda vector.

Continuous Borda Consensus is the corresponding static population problem. An instance consists of \(n\) objects and a support of \(\tau\) complete rankings \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_\tau\), each with a nonnegative binary-rational mass \(\mu_j\), with \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\). A type is one complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the population holding it. Define \(B_\mu(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\sigma_j^{-1}(a)\). The decision variable is an output ranking \(\pi\); one formulation is to minimize \(\sum_a B_\mu(a)\pi^{-1}(a)\). Its minimizers put objects in increasing order of \(B_\mu\), exactly the paper’s Borda aggregation rule; equal scores may be tied or ordered arbitrarily. The task is to return such a ranking.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large election or platform with many users, standardized complete ballots, and far fewer distinct rankings than users. The same preference ranking makes agents indistinguishable for this aggregation problem. Given the support representation, compute the \(n\) weighted averages and sort them using exact rational arithmetic. The problem is tractable in time polynomial in \(n\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. A discrete election represented by its ballot proportions has exactly the same Borda aggregate, while the continuous input also permits arbitrary rational proportions without one-voter granularity.

The authors should recognize this as the continuous form of the aggregate ranking they call the society’s consensus: their theorem itself defines success by agreement with the ordering of \(\bar b\). This case covers that target computation, not every result in the paper. It does not claim that the theorem’s random gossip process or its tail bound carries over.

That is also the weakest point. The distinctive contribution of Theorem 26 is the network-dependent convergence-time bound, whereas this mirror removes the communication dynamics and solves the target directly. A fuller continuization of that contribution would need a type-level network model and a continuous interaction process, then ask for consensus-time or probability bounds in that model. I would treat that as a further question, not pretend it is already answered by the static mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror does not reproduce Theorem 26. That theorem bounds the stopping time of a particular randomized, networked computation: \(T\) is the first round when every vertex has the correct ordering. Its bound depends on the edge process through \(\lambda_2(W)\), and on node-level initial conditions through the spreads \(d^j\). The proposed input, \(\mu\), determines only the population’s average Borda vector. Two societies with the same \(\mu\) can have different graphs and edge probabilities, and therefore different consensus times. Sorting the weighted scores computes the theorem’s target centrally; it does not mirror the theorem’s result.

A stronger attempt would give the population an interaction structure, perhaps a finite-type graphon with a kernel specifying how often each type meets each other type, then ask for the time until almost all mass shares the aggregate Borda ordering. That is a coherent research question, but the move from finite gossip to a continuum changes the object being studied. A finite edge update changes two vertices; in an atomless population, an individual pair has zero mass. A nontrivial limit therefore needs a kinetic or mean-field evolution. Under ordinary random interactions, positive mass can remain unupdated at any finite time, so “every agent has the correct ordering” may become infinite consensus time. Replacing it with an \(\varepsilon\)-fraction error target avoids that degeneracy, but it is a new target, and the paper’s spectral bound on every vertex does not settle it.

There is no other computational-complexity result here for the static Borda aggregate to anchor. The paper defines that aggregate by averaging scores and sorting them; the proposed weighted version is a natural high-multiplicity input to the same calculation, but not a continuous mirror of the network theorem. A type-level network model might still be worth studying as mean-field gossip with a ranking-based stopping criterion. I cannot rule that out. But it requires supplying the central interaction model and, likely, changing the stopping criterion; the case against this paper as a worthwhile continuous-computation mirror is strongest on that mismatch, and weaker against the broader mean-field question.
