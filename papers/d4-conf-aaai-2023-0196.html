The continuous mirror question

Given a rational mass vector \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete agent types, a finite anonymous mechanism with rational piecewise-polynomial aggregate transition and outcome rules, type utilities, and target per-type lotteries \(F_t\), does there exist a memoryless strategy kernel \(x_t(v)\in\Delta(A)\) that is a Bayesian best response for every positive-mass type, induces \(F_t\), and gives every such type nonnegative interim utility? A unilateral deviation has zero effect on the aggregate action masses \(q_{t,a}(v)=\mu_t x_t(v,a)\).

The model it lives in

Use types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), finite states \(v\), and finite actions \(A\). The decision variables are the type-state strategy probabilities \(x_t(v)\); these determine aggregate action masses \(q_{t,a}(v)\), which feed the mechanism's transition and individual outcome-lottery rules. Decide feasibility of Bayesian best-response, target-lottery, and interim-IR constraints.

The objection that survived

A unilateral deviation changes a named player's effect on the finite mechanism, while an atomless deviation leaves aggregate masses fixed; the paper provides no finite-population convergence argument connecting those equilibrium concepts.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers equilibrium verification from Theorem 2 and the implementation and interim-IR checks of Theorem 4, including outcome matching; it does not address the paper's expected-utility identity or all of its mechanism examples.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the feasibility problem become \(\exists\mathbb{R}\)-hard when aggregate rules are rational piecewise-polynomial?
  • Do affine aggregate rules admit a tractable algorithm?
  • How closely do atomless answers approximate equilibria and implementation in finite populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible mirror is an anonymous, atomless Bayesian mechanism: a setting where the population is continuous but the action set, mechanism states, and individual outcomes remain finite. The distributions in the paper are distributions over the private types of a finite set of named agents; they do not make the society itself continuous. The mirror would replace that finite agent set with a mass distribution over complete behavioral types.

A plausible regime is a large platform allocating standardized service slots to users. There may be millions of users but only a small number \(\tau\) of types, each fixing everything relevant to the mechanism: valuation, eligibility, available actions, and outside option. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each person still receives an individual lottery over finite allocation-and-payment alternatives; the aggregate share receiving each outcome can be fractional. That scaling of capacity and outcomes is part of making this population mirror coherent.

The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or otherwise. Its most useful computational anchors are named correctness results that reduce mechanism questions to model checking. The paper’s reported \(3\)-EXPSPACE bound for PSL model checking comes from Aminof et al. (2019); it is a result about that finite-system backend, not a complexity classification proved here for a continuous-population problem.

My first anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for a system \(G\) representing a mechanism \(M\), a strategy profile \(\sigma\), strategy variables \(s\), and an assignment \(A\) with \(A(s_a)=\sigma_a\) for every agent \(a\), the PSL formula \(E(s)\) holds at the initial state exactly when \(\sigma\) is an \(E\)-equilibrium of \(M\), for each \(E\in\{\mathrm{BRE}^{e.a.},\mathrm{BNE},\mathrm{NE}\}\). These notions respectively test best response ex ante, interim given an agent’s type, and ex post for a type profile.

Its continuous counterpart is Atomless Equilibrium Verification. An instance gives a rational mass vector \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types, a finite-state anonymous stochastic mechanism, and a proposed memoryless strategy \(x_t(v)\in\Delta(A)\) for each type \(t\) and state \(v\). The mechanism’s transition and terminal-outcome rules depend on the aggregate action masses \(q_{t,a}(v)=\mu_t x_t(v,a)\); a person’s own action may affect that person’s outcome, while a unilateral deviation has zero effect on the aggregate masses. The question is whether \(x\) is a Bayesian best-response equilibrium: every type’s prescribed strategy maximizes its expected utility against the induced population behavior. A solution is a yes-or-no answer.

I expect this fixed-strategy verification problem to be tractable for an explicit finite-state mechanism. Once \(x\) is given, the aggregate masses are determined, and each type’s best-response calculation is a finite stochastic decision problem. This is a useful algorithmic mirror of Theorem 2, though it is a specialized continuous model rather than a complexity result from the paper.

The stronger anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here. It says that if \(G\) represents \(M\), then an SCF \(f\) that is \(E\)-implemented by \(M\) is interim individually rational exactly when the model-checking condition holds: there exists a strategy profile \(s\) such that \(E(s)\) holds, the mechanism’s terminal outcomes match \(f\), and every type’s interim expected utility is nonnegative. In the paper, outcome matching is expressed by \(\phi_{f,s}\), and individual rationality by the conjunction of \(IR(s,\theta)\) over type profiles \(\theta\).

Its continuous counterpart is Atomless IR Implementation at Mass \(\mu\). An instance gives \(\mu\), a finite anonymous stochastic mechanism, a target mass-level SCF \(F\) specifying each type’s per-capita outcome lottery, and a zero outside option. The question is whether there exists a memoryless type strategy \(x\) that is a Bayesian equilibrium, induces the target lotteries at \(\mu\), and gives every positive-mass type nonnegative interim utility. A witness is the strategy kernel \(x\). This is a pointwise version of the paper’s implementation-and-IR condition; extending it to a specified domain of mass profiles would be a natural next question.

For that existence problem, I would expect continuum-specific hardness in a broad input model where aggregate transition and outcome rules are rational piecewise-polynomial functions of the mass variables. The equilibrium strategy determines aggregate masses, while best-response, outcome-matching, and IR conditions constrain that same strategy. Their joint feasibility is naturally a semialgebraic problem and may be \(\exists\mathbb{R}\)-hard. That is a conjectured direction for the mirror, not a result of this paper. Restricted affine mechanisms could still admit tractable cases.

The match to the paper is strongest for Bayesian equilibrium and economic-property verification in anonymous, scalable mechanisms. It does not cover every mechanism in the paper, especially mechanisms whose behavior depends on a particular individual’s pivotal effect. That is the main weakness: in a continuum, one agent has zero mass, so finite-auction incentives that depend on changing the population total or winning a single indivisible item may disappear. The case survives for large service or allocation markets where agents have meaningful individual outcomes but negligible aggregate influence. Further questions include the exact complexity under affine rules, whether equilibrium witnesses can be chosen rational, and how closely the atomless answers approximate finite populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that Theorem 2 and Theorem 4 are correctness results for an encoding, not complexity results about a computational problem. Theorem 2 says that PSL’s equilibrium formula has the intended meaning for a finite mechanism; Theorem 4 adds implementation and interim IR to that encoding. The \(3\)-EXPSPACE bound is inherited from prior work on PSL model checking. These results do not classify the complexity of equilibrium verification or implementation as the mechanism’s population grows. A continuous version could pose a new complexity question, but it would not mirror a complexity result established by this paper.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 2 also changes what is being verified. In the paper, a unilateral deviation changes one named agent’s action in the joint action profile, and the mechanism may use that change to alter both transitions and that agent’s outcome. In the atomless proposal, a deviation leaves the aggregate action masses unchanged. That is a coherent price-taking or Wardrop-style model, but it replaces finite-player strategic influence with negligible individual influence. The paper specifies no anonymity, aggregate transition rule, or sequence of mechanisms whose limit would select that model. Its \(d(\theta)\) is a prior over the possible types of named agents, not an empirical mass vector. One could build a sequence of anonymous games and study its limit, but choosing the sequence, scaling, and convergence assumptions would supply the substance of a new model.

Theorem 4 has the same gap. Its SCF maps each complete type profile of named agents to a lottery over outcomes that can specify a particular winner and each agent’s payment. The proposed mass-level SCF records per-type outcome lotteries at a given \(\mu\). That quotient is justified only under added symmetry and aggregation assumptions; the paper does not impose them. A stronger version could specify target lotteries for every mass vector and require a scalable anonymous mechanism to implement them. But then the chosen language for aggregate rules would determine the computational problem. For example, \(\exists\mathbb{R}\)-hardness obtained by allowing arbitrary piecewise-polynomial rules would principally be a result about that new input language, not a consequence of Theorem 4.

This does not show that atomless mechanism verification is uninteresting. It could be a worthwhile project for authors who want to study anonymous large-market mechanisms. The weak point in my negative case is precisely that they might recognize it as their problem. But the two anchors do not establish that connection: they establish that finite-agent economic properties can be expressed in PSL. On the evidence in this paper, the proposed atomless questions are new formal-verification problems inspired by its subject, rather than continuous mirrors of its results.
