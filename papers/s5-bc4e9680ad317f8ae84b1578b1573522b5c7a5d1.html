What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror: Theorem 6 is axiomatic, and the independence examples and concluding open question are also noncomputational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s committee interpretation: a large committee’s majority preferences form the tournament whose uncovered set supplies the bargaining solution. A natural population model would give each committee member a complete ranking type over a finite set \(A\) of bargaining alternatives, with rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each type. Assuming every pair has a strict majority comparison, those masses induce a tournament \(T_\mu\), and the problem could be to compute \(\mathrm{UC}(T_\mu|_A)\). This continuizes the committee population while leaving the bargaining alternatives discrete, and the committee scenario is one the authors themselves invoke.

But that is a proposed computational question, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. Theorem 6, proved here, says that a resolute bargaining solution is rationalized by a Pareto-consistent tournament exactly when it satisfies axioms 1–4. It is an axiomatic characterization; it gives no algorithm or complexity result. The paper contains no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifiable by complexity. So there are no qualifying anchors, and I can’t honestly assign this proposed question a continuization class on the paper’s evidence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive weakness is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 6 is an axiomatic characterization: it says when a bargaining solution is rationalized by a Pareto-consistent tournament. The independence examples and concluding open question are axiomatic too. None specifies a finite input, a computational task, or an algorithmic result. The \(\mathbb R^n\) in the model is the space of utility alternatives for \(n\) bargaining parties; it is not a continuous population. Continuizing that space would change outcomes, which is outside ChoCo’s scope.

The committee interpretation is the strongest candidate. A distribution over committee members’ ranking types could induce a majority tournament, whose uncovered set one could compute. That is a coherent continuous-population question, and it may be worth studying in its own right. But it changes what the paper asks: Theorem 6 concerns whether a solution correspondence is rationalized by *some* tournament across an admissible domain of bargaining problems. It does not model committee members, their rankings, or how they generate the tournament. Computing the uncovered set of one electorate-induced tournament on one menu does not test the theorem’s characterization. A closer extension would ask which solution correspondences have rationalizers generated by such populations, but making that a computational problem would require new finite representations for the correspondence, the domain, and committee preferences.

So the proposed committee model does not survive as a computational mirror of a named result in this paper; it supplies a new population-aggregation question around the paper’s motivation. This negative case is strongest under ChoCo’s stated requirement for a computational anchor. Its honest weakness is also clear: because the authors explicitly invoke committees, they might recognize that extension as a natural follow-on, even though it is not a continuization of a computational result they establish.
