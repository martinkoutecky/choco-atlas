The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of additive value types \(T\), rational population masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\) for indivisible items \(j\), compute a direct mechanism maximizing truthful per-capita revenue. Each type-\(t\) agent receives an integral bundle and pays a transfer; the mechanism must be individually rational and Bayesian incentive compatible, and its aggregate allocation must respect supply for every reported population composition. At the truthful composition, if \(q_{tj}\) is the expected allocation of item \(j\) to type \(t\) and \(p_t\) its expected payment, the objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t q_{tj}\le\rho_j\) and \(\langle v_t,q_t\rangle-p_t\ge\langle v_t,q_s\rangle-p_s\) for all \(t,s\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic market with a finite set of complete additive value types \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\), and indivisible goods supplied at rates \(\rho_j\) per agent. The mechanism assigns lotteries over integral bundles and payments; type-level expected allocations \(q_{tj}\) may be fractional, satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_tq_{tj}\le\rho_j\), and obey BIC and individual rationality. Maximize per-capita revenue \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\).

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8’s optimal BIC revenue-design result in a finite-type, per-capita-supply regime. It leaves the hierarchical-mechanism and reduced-form characterizations, the symmetry-specific results, and the general correlated-bidder and demand-constraint characterization untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can finite populations with independent draws from \(\mu\) and supplies scaled with population size approximate the continuum optimum while preserving BIC and feasibility, with a quantitative error bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper using its reduced-form feasibility algorithms together with the LP framework of [14]. It says that, given \(m\) independent additive bidders and their finite-support value distributions over \(n\) items, one can compute an exactly revenue-optimal BIC mechanism in time polynomial in \(n\), \(m\), and the largest support size. Values may be arbitrarily correlated across items for each bidder. The paper has no named hardness result, so there is no hardness anchor to prefer.

A continuous counterpart I find credible is Continuum BIC Revenue Design. Picture a large digital-services marketplace: many customers face the same catalog of service classes, and the number of licenses or service slots grows in proportion to the customer base. A type \(t\) is a customer’s complete vector \(v_t\) of values for those classes; customers with the same vector are interchangeable. If there are \(N\) customers, type \(t\) has mass about \(N\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is \(N\gg\tau\): perhaps millions of customers but only hundreds of distinct value types. The supply of class \(j\) is \(N\rho_j\).

Precisely, an instance gives a finite set \(T\) of types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational additive values \(v_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^k\), and per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\). Find a revenue-maximizing direct mechanism. At the truthful population composition, let \(q_{tj}\) be the expected quantity of class \(j\) given to a type-\(t\) customer, and \(p_t\) the expected payment. The mechanism must be individually rational and BIC:
\[ \langle v_t,q_t\rangle-p_t\ge \langle v_t,q_s\rangle-p_s \quad\text{for all }t,s, \qquad \langle v_t,q_t\rangle-p_t\ge0. \]
It must also respect aggregate supply, \(\sum_t\mu_t q_{tj}\le\rho_j\) for every \(j\). Its objective is to maximize per-capita revenue, \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\). Each person still receives an integral bundle; fractional \(q_{tj}\) means that a fraction of a type’s mass receives each bundle. A solution is the mechanism and allocation rule realizing those type-level outcomes, with a feasible rationing rule for other reported population compositions.

I expect this problem to be tractable, Class A. With finite types and additive values, the \(q_{tj}\) and \(p_t\) form a polynomial-size LP: the incentive constraints compare type pairs, and aggregate capacities replace finite-profile feasibility. Its input size depends on \(k,\tau\), and the bit length of the rational data, not on \(N\). This mirrors Theorem 8’s underlying question—optimal BIC revenue design—rather than treating the paper’s LP encoding as the problem. The paper’s prior \(F_i\) is uncertainty about each member of a finite bidder roster; here \(\mu\) is the composition of the society itself. That is the continuization.

The co-modification is explicit: supply scales with population. This is natural for a large market that expands its licenses or service capacity with its customer base, and it is within the programme’s scope. A customer’s type contains every preference parameter the mechanism uses; type-level fractional averages do not mean anyone receives a fractional license.

The mirror covers Theorem 8’s optimal BIC revenue result in this finite-type, per-capita-supply regime. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s hierarchical-mechanism characterizations or its general result for correlated bidders and demand constraints. Further questions include whether one can give a quantitative finite-\(N\) bridge that preserves BIC while rounding type masses and supplies, and which extensions—such as correlated population composition or hard demand constraints—still admit efficient feasibility or pricing algorithms.

My weakest point is the finite-to-continuum bridge. The paper’s finite mechanism must be feasible at every bidder profile, and a single bidder can affect a scarce allocation. In the continuum, a single customer has zero mass; BIC deviations leave the aggregate composition unchanged, while off-equilibrium rationing handles other compositions. That is a standard large-market limit, but it discards finite-population pivotality, and the paper’s fixed item supply does not itself establish the per-capita-supply model. The continuous problem remains a strong analogue of its BIC revenue-design question, but proving a tight discrete-to-continuous dictionary is a separate result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 8, and the strongest objection is that the proposed mirror removes the paper’s central feasibility problem. The theorem handles independent bidders with random types and needs a separation oracle to decide whether interim allocation probabilities can come from a feasible mechanism. In the proposed continuum model, the type masses are fixed. For each item, feasibility is just \(0\le q_{tj}\le1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_tq_{tj}\le\rho_j\); with additive bidders and no demand constraints, the type masses can be split to realize any such quantities. The remaining constraints are the familiar pairwise BIC and IR inequalities. The continuum model therefore asks a simpler screening question, without the reduced-form implementability problem that drives the paper’s result.

A more faithful bridge would study finite populations with random type counts and supplies growing with population, then prove convergence to the proposed LP. That could be valuable. But the paper’s theorem does not establish that dictionary, and the limit removes finite-population fluctuations and each bidder’s effect on allocation. The paper also permits continuous value distributions while keeping a finite bidder roster; that addresses continuity of types, not of the population. Continuum-agent revenue design is established in adjacent economic work—for example, Richter studies revenue-maximizing mechanisms for a population with a continuum of agents and a finite supply of a divisible good—but that work does not solve this paper’s multi-item computational problem, so it is not a novelty collision. [Richter, “Mechanism design with budget constraints and a population of agents”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2019.02.009)

This is a limited case against the mirror, not a decisive one. A deterministic type-mass market is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the BIC revenue objective remains recognizable. Per-capita supply is an allowed co-modification, and a simple LP is still a legitimate tractable result. My concern is that the proposal sidesteps the paper’s distinctive algorithmic obstacle; it does not show that the resulting question is unworthy. Under the programme’s gate, Theorem 8 may well survive as a mirror.
