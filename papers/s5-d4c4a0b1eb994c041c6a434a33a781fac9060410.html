The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support society of campaign-instance types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), token distributions \(p_\theta\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})\), and fixed bids \(b_\theta\) for all but a focal type \(\theta^\star\) of positive mass \(\alpha\), let the focal cohort choose a common bid \(x\), with \(B_- = \sum_{\theta\ne\theta^\star}\mu_\theta b_\theta>0\) and \(q_x(t)=\frac{\alpha x p_{\theta^\star}(t)+\sum_{\theta\ne\theta^\star}\mu_\theta b_\theta p_\theta(t)}{\alpha x+B_-}\). Construct a sampler that outputs distribution \(q_x\) for every \(x\), and for each fixed random seed changes at most once as \(x\) rises, from a token with \(q_0(t)>p_{\theta^\star}(t)\) to one with \(q_0(u)<p_{\theta^\star}(u)\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete advertiser-model and campaign-template descriptions; \(\mu_\theta\) is their share of campaign instances. The focal cohort’s action is a common bid \(x\); the decision object is a coupling between its baseline and preferred token distributions, with thresholds that implement \(q_x\). For this finite rational linear-mixture family, construct the coupling and sampler in time polynomial in the support size, token alphabet size, and input bit length.

The objection that survived

The cohort’s common bid is controlled by a portfolio manager, while the paper treats each advertiser as the strategic bidder; pooling placements also changes who bids on the shared output.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.12’s stable-sampling result for a focal campaign cohort under linear aggregation. It leaves the broader incentive results, second-price payments, KL and RL aggregation design, and demonstrations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the sampler construction be extended efficiently to succinctly represented monotone aggregation rules beyond the linear mixture?
  • How does the cohort-level model relate to finite markets where campaign instances bid separately?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive anchor is Theorem 3.12, proved here in Appendix B.2. It says: given a monotone distribution aggregation function \(q\), any agent \(i\) with robust preferences, and fixed bids and preferred distributions for the other agents, there exists a stable implementation of \(q\). “Stable” means that for each fixed random seed, raising \(i\)’s bid can move the sampled token at most once, from an oversampled token to an undersampled one, at a critical bid. This is a constructive structural result. The paper does not give a named complexity classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness, so this anchor supports a sampler-synthesis question, not a claim that the paper has established an algorithmic complexity result.

Here is a continuous version I think the authors could recognize. Consider an ad exchange with many repeated campaign instances, managed through a small set of shared LLM models and campaign templates. A type \(\theta\) is the complete advertiser model and preference description; at a fixed prefix \(s\), its preferred next-token distribution is \(p_\theta=f_\theta(s)\). The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\). Mass is the fraction of active campaign instances of each type. The intended regime is many instances, \(N\), but relatively few exact types, \(\tau\), because the same models and campaign templates are reused.

Suppose a campaign manager controls the common per-instance bid \(x\) for a positive-mass cohort \(\theta^\star\), of mass \(\alpha\); other types use fixed bids \(b_\theta\). For the paper’s linear aggregation rule, the next-token distribution is

\[ q_x(t)= \frac{\alpha x\,p_{\theta^\star}(t) +\sum_{\theta\ne\theta^\star}\mu_\theta b_\theta p_\theta(t)} {\alpha x+\sum_{\theta\ne\theta^\star}\mu_\theta b_\theta}. \]

The cohort’s action is its bid \(x\). This is a feasibility problem, not an optimization problem: construct a sampler whose output has distribution \(q_x\) at every bid and whose behavior for each fixed random seed makes only the permitted one-way transition. If \(q_0\) is the distribution induced by the other cohorts’ bid-weighted contributions, then \(q_x\) is a mixture of \(q_0\) and \(p_{\theta^\star}\), with the weight on \(p_{\theta^\star}\) increasing with \(x\). Thus each token probability moves monotonically toward the focal cohort’s preferred distribution. A finite transport plan can pair excess mass on oversampled tokens with deficient mass on undersampled tokens; a uniform random threshold on each pair gives the required stable switch. The expected direction is tractable (Class A) for this linear family: the plan can be built from the token probabilities using a finite transportation construction. Extending an efficient construction to broader, succinctly represented monotone aggregation rules is a further question.

This fits a plausible high-multiplicity regime when platforms run very many placements from a modest collection of repeated campaign templates and a portfolio manager sets a shared bid for each cohort. The model keeps the paper’s token-by-token decision, LLM-expressed preferences, bid, and aggregated distribution. It changes the population object to a distribution over complete advertiser types; it does not count the already-continuous token outcome as continuization. The mirror covers Theorem 3.12’s stable-sampling result, not the paper’s full auction design, training discussion, or demonstrations.

The weak point is the unit of strategic agency. In the paper, one bidder changes one bid; here a positive-mass campaign cohort changes its common bid. That is plausible for centrally managed portfolios, but it does not preserve unilateral incentives for an individual in an atomless population: that individual’s effect on the aggregate vanishes. The mirror should therefore be presented as a cohort-controlled version of the authors’ problem, not as a general continuum of independently bidding advertisers. Further questions include whether stable sampling remains efficiently constructible for richer monotone aggregation rules, how the construction composes across the full autoregressive text-generation process, and how the cohort model relates to finite markets where individual accounts can deviate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.12 is the only serious anchor, but it establishes existence of a stable implementation, not a complexity result. Its proof constructs a sampler from the whole bid-indexed curve \(q(b_i)\), using generalized inverses and derivatives. The paper gives no finite representation of an arbitrary \(q\), oracle model, or running-time bound. A question about efficiently constructing samplers can be posed, but it needs additional choices about how \(q\) is encoded before it becomes a computational problem.

The proposed population model also loses the theorem’s strategic unit. If advertisers bid independently in an atomless society, one advertiser has measure zero. Changing that advertiser’s bid leaves the aggregate distribution unchanged, so payment monotonicity forces their payment to stay unchanged too. The stable sampler can then be constant in that bid, with no meaningful threshold transition. A positive-mass cohort avoids this degeneracy only by giving a portfolio manager control over the cohort’s common bid. That manager acts as a single macro-bidder; the cohort’s instances are not individually exercising the strategic choice studied in the paper.

That change is hard to motivate as a mirror of the paper’s auction. If repeated placements have separate auctions, their number does not enter the token distribution for any one placement. If a platform pools them into one shared output, cohort size weights influence on that output by construction. That may define an interesting quantity auction, but it changes who bids and what the auction allocates. The campaign-template story makes the change plausible enough to investigate; it does not establish that the resulting model is the high-multiplicity version of Theorem 3.12.

Even granting that pooled model, the linear rule reduces the cohort’s role to the scalar \(\lambda=\alpha x/(\alpha x+B_-)\). Stable sampling is then a coupling between two finite token distributions, \(q_0\) and \(p_{\theta^\star}\), with a finite transport plan. Its computational input is the token alphabet and the distributions, not the population size or multiplicity; the type masses contribute only to computing the mixture weights. For richer monotone rules, the theorem supplies no finite representation to work from. Neither branch yields a complexity landscape driven by continuizing the society.

So the negative case is strongest at the boundary of the programme: this paper provides no finite-input computational result for Theorem 3.12 to mirror, and the proposed salvage either makes individual bids powerless or replaces advertisers with strategic cohort controllers. A weighted pooled-ad auction could still be worth studying in its own right. The material here does not show that it would be a worthwhile continuous-population mirror of this paper.
