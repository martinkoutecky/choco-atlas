The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive point is that the paper makes a high multiplicity setting unusually easy to believe: many agents share the same type, defined by their endowment and utility function, and a type’s mass is its share of the economy. Allocations assign bundles, subject to aggregate resource feasibility; strict fairness asks whether agents envy the average bundle of any admissible coalition. That is a coherent continuous population model, and the authors would recognize it as their problem.

But it is already the paper’s model. The paper studies atomless and mixed economies directly, rather than a discrete problem whose continuous population version remains to be formulated. More decisively, it contains no named computational result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or similar. Proposition 4.2, proved here, says that in atomless economies an agent who envies a coalition can envy a subcoalition of any specified smaller positive measure via the same redistribution; under its separability condition, the coalition can also be reduced to at most \(\ell\) small, small-diameter subgroups. That is useful structure, but it is not an algorithmic classification or a computation performed with a formulation.

So there is no qualifying anchor, and I cannot honestly state a continuous computational problem per anchor. A computational study of deciding strict fairness in a finite-type economy might be a sensible new question, but it would be a computational extension of this paper’s already-continuous model—not a mirror of one of its named computational results. The central weakness is therefore not the plausibility of high multiplicity; it is the absence of the required computational anchor and of discrete hardness for continuization to resolve.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that high multiplicity is natural here, but that supports the model’s plausibility, not a continuous mirror. The paper already makes the population continuous: it studies atomless and mixed exchange economies, groups agents by their endowment and utility, and defines strict fairness through envy of coalition-average bundles. There is no discrete computational result in the paper for a continuous version to mirror.

Proposition 4.2 is the closest candidate, but it is a structural theorem about an already atomless economy. It says that envy of a coalition can persist in a subcoalition of any specified smaller positive measure and, with a separability condition, in a coalition made from at most \(\ell\) small, small-diameter pieces. It gives no encoded input, computational task, algorithm, or complexity bound. Its setting allows arbitrary measurable allocations and coalitions, and its proof uses Lyapunov’s convexity theorem. Treating it as a computational anchor would change the question, not continuize the result.

The best salvage would be to restrict to finitely many types, specify finite representations for utilities and allocations, and ask for the complexity of deciding strict fairness or finding an envious coalition. That could be a legitimate new computational project. But the finite representative economy in Section 4.2 is constructed from an economy that is already continuous; it supplies no discrete computational result to lift. Any algorithmic question would need its own formulation and motivation.

So the negative case is strong for “no worthwhile *mirror* under this programme’s anchor requirement.” The broader claim that no such computational follow-on could be worthwhile in any scenario is weaker: the paper’s natural type structure may make one promising. What the paper cannot support is calling that new question a mirror of one of its results.
