The continuous mirror question

Given an integer population scale \(M\), \(N\ge M\) indivisible options, and a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete preference types \(t\), with rational preference vectors \(q_t\in\Delta_N\), find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{ti}\) minimizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\sum_{i=1}^N (x_{ti}/\mu_t-q_{ti})^2\), subject to \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\), \(x_{ti}\ge 0\), and \(\sum_t x_{ti}\le 1/M\) for each option \(i\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population has type masses \(\mu_t\); each type shares a preference vector \(q_t\), and each indivisible option has normalized capacity \(1/M\). The decision variables \(x_{ti}\) are type-to-option masses, the objective is the population-weighted squared marginal loss, and each person receives one whole option.

The objection that survived

The options must grow with the population, and Theorem 2.3 proves only overload infeasibility, not the proposed type-level optimization’s exact minimum loss.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror carries Theorem 2.3’s general-player overload obstruction into a type-mass assignment and extends the paper’s minimum-loss objective to that setting. It does not carry over Theorem 2.2’s two-player closed-form optimum, establish general zero-loss sufficiency, or resolve Conjecture 2.1.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact minimum be computed by a specialized flow method or convex optimization algorithm, with running time in \(M\), \(N\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length?
  • How does the model change when types have different eligibility constraints or options have additional capacities?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, W[1]-hard, or FPT. Its useful anchors are structural feasibility and optimization results instead. The case for a mirror is therefore that these results extend naturally to high-multiplicity allocation models—not that the paper has found a discrete hardness that disappears.

My lead is Theorem 2.3, proved in this paper. It says that for \(M>2\) players and \(N\) arms, if any arm has popularity \(S_i^\dagger=\sum_x X_i^\dagger>1\), then zero loss is impossible. The theorem gives a necessary overload condition; it does not prove the converse.

A direct mirror is Type-Continuous Conflict-Free Assignment MinLoss. Take a large population scale \(M\), \(N\ge M\) individual seats or options, and a finite set of complete preference types \(T\). A type \(t\) has population share \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational preference vector \(q_t\in\Delta_N\). Each seat has capacity \(1/M\) in normalized population mass. The decision variable \(x_{ti}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to seat \(i\). Find a feasible \(x\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t:\mu_t>0}\mu_t\sum_{i=1}^N\left(\frac{x_{ti}}{\mu_t}-q_{ti}\right)^2, \]
subject to \(x_{ti}\ge0\), \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{ti}\le 1/M\). The solution is the mass matrix \(x\), or a randomized conflict-free allocation realizing its type marginals. Each person still receives one whole seat; fractional values describe the share of a type assigned to each seat.

This fits a large placement round—housing, hiring, or another assignment market—with many applicants, \(M\) comparable to \(N\), and relatively few preference types. A type must include the full probability vector and any other feature the allocation uses. The continuum is the population distribution \(\mu\), not the paper’s already stochastic outcome matrix. In a finite \(M\)-person instance, the desired load on seat \(i\) is \(M\sum_t\mu_tq_{ti}\), exactly the popularity appearing in Theorem 2.3. Thus, if that load exceeds one, the mirror cannot achieve zero loss. In this complete-eligibility assignment model, the overload test is also sufficient for zero loss: setting \(x_{ti}=\mu_tq_{ti}\) meets the constraints when every seat’s load is within capacity. The paper proves the necessary direction for general \(M\); its own Conjecture 2.1 anticipates the corresponding sufficiency statement for the finite model.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: it is a rational convex quadratic program with \(\tau N\) variables. It is a Class A question, not a hardness-transfer claim. It also raises useful follow-ups: how the optimization changes with type-specific eligibility, whether the minimum can be computed by a more specialized flow method, and how the answer changes under KL loss. Its weakest point is that the continuous model makes the per-capita seat supply explicit, and so it is a high-multiplicity assignment formulation of the paper’s allocation problem rather than a model the authors already study.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 2.2, also proved here. For two players and \(N\) arms, it says that if any popularity \(S_i=A_i+B_i\) exceeds \(1\), zero loss is impossible. If arm \(N\) is most popular with \(S_N>1\), then \(L_{\min}=\frac{N}{2(N-1)}(S_N-1)^2\); one optimum has \(p_{i,N}=A_i+\epsilon\) and \(p_{N,j}=B_j+\epsilon\) for \(i,j<N\), all other off-diagonal entries zero, and \(\epsilon=\frac{S_N-1}{2(N-1)}\).

Its mirror is Type-Continuous Dyad Overload Optimization. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) over types of two-player sessions, where type \(t\) consists of the two players’ preference vectors \(a^t,b^t\). Require each type’s most popular arm \(k_t\) to have \(S_{t,k_t}=a^t_{k_t}+b^t_{k_t}>1\). Choose, for each type, a conditional joint lottery \(p^t_{ij}\ge0\) over ordered pairs of distinct arms, with \(\sum_{i\ne j}p^t_{ij}=1\), minimizing the population-weighted squared marginal loss. The output is the family of lotteries \(p^t\). Theorem 2.2 gives its exact optimum: the aggregate minimum is \(\sum_t\mu_t\frac{N}{2(N-1)}(S_{t,k_t}-1)^2\), with the stated matrix construction applied within each type. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for many repeated two-device channel-allocation sessions, with many sessions but few distinct preference-pair types. It is plainly tractable, and its main follow-up is what happens when sessions share a limited pool of arms.

The shared assignment market is the stronger continuous-population case; the dyad result is the closer closed-form optimization anchor. The main weakness overall is that neither anchor supplies a complexity boundary: the proposed mirrors are convex and tractable, and the paper’s results are feasibility and optimization statements rather than hardness results. The case survives that limitation as a plausible population mirror of conflict-free preference satisfaction, but it does not establish that continuization dissolves any hardness in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 2.1–2.3 establish feasibility and impossibility conditions for a finite joint lottery, and Theorem 2.2 gives a closed-form minimum loss. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The closest anchor, Theorem 2.2, is a formula for one finite two-player instance, not an algorithmic or complexity result. A new optimization problem can be built from these results, but that makes it a proposed extension of the paper, not a mirror of its computational contribution.

Theorem 2.3’s assignment-market version is the strongest case for a mirror, and I would not reject it on the grounds that type-level masses are fractional. If agents of a type share the same complete preference vector, their aggregate assignments can represent their individual marginals, and per-capita supply is an admissible co-modification. The strain is that a fixed set of \(N\) arms permits at most \(N\) players in a conflict-free assignment, so multiplicity requires supply to grow with the population. Then the model is a sequence of markets with growing arm sets and preference vectors, rather than a fixed finite-arm problem over a continuous society. That scaling is allowed, but it makes the central question a capacitated assignment problem introduced by the mirror. The theorem’s overload condition is a capacity inequality; the proposed minimum-loss computation comes from the new assignment model, not from a computational problem studied in the paper.

Theorem 2.2’s dyad mirror has the opposite problem. If each session has its own arms, the sessions do not compete with one another. The population-weighted objective separates by type, so the continuous instance just aggregates copies of the finite two-player calculation. If sessions instead share arms, their choices become coupled and Theorem 2.2’s per-dyad formula no longer supplies the result; the model has become a joint assignment problem like the first proposal.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight. A large placement round with many applicants, few preference types, and supply that scales with population is plausible, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as a useful extension. My best case against is narrower: that scenario motivates a new high-multiplicity assignment study, while neither of the paper’s anchors supplies the computational result this programme is meant to continuize. The dyad anchor adds little once aggregated; the assignment anchor remains plausible if the programme counts new optimization models as worthwhile mirrors.
