The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes the need-adjusted geometric rules by axioms; Theorem 2 characterizes three particular rules; and Theorem 3 says that lowest-rank consistency, superior independence, and canonical symmetric fairness characterize the need-adjusted serial rule, whose awards are
\[ \phi_i^s(M,r,z)=z_i+\sum_{j\le i}\frac{r_j-z_j}{n-j+1}. \]
These are named results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper contains no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror.

The most credible positive direction, therefore, is only a prospective mirror of the serial rule, not a mirror of a computational result. Consider a large organization with a continuum of positions \(s\in[0,1]\), ordered from lowest to highest rank, and piecewise-constant per-capita revenue \(r(s)\) and need \(z(s)\), with \(r(s)\ge z(s)\). Put \(q(s)=r(s)-z(s)\). A natural continuum limit of the paper’s serial rule is
\[ a(s)=z(s)+\int_0^s\frac{q(u)}{1-u}\,du. \]
For a finite partition into rank bands, one could ask for each band’s average award, to a specified additive error. The formula is the limit of the discrete serial awards, and its aggregate awards balance total revenue. I would expect this evaluation problem to be tractable for rational step-function inputs, though that is an inference, not a result of the paper. It could also prompt questions about convergence rates and about which axioms characterize this continuum rule.

The regime would be a large workforce spread across a small number of stable responsibility bands, with many people per band sharing per-capita revenue and needs. The main weakness is that the serial rule depends on exact rank: people within a band need not receive the same award. Treating their band average as the type-level outcome aggregates away that distinction; retaining exact rank instead gives a continuum of types. And even if the authors recognize the formula as a natural limit of their rule, the paper itself studies axiomatic characterizations, not the computational question above. So this is the strongest plausible positive lead I see, but it does not meet the paper’s required computational-anchor test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the ChoCo gate: this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes the need-adjusted geometric rules; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize particular rules. The later propositions also establish axiomatic characterizations. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a decision or optimization problem. The paper’s contribution is about which allocation rules satisfy which principles.

The serial rule is the proponent’s best candidate. Its continuum formula is a natural limit: for \(s<1\), \(a(s)=z(s)+\int_0^s q(u)/(1-u)\,du\). A stronger version would keep exact rank as a continuous type and compute the whole allocation profile, rather than average outcomes across rank bands. That avoids losing rank-specific awards. The formula can also diverge at the very top when surplus stays positive, but that singularity is integrable, so it is not a convincing objection by itself.

Even in that stronger version, the paper supplies no computational search problem. For piecewise-constant inputs, the profile reduces to prefix integrals, and band averages to elementary integrations. Asking about exact encodings or approximation precision could yield a complexity question, but those choices would define a new computational task; they would not mirror a result in this paper. The paper has no allocation objective or competing feasible decisions that might make continuous optimization technology necessary.

There is a modelling cost as well. In the paper’s linear hierarchy, rank affects the award, so agents at different positions are distinct types. Grouping a rank band into one type and reporting its average changes the outcome being asked about. Retaining a continuum of exact ranks avoids that loss, but yields a continuum of distinct types rather than high multiplicity over a small support. A many-per-rank interpretation would require extending the paper’s one-agent-per-position hierarchy.

A continuous axiomatic characterization could still be a legitimate project, and the paper may motivate one. That falls outside ChoCo’s computational remit. Someone could add a computational problem around the model, but it would be an addition to the paper rather than a continuous mirror of its results. That is the strongest negative case; it rests on relevance to the programme, not on proving that no useful extension can ever be invented.
