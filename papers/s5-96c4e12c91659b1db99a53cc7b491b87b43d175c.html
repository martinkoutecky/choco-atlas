The continuous mirror question

Given a network \(G\) with rational edge lengths and a rational finite-support society \( \mu \) over location types \(t\in G\), compute a facility location \(Y\sim\mu\) and compare \( \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}[\int d(t,Y)\,d\mu(t)] \) with \( \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu)=\min_y\int d(t,y)\,d\mu(t) \), under finite-mass strategyproof deviations; determine whether the resulting \(2\)-approximation can be improved.

The model it lives in

Types are locations \(t\in G\), masses are \( \mu_t \), the decision is a facility-location distribution \(P\), the anchored rule is \(P=\mu\), and the objective is expected social cost \( \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}[\int d(t,Y)\,d\mu(t)] \).

The objection that survived

The proposed finite-mass incentive condition is coalition-like rather than the paper's unilateral condition, and the broader optimization over all rules lacks a finite representation; these limit the computational scope of the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1 and its random-dictator social-cost guarantee; it leaves Theorem 4.2, the circle-specific hybrid mechanism, GSP characterizations, and conjectures untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is \(2\) the optimal worst-case social-cost ratio for finite-mass-strategyproof anonymous rules on arbitrary network metrics?
  • Can finite-mass-strategyproof distributional mechanisms be finitely represented so that rule optimization admits a separation or synthesis formulation?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s facility-location problem on the same network. The network is already continuous in the paper; what becomes continuous is the population.

A type is an ideal location \(t\in G\). For a finite set of occupied locations \(T\), the society is a distribution \(\mu\) with mass \(\mu_t\) at each \(t\in T\). Mass means the fraction of a very large population living at that location or attached to that network node. The mechanism receives reported mass and returns either a facility location or, for a randomized mechanism, a distribution \(P\) over facility locations.

The natural regime is a large city or telecommunications network with many interchangeable users at a relatively small number of hubs, neighbourhoods, offices, or routers: \(n\gg \tau=|T|\). The recursive trees in the paper can be read as hierarchical service networks with large cohorts at repeated locations. This is not a claim that every facility-location instance is high-multiplicity; it is a plausible instance regime for exactly the public-facility and network-user interpretations given by the authors.

There is one modelling issue that has to be handled explicitly. Ordinary strategyproofness becomes vacuous for a genuinely nonatomic population: one individual has zero mass and cannot change \(\mu\). I would therefore use the following nondegenerate high-multiplicity notion. A randomized rule \(F\) is finite-mass strategyproof if, whenever a mass \(\varepsilon>0\) of type \(t\) changes its report to \(t'\), with \(\mu'=\mu-\varepsilon\delta_t+\varepsilon\delta_{t'}\), the deviating agents do not improve according to their true location:

\( \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F(\mu')}[d(t,Y)]\ge \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F(\mu)}[d(t,Y)]. \)

This is the distributional form of the paper’s individual deviation condition, with a block of interchangeable agents standing in for one representative agent. It is deliberately stronger than ordinary atomless strategyproofness, but it is exactly what prevents the continuous mirror from becoming strategically empty.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper in Appendix C. Its content is:

For every \(n\), there exists a tree network \(G\) such that no randomized strategyproof mechanism has approximation ratio smaller than \(2-O\!\left(1/2^{\sqrt{\log n}}\right)\) for the maximum cost.

The maximum cost of a facility location \(y\) is the largest distance from \(y\) to any agent’s ideal location, and the randomized mechanism’s cost is the expected maximum cost. This is a lower-bound theorem about the approximation frontier, not an NP-hardness theorem.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

“Continuous Tree Maximum-Cost Mechanism Design.” An instance consists of a finite tree \(G\) with rational edge lengths and a finitely supported rational population distribution \(\mu\) over \(G\). For a randomized rule \(F\), define \( \operatorname{mc}(F(\mu),\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F(\mu)}[\max_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}d(t,Y)] \), and \( \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mc}}(\mu)=\min_{y\in G}\max_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}d(t,y) \). Find a finite-mass strategyproof rule \(F\) minimizing its worst-case ratio \( \sup_\mu \operatorname{mc}(F(\mu),\mu)/\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mc}}(\mu) \).

I expect this continuous problem to have optimal ratio exactly \(2\). The upper bound is immediate from the continuous random-dictator rule \(F_{\mathrm{RD}}(\mu)=\mu\): sample the facility location from the population distribution. If \(y^\star\) is an optimal center of radius \(R\), then every two support points satisfy \(d(x,y)\le d(x,y^\star)+d(y^\star,y)\le 2R\), so \(F_{\mathrm{RD}}\) is a \(2\)-approximation. It is also finite-mass strategyproof, because moving mass \(\varepsilon\) from \(t\) to \(t'\) changes the true-\(t\) expected cost by exactly \(\varepsilon d(t,t')\).

The lower bound from Theorem 4.2 should survive, and become cleaner. In the paper’s recursive tree construction, the successive profiles can be replaced by mass distributions: \(1/2\) at each endpoint initially, then masses \(1/(2m)\), \(1/(2m^2)\), and so on down selected branches. No leaf needs to contain an integral agent. The Appendix C induction then gives a ratio at least \(2-1/2^k-4/m\). Choosing \(m\) and \(k\) large gives a ratio arbitrarily close to \(2\). Thus the obstruction is not population multiplicity; it lives in the recursive tree geometry and the incentive constraint. In the programme’s trichotomy, this is a tractable Class A mirror with an exact approximation boundary, not a continuum-specific failure.

The second anchor is Theorem 3.1, also proved here, in Section 3.1. It states:

Let \(G\) be any graph and \(N=\{1,\ldots,n\}\). Mechanism 1, which chooses an agent uniformly at random and returns that agent’s reported location, is strategyproof and is a \((2-2/n)\)-approximation mechanism for social cost.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

“Continuous Social-Cost Facility Location.” Given a finite metric network \(G\) and a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\), output a randomized facility rule \(F\) that is finite-mass strategyproof and whose expected social cost \( \operatorname{sc}(F(\mu),\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F(\mu)}[\int d(x,Y)\,d\mu(x)] \) is within a specified factor of \( \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu)=\min_y\int d(x,y)\,d\mu(x) \).

The continuous random-dictator rule again solves this problem. Its social cost is \( \iint d(x,y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y) \). For an optimal location \(y^\star\), the triangle inequality gives \( \iint d(x,y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\le 2\int d(x,y^\star)\,d\mu(x) \), hence a \(2\)-approximation. For an empirical distribution of \(n\) equally weighted agents, excluding the diagonal terms recovers the paper’s sharper factor \(2-2/n\). In the continuum, the finite-\(n\) self-selection correction disappears. This is a direct high-multiplicity limit of the named theorem, with an explicit tractable solution.

This second mirror should also be recognised by the authors: it preserves their network, reports, randomization, strategyproofness, and social-cost objective. It changes only the profile representation from \(x\in G^n\) to a distribution \(\mu\). It does not claim novelty for continuous locations or randomization; the new question is computational and structural analysis of the distributional population model.

The scope is intentionally limited. I am mirroring Theorem 4.2 as the main negative approximation anchor and Theorem 3.1 as a positive algorithmic anchor. I am not claiming that the paper’s circle-specific hybrid mechanism, its GSP results, or its conjectures automatically extend to distributions; those deserve separate questions about how finite-mass deviations and coalitions should be defined.

The weakest point is the incentive model. If the referee insists on ordinary unilateral strategyproofness for a truly nonatomic population, every rule is strategically immune because one report has zero effect on \(\mu\); then the optimal center gives an exact but strategically vacuous solution. If the referee rejects finite-mass strategyproofness as too strong, the mirror loses its most interesting content. My reason for still defending it is that the paper’s own lower-bound proof is built from repeated movements of interchangeable agents, and those movements translate directly into mass transfers. The finite-mass formulation therefore preserves the paper’s strategic question while making the high-multiplicity limit mathematically meaningful.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is categorical: this paper does not contain the kind of computational result ChoCo is meant to continuize. Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.2 are distortion and impossibility bounds for globally defined strategyproof rules. They do not give a complexity classification, an instance-level optimization problem, or an algorithm whose running time depends on a finite representation of the population. The paper itself stresses that the underlying facility-location optimizations are tractable; approximation is used to obtain truthfulness, not to overcome computational hardness.

This matters especially for the proposed formulation

\[ \text{“find a finite-mass strategyproof rule }F\text{ minimizing its worst-case ratio.”} \]

That is not a computational problem on a continuous society. \(F\) is an entire function over all possible populations, and the strategyproofness constraints quantify over all mass transfers. No representation of \(F\), input size, verification procedure, or finite separation problem is supplied. One could make this into a new mechanism-synthesis programme, but that would be a new problem rather than a continuization of a named computational result in the paper.

Theorem 4.2 does not repair this. Its recursive proof can indeed be rewritten with masses \(1/2,1/(2m),1/(2m^2),\ldots\). But for maximum cost the masses are irrelevant:

\[ \operatorname{mc}(P,\mu) = \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ \sup_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}d(t,Y) \right]. \]

Changing the positive weights while preserving the support changes neither the objective nor the optimum. For an atomless distribution, the same issue appears with the essential supremum. Thus the proposed “continuous” lower bound is really a support-geometry theorem with fractional notation. The population has not become a computationally meaningful continuous object. Making mass matter would require replacing maximum cost by a quantile, an expectation, or another risk functional, which would no longer mirror Theorem 4.2.

The incentive repair also changes the problem fundamentally. Ordinary unilateral strategyproofness for a rule \(F(\mu)\) is vacuous: one individual has zero mass and cannot change \(\mu\). The proposed finite-mass condition instead treats an entire positive-mass block of identical agents as a deviating coalition. That is a group-strategyproofness-style condition, not the paper’s SP condition. If one instead preserves individual deviations by retaining labels or tagged atoms, then identity becomes part of the type. The types are no longer repeated in the high-multiplicity sense. There is no canonical limit preserving both a distribution-only society and the paper’s individual incentive constraint.

Theorem 3.1 is the better anchor, because social cost genuinely depends on the population weights and the random-dictator rule has a natural distributional form:

\[ F_{\mathrm{RD}}(\mu)=\mu. \]

A large population at a finite set of hubs is also a plausible scenario. But the resulting statement is only the measure-theoretic extension of the same triangle-inequality proof:

\[ \iint d(x,y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y) \le 2\min_z\int d(x,z)\,d\mu(x). \]

The finite-\(n\) correction \(2-2/n\) disappears because self-selection disappears. That is a legitimate observation, but not a computational question of the ChoCo kind. If one fixes random dictatorship, evaluation is merely integration and sampling. If one asks for the optimal strategyproof rule, one returns to the unrepresented global optimization over functions \(F\). Restricting to anonymous rules makes the problem distributional but changes the mechanism class; retaining identity-dependent rules destroys the high-multiplicity compression.

So the proponent is right about one limited point: the recursive lower-bound proof can be transported to fractional populations, and a high-multiplicity public-facility story is not implausible. That is evidence for a mathematical analogue. It is not evidence for a worthwhile continuous computational mirror. Every route that preserves the paper’s target loses the mass information; every route that preserves individual strategyproofness loses the population compression; and every route that makes rule design computationally explicit introduces a new mechanism-synthesis problem absent from the paper.
