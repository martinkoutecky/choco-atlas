The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive obstacle: the paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem to mirror. Proposition 1 compares the immediate cost of the next trade in INT and PAR, given identical market-maker states and order books. Its inequality holds trade by trade, so averaging over a distribution of trader types adds nothing; the population distribution does no work in the result.

The strongest alternative would be to model a large market with many traders sharing a few posterior-value types, then ask about expected cumulative loss or price discovery. But that is a new stochastic market-dynamics problem. To preserve the paper’s mechanism, it needs the evolving order books, order quantities, and arrival priority as well as trader types. The distribution of types alone does not describe that state. A fluid or mean-field treatment might be worthwhile, but it would be a separate analytic model, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper. And despite its title, “continuous” here describes the auction’s operation over time, not a continuous population.

So no anchor survives: the one numbered proposition is not a computational-complexity result, and the simulations report finite-horizon outcomes rather than an algorithmic problem. The honest weakness in this negative case is its universal scope: someone could formulate a valuable computational question for a continuous-population market. But that would originate in a new model, not in a named computational result this paper supplies.
