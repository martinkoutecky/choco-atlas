The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named complexity result in this paper: no theorem classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or similar. Theorem 1, proved here, is the strongest operational anchor—it characterizes the choice rules that satisfy the paper’s cutoff-verifiability requirement. Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes mechanisms with incentive and verifiability properties, but does not give a complexity bound. Deferred acceptance is an algorithm used in the paper, but its running time is not a stated result. So there is no anchor that meets the requested complexity-result criterion. The best positive case is narrower: treat Theorem 1 as a structural anchor for a continuous *mass-allocation* problem, and be explicit that this is not a complexity claim from the paper.

The most plausible regime is a large city’s centralized high-school admissions cohort. A complete type would include a student’s middle-school category, school preference list, score or priority data at each school, and any relevant tie-breaking information. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_\theta\) the fraction of applicants of type \(\theta\). A target regime might have \(10^5\)–\(10^6\) students but only thousands of distinct complete types; the paper does not establish that those figures hold in practice. The mirror is useful only if the relevant preferences and priority data actually repeat enough to keep \(\tau\) well below \(N\).

My lead mirror is Continuum Verifiable School Choice at One School (CVSC). An instance gives a school’s total capacity \(q\), open-seat capacity \(q^o\), reserve capacities \(q^m\) for categories \(m\), and a rational-mass distribution of applicants over complete types. Each applicant type has a category and a strict priority rank at the school. All applicants in this local choice problem regard the school as acceptable. The decision variables are masses \(x_{\theta,r}\) and \(x_{\theta,o}\) assigned from each type to reserved and open seats. They must respect type masses and reserve capacities; unfilled reserve capacity may be used for open-seat assignments, as in the paper. The requested solution is the set of admitted masses and their seat labels satisfying the cutoff rules—not an allocation that maximizes a new welfare objective.

Theorem 1 says, in full: a choice rule is reserve-first verifiable if and only if it is exactly \(C_{\mathrm{SimRO}}\), which assigns reserved seats first to the highest-priority applicants in each category, then fills the remaining capacity with the highest-priority applicants overall. A choice rule is open-first verifiable if and only if it always selects the same applicants as \(C_{\mathrm{SimOR}}\), and for every applicant set there is a selected applicant \(j\) who receives an open seat under \(C_{\mathrm{SimOR}}\) such that the rule assigns reserved seats exactly to selected applicants ranked below \(j\). \(C_{\mathrm{SimOR}}\) itself uses the lowest-priority open-seat recipient as that threshold and therefore minimizes reserved-seat assignments among these open-first rules. The theorem is proved in this paper.

The continuous question is: given the applicant mass distribution and seat capacities, characterize all assignments satisfying the paper’s cutoff definitions and either verification protocol. Under reserve-first, is the only feasible mass choice the mass version of \(C_{\mathrm{SimRO}}\)? Under open-first, is the admitted mass set the one selected by \(C_{\mathrm{SimOR}}\), with all other valid seat labellings obtained by choosing a threshold \(j\) and labelling the selected applicants below it reserved? A solution is the resulting mass allocation—or a proof that none exists. I would expect this local problem to be tractable by priority sorting and mass cutoffs. A multi-school extension could ask whether mass deferred acceptance computes the corresponding society-wide assignment efficiently, parameterized by the number of schools and support size.

The mirror is plausible for the authors: their setting is already large-scale admissions, their central rule uses scores, categories, quotas, and disclosed cutoffs, and their own discussion builds on cutoff characterizations of stable matchings. Prior cutoff machinery would support this extension; it would not by itself establish that the paper’s reserve-seat verification characterization had already been studied for a continuous population.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s local choice-rule characterization. It does not cover Theorem 2’s strategy-proofness characterization or the paper’s analysis of sequential mechanisms. In an atomless population, an individual’s report has zero effect on the outcome, so individual strategy-proofness can become vacuous. A nontrivial continuous analogue of Theorem 2 would need a different incentive notion, which risks changing the authors’ question.

The weakest point is the type boundary. If type includes preferences, scores, and tie-breaking data, the number of distinct types may approach the number of students. And if a positive mass of indistinguishable applicants sits at a cutoff, splitting that mass across seat labels may prevent each person from verifying their own assignment from their score and the published cutoffs. A clean finite-support version can restrict to instances where cutoffs fall between complete types, or refine boundary types using the public tie-break rule; either choice needs stating as part of the model. That limitation leaves the local mass-choice question intact, but makes the paper’s strongest individual-level and strategic claims much harder to continuize faithfully.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The main problem is that Theorem 1 is not a computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. It classifies choice rules: reserve-first verifiability holds exactly for \(C_{\mathrm{SimRO}}\), while open-first verifiability restricts the chosen set and the possible seat labels. Theorem 2 is also a characterization; Proposition 1 describes how to verify sequential outcomes. None states a complexity bound or gives an algorithmic question whose complexity the paper investigates. The proposed CVSC acknowledges this, then supplies a new input-output problem: compute a mass assignment satisfying the verification rules. That may be a coherent extension, but it is not a continuous mirror of Theorem 1’s claim about which rules have a property. A more literal continuous version would classify choice correspondences over measure-valued applicant sets, which remains a structural characterization rather than the computational landscape ChoCo targets.

The type issue makes CVSC’s finite-support version especially difficult to ground in this paper’s model. In its proposed one-school instance, a type includes a strict priority rank. Under the paper’s strict priority order, each rank identifies one student, so preserving the information the choice rule uses gives \(\tau=N\). If applicants are instead grouped by category and test score, a quota can cut through a tied-score group. Members of that group have identical scores for the public cutoff tests, so a deterministic cutoff protocol cannot certify different seat labels for them. The possible repairs each change the setup: include tie-break information and lose the repeated types, allow a split that participants cannot verify from the stated information, or use an atomless score distribution. The last option is a plausible population model, but it needs a distribution or density input beyond the paper’s finite applicant set and the programme’s rational-mass support representation. Replacing Theorem 1’s boundary student \(j\) with a score quantile handles the notation; it does not supply the missing computational target.

The strongest case for the mirror is a large admissions cohort with repeated categories and score profiles. That makes population continuization plausible, and an atomless score model could preserve a meaningful cutoff interpretation. But the paper’s wider mechanism results also depend on full preference lists and priority data, which can make complete types close to unique. The paper establishes that these are large systems; it does not establish a high-multiplicity regime for the complete types its mechanisms use. The one-school CVSC avoids preferences, but in doing so narrows the object to a local choice problem and still has no computational result in the paper to mirror.

So the negative case is strongest on scope and representation: the named anchors are structural results, and the proposed computational problem must be added rather than carried over. I would not claim this proves there is no worthwhile continuous model of admissions. Large cohorts and score distributions make that broader claim hard to defend; a continuous-population study of admission cutoffs could be worthwhile. The narrower judgment is that this paper offers no computational anchor for such a study, and the specific CVSC proposal does not yet bridge that gap.
