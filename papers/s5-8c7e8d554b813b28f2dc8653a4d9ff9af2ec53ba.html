The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large annual school-admissions cohort in which many students share the same complete preference, priority, and socioeconomic profiles. Let each type \(t\) include its true school ranking, SES group, priority at each school, and a specified reporting rule under immediate acceptance (IA) and deferred acceptance (DA). Let \(\mu_t\) be its population share, and let each school’s capacity be a share of the cohort. Such a setting is credible for large districts with repeated profiles and categorical priorities, although the paper does not establish that its own data have small type support.

One possible follow-up problem is: given schools, per-capita capacities, rational type masses, school priorities, school-quality scores \(v_s\), and type-specific reporting rules under IA and DA, compute the mass assignments \(x^{IA}_{ts}\) and \(x^{DA}_{ts}\). Also compute the change in the low- versus high-SES gap in average assigned school quality,
\[ \Delta = (Q_L^{DA}-Q_H^{DA})-(Q_L^{IA}-Q_H^{IA}), \qquad Q_g^M=\frac{1}{\mu_g}\sum_{t:g(t)=g}\sum_s x^M_{ts}v_s. \]
This is recognizably about school assignment and captures an outcome central to the paper: Table 4 reports that the IA-to-DA transition reduced low-SES students’ school value-added relative to high-SES students. With reporting rules supplied, I would expect the mass-assignment computation to be tractable by type-level implementations of IA and DA. Endogenizing strategic reports would raise further questions about equilibrium and complexity.

But this is an unanchored research lead, not a mirror of a qualifying result in the paper. The supplied text contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered equations and its empirical findings—such as the estimates in Tables 4 and 5—do not meet the stated anchor rule. The paper’s contribution is estimating effects of a real reform; computing assignments from given reports would not reproduce that causal estimate. Its reported behavioral responses are part of the treatment effect, and the paper lacks the individual preference data needed to specify them in this proposed instance.

So the positive case is that a continuous population model of school assignment is plausible and close to the paper’s substantive setting. The weakest point is decisive for this exercise: there is no named computational result to anchor the prover question to, and the paper does not establish that the required complete student types are few.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The paper’s numbered equations are regression specifications, and its central findings—Table 4’s changes in student outcomes and Table 5’s changes in school composition—are empirical causal estimates. Neither states a complexity result or an algorithmic problem. A continuous rewrite of those estimates as population shares or integrals would preserve the empirical question, but it would not create the computational question this programme is meant to chart. The proponent identifies no qualifying anchor either.

Table 4 is the closest candidate, but the proposed mass-assignment calculation does not reproduce its result. The paper estimates the effect of a real IA-to-DA reform, including changes in parents’ reporting behavior. With reporting rules fixed as inputs, a type-level implementation computes assignments under two supplied regimes; it omits the behavioral response that is part of the paper’s total effect. Endogenizing that response would require a model of preferences, beliefs, sophistication, and learning. That could be a worthwhile school-choice project, but it would add a new mechanism-design question rather than mirror a computational result in this paper. The paper itself lacks the preference data needed to specify the proposed instance: it says it has no individual reported preferences, and under IA a reported first choice need not be a student’s true favorite.

Table 5 has the same mismatch. Its estimate concerns how enrollment changed at schools that had chosen FPF, relative to comparison schools, after the ban. Turning student counts into masses would restate those composition outcomes. Modeling why schools chose FPF, how parents responded, and where students enrolled would instead be a new strategic model; it would not compute the paper’s reduced-form causal estimate. The treatment variation is at the local-authority and school level, while the millions of students are observations within those units. Compressing repeated student profiles does not supply more treatment variation or resolve the identification problem.

The proponent’s stronger scenario—a large cohort with repeated preference and priority profiles—does make sense as a possible high-multiplicity school-assignment setting. Given reports, priorities, capacities, and tie-breaking, one could ask how to compute IA and DA assignments over type masses. But that asks how a mechanism processes a supplied profile. The paper asks what happened when the mechanism changed, including how families adapted. Those questions are distinct. Adding a richer type space or endogenous reporting does not close that gap; it changes the target into a theory of strategic response.

So the negative case rests on the mismatch between the paper’s contribution and the programme’s target, not on any claim that continuous populations are inappropriate for school admissions. A continuous school-assignment problem may be worth studying in its own right. On the material here, though, it would be a new computational social choice problem attached to the paper’s subject, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.
