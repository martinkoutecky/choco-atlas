What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue tracks Theorem 3’s Borda robustness statement; it leaves the paper’s mode-selection, Condorcet, plurality, and multimodal results untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor: no numbered result classifies a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. It does have named results about voting outcomes. The closest positive foothold is Theorem 3, proved in Section 5: if a finite profile has a \(\rho\)-tail-perturbed unimodal frequency distribution around \(R^\ast\), then the Borda rule’s outcome is \(R^\ast\). In full, the distribution’s frequencies are constant within each Kemeny-distance shell around \(R^\ast\), decline strictly with distance up to the radius, and stay below the radius-shell frequency in the tail; under those conditions, Borda ranks every candidate pair in the order given by \(R^\ast\). This is a structural outcome theorem, not a complexity result, so it cannot honestly serve as a formal anchor under the requested criterion.

The strongest continuous follow-on I can make from it is: Borda Outcome on a Tail-Perturbed Population. An instance gives \(m\ge3\) candidates, a supplied order \(R^\ast\), and an explicit list of ranking types \(R\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_R\) summing to \(1\). It promises that \(\mu\) satisfies Theorem 3’s \(\rho\)-tail conditions. The task is to return the Borda collective order; the solution is the weak order induced by the candidates’ aggregate Borda scores. Here a type is a complete ranking, and \(\mu_R\) is the population share holding that ranking. There is no separate optimization objective: the decision is the Borda outcome. The expected answer is tractability: Theorem 3 gives \(R^\ast\) directly; even without using the promise, aggregate Borda scores can be computed from the listed support.

A plausible regime is a very large electorate choosing among a small fixed slate, so many people share each possible ranking and the number of people greatly exceeds the number of ranking types. The authors would likely recognize this as a continuous version of their frequency-based Borda question: normalizing finite counts to masses preserves the score comparisons. This case covers only Theorem 3’s Borda robustness result; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s other results.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 3 studies finite profiles, and its radial symmetry conditions are restrictive. For fixed \(m\), the population can be large relative to the at most \(m!\) types, but the theorem’s shell conditions may still be an artificial fit to an actual electorate. And computing Borda’s outcome is already straightforward, so this mirror gives little of the computational complexity landscape the programme seeks. A more consequential question suggested by the theorem is the minimum mass transfer needed to change the Borda order, but that would be a new robustness problem, not a complexity result established by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this anchor is that Theorem 3 is not a computational result. It says which order Borda selects under a specified family of distributions; it gives no complexity classification or algorithm for an optimization problem. In the proposed mirror, \(R^\ast\) is supplied and the theorem’s conditions are promised, so the task is simply to return \(R^\ast\). Without that promise, computing Borda’s outcome from the listed ranking types is just an aggregate score tally. Neither task mirrors a computational result in the paper.

The population setting itself is convincing: the paper explicitly studies large electorates through frequencies of ranking types, and national elections are a sensible high-multiplicity regime. That is evidence for a continuous model, not against one. But the proposed rational masses do not add a computational question to Theorem 3. Scaling them by a common denominator gives integer frequencies, with the theorem’s equalities and strict inequalities preserved.

The strongest repair would be a quantitative robustness problem: how much mass must move before Borda’s outcome changes? Adding Kendall-cost transfers would make that a genuine continuous optimization problem, and perhaps a worthwhile one. But it supplies an intervention objective and cost model that Theorem 3 does not study. It is a new problem suggested by the paper’s robustness discussion, not a computational mirror of its result.

So the proponent’s stated anchor does not survive. The broader claim that *no* continuous mirror could be worthwhile is harder to defend: the paper’s frequency model makes that setting especially natural, and a robustness follow-on is plausible. The negative case is strong against this anchor, but weak as a universal argument.
