The continuous mirror question

Given a finite list of student types with rational masses, strict college rankings, priority tiers, and per-capita college quotas, does the mass analogue of irrelevance of low-tier agents imply that the profile produced by iterative mass-based DA is a P-SSN against deviations by measurable subpopulations, including fractional parts of a type? A passively weak deviation has positive mass of strict beneficiaries, while every coalition member weakly prefers the joint-deviation outcome to the outcome when only those beneficiaries deviate.

The model it lives in

Represent applicants by a finite support of types \(t\), each recording a true college ranking and priority tier, with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Scale college quotas per capita and let DA assign mass fractionally at priority cutoffs. Students report rankings; coalitions may move measurable submasses between reports. The target is the iterative-DA profile and its P-SSN status under the mass version of the paper's low-tier condition.

The objection that survived

Allowing measurable coalitions to split a type changes the deviation space, so the finite proof may not carry over to the mass model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2's conditional guarantee for the iterative-DA profile; it leaves Proposition 1 and general P-SSN existence or verification aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does irrelevance of low-tier types remain sufficient for P-SSN when DA and coalition deviations operate on type masses?
  • If so, can the condition and resulting profile be computed in time polynomial in the support size, number of colleges, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper offers a plausible high-multiplicity mirror, but it has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule asks for: no proposition establishes that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named results concern equilibrium properties. So I can make a positive case from the closest structural result, but it would not honestly qualify as a complexity anchor.

My lead is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says: under SODA, if the matching from the iterative DA construction satisfies *irrelevance of low-tier agents*—each student in a later last-proposer layer strictly prefers their own assignment to the assignment of every student in any earlier layer—then the resulting DA-SSN strategy profile is a P-SSN. In other words, under that condition, no coalition has a passively weak deviation.

A natural continuous market has a finite set of colleges and a distribution \(\mu\) over student types. A type records a student’s true ranking of colleges and their priority category at each college; its mass is the fraction of the applicant population in that category. Each college has a quota per unit of population, and ranks the types by priority. The continuum version lets a college accept a fraction of a type at a cutoff. Students choose reported rankings, and student-proposing DA maps those reports to assignments. The iterative DA construction gives the candidate strategy profile.

The underlying question is: given such a market and its iterative-DA profile, is that profile a P-SSN? A coalition may deviate with any measurable submass of students, including only part of a type. It is a passively weak deviation if a positive mass of coalition members strictly prefer the joint outcome to the baseline, while every coalition member weakly prefers that joint outcome to the outcome that would result if only the strict beneficiaries made the same reports. A “yes” solution means no such deviation exists; a “no” solution can give the deviating mass and reports. The proposed continuous counterpart of Proposition 2 is that the DA profile is P-SSN whenever the mass version of irrelevance of low-tier agents holds.

This regime is plausible for a large centralized admissions market: millions of applicants may fall into far fewer preference-and-priority categories. The type definition matters. If every student has a unique priority or a unique relevant preference, then the support is as large as the population and there is little high-multiplicity gain. Per-capita quotas and fractional acceptance at a type cutoff are natural co-modifications of the population limit.

For the low-tier condition, I expect the result to remain tractable: with explicit finite type lists, the iterative construction and the layer comparisons appear amenable to finite computation. That is a prediction about the mirror, not a complexity claim proved by this paper. For unrestricted P-SSN verification, I cannot responsibly assign a class from the evidence here. The continuum game may make threats easier to express as mass reallocations, but the paper does not establish that this reduces verification to an LP or another tractable problem. Further questions include whether the sufficient condition is necessary in any useful regime, whether P-SSN verification is decidable efficiently for rational type masses, and whether a discrete hardness reduction survives fractional coalitions.

I would not add Proposition 1 as a second anchor. It says that, when the Core-SSN exists and its matching differs from the DA-SSN matching, neither strategy profile is a P-SSN. That is a genuine structural result, but its core construction depends on a house-allocation problem with indivisible endowments, so the continuum counterpart needs more model work than the DA result.

The weakest point is that the paper proves equilibrium statements, not computational statements. Proposition 2 can motivate a continuous P-SSN verification problem and a tractable sufficient regime, but it does not itself chart a complexity landscape. The positive case is that the underlying admissions game has a credible large-population regime and a precise population mirror; the paper’s computational-program fit remains unproven.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 2 is a sufficient condition for an equilibrium property: if the iterative-DA matching satisfies irrelevance of low-tier agents, then its strategy profile is a P-SSN. It says nothing about the complexity of finding or verifying a P-SSN, or about an algorithm for doing either. Checking the stated condition appears to be a finite comparison after computing the iterative-DA profile; making the agents’ masses rational does not turn that structural implication into a complexity result.

The proponent’s better proposal is to ask whether a given continuous-market profile is P-SSN, allowing coalitions to choose measurable submasses and reports. That is a coherent computational question, but it broadens the paper’s result: unrestricted P-SSN verification is not what Proposition 2 establishes. It would require its own encoding of fractional assignments, coalitions, and the “passively” better-off subgroup. The paper gives no reason to expect its low-tier condition to characterize that problem or to expose the relevant computational bottleneck. The proposed mirror therefore rests on a new problem one could build around the paper, rather than a computational result the paper contributes.

The population model itself is plausible. Applicants could be grouped by complete preference and priority type, with college capacities scaled per capita and type masses split at cutoffs. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and fractional assignments can be represented as masses receiving each college. So I would not reject the mirror on grounds of identity, indivisibility, or an allegedly unsuitable admissions scenario. Those objections would miss the best version of the model.

That leaves a narrow negative case: the paper may be a poor target for ChoCo because its contribution is an equilibrium refinement and conditional equilibrium-existence result, not a computational contribution. I cannot make the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists here. In fact, P-SSN verification in a continuous admissions market could be worth studying on its own. But that would be a new computational project motivated by the paper’s setting, not a continuous mirror of its named result.
