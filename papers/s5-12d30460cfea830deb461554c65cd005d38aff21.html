The case FOR (proponent)

The best foothold for a positive case is that the paper treats the population as a continuum from the outset: Section 2 has a measure-two population split between consumers and producers, and later sections add distinct asset types. A high-multiplicity OTC setting could therefore be economically natural. But that does not give this paper a continuous *computational* mirror: its central questions concern bargaining paths and general-equilibrium outcomes, not algorithms over an input distribution of agent types.

Under your anchor rule, the paper has no qualifying named computational-complexity result. The closest is Theorem 1, which gives a unique gradual bargaining solution characterized by differential equations; it applies O’Neill et al. (2004), rather than classifying an algorithmic problem. Proposition 1, proved here, characterizes the subgame-perfect equilibrium and its convergence as the number of bargaining rounds tends to infinity. Proposition 7, also proved here, establishes existence and uniqueness of a general-equilibrium outcome. None asserts tractability, hardness, or another computational classification. In Proposition 1, the limit is over agenda rounds, not population size.

So I can’t honestly state a prover-ready continuous problem per anchor or predict its complexity class. An equilibrium-computation problem built from the paper might be worth studying, but it would be a new question without a named discrete computational result to mirror. The weakest point is decisive: this paper already uses a continuum population, yet supplies no computational-complexity anchor for the programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that none of the cited results gives ChoCo a computational problem to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes an axiomatic bargaining path by differential equations; Proposition 1 characterizes a two-player game and takes a limit in the number of bargaining rounds; Proposition 7 proves existence and uniqueness of a market equilibrium. None defines an encoded input, an algorithmic task, or a complexity claim. The paper’s results are analytic.

The best computational version of Theorem 1 would ask for the bargaining path given utility functions and other primitives. That is a question about evaluating a solution for one bilateral match. Its continuum is over asset transfers and agenda time; the number of rounds \(N\) in Proposition 1 is also an agenda parameter, not a population size. Turning either continuum into a population axis would change what the result is about.

The best version of Proposition 7 would ask for the equilibrium given asset supplies and market parameters. The paper’s population is already nonatomic, with consumers and producers in fixed proportions. In Appendix D it adds high- and low-valuation agents. Those are sensible high-multiplicity regimes, and I would count them as evidence that a continuum population is natural here. But the propositions do not take a distribution over agent types as algorithmic input, or establish a discrete computational problem whose high-multiplicity version they characterize. Distributions of balances or inventories in the paper are equilibrium objects, not an input society to be optimized over.

One could enrich the valuation space, provide rational type masses and finitely encoded utility functions, and ask for an equilibrium or a welfare-maximizing agenda. That is the strongest rescue: the authors clearly care about bargaining outcomes and market equilibria. It would also require specifying the enriched type-dependent bargaining and matching model and its computational inputs. The resulting task could be worthwhile, but it would be a new computational economics project rather than a continuous mirror of any named result here. The many-asset results do not supply a missing population axis: \(J\) indexes assets, and the paper already gives an analytical pecking order over them.

So the case against a mirror is strong for this paper’s stated results, though weaker as a universal claim that no algorithmic question could ever grow from its model. Its continuum population makes high multiplicity plausible; the gap is that the paper offers no computational result to continuize.
