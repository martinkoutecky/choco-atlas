The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of social roles \(R\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete agent types (including role and initial signal), a mass-dependent role interaction kernel, and signal laws with variances \(\sigma_r^2\), choose role-level conformity parameters \(\delta_r\in(-1,1)\) so the induced continuum opinion dynamics converge and the expected squared error of the limiting consensus from the true state is minimized. Determine an optimal vector and its error.

The model it lives in

A continuum population with finitely many social roles, represented by masses over complete types; role members interact through a kernel determined by the role masses. The decision variables are role-level conformity parameters, and the objective is the expected squared error of the limiting consensus.

The objection that survived

With independent individual signals, exact continuum aggregation can eliminate sampling variance, and the proposed role types omit realized initial signals, so the wisdom objective may collapse.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers optimal conformity allocation for wisdom in Corollary 2; it leaves the convergence, influence, and rest-of-world wisdom results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the role-level interaction kernel preserve convergence and the optimality formula when it depends on role masses?
  • How should the independent-signal uncertainty be represented so the continuum wisdom objective retains its intended meaning?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are proved here, but they concern opinion dynamics and statistical accuracy: Proposition 2 gives a sufficient condition for convergence; Theorem 1 characterizes long-run opinions; Corollary 1 gives the influence weights; and Proposition 4 and Corollary 2 analyze wisdom. In particular, Corollary 2 says that a strongly connected group’s mean squared error is at least \(1/\sum_{i\in C_k}1/\sigma_i^2\), with equality exactly when \(v_i\sigma_i^2\) is the same for every member. These are analytic results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise has a stated complexity classification.

The strongest plausible positive lead is a *new* computational question based on that conformity-allocation analysis. Consider a large online community with a small number \(\tau\) of repeated social roles: members of a role share a contact profile and signal precision, and role \(r\) has population mass \(\mu_r\). Given the role interaction rule and signal model, choose each role’s conformity level so that the resulting opinion dynamics converge and the long-run consensus has minimum mean squared error; output the conformity choices and the attained error. The intended regime is many members but few role types. This is close to the paper’s question of how conformity should be distributed to improve wisdom, and a tractable result might follow from a type-level analogue of its centrality and precision balancing.

The weak point is substantial: this would require a new model and a new computational result. The paper’s network is defined over named agents, and merging agents into roles preserves its dynamics only under restrictive repeated-contact patterns. As those patterns become more individualized, the number of types can approach the population size. So this is a plausible follow-on, but it is not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a mirror of this paper is strongest at the anchor stage: it states no computational complexity result. Proposition 2 concerns convergence, Theorem 1 characterizes long-run opinions, Corollary 1 gives influence weights, and Proposition 4 and Corollary 2 analyze wisdom. Those are analytic results about a finite network, not classifications of computational problems. So the proponent’s conformity-allocation proposal is a new follow-on question, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

Even as a follow-on, the cleanest high-multiplicity version looks too directly solved to motivate a complexity study. Suppose many agents share a finite set of network roles, with interactions specified by a role-level matrix. The dynamics then reduce to a finite role system. In each closed strongly connected group, the paper already gives the optimal conformity condition, \(v_i\sigma_i^2\) equal across members, and an explicit formula for the corresponding conformity levels. Computing the centrality weights and applying that formula is ordinary finite-dimensional linear algebra, not the kind of algorithmic bottleneck the programme is designed to uncover.

The more faithful version runs into a modelling tradeoff. If roles preserve the original network exactly, they must capture agents’ contact patterns, and arbitrary networks may require almost one role per agent. If roles instead share only type-level interaction rates, the reduction is exact only for special network structures. And under the paper’s independent-signal assumption, averaging over very large role populations drives signal noise toward zero, making the wisdom objective degenerate. Keeping nonzero uncertainty by giving each role a shared signal would change that assumption and the object being studied.

That is a strong case that this paper does not offer a worthwhile ChoCo mirror. It is not proof that no useful continuous-population research could grow out of opinion dynamics: one could study new mean-field models or constrained conformity design. But those would be new analytic or optimization questions, not computational mirrors of this paper’s results.
