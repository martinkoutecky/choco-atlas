The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the hospital–resident problem with lower quotas. The paper already has an efficient algorithm for the finite problem, so the continuous question is whether its result survives when the input is a distribution over a small set of complete agent types. It is a distinct question: the paper’s stated running time depends on the number of residents, not just on the number of types and the bit length of their masses.

My anchors are Theorem 10 and Lemma 11 in Nasre and Nimbhorkar’s paper. Theorem 10 says that, for the matching \(M\) mapped from a stable matching in the reduced instance, and for any feasible matching \(N\) and any correspondence function from \(N\) to \(M\), switching along any alternating component does not give \(N\) a positive vote margin: this holds for alternating cycles, paths with exactly one endpoint matched in \(M\), and paths with both endpoints matched in \(M\). Summing over the components gives the popularity guarantee. Lemma 11 adds that if a feasible \(N\) is larger than \(M\), then \(M\) beats \(N\) strictly under every correspondence function. Together with Theorem 10, that makes \(M\) a maximum-cardinality popular matching. The paper proves Theorem 10 there; the conference version points to the authors’ full version for Lemma 11’s proof. The paper states the first algorithm’s running time as \(O(|R|(|R|+|H|+|E|))\); that bound appears in its contribution statement rather than in either theorem statement. [The paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol093-fsttcs2017/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2017.44/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2017.44.pdf)

Here is the regime I would defend: a large centralized placement market, such as a regional healthcare system assigning a large cohort of trainees to hospitals or clinics. There may be tens of thousands of applicants but only hundreds of complete profiles: each profile specifies the applicant’s ranking of programs and the priority class they occupy at each program. Programs rank these profiles, and their minimum staffing requirements and maximum capacity are naturally stated as shares of the cohort. The type count \(\tau\) is then much smaller than the population size. That is a plausible instance regime, not a claim that every matching market is large or homogeneous.

The continuous instance has a finite hospital set \(H\), a finite set \(T\) of resident types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Each type has a complete ranking of hospitals, and each hospital ranks the resident types it accepts. Members of a type are exchangeable in those rankings. Hospital \(h\) has lower and upper quota shares \(\ell_h,u_h\). A decision is a mass assignment \(x_{th}\geq 0\): the fraction of type \(t\) assigned to \(h\). Feasibility means \(\sum_h x_{th}\leq\mu_t\) for each type and \(\ell_h\leq\sum_t x_{th}\leq u_h\) for each hospital. This is aggregate assignment, not fractional employment for an individual: every unit of population mass represents people who each receive one hospital or remain unmatched.

The popularity comparison also needs a population-level definition. For two feasible assignments \(x,y\), let \(\pi^t_{ab}\) be the mass of type \(t\) moving from assignment \(a\) under \(x\) to assignment \(b\) under \(y\), where \(a,b\in H\cup\{\bot\}\). Its row and column sums are the respective assignment masses. These transports represent which members of a type switch assignments; atomless type cohorts can realize any such transport. At each hospital, the resident mass leaving and arriving, together with its old and new unfilled capacity, can be paired in any way, just as the paper allows a correspondence between the hospital’s differing assignees. Each resident and hospital unit contributes \(+1\), \(0\), or \(-1\) according to whether it prefers the new assignment, is indifferent, or prefers the old one. Call \(x\) popular if no feasible \(y\) has positive total vote margin for any legal set of these type transports and hospital correspondences.

My lead question is Maximum-Mass Popular HRLQ: given these rational type masses, rankings, and quota shares, find a feasible popular assignment \(x\) maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}x_{th}\). A second, narrower question anchored in Theorem 10 is Popular-Mass HRLQ: on feasible instances, find any popular assignment. The first mirrors the maximum-cardinality popular matching result; the second isolates the paper’s popularity guarantee. Both ask about the underlying matching problem, not a continuous encoding. The paper’s reduction first ensures feasibility of the mapped matching (Theorem 7), then Theorem 10 supplies popularity and Lemma 11 supplies the maximum-cardinality property. Its main result is therefore a credible algorithmic anchor, rather than a hardness result. [The paper’s theorem statements and algorithm overview](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol093-fsttcs2017/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2017.44/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2017.44.pdf)

I would expect both mirrors to be Class A, with maximum mass the harder one: the level-copy reduction and stable-matching construction suggest structure that may survive aggregation, while the mass formulation makes type support and rational input length the relevant scale. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of the discrete running time. The questions it generates are concrete: can the reduction be done in time polynomial in \(|H|,\tau\), and input bit length; does a continuous optimum lift to a finite market formed by duplicating types; and do solutions remain valid when quota shares have large denominators?

The weakest point is that the paper’s construction is tied to individual counts: its expanded graph has a number of levels depending on the integer lower quotas, and its runtime depends on \(|R|\). Those quantities can be enormous after clearing denominators in a mass instance. There is also a preference-model adjustment: repeated residents with identical complete types require hospitals to treat members of a type as exchangeable, which appears as a tie within that type. If strict, identity-specific hospital priorities are essential to the authors’ problem, this mirror would lose fidelity. But in the stated large-cohort regime, type-level priorities and proportional staffing requirements are the natural high-multiplicity version of the same assignment question. The paper does not already answer its complexity; it provides the discrete algorithmic structure that makes the continuous question worth asking.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to both anchors is that anonymity is not a harmless change in this problem. A resident’s complete type must include how every hospital ranks them: hospital votes are part of popularity, with one vote per hospital position, and the paper allows those votes to depend on how exchanged residents are paired. The paper assumes strict rankings. If two residents have the same complete type, they share an acceptable hospital, where its strict ranking gives them different positions. So in the paper’s model, residents cannot form repeated complete types: \(\tau=|R|\). Defining types only by residents’ hospital rankings loses information that determines the popularity comparison. [The paper’s definitions of preferences and popularity](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol093-fsttcs2017/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2017.44/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2017.44.pdf)

A two-hospital example shows what gets lost. Let both hospitals have lower and upper quota \(1\); let both residents prefer \(a\) to \(b\). Hospital \(a\) ranks \(r_1\) above \(r_2\), while \(b\) ranks \(r_2\) above \(r_1\). Compare \(N=\{(r_1,a),(r_2,b)\}\) with \(M=\{(r_1,b),(r_2,a)\}\). The residents’ votes cancel, but the hospitals give \(N\) two more votes. If the residents are collapsed into one type and hospitals rank types, the two matchings become the same mass assignment and both hospital votes disappear. Thus the proposed aggregation changes the very comparison that Theorem 10 controls; Lemma 11’s maximum-cardinality popularity claim depends on that same comparison.

The natural repair is to give residents in a type the same priority class and make hospitals indifferent among them. Rational masses and quota shares then make a coherent continuous model, but it is HRLQ with ties, not the paper’s strict-priority problem. The example shows that the change can alter which matching defeats which. A model retaining strict priorities could instead give residents distinct priority scores, but then those scores distinguish their types; representing them as a continuum would require a different input model from the finite-support, low-\(\tau\) mirror proposed here.

That is a substantial reason to reject the proposed mirrors of Theorem 10 and Lemma 11 as faithful continuizations: the paper’s central popularity guarantee relies on identity-sensitive hospital priorities, while the proposed small type space erases them. I cannot honestly make the universal negative airtight, though. If the authors accept tied priority classes as a natural HRLQ regime, the resulting mass-popularity question may be worthwhile in its own right. The case against is strongest as a challenge to the claimed match with these theorems, not as proof that no useful continuous matching problem can be built.
