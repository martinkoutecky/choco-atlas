The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuization case. Propositions 1 and 2 are named theoretical results, but they establish statistical ranking guarantees, not that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar.

Specifically, Proposition 1 says that under A1 and A2, SSR asymptotically ranks agents by their mean strictly proper scoring rule score; and that PSR does so when all agents are scored against the same unbiased ground-truth estimate. Proposition 2 says that, under its assumptions and truthful reports, DMI ranks agents by squared determinant mutual information with the outcome, CA by determinant mutual information, and PTS by inverse expected weighted \(0\)-\(1\) loss when the mean prediction’s true-positive and true-negative rates both exceed \(0.5\). These are not eligible anchors under the requested criterion.

There is a plausible population analogue of Algorithm 1: let a type specify a forecaster’s complete report and question-coverage profile, let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of forecasters of type \(t\), calculate type-level peer assessment scores, select a specified fraction of the population, and output event predictions by aggregating the selected reports. A large forecasting platform with many repeated report profiles could supply a high-multiplicity regime. But exact repetition may be rare for probabilistic forecasts, and the paper’s goal is empirical accuracy against outcomes that are unavailable when aggregation occurs—not an optimization problem with a stated computational objective. So this is a possible modeling direction, not a defensible anchor-based continuous mirror of a named complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational-complexity result to carry over. Proposition 1 gives asymptotic ranking guarantees for SSR and PSR; Proposition 2 gives such guarantees for DMI, CA, and PTS under stated assumptions. These are statistical claims about whether peer scores track accuracy, not complexity classifications of a computational problem. The empirical comparison in Algorithm 1 does not add one. So none of the proponent’s proposed analogues anchors the kind of complexity question ChoCo is meant to chart.

There is a sensible population model available: define a type by a forecaster’s complete reporting policy and coverage across a shared event set, let \(\mu_t\) be its population mass, compute type-level PAS, select the top fraction, and aggregate their forecasts. Large forecasting platforms make the population regime plausible; lack of multiplicity is not a decisive objection. The difficulty is that the paper’s signal is each forecaster’s trajectory across tasks. If type means the full realized trajectory, reports and coverage are part of the type, so continuous-valued forecasts can leave almost every person as a separate type. If type instead means latent ability or reporting behavior, many agents can share it, but the model now estimates ability classes and aggregates their reports. That changes the statistical question the paper studies: selecting accurate forecasters from observed cross-task predictions.

Even under that stronger model, the paper’s central operation—score, rank, select a fraction, average—becomes calculating sums and a quantile. The resulting questions are naturally about consistency, estimation error, and aggregation risk in a large population. An interesting optimization question could be added, such as choosing weights to minimize expected loss, but that objective is absent from the paper and its outcomes are unavailable at aggregation time. It would be a new problem, rather than a continuous mirror of a named result.

This makes the negative case strong for ChoCo’s computational-complexity agenda, but weak as a universal claim. A continuous-population study of forecast aggregation could be worthwhile in statistical learning or peer prediction. The paper gives little reason to expect a meaningful new complexity landscape from it.
