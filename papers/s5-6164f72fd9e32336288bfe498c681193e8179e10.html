The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(0\leq\alpha_1\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_m\) and rational rank proportions \(\rho_i>0\) summing to one, consider a society with buyer type \(i\) and seller type \(i\) each having mass \(\rho_i/2\), where a type \(i\) buyer and type \(j\) seller generate surplus \(\alpha_{\min(i,j)}\). For each scale \(n\) such that every \(n\rho_i\) is integral, form the finite assignment game with \(n\rho_i\) agents of each side and rank \(i\). Determine whether each type’s per-agent Shapley payoff converges as \(n\) grows through these scales, and compute the limiting vector.

The model it lives in

Types are side and rank pairs, with buyer and seller type \(i\) each carrying mass \(\rho_i/2\). A coalition with buyer submasses \(x_i\) and seller submasses \(z_j\) has worth \(W(x,z)=\max_{y_{ij}\geq0}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{\min(i,j)}y_{ij}\), subject to \(\sum_j y_{ij}\leq x_i\) and \(\sum_i y_{ij}\leq z_j\). Define each type’s payoff as the limit of its per-agent Shapley payoff in the finite games obtained by replication.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 5 and the paper’s four-buyer, four-seller assignment computation through replicated assortative rank types; leaves Theorems 1–4, the tax-cooperation algorithm and results, and general assignment matrices outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For arbitrary rational buyer–seller type values and rational type masses, do the replicated finite assignment games have limiting per-type Shapley payoffs, and can those limits be computed efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one narrow lead case from the assignment-market section. The paper states no named complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Its strongest relevant anchor is Theorem 5, which is proved in the paper and then used in its assignment-market computation.

Theorem 5 says that in a square assignment game with \(m\) buyers and \(m\) sellers, if the surplus matrix has entries \(a_{ij}=\alpha_{\min(i,j)}\), where \(0\leq\alpha_1\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_m\), the Shapley value equals the Becker solution. The paper defines that solution by
\[ u_i^*=\frac12a_{ii}+\frac12\sum_{p=1}^{i-1}a_{p+1,p}-\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}a_{k,k+1}, \qquad v_j^*=\frac12a_{jj}-\frac12\sum_{p=1}^{j-1}a_{p+1,p}+\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}a_{k,k+1}. \]
For this matrix, the two sums cancel, so each buyer and seller of rank \(i\) receives \(\alpha_i/2\). This is a named exact allocation result, not a complexity theorem; the paper computes with it in its housing-market example.

A continuous counterpart I would hand to a prover is the High-Multiplicity Assortative Assignment Shapley Problem. An instance consists of \(m\), rational values \(0\leq\alpha_1\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_m\), and rational rank proportions \(\rho_i>0\) with \(\sum_i\rho_i=1\). The society has buyer type \(i\) with mass \(\rho_i/2\) and seller type \(i\) with mass \(\rho_i/2\). A type completely specifies the side, rank, and the agent’s assignment-relevant values; surplus between buyer type \(i\) and seller type \(j\) is \(\alpha_{\min(i,j)}\).

For a coalition with buyer masses \(x_i\) and seller masses \(z_j\), its worth is the maximum assignment surplus
\[ W(x,z)=\max_{y_{ij}\geq0}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{\min(i,j)}y_{ij} \]
subject to \(\sum_j y_{ij}\leq x_i\) and \(\sum_i y_{ij}\leq z_j\). The decision variable \(y_{ij}\) is matched mass. The problem asks for the limiting Shapley payoff per agent of each type: take \(n\) buyers and \(n\) sellers, with \(n\rho_i\) agents of each rank on each side, form the ordinary finite assignment game, and let \(n\) grow through multiples that make all counts integral. A solution is the vector of type-level payoff rates, with existence of the limits established.

The high-multiplicity regime is a repeated housing or standardized leasing market with many participants in each of a modest number of stable valuation and reservation-price ranks. Members of a type have identical assignment-relevant data, and the number of agents grows while the number of types stays fixed. The mass matching LP is the aggregate form of the paper’s one-to-one assignment problem: each unit of mass can be matched at most once. This keeps the paper’s surplus-maximizing matching and Shapley allocation questions intact.

For this structured instance, I expect Class A, tractable. The answer is \(\alpha_i/2\) for buyer and seller type \(i\). To see why the limit exists, expand each rank into \(n\rho_i\) identical agents. The resulting finite matrix still has the form in Theorem 5, with each \(\alpha_i\) repeated \(n\rho_i\) times. The theorem therefore gives the same per-agent payoff at every such scale. The grand-coalition matching has \(y_{ii}=\rho_i/2\); the mass-weighted Shapley allocations sum to its value. For this mirror, the output is computable directly from the input values, without expanding a possibly large replicated market.

The mirror covers Theorem 5 and the paper’s assignment-market computation; it does not cover the tax-cooperation results. Further questions include whether limiting type-level Shapley payoffs are efficiently computable for general assignment surplus matrices, how the answer changes with unequal buyer and seller rank proportions, and how LP degeneracy affects the limit.

My weakest point is the narrow assortative structure. The replication argument carries the paper’s result into the continuum cleanly, yet it does not expose a new hardness boundary or establish a broad complexity landscape. I think the case still holds as a continuous mirror: it gives a precise high-multiplicity version of a named result the authors actually compute, with masses, matching flows, and per-type allocations all specified.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper does not actually study a computational problem family. Its tax algorithm enumerates the power set of coalitions, but gives no complexity analysis. In the assignment section, the authors formulate a standard matching LP, solve one \(4\times4\) instance, and prove Theorem 5’s identity between two allocation rules for a special matrix. That is an exact allocation result, but not a theorem about the complexity of computing allocations or matchings. A ChoCo-style complexity mirror would have to supply a computational question the paper itself never analyzes.

That objection puts pressure on the proponent’s anchor. With repeated ranks, each finite replicated market still has the matrix form in Theorem 5, so the claimed per-agent payoff follows at every finite scale. This does not uncover a continuous optimization problem or a complexity boundary. But I cannot use the inherited answer as a reason to reject the mirror: under the programme’s stated standard, a well-posed question with an already-known answer still counts.

Nor can I rule out a stronger assignment mirror. Many buyers and sellers can share a finite set of complete valuation types; their masses give a natural matching-flow LP, and the limiting Shapley question can be defined by replication. Standardized housing or employer–employee types make that regime recognizable. Computing the limiting allocation for general type-level valuation matrices would be a new computational question, but it is a plausible one that grows naturally from the paper’s assignment model. The tax model also admits a possible high-multiplicity version by grouping firms with identical tax-relevant data.

So the negative case is weak where it matters: I can argue that the paper offers little computational-complexity result for ChoCo to continue, and that the proponent’s narrow mirror adds little beyond Theorem 5. I cannot honestly show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The assignment mirror survives the programme’s high-multiplicity test.
