Why no mirror

Theorem 1.6 rules out a welfare guarantee for OSP mechanisms even with unbounded computation and communication; it does not establish computational hardness or an algorithmic approximation result. A high-multiplicity limit of the same mechanism question is recognizable, but it cannot supply the missing computational character.

What the mirror covers

The proposed asymptotic question concerns only Theorem 1.6's multi-unit, unknown single-minded welfare guarantee; it leaves the unit-demand, additive, combinatorial-auction, and restricted-value results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize the optimal asymptotic OSP approximation factor for each fixed \((\lambda,\mathcal D,\mu)\), and determine whether Theorem 1.6's lower bound persists when the type set and limiting type proportions are fixed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 1.6, proved in this paper’s Section 3.1. It says that for a multi-unit auction with \(m\ge2\) identical items and \(n\ge2\) unknown single-minded bidders, no deterministic obviously strategy-proof mechanism satisfying individual rationality and no-negative-transfers can guarantee a welfare approximation strictly better than \(\min\{m,n\}\). Here a bidder’s type specifies a quantity \(q\) and value \(\alpha\), with value \(\alpha\) for receiving at least \(q\) items and zero otherwise. This is the paper’s clearest hardness-style anchor, though it is an impossibility about mechanism approximation, not a theorem classifying an algorithmic problem as NP-hard or in P.

A plausible mirror is \(\textsc{Cont-OSP-MUA}\). Its instance is a rational per-capita supply \(\lambda>0\), a finite allowed type set \(\mathcal D\subseteq\mathbb N_{>0}\times\mathbb Q_{>0}\), and a requested factor \(\rho\). The society is the unit interval with Lebesgue measure. Each agent \(x\) privately has type \(\theta(x)=(q_t,\alpha_t)\in\mathcal D\); the mass of type \(t\) is \(\mu_t\). The mechanism must work for every measurable type profile over \(\mathcal D\), without being told which agent has which type.

A deterministic measurable mechanism assigns each individual an integer quantity \(a(x)\ge0\) and a nonnegative payment \(p(x)\), with \(\int_0^1 a(x)\,dx\le\lambda\). Agent \(x\)’s utility is \(\alpha_t\mathbf 1[a(x)\ge q_t]-p(x)\). Require truthful play to be obviously dominant: at every information set reached by truthful play, each truthful continuation must give the agent at least as much utility as any continuation after choosing a different action there, under every pair of compatible continuations of the other agents. Also require individual rationality and no-negative-transfers. The question is whether such a mechanism guarantees \(W\ge W^\star/\rho\) on every profile, where \(W=\int_0^1 v_{\theta(x)}(a(x))\,dx\) and \(W^\star\) is the optimal feasible welfare.

The benchmark has a concrete mass formulation. Let \(x_{t,r}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(r\) units, with \(r\in\{0,\ldots,q_t\}\). Then

\[ \sum_r x_{t,r}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,r}r x_{t,r}\le\lambda,\qquad W^\star=\max\sum_t\alpha_t x_{t,q_t}. \]

Every individual still receives an integral quantity; only the type-level averages can be fractional. This is a meaningful co-modification: in the motivating regime, \(N\) organizations share a few recurring demand-and-value types, while identical service units scale with the population, \(m_N/N\to\lambda\). The population is continuous, the type support \(\tau\) stays small relative to \(N\), and the mechanism still faces the paper’s individual strategic question.

I would treat this as an open approximation question, not claim that Theorem 1.6 already answers it. For each fixed \((\lambda,\mathcal D)\), ask for the optimal worst-case factor \(\rho^\star(\lambda,\mathcal D)\) over such mechanisms. My tentative expectation is that the finite-population lower bound need not transfer, and that a finite-factor mechanism may be possible for fixed type sets: the aggregate allocation benchmark is an LP, and atomless mass removes some finite-market pivotality. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of the LP; OSP constraints may still obstruct it.

The weak point is substantial. The theorem’s proof uses two exceptional, named bidders among \(n\) people, and its demand and value scales change with \(m,n\). Their mass vanishes in the limit, while a fixed type distribution retains only positive-mass cohorts. A referee could therefore say the continuous problem has changed the strategic obstruction the authors study. The mirror is strongest as a new question about how OSP welfare guarantees behave in a genuine high-multiplicity auction; it does not cover the combinatorial-auction or additive-valuation results, and it does not establish a classical computational-complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.6 is a clear impossibility result, but its content is mechanism implementability, not computational complexity. It rules out a welfare guarantee even when computation and communication are unbounded. The proposed \(\textsc{Cont-OSP-MUA}\) therefore asks whether a continuum of agents admits an OSP mechanism with a given approximation factor; it does not ask for an algorithm’s running time, or for an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm on an input society. Its LP computes the welfare benchmark, but does not capture the OSP constraints. Making mechanism synthesis computational would add a new problem that the paper does not study.

There is also a modeling gap in the proposed continuum version. A measurable allocation rule does not specify the information sets, actions, or order of moves needed to define obvious strategy-proofness. In an atomless society, one agent’s report changes no type mass, while OSP compares that agent’s outcomes across branches of a protocol. A pointwise incentive definition might be possible, but it needs a protocol model; allowing uncountably many sequential moves or switching to simultaneous revelation changes the strategic setting. The aggregate vector \(\mu\) and the mass-allocation LP do not settle those choices.

The stronger repair is a sequence of finite auctions: \(N\) agents in repeated types, supply \(m_N\) with \(m_N/N\to\lambda\), and an asymptotic question about the best OSP approximation. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; repeated demand-and-value cohorts are plausible, and I would not use multiplicity as an objection. But this repair studies a limit of mechanism-approximation questions. If mechanisms may use agent labels or move order, the type distribution alone need not describe their strategic environment; imposing anonymity to make it do so adds a restriction absent from the paper. Either way, the central result remains about OSP implementability, not an algorithmic problem over a continuous society.

So the strongest case against this anchor is a scope objection: it does not furnish the kind of computational result ChoCo is set up to continuize. I would not claim that the auction regime is implausible or that an asymptotic OSP study lacks scholarly value. The negative case is weak as a claim that *no* continuous question is worthwhile; it is stronger as a claim that this paper offers no direct computational mirror for the programme.
