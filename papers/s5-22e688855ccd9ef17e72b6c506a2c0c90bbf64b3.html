The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-claimant liabilities \(\ell_t>0\), and a per-capita estate \(A\) with \(0<A<L=\sum_t\mu_t\ell_t\), define the worth of coalition mass vector \(z\), where \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), by \(V(z)=\max\{0,A-\sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\ell_t\}\). Compute for every type its continuous Shapley density \(\phi_t=\int_0^1\partial_{z_t}V(x\mu)\,dx\) and total type allocation \(\Psi_t=\mu_t\phi_t\), outputting exact rational values.

The model it lives in

A continuum of claimants is partitioned into liability types \(t\), with type \(t\) carrying mass \(\mu_t\) and each claimant owing \(\ell_t\). A coalition is represented by its type-mass vector \(z\); its normalized bankruptcy worth is \(V(z)=\max\{0,A-\sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\ell_t\}\). The requested outputs are the per-claimant densities \(\phi_t\) along the radial path \(x\mu\) and the aggregate allocations \(\Psi_t=\mu_t\phi_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the bankruptcy-game subclass under per-capita liability and estate scaling. It leaves the general linearly representable games, weighted voting games, and the paper’s other algorithmic results untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one give explicit convergence rates, in the type counts and liability parameters, for finite replicas’ per-capita Shapley allocations approaching \(\Psi_t\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one focused case, for bankruptcy games at high multiplicity. The paper’s strongest anchor for it is Theorem 5.3, proved here in Section 5.2. It says that for a linearly represented game \((f,\{a_j:j\in N\})\), if each \(a_j\le 2^{|N|}\), \(f\) can be evaluated in linear time and space, and there is a game-independent constant \(\alpha>0\) with \(|v(S)|\le n^\alpha\) for every coalition, then all players’ Shapley values can be calculated in \(O(Kn^3\log n)\) time and \(O(n^2K)\) space, where \(K=\sum_j a_j\). The theorem is proved in this paper; when \(K\) is polynomial in \(n\), its bound is polynomial. The introduction mentions hardness results for some example families through earlier citations, but it gives no numbered hardness theorem to use as an anchor under the requested rule.

Consider a large bankruptcy or compensation pool with many claimants but relatively few claim classes. A type \(t\) specifies a claimant’s per-person liability \(\ell_t\); agents of that type are identical in the game’s relevant details. The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) active liability types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The number of claimants \(n\) is much larger than \(\tau\), and the estate and aggregate liabilities grow with \(n\).

My lead question is Bankruptcy Shapley Density for a Continuum of Claimants. Its input is rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), rational liabilities \(\ell_t>0\), and a per-capita estate \(A\) satisfying \(0<A<L\), where \(L=\sum_t\mu_t\ell_t\). A coalition is described by its mass vector \(z\), where \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), and has normalized worth \(V(z)=\max\{0,A-\sum_t(\mu_t-z_t)\ell_t\}\). The task is to output, for every type, its continuous Shapley density \(\phi_t=\int_0^1 \partial_{z_t}V(x\mu)\,dx\), and its total type allocation \(\Psi_t=\mu_t\phi_t\). The derivative is taken wherever it exists; the single kink on the path does not affect the integral. The exact answer is \(\phi_t=\ell_t A/L\) and \(\Psi_t=\mu_t\ell_t A/L\). Thus this problem is tractable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the rational input bit length.

This is a direct high-multiplicity continuation of the paper’s bankruptcy example. To see the bridge, take \(n\) claimants with \(n\mu_t\) of type \(t\), and estate \(nA\). Their finite game is \(v_n(S)=\max\{0,nA-\sum_{j\notin S}\ell_{t(j)}\}\), the paper’s bankruptcy characteristic function. Its normalized worth is the continuum function evaluated at the coalition’s type masses. In the paper’s integral representation, other players join independently with probability \(x\); as \(n\) grows, the mass of each type in that random coalition concentrates at \(x\mu_t\). Adding one claimant changes mass by \(1/n\), while the game’s worth is scaled by \(n\), so that claimant’s marginal contribution approaches \(\partial_{z_t}V(x\mu)\). Integrating over \(x\) gives the stated density. The resulting type allocations sum to \(A\).

The mirror covers the bankruptcy subclass of Theorem 5.3, where liability determines coalition worth through an aggregate sum. It does not cover arbitrary games in the theorem, or weighted voting games with a discontinuous threshold rule. The continuous object is the claimant population; mass is the amount of society in each liability type. The paper’s own random-coalition integral makes the limiting question especially recognizable to its authors.

The expected direction is tractable. A useful follow-up is to bound how quickly finite Shapley values converge to these densities, and to ask what replaces this limit for discontinuous characteristic functions such as weighted voting games. The soft spot is that the mirror returns an asymptotic density, not the exact finite-game Shapley value. In this fixed-liability regime, \(K=nL=O(n)\), so Theorem 5.3 already gives polynomial time for the finite replicas; this mirror demonstrates a meaningful continuous limit more than a case where continuity changes the complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.3 is a real anchor, and the bankruptcy example has a credible high-multiplicity regime: many creditors can share a liability type, with per-capita liabilities and estate scaling with the population. The proposed scaling also works. For replicas with \(n\mu_t\) creditors of type \(t\) and estate \(nA\), the random coalition’s type masses concentrate around \(x\mu\). So the proposed density is genuinely a limit of the finite Shapley values, not a mistaken analogy. I can’t defeat it by questioning multiplicity, scaling, or the authors’ likely recognition of the problem.

The strongest negative case is narrower: this limit changes the computational object. In the continuum, the proposed density is a radial-gradient allocation. For a worth function depending on aggregate liabilities, \(V(z)=F(\sum_t \ell_t z_t)\), it is
\[ \phi_t=\int_0^1 \ell_t F'(xL)\,dx. \]
For bankruptcy, this gives \(\phi_t=\ell_t A/L\). The finite algorithm in the paper instead computes an average over discrete predecessor coalitions. Concentration replaces those coalition fluctuations with one deterministic path. That is a coherent asymptotic allocation, but by itself it does not pose the paper’s kind of finite-bit complexity problem.

A stronger computational version would ask for certified additive error bounds between this density and the finite-\(n\) Shapley allocations, perhaps uniformly over many liability types. But the finite corrections depend on \(n\), the integer type counts, and lattice effects near the bankruptcy threshold. Supplying those details makes the task a finite high-multiplicity Shapley approximation problem. Theorem 5.3 already gives a pseudo-polynomial exact algorithm for the replicas when the scaled liability sum is moderate. That fact supports the regime’s relevance; it does not count as prior continuous work. It does, however, make it harder to argue that the continuous formulation supplies a distinct computational problem.

This is not a decisive objection. A quantitative limit theorem with useful bit-complexity bounds could still be worthwhile, and nothing here rules one out. The honest negative case is that the proponent has identified a sound continuous limit, but has not shown a substantial computational question beyond that limit. On the standards given, I cannot make a strong enough case that this anchor should be rejected.
