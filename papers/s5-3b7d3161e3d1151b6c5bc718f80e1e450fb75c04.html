The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), integral capacities \(q_t\), and rational subjective-homophily scores satisfying approximate symmetry, together with a feasible initial matching represented by rational masses over integral partner-type bundles and consistent pairings of matching slots, can one compute a finite sequence of rational mass updates to a pairwise-stable state? Each update pairs equal positive masses of mutually blocking types, using disjoint cohorts for a same-type pair; an agent at capacity drops a least-preferred current partner. Stability means no pair of types has positive mass on both sides whose agents mutually block. The target complexity question is whether such a path can be produced in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(Q=\max_t q_t\), and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

An atomless one-sided many-to-many market with type \(t\) carrying mass \(\mu_t\), an integral capacity \(q_t\), and a complete homophily preference row. A state records the distribution over agents’ integral partner-type bundles and compatible matching-slot masses. The task is to find a finite sequence of equal-mass blocking updates ending at a state with no blocking types; there is no cost objective.

The objection that survived

The paper’s individual-pair ordering does not show that mass updates preserve feasible pairings or terminate when agents are distributed across integral partner bundles.

The mirror covers mass analogues of Lemma 3’s finite path and Lemma 2’s stable-matching existence result. It leaves Proposition 1’s random discrete process, the strategy-proofness results, and the remaining applications untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every feasible rational mass matching admit a finite path under a precise, feasible mass-update rule?
  • Can such a path be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(Q\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Lemma 3, proved in this paper. It says that when preferences come from subjective homophily functions satisfying approximate symmetry, *every* starting matching has a finite sequence of matchings ending in a pairwise-stable matching, with each step obtained by satisfying a blocking pair. The paper proves this constructively. This is an algorithmic reachability result, though the paper gives no running-time bound or standard complexity classification such as P or NP-hardness.

I would mirror it in a large one-sided market of organizations that form several partnerships each: for example, a nationwide network of service providers that can contract with multiple counterpart organizations. Many providers could share a small number of profiles—service line, scale, region, capacity, and a common ranking of counterpart profiles. Here \(n\), the number of organizations, is much larger than \(\tau\), the number of profiles. If those attributes or preferences are largely individual-specific, then \(\tau\) grows with \(n\) and the high-multiplicity case offers little. The scenario depends on repeated, genuinely interchangeable profiles.

Call the proposed problem Mass-Path-to-Stability. Its input is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), integral capacities \(q_t\), rational subjective-homophily scores \(\sigma_t(u)>0\), and an initial feasible matching. A type includes its capacity and its entire preference row, so agents of that type are indistinguishable for this problem. Require the score table to satisfy the paper’s approximate-symmetry condition: for all types \(t,u,v,w\), if \(\sigma_t(u)\leq\sigma_v(w)\), then
\[ \max\{\sigma_t(u),\sigma_u(t)\}\leq\min\{\sigma_w(v),\sigma_v(w)\}. \]

The population is atomless, with mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\). A matching gives each agent an integral bundle of partners, of size at most \(q_t\); a finite certificate can record the mass of each type receiving each possible partner-type bundle, together with compatible pairings of the resulting slots. Thus aggregate bundle counts may be fractional even though each agent’s bundle is integral. A step selects equal positive mass of two types who mutually block: each selected agent either has spare capacity or strictly prefers the proposed type to at least one current partner. The selected agents are paired, and any agent at capacity drops their least-preferred current partner, as in the paper. For a same-type pair, select two disjoint cohorts. The task is to output a finite sequence of these mass updates from the supplied initial matching to a state with no blocking pair involving positive mass on both sides. A witness is that sequence and its stable endpoint; there is no cost objective.

I expect this problem to be tractable, subject to a proof. With \(\tau\) types, there are only \(\tau^2\) type-pair classes to order by homophily scores, so the paper’s ordering argument has a plausible batch version that works on cohorts rather than processing agents one at a time. The useful complexity question would be whether a path and endpoint can be produced in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the largest capacity \(Q=\max_t q_t\), and the input bit length \(L\). This mirror covers Lemma 3’s finite-path result; it also addresses the stable-fixtures existence result in Lemma 2. Proposition 1, proved here as well, adds a related discrete conclusion: randomly chosen blocking pairs converge to pairwise stability with probability one. A continuous random scheduler over type-pair classes and its expected number of mass updates are natural follow-up questions, rather than a second anchor here.

The authors could plausibly recognize Mass-Path-to-Stability as their problem: it keeps their one-sided many-to-many fixtures, capacities, approximate-symmetry condition, pairwise stability, and blocking-pair transitions. Making capacities aggregate to \(q_t\mu_t\) is part of moving to a population distribution; it does not change the matching question. This proposal does not mirror the paper’s strategy-proofness results or its broader strategic discussion.

The weak point is the transition from individual events to mass updates. The paper’s finite proof orders individual pairs and handles the affected partners after each move. A continuum can contain fractional cohorts in many different integral partner bundles, and approximate symmetry alone does not establish that batch updates preserve a realizable matching or terminate after finitely many steps. That is the main theorem a prover would need to supply. The paper does not study complexity, so any tractability claim is a prediction for the mirror, not a result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Lemma 3 is a finite-path existence result, not a complexity result. The paper states no running-time bound or complexity classification. Its constructive proof orders at most \(n(n-1)\) individual-pair scores and processes them; Lemma 2 gives a related existence construction, while Proposition 1 derives almost-sure convergence from finite-state Markov-chain reasoning. So the paper offers no computational bottleneck for a continuous mirror to classify.

The proposed mass process also needs a substantive new definition. In an atomless population, a randomly selected pair of individuals has zero mass, so finitely many such encounters cannot change the society. Bulk updates can address that, but the paper’s proof does not establish that a particular batch rule preserves feasible pairings or terminates. A precise input also needs the initial population’s distribution over partner bundles: \(\tau\) type masses alone do not determine who is willing to block. The resulting complexity question should account for the support size of that bundle distribution, or give a compressed representation.

Those points weaken this particular anchor; they do not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Repeated profiles in a large many-to-many market are plausible, and the nearby continuous mass-matching literature supports the regime’s relevance without already answering this paper’s path question. The honest negative case is therefore limited: the paper itself supplies little complexity theory to mirror, and the strongest proposed version would require a new, carefully specified mass-dynamics model.
