The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 5.3 characterize voting methods by axioms; Proposition 5.2 derives the Condorcet Criterion. None gives an algorithm, complexity classification, or optimization result. The proposed mirror in the case for—compute the MWSL winner from ballot-type shares—is therefore a new computational question attached to the paper’s rule, not a continuous version of a result the paper proves.

The best population model is quite natural: take complete ballots, including the paper’s treatment of unranked candidates, as types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and derive margins by \(m_\mu(A,B)=\sum_t\mu_t(\mathbf{1}[A\succ_t B]-\mathbf{1}[B\succ_t A])\). But the rule depends only on those margins. Recasting its axioms over real-valued margins would continuize an axiomatic domain, which is outside the programme’s scope. Asking instead for bribery or winner robustness under mass changes would produce a computationally shaped question, but it would add interventions, costs, or an objective that this paper neither studies nor establishes as its problem.

This does not make the population regime implausible: ranked elections can have many voters sharing a relatively small set of ballot types. That supports studying the rule in a high-multiplicity setting; it is not a prior-art collision or, by itself, a computational mirror. The negative case is strong for this paper’s results, though it cannot show that the rules would never motivate a worthwhile, separate computational project.
