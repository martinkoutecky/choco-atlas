The continuous mirror question

Given a finite directed graph of firm types with terminal seller and buyer types, rational masses \(\mu_v\) satisfying \(\sum_v \mu_v=1\), contract types \(e=(u,v)\) with rational per-capita capacities, and strict upstream and downstream rankings for each nonterminal type, does there exist an individually rational balanced mass flow with no blocking path or cycle of positive feasible mass? Each type accepts equal total mass on both sides, choosing its most-preferred offered contract types first and allowing partial mass at the cutoff; a blocking path or cycle uses unused capacity and added contract mass that every participating type would accept alongside its current flow.

The model it lives in

A directed network of firm types carrying population masses, with type-to-type contract flows as decision variables and existence of a balanced, individually rational flow without positive-mass path or cycle blocks as the feasibility objective.

The objection that survived

Pooling counterparties into contract-type edges can create mass blocks unavailable in the paper’s named-firm graph, and the reduction has not been lifted to the proposed model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s existence question; it leaves Theorem 1, Theorems 3 and 4, their corollaries, and the weak trail-stability conjecture aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Theorem 2’s graph-partition reduction lift to the type-pool mass model with its blocking paths and cycles?
  • How should blocks account for distinct agents when a path uses the same type more than once?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is the existence problem for path-or-cycle stability in a mass trading network. The natural regime is a large market of small energy producers, storage operators, and buyers grouped by operating role. Each type describes agents with the same contract options, capacities, and upstream and downstream rankings. There are \(N=K\tau\) agents in the corresponding finite market, with many agents \(K\) per type and \(\tau\) types; the continuous instance records each type’s population share \(\mu_v\). A type’s role includes its network position, so \(\tau\) may grow with the network, but multiplicity can still be high within each role.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: “It is NP-complete to decide if a flow network admits a path-or-cycle-stable outcome.” Its reduction is from the problem of partitioning a directed graph’s vertices into two acyclic sets, whose NP-completeness is cited to Bokal et al. (2004). The paper also says its result holds when flow values are real. That is useful evidence for the mirror: the reduction’s hard combinatorics lie in the network of roles and contracts, not in counting individual agents.

I would pose Mass-Flow Path-or-Cycle-Stable Outcome Existence as follows. An instance consists of a finite directed graph of firm types, including terminal seller and buyer types; positive rational type masses \(\mu_v\) summing to one; rational per-capita capacities for each contract type \(e=(u,v)\); and strict rankings over each nonterminal type’s upstream and downstream contract types. Every agent within a type shares its rankings and capacities. For mass offers on the two sides, a type chooses the same total mass from each side, up to the smaller total offered, taking its most-preferred contract types first and allowing a partial quantity at the cutoff. Terminal types accept all offers.

A candidate outcome is a rational vector of traded masses \(x_e\), within the contract capacities and balanced at each nonterminal type: total incoming mass equals total outgoing mass. The question is whether such an individually rational outcome exists with no blocking path or cycle. A block is a positive mass \(\delta\) of unused contract capacity along a simple directed path or cycle, with every type on it willing to accept its added contract mass alongside its existing outcome. The decision procedure returns an outcome \(x\) if one exists, and “no” otherwise.

I expect this problem to be hardness transfers (Class B). The paper’s real-flow observation suggests fractional quantities do not remove the graph-partition obstruction, and representing firms as repeated agents of each type gives the population its continuous meaning. The mirror covers Theorem 2’s existence question; it does not try to continuize every result in the paper. Further questions include whether the graph-partition reduction lifts unchanged to this precise cohort model, and whether restrictions such as bounded degree or treewidth of the type network yield efficient algorithms.

The weak point is the cohort interpretation. If a firm’s complete type must encode its particular neighbors in the network, many firms may have unique types, leaving little high multiplicity. The mirror is most plausible when firms interact through shared contract pools between role classes, as in a market with repeated facilities or prosumers. Whether the authors would accept that type-pool formulation is the key issue for a prover to settle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proposed Theorem 2 mirror is that its type pooling changes what a contract is. In the paper, a firm’s complete type includes its exact contract options, which name particular counterparties. In the reduction, each graph vertex’s incident edges carry the hard combinatorics. Duplicating a vertex while preserving those exact bilateral links gives each copy different counterparties, so the copies are not the same type. Pooling them into role types solves that problem by making contracts relations between classes of firms—but then an edge in the paper’s graph becomes a pool of possible trades, and a mass block need not reproduce the paper’s blocking path or cycle. The proposed lift therefore needs more than replacing each firm with many copies.

The paper does say its flow-network hardness result also holds when flow values are real. That supports continuous trade quantities on a finite network; it does not establish a continuous population model. It is relevant evidence that the result tolerates fractional flows, but it does not settle whether a population mirror preserves the firms’ bilateral relationships.

A stronger cohort model is still plausible. For example, standardized energy-market participants could share supplier and buyer categories, rankings, and capacities, with trades represented as mass between categories. That gives a sensible continuous-population question about path-or-cycle stability. But it is a different formulation, and the proponent has not shown that Theorem 2’s reduction carries over to it. This is the best negative case: the paper’s exact network structure resists aggregation, and the suggested mirror has a real modeling gap.

That objection does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A market with pooled contract categories could be one the authors recognize, and proving what its mass blocks and stability conditions should be is itself a legitimate computational question. I can’t honestly defend the universal negative claim: the case against this particular lift is meaningful, but it does not rule out a good population mirror.
