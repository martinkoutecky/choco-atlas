The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has computational results, but the supplied text prints none as a numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition. I can’t give the numbered anchor your rules require without inventing one. The closest anchor is the unnumbered claim in §4: at every step \(k\in\{2,\ldots,n\}\), a dynamic DRF solution can be found in \(O(k)\) time, treating \(m\) as constant. It is proved here; the two structural lemmas used in its proof are attributed to Kash et al. This is the paper’s strongest candidate for a mirror, though it does not meet the numbering requirement.

The continuous problem I would attach to that claim is Batch-Arrival Dynamic DRF Computation\(_\infty\). Let each type \(t\) be a cohort’s arrival epoch together with its normalized demand vector \(d_t\in(0,1]^m\), where \(\max_r d_{tr}=1\). The input is a finite list of \(\tau\) cohorts with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\), and their arrival order. At epoch \(j\), let \(q_j\) be the total mass that has arrived. Each unit of cohort mass receives a per-capita dominant share \(z_t^j\), and hence a bundle proportional to \(d_t\). Compute an optimal allocation at every epoch by maximizing the minimum current share \(M^j\), subject to irrevocability and resource capacity:

\[ z_t^j\ge M^j,\qquad z_t^j\ge z_t^{j-1}\ \text{for previously arrived cohorts},\qquad \sum_{t\text{ arrived by }j}\mu_t d_{tr}z_t^j\le q_j \quad\forall r. \]

Any sequence of rational allocations optimal at each epoch is a solution. This normalization matches the discrete model for batches of identical users: if the final population has \(N\) users and a cohort has \(N\mu_t\) members, set \(z_t^j=Nx_t^j\). Then the aggregate load is the paper’s resource load and \(q_j\) is its capacity \(k/N\).

A plausible regime is a large cloud service assigning resources to many jobs drawn from a small catalogue of workload templates, arriving in service waves. A type records both demand and arrival epoch, since arrival history can affect allocation. The high-multiplicity claim is that \(N\) is large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct cohort templates is much smaller. The mirror keeps the paper’s central choices: dominant-share fairness, normalized demands, irrevocable allocations, and a minimum-share objective. The population becomes mass; resource capacities scale with arrived mass.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable. At each of at most \(\tau\) epochs, the displayed problem is a rational LP with at most \(\tau\) allocation variables and \(m\) capacity constraints, so exact computation should be polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). That is a continuous analogue of the paper’s computation result, not a claim that its \(O(k)\) algorithm directly yields an \(O(\tau)\) algorithm.

The weakest point is the arrival model. The paper processes agents one by one; this mirror groups identical users into arrival cohorts. The authors may regard simultaneous batch arrival as a meaningful dynamic DRF variant rather than the exact high-multiplicity version of their single-arrival mechanism. It is a defensible co-modification because the cohort still carries every feature relevant to this model, and the mass-scaled capacity follows directly from \(k/N\). Still, the paper’s runtime theorem does not itself prove the continuous LP result. The mirror covers only the computation of dynamic DRF allocations; it does not claim to cover the paper’s competitive-ratio analyses. Two natural follow-up questions are whether the maxsum and maxmin ratios remain \(1/m\) for this cohort model, and whether a more general continuous arrival stream has an efficient exact update algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered or named computational theorem here. The closest anchor is the unnumbered claim in §4 that a dynamic DRF allocation can be found in \(O(k)\) time at step \(k\). It is proved in the paper, so the missing theorem label is a formal weakness in the proposed anchor, not a reason to pretend the result is absent.

The stronger objection is that this runtime claim depends on the arrival sequence. Irrevocability gives earlier agents a different state from later agents, even when their demand vectors match. To preserve the paper’s one-agent-at-a-time process, a type must record arrival position; then there may be as many types as agents, and the proposed support-size advantage disappears. To get a small \(\tau\), the mirror groups arrivals into cohorts and updates at batch epochs. That is a plausible cloud workload model, but it changes the claim: the paper’s per-arrival \(O(k)\) algorithm is no longer what is being mirrored. Its output also includes an allocation at every arrival, which a cohort-level computation need not produce.

That still does not defeat the best cohort mirror. With batch arrivals, the mass-weighted constraints give a rational LP with at most \(\tau\) allocation variables at each epoch, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). This makes the continuous computation question immediate from the model, rather than an analogue of the paper’s linear-time algorithm. But “immediate” is a novelty concern, not a decisive objection under ChoCo’s standard: a well-posed Class A mirror can still be worthwhile.

So the negative case is weak. I can argue that §4’s specific runtime result is a poor anchor for a continuous mirror of the original arrival process. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile cohort model. Nor does that refutation rule out a mass-weighted mirror of §3’s competitive-ratio results; the maxsum tight example already uses only \(m+1\) demand types, with small but positive masses for the specialist types. A universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror would go beyond what this paper supports.
