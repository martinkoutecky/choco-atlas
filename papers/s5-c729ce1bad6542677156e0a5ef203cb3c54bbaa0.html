The continuous mirror question

Given resident types \(R\) and position types \(H\), with binary-rational masses \(\mu_r\) and \(\nu_h\) summing to \(1\) on each side, strict preference lists of length at most three, and an acquaintance relation \(A\) on acceptable type pairs, does there exist a nonnegative flow \(x_{rh}\) on acceptable pairs such that \(\sum_h x_{rh}=\mu_r\) and \(\sum_r x_{rh}=\nu_h\), and for every \((r,h)\in A\), either \(D_{rh}=0\) or \(W_{rh}=0\)? Here \(D_{rh}\) is the mass of type \(r\) assigned below \(h\), and \(W_{rh}\) is the mass of type \(h\) assigned below \(r\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided society given by distributions over resident and position types. The decision variables \(x_{rh}\) allocate mass across acceptable pairs; all mass must be matched, and an acquainted type pair must not have positive mass on both sides preferring one another.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the complete socially stable matching decision problem in Theorem 4.2. It leaves the paper's approximation results, special-case algorithms, and other results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding feasibility as the number of types and the binary encoding length of the masses grow?
  • Does the mass-flow formulation have an integrality property, or can fractional allocations change the discrete hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a continuous version of the paper’s complete socially stable matching problem. The anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper using an earlier hardness result: \((3,3)\)-COM SMISS is NP-complete. In full, given a one-to-one social-stability instance with at most three acceptable partners on each agent’s preference list, the question is whether there is a socially stable matching that matches every man and every woman. The paper reduces from the corresponding complete-matching problem with ties, relying on Irving et al. [20] for its restricted NP-hardness.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large, centralized placement market—for example, recurring cohorts of medical graduates matched to residency positions. Many people on each side could share a preference ranking and the same access to referral or communication channels. There might be tens of thousands of agents but only dozens or hundreds of such types. A type must include the agent’s full preference and social-access profile. In this version, acquaintance is a relation between types: if resident type \(r\) and position type \(h\) are acquainted, every pair across those groups can communicate; otherwise none can. That is a meaningful regime for channel-based access, though a simplification of personal friendship networks.

Here is the corresponding decision problem, which I would call Perfect Mass-SMISS. An instance has finite resident types \(R\) and position types \(H\), with rational masses \(\mu_r\) and \(\nu_h\), each side’s masses summing to \(1\). Each type has a strict ranking of its acceptable types on the other side, and an acquaintance relation \(A\) among acceptable type pairs. The lists can be restricted to length at most three to track Theorem 4.2. The question is whether there is a socially stable allocation matching all the mass on both sides.

A solution is a nonnegative mass flow \(x_{rh}\) on acceptable pairs, with
\[ \sum_{h\in H}x_{rh}=\mu_r \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{r\in R}x_{rh}=\nu_h. \]
Each unit of population gets one partner; \(x_{rh}\) records what fraction of a type is matched to each other type. Thus splitting \(x\) does not give anyone a fractional partner. For every acquainted pair \((r,h)\), let \(D_{rh}\) be the mass of type \(r\) assigned to positions it ranks below \(h\), and let \(W_{rh}\) be the mass of type \(h\) assigned to residents it ranks below \(r\). The allocation is socially stable exactly when, for every \((r,h)\in A\), \(D_{rh}=0\) or \(W_{rh}=0\). If both are positive, a small amount of resident mass can move to \(h\) and displace a less-preferred assignment there, forming a social blocking pair. The input is the type lists, acquaintance relation, and binary rational masses; the requested output is either such a feasible flow or a proof that none exists.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime: the matching, complete-coverage goal, and pairwise social-stability condition are retained. The supply masses and type-level flows are the necessary population-scale version of the same assignment question. The society itself is what becomes continuous; this is not continuity of outcomes or probabilities.

My tentative expectation is Class C, continuum-specific hardness. The acquainted type pairs still create coupled choices about which side of each pair must be protected from blocking, and those choices interact through the mass-conservation constraints. That combinatorial structure survives when people are represented by mass. But Theorem 4.2 does not establish this prediction: fractional flows may create solutions that no discrete matching has. The main prover questions are whether Perfect Mass-SMISS is tractable as the number of types \(\tau\) grows, whether its feasible flows have an integrality property, and whether maximizing matched mass has a useful approximation or parameterized algorithm.

This mirror covers Theorem 4.2 only. The weakest point is the type-level acquaintance relation. The paper’s hardness construction uses individualized agents and social edges; reproducing those edges as complete profiles could require nearly one type per person, losing the high-multiplicity regime. The positive case therefore rests on a different, plausible market regime where shared communication channels make cohort-level acquaintance meaningful—not on a claim that the paper’s hard instances already have few types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.2 is a genuine computational anchor: \((3,3)\)-COM SMISS is NP-complete. The strongest case against its mirror is that HRSS is built around an identity-specific social graph. A type must include everything the problem uses, including whom an agent can contact. In a sparse friendship or referral network, those neighborhoods usually differ from person to person. The resulting type count \(\tau\) may grow with the population, leaving little high-multiplicity structure.

The proposed type-level acquaintance relation fixes that problem by making each acquainted type pair a complete bipartite block: every resident in one type can contact every position in the other. That is a substantial assumption about the network. It can turn sparse personal ties into dense cohort-wide access. If instead the model retains sparse edges, they may vanish in an atomless limit: requiring a positive mass of blocking edges can make stability vacuous, while preserving the paper’s “there exists an acquainted pair” condition requires retaining individual-level network detail.

That is a real limitation, but it does not defeat the best mirror. Repeated cohorts with shared rankings and access through common institutional channels are plausible. For that regime, the proponent’s condition \(D_{rh}=0\) or \(W_{rh}=0\) correctly captures whether a positive amount of mutually dissatisfied mass can block. It retains the matching question and makes the population continuous. The paper itself allows communication channels as a reason a pair can deviate, so this is not obviously outside what its authors would recognize.

I therefore cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The network argument supports a narrower verdict: the mirror is most credible for dense, type-level access regimes, and it should not be presented as a faithful limit of arbitrary sparse social networks. That caveat does not eliminate the computational question. Fractional mass may change the hardness result, but whether it does is precisely something worth studying. Since Theorem 4.2 is the only anchor raised and its mirror survives in a plausible regime, the negative case falls short.
