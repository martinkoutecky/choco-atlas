The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a monotonic ranking with \(K\) classes whose cumulative upper sets \(W_k\) are specified by finite antichains of minimal mass vectors, compute the lexicographic preorder of types induced by \(G_{t,k}=\int_{W_k\setminus W_{k-1}} (x_t/\mu_t)\,d\nu(x)\), where \(x\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\) and \(\nu\) is normalized Lebesgue measure.

The model it lives in

A society has types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\); a coalition is a mass profile \(x\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), and its monotonic ranking is represented by finite antichains defining the cumulative upper sets. There are no decision variables or optimization objective: compute each type’s class scores by integrating its participation fraction \(x_t/\mu_t\) over the class, then lexicographically rank the score vectors.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers score computation for lex-cel on monotonic coalitional rankings, extending the score to types and fractional coalition profiles. It leaves the paper’s finite inclusion–exclusion bounds, comparisons between lex-cel and \(L^{(1)}\), and simulations unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of computing the type preorder as a function of the number of types, minimal mass vectors, ranking classes, and input bit length?
  • Can the computation be fixed-parameter tractable in the number of types, or admit useful approximation guarantees?
  • Which finite-to-continuous comparisons, if any, persist when coalition profiles are sampled using the finite model’s induced measure?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a type-level continuization of lex-cel for monotonic coalitional rankings. The strongest anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper’s Appendix A.1: given \(n\) and the inclusion-minimal family \(\mathcal F^i(\Sigma)\), the worst-case running time to calculate player \(i\)’s lex-cel or \(L^{(1)}\) score is \(O\!\left(2^{\binom{n-1}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}}\right)\). The proposition bounds an algorithm’s running time; it does not establish hardness or polynomial-time solvability. [Proposition 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.24596#page=10)

Call the mirror Mass-Lexcel Ranking. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of member types, each with positive rational population mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A coalition is represented by its mass profile \(x\in X_\mu=\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\), with \(x_t\) the mass of type \(t\) in the coalition. The ranking has \(K\) ordered classes; each cumulative upper set \(W_k\) is specified by its finite antichain of minimal winning mass vectors. Thus the input records the coalition-strength structure through a compressed boundary, just as the paper records winning coalitions through minimal coalitions.

For \(q_t=x_t/\mu_t\), the fraction of type \(t\) participating in that coalition, use normalized Lebesgue measure on the profile cube. For the \(k\)-th class \(R_k=W_k\setminus W_{k-1}\), define the type’s score as
\[ G_{t,k}=\int_{R_k} q_t\,d\nu(x). \]
The output is the total preorder of types obtained by comparing \((G_{t,1},\ldots,G_{t,K})\) lexicographically. An exact solution returns that preorder, or the rational score vectors that determine it. This is a computation problem rather than an optimization problem: there is no action to choose, and the output is the social ranking.

A plausible regime is a large cooperative or federation with many members but relatively few roles, regions, or professional categories. Members within a type share every feature relevant to the coalition ranking; for example, coalition strength might depend on the representation shares of eight categories, even though the association has hundreds of thousands of members. The result ranks those categories, tying the members of each category. Its parameters are \(\tau=|T|\), the number \(s\) of minimal mass vectors, the number of ranking classes, and their rational input bit length \(L\). This keeps the paper’s object—a social ranking induced by a monotonic ranking of coalitions—and gives its “minimal winning coalition” representation a direct mass-vector counterpart.

The authors could recognize this as a continuous extension of their calculation: a finite membership indicator becomes a type’s participation fraction, and the Boolean cube of coalitions becomes a cube of fractional coalition profiles. For fixed \(\tau\), exact scores can be computed by partitioning each coordinate at the input thresholds and integrating over the resulting cells, in time \(O((s+1)^\tau\operatorname{poly}(L))\). For unrestricted \(\tau\), I would expect exact score computation to be hard for continuum-specific reasons: it becomes an exact integration problem over a variable-dimensional union of boxes. That is a prediction about the mirror, not a hardness result in this paper, so I would not claim hardness transfers from Proposition 1. Further questions are whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), whether scores admit useful approximation, and whether a finite-to-continuous dictionary preserves any discrete ranking comparisons.

The weak point is the measure over coalition profiles. The paper counts finite coalitions equally, while this mirror uses uniform volume over fractional profiles. In a finite population grouped into types, uniform counting over individual subsets induces binomial weights on type participation levels; as the groups grow, those weights concentrate near half participation rather than approaching uniform volume. So this is a well-defined, population-continuous extension, but it is not yet shown to be the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s exact scores. The positive case depends on the authors accepting volume over fractional coalition profiles as a principled way to extend their score semantics.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is real: Proposition 1 is a computational result, so I would not dismiss the mirror on the ground that the paper has nothing computational to mirror. The difficulty is that the proposed mass score has no canonical measure inherited from the paper’s score, and the natural measure that *is* inherited makes the high-multiplicity limit mostly tie every type.

To see this, take \(n_t\) members of each type \(t\), with \(n_t/n\to\mu_t>0\), and choose a coalition uniformly from the \(2^n\) finite coalitions. If \(K_t\) members of type \(t\) are in it, then \(K_t\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n_t,1/2)\), independently across types. The normalized lex-cel score of a particular member of type \(t\), for class \(k\), is
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\frac{K_t}{n_t}\,\mathbf 1\{K/n\in R_k\}\right]. \]
This is the finite paper’s occurrence count, normalized by the number of coalitions. As multiplicities grow, \(K_t/n_t\) concentrates at \(1/2\). If the central profile \((1/2,\ldots,1/2)\) lies inside one class, every type’s limiting score vector is \(1/2\) in that class and zero in the others. The continuum then ranks all types equally, regardless of most of the coalition ranking. If the central profile lies on a class boundary, the leading scores are still common to all types; distinctions can survive in smaller, typically \(n^{-1/2}\)-scale corrections.

That is a serious weakness for the proposed Mass-Lexcel Ranking: its uniform volume measure avoids this collapse, but it does not arise as the limit of counting finite coalitions. Uniformly sampling fractional participation profiles is a different weighting of coalitions, and other choices of measure could induce other rankings. The issue is not that a new weighting is forbidden. It is that the paper’s central object—the number of coalitions containing a player—does not choose one for us. The authors would need to endorse a new measure as part of the ranking solution before the integration problem becomes their mirror.

The finite-antichain representation also does not pass cleanly to general continuous societies. In the Boolean cube, a monotone winning set has a finite family of minimal coalitions. In a continuous profile box, a natural monotone threshold such as \(x_1+x_2\geq a\) can have an entire continuum of minimal boundary points. Restricting to finitely many minimal mass vectors gives a valid but narrower family of unions of orthants; allowing general polyhedral or curved boundaries requires a new input model and makes the calculation a volume-integration problem. Either route can be studied, but neither is simply Proposition 1 with the population made continuous.

There is a stronger repaired direction: retain the finite-induced measure and study the first nonzero asymptotic corrections near the central profile. For majority-like boundaries, those corrections may yield nontrivial type rankings and connect to influence-style quantities. That is plausibly worthwhile, but it is a local asymptotic theory of boundary behavior, rather than a direct continuum version of the paper’s global coalition-occurrence scores. I cannot rule out that the authors would find it a compelling extension.

So the negative case is not airtight. Its strongest point is that the most faithful high-multiplicity limit often erases the ranking, while the proposed nondegenerate alternative depends on a new, unmotivated coalition measure. That makes this a weaker candidate than a mirror whose continuous score semantics and computational input follow directly from its discrete result—but it does not establish that no version would be worth pursuing.
