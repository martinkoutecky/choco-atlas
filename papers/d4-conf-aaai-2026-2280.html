The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a plausible extension of the paper’s revision framework, but it does not meet your anchor requirement. The paper has no named result classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard, and no formulation theorem that it uses to compute one. It says complexity analysis is beyond its scope.

The closest conceptual anchor is Theorem 5.3, proved in this paper. For fixed constraints \(\phi\), horizon \(n\), and coalition-priority preorder \(\sqsubset\), it states that an intention-system revision operator satisfies \((I1)\)–\((I5)\), \((I7)\), \((I8\text{-}BI)\), \((IPrio)\), and \((IDL)\) if and only if it is compatible with a faithful, logical assignment of preorders over strategy profiles that respects those priorities. This is a semantic representation theorem: revision selects minimal profiles satisfying the new intentions. It is not an algorithm or complexity result. Theorem 5.2, also proved here, establishes the logical properties needed for that representation; it does not classify a computational problem either.

A plausible *new* question suggested by the theorem would concern a large collaborative operation with many interchangeable workers or devices and only a few operational roles. Let \(T\) be the set of complete role types—same available actions, transition-relevant properties, group memberships, and revision priorities—and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\). In a finite-horizon anonymous game, let \(x_{t,\pi}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to policy \(\pi\), with \(\sum_\pi x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\). Given environmental constraints \(\phi\), old and incoming group intentions \(I,J\), and a specified rational revision cost, ask for all minimum-cost mass allocations whose aggregate action and outcome paths satisfy \(J\) under \(\phi\). Each individual still follows an integral policy; the fractional variable records how much of a type follows each policy.

That is a recognizable adaptation of the paper’s strategy-profile and minimal-change ideas, and the regime is credible when a large workforce or fleet falls into relatively few complete operational types. But it requires defining mass-based group intentions and strategic enforceability, which the paper does not do. Theorem 5.3 does not establish that this problem is an LP, or predict its complexity. Its likely follow-up questions are whether weak and strong coherence have useful mass semantics, whether the representation result survives that semantics, and how complexity scales with \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length.

The weak point is decisive for this task: the paper’s agents and coalitions are finite and individually specified, and the authors expressly leave computation aside. So I cannot honestly offer this as a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result. It is a research direction suggested by the paper, not a result of the paper whose discrete hardness might dissolve under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 5.3 characterizes revision operators by an equivalence between rationality postulates and preorder assignments over strategy profiles. Theorem 5.2 establishes the logical properties needed for that characterization. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a classification of a computational problem. The paper calls the representation construction “operational,” but it constructs an operator from a supplied preorder assignment; it does not say how to compute that assignment or revise an input efficiently. The paper does mention decidability of satisfiability in passing, but for a fixed finite signature, by enumerating bounded models. That observation supplies no population parameter or complexity landscape.

The suggested large workforce or fleet is the most plausible route to a population model, but the paper’s semantics make aggregation unusually difficult. It assigns intentions to every nonempty coalition, so with \(N\) agents there are \(2^N-1\) coalition-indexed intentions. Formulas can refer to named agents’ actions, and strong coherence asks whether each coalition can achieve its intention regardless of what agents outside it do. Meanwhile, the transition function may depend on the full joint action, not just type-level action totals. A distribution over roles and policies will therefore lose distinctions the framework permits unless the model is also restricted to symmetric transitions and symmetrically specified coalitions and intentions. In an atomless version, singleton agents raise a further problem: retaining their individual actions means retaining measure-zero agents in the semantics; recording only aggregate outcomes can make their strategic influence disappear.

Those restrictions could define a sensible new model. But the proposed minimum-cost mass allocation also adds a cost function the paper does not provide. The theorem permits arbitrary faithful, logical, potentially non-total preorders; it does not imply a numeric additive revision cost. To get an algorithmic question, one would need to specify such a cost, define mass-based satisfaction and enforceability, and restrict the game structure enough for aggregate policies to determine outcomes. That could be worthwhile work, but it would be a new computational problem, not a continuous mirror of either theorem. A direct continuous extension of Theorem 5.3 would instead be an axiomatic representation question, outside ChoCo’s computational aim.

So none of the proponent’s anchors survives as a computational result of this paper. The negative case is strongest at that boundary: the paper offers representation theory, not a complexity result whose behaviour under continuization could be charted. It cannot establish that no useful continuous strategic-revision problem exists; the symmetric-fleet variant might be worth studying on its own. Its weakness is that universal claim. Its strength is that such a variant would need enough new semantics and computational structure that it would no longer be this paper’s mirror.
