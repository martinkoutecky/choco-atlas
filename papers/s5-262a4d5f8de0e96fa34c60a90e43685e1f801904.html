The continuous mirror question

Given resource kinds \(R\), complete utility types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_r\), rational \(k\)-additive utility coefficients, and a rational threshold \(K\), decide whether there exist masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) for bundles \(B\subseteq R\) such that \(\sum_{B\subseteq R}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T,\,B\ni r}x_{t,B}=q_r\) for each \(r\), and \(\sum_{t\in T,\,B\subseteq R}x_{t,B}u_t(B)>K\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over complete utility types. Each individual receives an integral bundle, while \(x_{t,B}\) records the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\). Resource kinds have per-capita supplies \(q_r\), and the objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)\) subject to type-mass and resource-supply constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the thresholded welfare-allocation complexity results in Propositions 7 and 8, including \(k\)-additive and explicit bundle-list representations. It leaves the negotiation convergence and deal-size results, and the preference-representation results, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can finite populations approximate the continuous optimum with useful additive guarantees as population size grows?
  • How does the pricing complexity vary with \(k\), coefficient restrictions, and the number of utility types?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous \(k\)-Additive Welfare Allocation. It keeps the paper’s central question—maximizing utilitarian social welfare—and makes the population the continuous object. The authors explicitly study that centralized allocation problem, even while distinguishing it from the agents’ negotiation process.

An instance has resource kinds \(R\), a finite set \(T\) of complete utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_r\in[0,1]\). Each type’s utility \(u_t(B)\) for a bundle \(B\subseteq R\) is given by rational \(k\)-additive coefficients. The question is whether there is a mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:r\in B}x_{t,B}=q_r\), and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)>K\), for a given rational threshold \(K\). A solution is a finite list of type, bundle, and mass triples. Each agent still receives an integral bundle; only the fraction of a type receiving each bundle is continuous. The supplies are a co-modification: in a population of \(N\), there are \(Nq_r\) interchangeable copies of resource kind \(r\).

The anchor is Proposition 8 (Complexity wrt. \(k\)-additive form), proved in this paper. It says that for every \(k\ge2\), deciding whether some allocation has social welfare greater than a given threshold is NP-complete when utilities are represented in \(k\)-additive form. The reduction already works for \(k=2\) and two agents, one with zero utility: it reduces MAXIMUM 2-SATISFIABILITY by encoding truth assignments as bundles of resources and using a 2-additive utility whose value tracks the number of satisfied clauses.

The same reduction survives in the continuous question. Use two types of mass \(1/2\), with the clause-based utility for one and zero utility for the other, and set every resource supply to \(1/2\). For any bundle \(B\), assign all of the first type to \(B\) and all of the second type to \(R\setminus B\); this meets every supply exactly. Conversely, fractional mass splitting cannot raise the first type’s average utility above \(\max_B u_1(B)\). Thus the continuous optimum is exactly half the best assignment value in the reduction, with the threshold scaled accordingly. The mass relaxation does not remove the hardness: the combinatorics live in the number of resource kinds and their pairwise synergies, not in the number of agents. I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers.

A plausible regime is a large service or software-allocation platform with many automated agents, repeated copies of a fixed set of service or compute-resource kinds, and relatively few complete preference profiles. For example, two large cohorts might share their respective pairwise complementarity profiles over the same resource kinds. The relevant comparison is population \(N\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of types; the lead reduction itself uses just two types. That is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s welfare-allocation problem. The indifferent type is an edge case, but a remainder or reserve cohort with no contribution to the welfare objective gives it a possible operational interpretation.

A useful second anchor is Proposition 7 (Complexity wrt. bundle form), also proved here. It says that the threshold decision problem for maximum-social-welfare allocation is NP-complete when utilities are represented in bundle form; the paper proves this by reduction from SET PACKING. For its continuous mirror, Continuous Bundle-Form Welfare Allocation, use the same masses, supplies, and allocation variables, but represent each type’s utility by a list of its nonzero bundle values, with unlisted bundles valued at zero. I expect this version to be Class A: tractable. Its exponentially many possible bundle assignments form an LP, and pricing can be done in polynomial time: check listed nonzero bundles and optimize the additive resource-price term over the zero-valued bundles. The dual has only \(\tau+m\) variables, so separation and ellipsoid methods give a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the utility-list input size. In the paper’s SET PACKING reduction, fractional mass turns the discrete packing choice into fractional packing, which is an LP. This is a concrete case where discrete hardness can dissolve. The tractability claim depends on the explicit bundle-list representation; compact implicit utility encodings may have hard pricing.

These two anchors make the mirror useful in opposite ways: Proposition 8 identifies a hardness boundary that persists, while Proposition 7 offers a tractable continuous counterpart to a discrete NP-hard problem. The scope is the paper’s centralized welfare-allocation results in Propositions 7 and 8, not its results about negotiation sequences, convergence, or deal size. This model continuizes the population and its allocation across bundles; it does not make an individual bundle fractional.

My weakest point is the resource regime. The paper has a finite set of indivisible resources, while this mirror treats resources as repeatable kinds whose stock scales with population. That fits recurring service slots or compute units better than one-off assets, and an application with many agents but fixed unique inventory may not admit a useful continuum. The case remains plausible for scalable allocation settings because the modification preserves each agent’s complete utility over integral bundles and the paper’s utilitarian objective. It is a scenario-specific mirror, not a claim that all multiagent allocation is naturally high-multiplicity.

The follow-up questions are whether the finite-population grid approximates these continuous optima with useful guarantees, which compact utility representations retain polynomial pricing, and where the transition from Proposition 7’s tractable pricing to Proposition 8’s hard pricing occurs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the resource regime. If the paper’s finite set of indivisible goods stays fixed while the population grows, each good can reach only finitely many people. After normalizing welfare per capita, the gains from allocating those goods vanish; a pure atomless model cannot represent the exceptional recipients. Keeping the original inventory therefore gives a degenerate mirror.

But the proposed mirror scales inventory with population. That changes the setting to one with repeatable resource kinds, such as service slots or compute capacity. It is a material change, though a plausible one, and the programme explicitly allows such co-modification when it is part of a recognizable problem. I can’t make the claim that authors of this paper would reject that model.

The two anchors survive that objection. For Proposition 8, the proposed construction preserves the optimum exactly: put mass \(1/2\) on each of the clause-based and zero-utility types, and give every resource supply \(1/2\). Assigning the first type to bundle \(B\) and the second to its complement realizes the value of \(B\); splitting the first type across bundles cannot exceed the best bundle’s value. The continuous question is well-posed, and the hardness boundary is a legitimate result even though the reduction’s combinatorics lie in the resource set.

Proposition 7 is harder to dismiss. With explicit bundle lists, its mass-allocation formulation is an LP, and pricing can compare listed bundles against the best unlisted bundle under additive resource prices. That gives a credible tractability question, with the discrete set-packing choices becoming fractional. The paper explicitly studies centralized welfare maximization, so this is a mirror of a named result, not an unrelated negotiation question.

So the negative case is limited: fixed, unique inventory does not admit a useful atomless population limit. Once inventory scales with the population, both anchors look worthwhile under the programme’s stated criteria. I cannot honestly argue that no scenario yields a valuable continuous mirror.
