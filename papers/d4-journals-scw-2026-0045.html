What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

On a strict reading of your anchor rule, this paper has no named computational-complexity result. Its results concern Bayesian learning, the structure of welfare-optimal networks, and equilibrium—not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest possible lead is Proposition 2, proved here: under the paper’s parameter restrictions \(m=(2n+1)l\) and \(m=2n+1+(2n-1)k\), the egalitarian-optimal network is the complete-component network \(c\) when \(\sigma^2\tau^2>(m-(2n+1))/2\), the flower network \(f\) when \(\sigma^2\tau^2<(m-(2n+1))/2\), and either at equality. Here \(c\) partitions players into fully connected components of size \(2n+1\); \(f\) has a hub, one petal of size \(2n\), and \(k\) petals of size \(2n-1\). This explicit characterization lets one construct an optimum for those restricted instances, but the paper does not formulate or analyze a complexity problem. Proposition 3, also proved here, says the network from Proposition 2 is the most efficient Nash equilibrium; that too is a structural game-theoretic result, not a complexity claim.

There is a plausible positive research direction behind these results. In a large community learning about an agricultural technology or public policy, many people might share the same prior and signal parameters and connection capacity, while occupying only a few recurring communication roles. One could describe the population by masses of those roles and ask a planner to choose a feasible distribution of two-round communication environments that minimizes the worst posterior variance. That continuizes the population and keeps the paper’s learning-and-network-design question in view. Proposition 1’s loss formula, which depends on closed, local, and distance-two neighbors, suggests that an aggregate description might help organize the optimization.

But that sketch is not a prover-ready mirror of a named computational result. The paper supplies no mass-based network-feasibility model, and its flower depends on a single hub whose mass tends to zero as the population grows. A standard population distribution can lose that hub even though it matters to the finite society’s Rawlsian maximum. Preserving it would require extra structure for sparse networks and negligible but influential agents. Nor does the paper establish whether the resulting optimization would be tractable or hard. So the honest positive case is that this is a sensible computational extension for authors to recognize—not that the paper already contains a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational-complexity result to mirror. Propositions 1–3 characterize learning losses, Rawlsian-efficient networks, and an equilibrium network. None asks for or establishes a complexity classification or algorithm for a computational problem. Proposition 2 is the closest candidate, but it gives a closed-form characterization for restricted relationships between \(m\) and \(n\); calling that a computational anchor would stretch the programme’s criterion.

The best version of the proposed mirror would ask a planner to choose a network for a large population with a small number of prior and capacity types, minimizing worst-case posterior variance. But the object being chosen is a graph, not a distribution of agents over independent types. Each agent’s learning depends on the specific local and distance-two links, and feasible neighborhoods must fit together into a realizable network. Proposition 1’s loss formula summarizes some of that structure for an individual under simplifying assumptions; it does not make the population’s feasible networks into a linear optimization over type masses. A model over rooted neighborhood roles might be possible, but it would need additional consistency constraints for edges and overlapping neighborhoods. That is a new graph-optimization formalization, not a population mirror supplied by the paper.

The three apparent anchors do not fix this gap. Proposition 1 is a formula for evaluating loss, not a computational result; moreover, its analytic expression relies on weakly correlated priors and a particular assumption about perceived distant links. Proposition 2’s flower network depends on one hub connected to the population. In a standard mass distribution, that hub has zero mass, while it remains structurally decisive in the finite graph. Replacing the graph by dense links between positive masses changes the paper’s finite-neighbor communication process. Preserving the hub and sparse links calls for a specialized sparse-network limit, potentially with exceptional agents and explicit link-consistency rules. Proposition 3 adds a further obstacle: Nash equilibrium turns on unilateral deviations by individual players. An atomless population description does not by itself preserve those deviations or the influence of a negligible hub.

There is a real weak point in this negative case: one could build a sparse graph-limit model with repeated neighborhood roles and a separately represented hub, then ask algorithmic questions about efficient networks or equilibria. That could be worthwhile if the authors recognize it as their problem. But it would require a new formalization of the network object and its strategic constraints, and this paper provides no computational result to anchor it. The paper’s existing continuous-neighbor literature is analytic background, not a collision; the objection is that a computational population mirror has not yet been made concrete.
