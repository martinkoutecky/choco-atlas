What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • How quickly do finite Shapley allocations for replicated household types converge to this path allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the required anchor rule, this paper has no admissible anchor: the supplied text contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Section 4.2 describes a Shapley-value algorithm; Table 2 and the 3,000-home timing are empirical results, not numbered complexity statements. So I cannot honestly provide a continuous problem *per named anchor*.

The strongest positive candidate is the paper’s group-buying and fair-allocation problem in Section 4. It has a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large energy collective under a shared tariff, with many households but relatively few exact classes of predicted consumption and uncertainty. For this problem, a type can be the sufficient statistics \(\theta=(M_\theta,V_\theta)\) used by the paper’s coalition-value function: integrated expected consumption and integrated predictive variance. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be each type’s population share, with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\), and let \(N\) be the population scale. Then \(x_\theta=N\mu_\theta\) is the mass of type \(\theta\).

A precise candidate mirror is: given finitely many such types, rational shares and statistics, and the paper’s tariff parameters \(p,k\), compute the continuous Shapley-style allocation of the coalition value \(v(x)=p\sum_\theta x_\theta M_\theta-k\sqrt{\sum_\theta x_\theta V_\theta}\). Define the allocation to type \(\theta\) by \(a_\theta=x_\theta\int_0^1 \frac{\partial v(tx)}{\partial x_\theta}\,dt\). For \(S=\sum_\phi x_\phi V_\phi>0\), the solution is \(a_\theta=x_\theta(pM_\theta-kV_\theta/\sqrt S)\); if \(S=0\), it is \(a_\theta=x_\theta pM_\theta\). This is computable in \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations, and the allocations sum to \(v(x)\). It is the limiting allocation suggested by randomly ordering many replicated households: at population scale, the fraction preceding a household ranges over \([0,1]\), while predecessor type proportions concentrate around \(tx\).

This candidate mirrors the paper’s objective directly: allocate the collective value fairly among consumers. The authors could plausibly recognize it as the large-population form of their Shapley calculation, and the paper’s use of groups and 3,000 homes supports the scenario’s plausibility. Its expected outcome is Class A, by a closed-form calculation. Further questions include how quickly finite Shapley allocations approach this limit, and whether the result persists under richer coalition values or type-specific tariffs.

The weakest point is also the key limitation: the paper does not establish that its households have a small number of exact consumption types, and its stated fairness solution is the finite Shapley value. The continuum allocation needs a replicated-type regime and a limiting argument, so it is a credible candidate mirror, not a result that can be attached to a named anchor in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest case against is that there is no admissible anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing the complexity of a computational problem. Section 4.2 reports an algorithm, an informal linear-time initial approximation, and empirical comparisons; it does not give a worst-case complexity result. So the proponent has not supplied a continuous question attached to a named computational result.

That point does not dispose of their group-buying proposal. It is a coherent high-multiplicity regime: for the paper’s coalition value, \(M_i\) and \(V_i\) are sufficient statistics, so consumers sharing them are interchangeable for the Shapley calculation. The path-integral allocation is a plausible large-population limit. I cannot reject it on the grounds that it changes the finite Shapley value, makes the answer easy, or treats identical consumers as a type: those are not objections under the programme’s rules.

The strongest substantive reservation is narrower. The continuous allocation answers a newly formulated path-allocation problem; it does not continue the paper’s finite algorithm for averaging marginal contributions over subsets. And the reduction to types \((M,V)\) applies to the paper’s simplified coalition-value formula. If the mirror also needs to capture the paper’s coalition formation by consumption profile or preferences, those distinctions must enter the type, potentially leaving little compression. Restricting the question to the scalar formula avoids that problem, but makes the result a short calculation rather than a complexity landscape.

That is a reason to doubt whether this is a strong ChoCo project, not a reason to rule it out. The restricted mirror is sensible, and the paper gives a plausible large-collective setting for it. On the evidence here, I can make the case against the proposed *anchor* and question how much computational substance the best mirror has; I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
