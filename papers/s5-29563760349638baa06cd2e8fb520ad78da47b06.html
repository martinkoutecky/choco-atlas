The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right on the narrow formal point: Theorem 1 is Hoeffding’s inequality, and the NP-completeness claim is attributed to earlier work. There is no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary in this paper that classifies a computational social-choice problem. But “no computational result” goes too far: Algorithms 1 and 4 are named procedures with explicit runtime claims. Those claims could be treated as candidate anchors, even though they do not meet the paper’s strict theorem-number criterion.

For either algorithm, the central question is unilateral manipulation: can one voter change their ballot so the preferred candidate wins? In a society of \(n\) voters, represent the ballots by a distribution \(\mu^{(n)}\). Changing that one ballot from \(r\) to \(r'\) changes the distribution by
\[ \frac{1}{n}(\delta_{r'}-\delta_r), \]
which vanishes as \(n\) grows. If the limiting society has strict pairwise margins, the voter eventually cannot change any contest. If a margin is exactly tied, the ballot can matter, but the limit distribution alone loses the \(O(1/n)\) information that determines the contest’s direction. Since second-order Copeland depends on those pairwise win and loss signs, the ordinary continuous society either erases the manipulator’s effect or leaves the answer dependent on information it does not contain.

The best repair is to retain a distinguished voter as an atom alongside a continuous electorate, or to track first-order margin corrections. That can make a sensible hybrid sensitivity question, but the operative manipulator remains discrete; the population limit has not supplied a continuous mirror of the paper’s unilateral problem.

A positive-mass coalition is the stronger alternative: ask for the least mass of voters of a given type that must change reports to make the target win. That is a coherent continuous-population problem. It also changes the paper’s question from finding one voter’s successful ballot to coordinating a bloc and reallocating its reporting mass. The algorithms’ search over one voter’s possible permutations does not answer that question. It may be worthwhile as a separate coalition-manipulation project, but it is a weak basis for claiming this paper has a faithful mirror.

So the negative case is strongest for the paper’s actual unilateral results: their effect disappears in the population limit unless a special individual or vanishing-scale correction is retained. The universal claim that no worthwhile related scenario exists is less secure, because a continuous coalition problem is plausible. I would distinguish that adjacent problem from a mirror of this paper rather than claim it could never be worth studying.
