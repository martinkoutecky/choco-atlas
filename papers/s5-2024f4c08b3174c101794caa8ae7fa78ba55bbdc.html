The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, a Borda score vector \(a_1>\cdots>a_m\), and a list of \(\tau\) complete ranking types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), compute the exact winner set \(\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{c\in C}\sum_t \mu_t s(t,c)\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\), represented by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and binary-rational masses. Compute each candidate’s weighted Borda score and return the full set of maximizers; exact score summation takes \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations and polynomial bit time in the input length.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers aggregate Borda score computation and winner determination; it leaves out voter-specific secrecy, zero-knowledge proofs, self-tallying protocol design, and the Ethereum implementation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is Mass-Borda Tally. Its instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a list of \(\tau\) complete rankings, each with a binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\ge 0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For the paper’s Borda scores \(s(t,c)\), the task is to output
\[ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{c\in C}\ \sum_t \mu_t s(t,c). \]
A solution is the exact set of winners. Computing all scores takes \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations, with bit complexity polynomial in the input length. A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large organization or electorate with many voters but relatively few distinct complete rankings. This continuizes the population, and the authors would recognize it as Borda winner determination.

But I cannot make this a qualifying anchored case under your criteria. Proposition 1 is proved in the paper: with \(y_{ij}=\sum_{l<i}x_{lj}-\sum_{l>i}x_{lj}\), the masking exponents cancel, so \(\sum_i x_{ij}y_{ij}=0\) for every candidate \(j\). That identity lets observers recover the aggregate score from the encrypted ballots. It is a correctness identity, not a result classifying the complexity of a problem. The named Lemmas 1–6 concern secrecy and indistinguishability, not tractability or hardness. The paper’s \(O(k^2)\)-per-voter cost discussion and comparison table are unnumbered performance analysis, not named complexity results of the required kind. So there is no qualifying named computational anchor, and Mass-Borda Tally is only a plausible extension, not a problem I can honestly claim is anchored in one.

That distinction matters because the paper’s central contribution is a decentralized cryptographic protocol: each voter has an identity, independent secret keys, and an individual ballot, and the protocol relies on those voter-level objects for privacy and public verification. Replacing voters with ranking masses preserves Borda tallying but does not preserve the protocol question. The positive case is therefore narrow: a continuous population is natural for the paper’s underlying Borda election, but the paper supplies no named computational result that makes that mirror the requested continuization problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive weakness is that the paper proves no named computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. Proposition 1 is an algebraic cancellation identity that makes tally recovery work; Lemmas 1–6 concern privacy and indistinguishability. The tables and experiments measure the protocol’s cost, but do not establish a complexity classification for a computational social choice problem.

Mass-Borda Tally is the strongest replacement candidate: given ranking types with rational masses, compute the winners. But it asks only for the score aggregation that the protocol must support, not for a result this paper establishes about computational complexity. Its linear-time solution would be a useful baseline, yet it would not continuize the paper’s contribution: constructing a self-tallying, publicly verifiable protocol with voter privacy.

The closer mirror—asking whether those guarantees survive a continuum of voters—runs into a more fundamental mismatch. The protocol relies on each voter’s identity, independent secret keys, proofs, and individual bulletin-board transactions. Replacing voters by masses removes the objects those guarantees are about. Keeping them all preserves the finite voter-by-voter protocol, so the population has not become continuous in the relevant sense. A nonatomic version also makes privacy for a single voter vacuous; defining privacy for positive-mass groups would be a different threat model and a new security question, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

A large organization may well have many voters sharing rankings, so continuous Borda tallying is a sensible model of its aggregate preferences. That makes the tally mirror natural, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor or carry over the paper’s central protocol question. The negative case is strongest on that boundary: the paper has a continuous-looking social-choice input hiding inside it, but its proved contribution is tied to finite, individually authenticated participants.
