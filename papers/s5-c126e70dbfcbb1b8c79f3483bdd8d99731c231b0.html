Theorem 1 derives an affine update, Theorem 2 gives convergence conditions, Theorem 3 characterizes statistical efficiency, and Theorem 4 describes limiting beliefs. In particular, “efficient” in Theorem 3 means statistically optimal estimation, not polynomial-time computation. The paper studies social learning and estimation, rather than a social choice rule that selects winners or allocations.

The strongest possible mirror would define agent types by their signal structures, priors, utilities, and network roles; represent the population by a type distribution \(\mu\); and use a contact kernel \(W\) to describe interactions. Replacing finite sums with integrals would yield a legitimate continuous-population model of the paper’s dynamics. But that would continue its analytic questions about convergence and consensus; it would not mirror a computational result the paper established.

One could turn that model into an algorithmic project by asking, for example, how to change the type masses or contact kernel at minimum cost so that consensus becomes statistically efficient. That is a plausible research question, but it adds intervention choices, costs, and constraints absent from the paper. Its computational core would come from that new network-design problem. Likewise, encoding a fixed finite-type kernel gives a finite-dimensional interaction matrix, while preserving arbitrary individual network positions may require as many types as agents. Neither supplies a computational anchor in the paper itself.
