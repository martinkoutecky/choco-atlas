The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is at the level of the model. A plausible high-multiplicity setting is siting two unwanted facilities along a corridor with many residents grouped into a relatively small number of exposure types. A type \(t\) has a dip \(x_t\), the location where either facility is most harmful; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of residents. The decision remains an ordered pair of sites \((a,b)\), with \(0\le a\le b\le1\), and each type ranks pairs by the paper’s lexmin preference: first prefer greater distance from the nearer site, then, in a tie, greater distance from the farther site. Here \(n\) could be very large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct dip types is modest. That is a recognizable continuous-population version of the paper’s siting model.

The closest named results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in this thesis, but neither is a computational anchor of the required kind. Theorem 1 says that for each fixed \(0<a\le b<1\), the rule \(h_{(a,b)}\)—which selects \((1,1)\) when both dips are at most \(1/2\), \((0,0)\) when both are above \(1/2\) (including the stated boundary cases), \((a,b)\) when \(2y_1<a\le b<2y_2-1\), and \((0,1)\) otherwise—is strategyproof, anonymous, and unanimous. Theorem 2 says that a rule is strategyproof, anonymous, unanimous, and selects inner points if and only if it is \(h_{(a,b)}\) for some \(0<a\le b<1\). Theorem 2 is the paper’s strongest structural result, but these are axiomatic characterisation results: neither asserts a complexity classification or an algorithmic result.

A natural extension would take a rational, finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over dip types and ask for deterministic rules \(F\) mapping \(\mu\) to a pair of sites, with suitable distributional versions of the paper’s axioms. But that does not yet give a convincing computational mirror of Theorem 2. The paper’s strategyproofness concerns individual reports by two agents. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass and cannot change the distribution; preserving a meaningful incentive condition would require an additional convention, such as allowing a positive mass of a type to misreport. That changes the axiom. Nor does the paper specify an aggregate welfare objective that could simply be optimized instead. Adding one would produce a different siting problem.

So the population regime is plausible, and the authors might recognize the siting setting. The gap is that there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous-complexity question. The paper contains no named result asserting that a problem is in \(P\), hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Under the requested standard, there are therefore no qualifying anchors and no per-anchor continuous problem I can responsibly put to a prover. The weakest point is decisive: turning this model into a computational question appears to require replacing the paper’s axiomatic contribution with a new objective or a new incentive notion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 shows that \(h_{(a,b)}\) satisfies strategyproofness, anonymity, and unanimity; Theorem 2 characterizes the rules with those properties that select inner points. The eleven lemmas support those axiomatic claims. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or even a computational problem with an encoded input. Replacing the two dips with a population distribution would not turn these theorems into computational results.

The strongest proposed setting is still plausible: residents along a corridor could be grouped into dip types, with a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) and two facility locations as the outcome. But to extend these theorems, one must choose how the rule uses \(\mu\) and what counts as a report. Individual misreports leave an atomless population’s distribution unchanged, making individual strategyproofness vacuous. Allowing a positive mass to misreport gives a meaningful condition, but changes the paper’s individual incentive notion. Either extension could support a new axiomatic characterization; neither supplies a computational question from this paper. Adding an objective such as minimizing aggregate harm would create an optimization problem, but the paper defines no such objective, and its lexmin preferences do not specify one.

So the case against is strong on scope, but weak as a universal claim about the siting problem: the corridor scenario makes a continuous population model quite sensible. What fails is the proposed connection to this paper’s results under ChoCo’s computational remit. A valuable continuous siting project may exist here; the paper itself does not provide a computational anchor for it.
