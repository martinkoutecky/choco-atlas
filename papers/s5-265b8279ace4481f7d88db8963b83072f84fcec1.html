The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case anchored in this paper: it contains no numbered computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or another complexity classification.

The sole numbered result, Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that in simple coalition-formation scenarios satisfying Sovereignty, Equivalence, Redundancy, Context Independence, and Consistency of Expectations, the Proportionality and Stability laws hold with the critical resource \(X_i=\operatorname{Shi}\), the player’s unconditional Shapley value. It is an axiomatic result, not a computational one. The empirical comparisons across elections are not numbered complexity results either. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor continuous problems to hand to a prover.

The best possible lead is that real elections have many voters and relatively few party groupings: voter support could be represented as mass over voter types and used to derive parties’ seat shares. But that would make the electorate continuous while leaving the paper’s central objects—distinct parties, feasible party coalitions, and Shapley power—discrete. Continuizing the party players themselves would require a new model of divisible coalitions and a continuous analogue of the Shapley value. That could motivate new work, but this paper neither studies nor establishes a computational result for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with a decisive mismatch: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization: five conditions imply the Gamson–Shapley proportionality and stability laws. The other results are empirical comparisons and a policy-distance extension. None asks for a complexity classification or an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm.

The strongest proposed bridge—turn voter support into continuous mass, then compute coalition predictions—does not change that. The electorate supplies seat shares, but the paper’s players are political parties. Its coalition choices, compatibility constraints, and Shapley power remain attached to a finite set of distinct parties. Continuous voter proportions would be input to the same finite-party model; they would not create a computational question the paper poses.

A more ambitious mirror would make parties themselves a population of types and ask for a mass-level coalition and power allocation. That runs into a deeper mismatch: the paper’s Shapley value and stability notion depend on which parties can join or leave a coalition. In a nonatomic party population, individual parties have zero mass, so preserving those notions requires a new theory of power and coalition deviations. That could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new model, not a continuous version of one of this paper’s results.

The case is weakest if “mirror” is allowed to mean any computational problem suggested by the paper: for example, selecting a stable coalition from a large party compatibility structure. That is plausible work, but the paper does not formulate it as a computational problem, and making parties into a continuum adds a separate modeling step. On the paper’s actual results, the proponent’s voter-mass suggestion does not establish an anchor.
