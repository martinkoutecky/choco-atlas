The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive difficulty is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its numbered formal statements are definitions, and its findings compare observed persistence with stability concepts in a three-player experiment. It neither poses an algorithmic problem nor establishes a complexity result. The match between LFC/CPS predictions and the empirically stable networks at \(\bar q=0.5\), and the better fit of myopic concepts at stricter thresholds, are empirical findings—not discrete algorithms awaiting a continuous-population counterpart.

The strongest proposed mirror would take those findings as motivation for a large-population network game and ask which graphons satisfy myopic or farsighted stability, perhaps with an algorithm for finding them. But that runs into a basic mismatch. The paper’s stability concepts turn on deviations by one player or one consenting pair. In a graphon, a particular player or link has measure zero, so that deviation leaves the aggregate object unchanged. Stability defined only through aggregate graphon changes would make such deviations invisible. Allowing a positive mass of links to change would restore an aggregate effect, but that is a collective deviation, not the pairwise move the paper studies.

Nor does grouping agents by shared payoff parameters fix this. In these games, payoff depends on an agent’s position in the current network. A complete type would therefore need to capture relevant neighborhood and relational structure, which changes as links form and break. Encoding that structure in the types risks losing the small-support advantage that makes a population distribution useful. Modeling many independent copies of the three-player experiment avoids that problem, but then the continuum describes frequencies of group outcomes; it adds no population-level stability question or computational task.

The paper also cautions that the cooperative concepts are not formally defined for its repeated-payoff experimental setting; the authors use them to generate hypotheses about observed behavior. A graphon version would have to specify a new large-population game, a new notion of deviation, and a computational objective. That may be a worthwhile project in network formation, but the paper supplies no computational result for it to mirror, and its stated contribution is experimental prediction in small games. A continuum of time steps, repeated observations, or behavioral uncertainty would not address the programme’s population-continuization aim.

I cannot rule out a future computational theory of large network stability. The negative case is weaker at that broader field-level claim. But that would be a new research direction, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s results.
