The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational anchor in this paper. It states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a problem as tractable or hard. The “Proposition 1” in Section II.B is from Hout and McGann’s earlier work, and establishes an axiomatic implication: anonymity, neutrality, and nonnegative responsiveness imply weak plurality ranking (and, with two parties, the majority rule property). That is a social choice result, not a computational complexity result. Section IV reports computations and simulations, but gives no numbered complexity result to anchor.

The strongest positive lead I can offer is therefore unanchored: an exact continuous-population version of the paper’s partisan-symmetry calculation. Consider a state with a fixed plan of \(k\) districts and a continuous electorate of total mass \(1\). A voter type \(t\) specifies a district and a complete, rational piecewise-linear response \(q_t(p)\): the fraction of that type voting Democratic along the paper’s uniform-swing counterfactual when the statewide Democratic vote share is \(p\). The instance gives the support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and response curves satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t(p)=p\).

For each \(p\), let \(S(p)\) be the fraction of districts won by Democrats, counting a district when its Democratic vote mass exceeds half its total mass, under a fixed tie rule. The question is to compute the partisan-symmetry discrepancy
\[ G(p)=S(p)+S(1-p)-1 \]
for all \(p\), and its maximum absolute value. A solution is the exact piecewise description of \(G\) and that maximum. In a plausible high-multiplicity regime—millions of voters grouped into a modest number of district-by-response cohorts—\(\tau=|T|\) could be far smaller than the electorate. This keeps the paper’s district outcomes and counterfactual symmetry question, while making voter mass continuous. With piecewise-linear rational inputs, enumerating curve breakpoints and district-majority crossings should make this evaluation tractable in \(k,\tau\), and the input bit length.

This candidate is recognizable from Section IV’s seats-votes calculation under uniform swing, but it cannot honestly be presented as a mirror of a named result in the paper. The weakest point is substantive too: the authors’ analysis uses local perturbations to represent uncertainty, and this deterministic formulation does not capture that statistical component. So the case supports a plausible continuous-population computational question arising from the paper; it does not supply the required anchored complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result to anchor a mirror. The “Proposition 1” in Section II.B is an axiomatic implication cited from Hout and McGann, not a complexity or algorithmic result of this paper. Section IV’s symmetry scores and simulations are empirical calculations; the paper states no computational problem whose complexity it classifies.

The strongest candidate is the exact seats-votes calculation proposed in the case for. A type defined by district and response curve is a coherent high-multiplicity model: many voters can share each type, and fractional mass is meaningful. I would not reject it on multiplicity grounds. But once those curves are given, each district’s vote share is a finite weighted sum, \(V_d(p)=\sum_{t:\,d(t)=d}\mu_t q_t(p)\). The seat count changes only where a district’s curve crosses its majority threshold. With piecewise-linear rational inputs, computing \(G(p)\) reduces to scanning breakpoints and threshold crossings. That is a well-defined calculation, but the population continuum contributes little to its computational structure.

The harder work in the paper is estimating counterfactual vote movement and local uncertainty. Supplying response curves already encodes those assumptions; a population distribution alone does not determine how voters would switch under a uniform swing. A stronger question—such as whether symmetry persists across plausible response curves—could be useful, but it needs a specified uncertainty model or feasible set. That would be a new modeling project, rather than a computational result this paper establishes.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored ChoCo example: it offers no named complexity result, and its most plausible continuous calculation appears to be a straightforward finite threshold sweep once the statistical inputs are fixed. The universal claim is weaker. A district-by-response cohort model is sensible, and a carefully grounded robustness problem might be worthwhile; this paper alone does not rule those out.
