The continuous mirror question

Given schools, a finite set of complete student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational priority scores and utilities, capacity shares, and an application limit \(\ell\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium of the nonatomic deferred-acceptance game. A strategy specifies for each type a distribution over ordered lists of at most \(\ell\) schools; the resulting type mass is assigned by school priorities up to capacity, splitting mass uniformly at cutoff ties. Require that no infinitesimal student of any type can gain more than \(\varepsilon\) by changing their list.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic school-choice game with complete types carrying population mass \(\mu_t\). Decision variables are each type’s probabilities over application lists; deferred acceptance allocates the submitted mass under capacity shares and priority scores, and each type chooses lists to maximize its expected school utility.

The objection that survived

Finite-cohort hypergeometric admission effects do not automatically converge to nonatomic cutoff outcomes, and an infinitesimal student’s deviation has no aggregate effect; the proposed game therefore needs its own limit and complexity analysis.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is Mass-Constrained School Choice Equilibrium. The anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in the paper: for its finite-support agent-form game \(\mathcal G'_\mu\), computing an exact equilibrium is in FIXP, and computing an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium for \(\varepsilon>0\) is in PPAD. The theorem treats each of the \(k\) types as a player, with actions in \(A\) and payoffs inherited from the Bayesian game; its proof applies standard normal-form equilibrium results to a payoff matrix of size \(k|A|^k\). So this is a computational anchor, but only an upper-bound classification: it proves neither hardness nor polynomial-time solvability. It is proved in the paper, using general results cited there. [Theorem 4.2 in the arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.09089)

Here is the continuous problem. An instance has \(m\) schools, a finite set \(T\) of \(\tau\) complete student types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational school capacities \(\kappa_j\) measured as shares of the population, and for each type and school a rational priority score \(s_j(t)\) and utility \(v_j(t)\). A type includes everything the market uses about a student: preferences, school values, scores, and application constraints. Each massless student chooses an ordered list of at most \(\ell\) schools, so \(A\) is the set of such lists.

A strategy is a distribution \(p_t\in\Delta(A)\) for each type. It produces mass \(\mu_t p_t(a)\) of type \(t\) submitting list \(a\). Run student-proposing deferred acceptance on these masses: schools hold the highest-priority applicants up to their capacity, reject excess mass, and rejected students proceed to the next school on their list. If a priority tie meets a capacity cutoff, split that type’s mass uniformly at the cutoff. Individual students still receive one school or remain unmatched; the fractional quantities describe type-level mass and lottery probabilities.

The task is to output the strategy distributions and their resulting allocation so that, for every type \(t\), no unilateral change of application list raises expected utility by more than \(\varepsilon\):
\[ \max_{a\in A} U_t(a;p)-\sum_{a\in A}p_t(a)U_t(a;p)\leq\varepsilon. \]
Here \(U_t(a;p)\) is the utility of an infinitesimal type-\(t\) student using list \(a\) against the aggregate strategy \(p\), including the lottery at any cutoff tie. Setting \(\varepsilon=0\) gives the exact-equilibrium variant. The students’ objective remains their own expected utility; this is equilibrium computation, not a planner’s optimization problem.

A plausible regime is a large centralized school intake in which applications and priorities depend on a relatively small catalog of complete profiles: for example, combinations of standardized grade bands, priority scores, and common preference patterns. Then there may be hundreds of thousands of applicants but far fewer distinct behaviorally relevant types. Each school’s seats scale with cohort size, so \(\kappa_j\) stays fixed as the number of students grows. This is plausible for the kind of clearinghouse the paper discusses, which itself describes a system with more than 900,000 applicants. The mirror retains students’ constrained application choices, school priorities, deferred acceptance, and utility incentives; it changes the society from finitely many strategic players to population mass.

I would provisionally expect the general \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium problem to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C): population size disappears, but the choices of each type still interact through aggregate application masses and capacity cutoffs. I do not see a basis for claiming that Theorem 4.2’s complexity transfers, since that theorem gives no lower bound. The key follow-up questions are whether approximate equilibria admit a polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\); whether hardness can be proved for the mass game itself; and when finite-cohort equilibria converge to equilibria of the mass model.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 4.2’s equilibrium-computation problem, not the paper’s results about stable outcomes, uniqueness, or approximation of a type distribution while the number of students stays fixed. The weakest point is also the main distinction to defend: the paper already allows a continuous distribution over student types, but its game still has a fixed finite number of students, and its theorem’s \(k\) types satisfy \(k\geq n\). A reviewer could regard the proposed mirror as a mean-field restatement rather than a new computational question. The case survives only if the authors accept the scaled-capacity, population-mass equilibrium as their school-choice problem in a genuinely large-cohort regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.2 does not study complexity driven by population multiplicity. Its FIXP and PPAD claims come from treating the \(k\) support types as players in an agent-form game, then applying standard results for finite games with an explicit payoff table. The number \(n\) of students remains fixed, and types are drawn without replacement. The proposed mass game changes that object: with positive masses and scaled capacities, one student’s deviation has zero effect on the aggregate cutoffs. That is a coherent nonatomic equilibrium problem, but it is not a direct continuation of the theorem’s finite-player computation, and the theorem supplies neither a hardness result nor a useful algorithmic bound for it. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09089)

The best version of the mirror can answer that objection by making its aim explicit: study approximate equilibria in large centralized intakes with recurring complete student types, capacity shares, and lottery treatment at priority cutoffs. That regime is plausible, and the paper itself motivates its model with very large clearinghouses. Nor is the issue that the resulting assignments are fractional: type-level masses can describe individuals who each receive an integral outcome. So I cannot rule out that the authors would recognize this as their school-choice problem.

What remains a serious burden is the finite-to-continuum bridge. The paper’s hypergeometric sampling and finite-cohort admission probabilities disappear when a student becomes negligible relative to the market. Deferred acceptance also does not automatically commute with that limit: recent work shows that finite sampled markets can remain far from the continuum DA assignment without additional conditions, while giving conditions that restore convergence. That makes the proposed equilibrium problem a new large-market extension whose relation to the paper’s computation would itself need to be established. [Grigoryan, “A continuum model as a limit of large finite matching markets”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.3982/TE5478)

Continuum school-choice models and cutoff methods already exist, which narrows the novelty claim; for example, [Kapur’s nonatomic admissions model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.01340) studies continuum admissions and DA-style cutoff dynamics. But those treatments do not, on the evidence here, classify the complexity of computing equilibria in the constrained strategic-application game. Under ChoCo’s rules, their analytic focus is not a collision, and their existence is not a reason to reject the mirror.

So the strongest negative case is that Theorem 4.2 is a thin anchor for a population-limit question: its generic upper bounds do not transfer, and the proposed mass equilibrium needs its own limit and complexity analysis. That is a real reason to treat the proposal as an extension rather than a faithful mirror. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. Given the stated large-cohort regime, my honest assessment is that the negative case is weak.
