The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: when \(|C\cup S|\ge 3\), the stable college-admissions rule is implementable in Nash equilibria. Proposition 3, also proved here, says that game form \(A\) implements the stable rule in strong Nash equilibria. These are strategic implementation results: they concern whether a game form has the desired equilibrium outcomes. Neither asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifiable by complexity. Proposition 2’s non-implementability result has the same limitation. So this paper has no named computational-complexity anchor of the kind your brief requires; treating implementation as tractability would change what the results say.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the stable-rule results. In a large residency match, student types could be complete rankings over program types and the outside option; college types could be responsive rankings over student types, with capacities. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of students of type \(t\), and let \(\lambda_h\) be the number of campuses of type \(h\) per student. Replicated cohorts and programs could have very many agents but relatively few preference and capacity types. Scaling campus supply with the cohort is a natural co-modification. The paper itself points to hospital–intern matching as an application, though it does not establish that actual markets have small support.

One precise adjacent question is Continuum Nash Implementation of the Stable Rule. Given finite student and college type sets, binary-rational masses \((\mu_t)_t\) and \((\lambda_h)_h\), capacities, and strict student and responsive college preferences, ask whether there is a measurable game form whose Nash-equilibrium outcomes, for every such preference profile, are exactly the stable measurable matchings of that society. A solution is the game form together with a proof of that equality. Theorem 2 is the conceptual lead for this question; Proposition 3 suggests the corresponding strong-Nash version. But neither theorem establishes the continuum claim, and this question is about mechanism existence rather than computational complexity, so I cannot honestly assign it to the programme’s tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hard classes.

The weakest point is also the central one: passing from named agents to a few complete types may be plausible for repeated cohorts, but Nash implementation depends on agents’ strategic deviations. In an atomless society, a single agent’s deviation has zero mass, so the equilibrium notion and the mechanism may need substantial reformulation. The paper’s results make stable matching a credible object to study in a continuous population; they do not supply the computational anchor needed for a positive case under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 and Proposition 3 are the only anchors the case for raises, and both concern existence of a game form that implements the stable rule. Neither gives a computational problem with an encoded input, an algorithmic output, or a complexity claim. The proposed “Continuum Nash Implementation” question preserves that mismatch: its answer is a measurable game form and a proof about equilibrium outcomes. That may be a worthwhile mechanism-design question, but it does not put this paper’s result into ChoCo’s computational programme.

The best computational repair would be to ask for an algorithm that finds a stable allocation from type masses and capacities. That is a sensible adjacent question, but it drops the paper’s implementation result: finding a stable allocation is different from constructing a game form whose equilibria implement the stable rule. The paper does not supply an algorithmic result to continuize. And if the target remains Nash implementation, the population limit forces a substantive choice about deviations. An atomless student’s deviation has zero mass; treating a whole type as one deviating player instead changes the strategic unit; keeping finitely many replicated students keeps the discrete game. Those may each define research questions, but they do not yield a canonical computational mirror of Theorem 2 or Proposition 3.

There is a real weakness in this negative case: high-multiplicity matching is plausible, and the paper itself points to hospitals and interns. Azevedo and Leshno also give a continuum college-admissions model with a continuum of students and stable matchings characterized by supply and demand; that is evidence the population regime makes sense, though it is an analytic treatment rather than a computational collision ([Azevedo and Leshno, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1086/687476)). So I can make a strong case that the proponent’s implementation anchors do not meet the programme’s computational brief. I cannot honestly establish the stronger universal claim that no worthwhile computational question could be built around continuous matching.
