The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify an eligible anchor: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its closest computational content is the minimum-payment LP in Section 5, constraints (1)–(3), extended for collusion in (4)–(5), with a worked instance in Figure 2. The paper computes with that LP, but it is not a numbered result, and the cited results [4] and [17] are not results of this paper. I shouldn’t present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

The best candidate mirror is still fairly natural. Consider a high-volume service used by many clients under one SLA during a fixed reporting period. Let each type \(\theta\) be a complete incentive-relevant client profile—for example, its reporting incentives and belief parameters—and let \(\mu_\theta\) be that type’s share of the cohort. Clients receive observations \(o\) from a finite quality-outcome space and submit reports \(r\). The RM chooses one payment rule \(\tau(r,r')\), where \(r'\) is the peer report used as reference. Given the type masses, observation distributions, and the paper’s incentive assumptions, the question is: find a payment rule minimizing expected total payout per unit of client mass, subject to truthful reporting being worthwhile for every type and the stated small-coalition robustness condition holding. A solution is the payment rule and its minimum expected cost.

This regime fits the paper’s own setting: Section 2 assumes a large group of clients share an SLA in a period, and Section 5 designs payments for their feedback. The continuous object here is the distribution of client profiles, not the probability distribution over QoS outcomes; those outcome probabilities are part of the mechanism’s input. The candidate problem appears tractable as a finite LP when the type and observation supports are explicit. The authors would likely recognize it as their payment-design problem aggregated across a large cohort, though the paper does not study heterogeneity in client incentive parameters this way.

That makes this a plausible positive mirror, but not an anchored case under your rules. Its weakest point is precisely that the paper’s LP is already formulated and computed without population masses, so the mirror may amount to a weighted extension rather than a new complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing the complexity of a computational problem. Its closest candidate is Section 5’s LP for choosing minimum-cost truthful-reporting payments, with the collusion constraints in Section 6. That is a computational formulation, but not a named complexity result of the kind this review was asked to mirror.

The fit is weak even for that candidate. Within a period, clients share an SLA and are treated equally. The LP’s variables and constraints depend on the QoS attributes, their values, and the incentive parameters—not on how many clients report. Increasing the cohort size \(n\) scales expected total payouts; it does not change the payment-design problem. The clients’ observations are draws from the advertised QoS distributions, so turning those observations into “types” would continuize outcomes or uncertainty, not the population in the programme’s sense.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s heterogeneous-client model: let stable, incentive-relevant profiles be types, and optimize one payment rule across their mass distribution. That could be a sensible research question for a large service market. But its computational content comes from adding type-dependent incentive constraints that the paper does not formulate, rather than from continuizing one of its stated computational results. It is a plausible follow-on in mechanism design, not an anchored mirror of this paper.

That makes the negative case strong for rejecting this paper as an anchored mirror, but weak as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise from it. Large client cohorts are natural here, and a type-heterogeneous extension may be worth studying on its own. The honest limit is that it would be a new problem, while the paper supplies no qualifying computational result for this task.
