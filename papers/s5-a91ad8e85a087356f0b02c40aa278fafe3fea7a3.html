The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(\nu^1_a,\nu^2_b\), utilities \(u_{tab}\), and accuracy \(\epsilon>0\), with feasible positive utility for each positive-mass type, can one compute a feasible allocation \(x_{tab}\ge 0\) within additive error \(\epsilon\) of the maximum of \(\sum_t \mu_t\log U_t\), where \(U_t=\frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_{a,b}u_{tab}x_{tab}\), subject to \(\sum_{a,b}x_{tab}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{tab}=\nu^1_a\), and \(\sum_{t,a}x_{tab}=\nu^2_b\)?

The model it lives in

A society distributed over complete student utility types, with type masses \(\mu_t\), paired seat types, and per-capita supplies \(\nu^1_a,\nu^2_b\); decision variables \(x_{tab}\) allocate student mass to seat pairs, maximizing weighted Nash welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns Theorem 2’s multi-good assignment setting and its regularized optimization approach; it leaves the paper’s other market models, fairness bounds, and experiments outside the proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the typed problem admit an additive-\(\epsilon\) algorithm polynomial in the type and resource supports, input bit length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\)?
  • Can the paper’s entropic Frank–Wolfe and Sinkhorn approach yield that guarantee with explicit dependence on regularization and the smallest positive type mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s 1LF2G problem: allocate pairs of course-seat types to a large student population to maximize Nash welfare. The paper’s own course-allocation example makes this a plausible setting, and its multi-good algorithm is the strongest anchor for it.

Take a large university system with \(N\) students, grouped into \(\tau\) types by their complete cardinal utility table over pairs of course types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of students of type \(t\). There are two course families; within each, seats with identical roles in the utility tables form a resource type, with per-student supplies \(\nu^1_a\) and \(\nu^2_b\). For example, \(N\) might be tens of thousands while \(\tau\) is a few hundred. A type must describe everything that affects the problem, so students in one type have the same utility for every pair of course types.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover, Typed Two-Family Nash Assignment. The input is the rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(\nu^1_a,\nu^2_b\), a rational utility table \(u_{tab}\), and an accuracy \(\epsilon>0\). Assume positive utility for every positive-mass type is feasible, and that the supplies in each family sum to one. Choose \(x_{tab}\ge 0\), the mass of type-\(t\) students assigned the bundle type \((a,b)\), subject to
\[ \sum_{a,b}x_{tab}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,b}x_{tab}=\nu^1_a,\qquad \sum_{t,a}x_{tab}=\nu^2_b. \]
The per-student expected utility of type \(t\) is \(U_t=\frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_{a,b}u_{tab}x_{tab}\). The task is to return a feasible \(x\) whose value is within \(\epsilon\) of the maximum of \(\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t\). The solution is the mass allocation \(x\), not merely the optimum value.

This is a population continuum: student mass is distributed across types and the decision assigns that mass across bundle types. It is not the claim that fractional outcomes alone make the paper continuous; the paper already uses fractional assignments. If the masses have denominator \(N\), then \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(\nu^r_a=s^r_a/N\) describe a finite cohort and its per-capita seat supplies. Each type’s row \(x_{tab}/\mu_t\) gives the common ex-ante assignment for a student of that type. Averaging assignments within a type preserves feasibility, and concavity of the logarithm means it cannot lower Nash welfare. Thus the formulation has a clear finite-clone dictionary. Scaling seat supplies with the cohort is a necessary part of this mirror.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the full paper’s §5.1.3. It states that, for fixed positive entropy parameter \(\zeta\), the generalized Frank–Wolfe algorithm converges linearly in its number of iterations to an optimum of its entropically regularized problem (29); each iteration takes \(\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{m n^{m+1}\|\mathbf g\|_\infty^2}{4\log(n)\zeta^2}\right)\) arithmetic operations. Here the objective is the paper’s smoothed log-utility sum \(f(x)\) plus \(\zeta h(x)\), with \(h(x)=-\sum x(\log x-1)\); the paper says the regularized optimum converges to the unregularized one as \(\zeta\to0\). In 1LF2G, \(m=2\), so the assignment tensor has \(n^3\) entries. The authors state the theorem using their cited generalized Frank–Wolfe and multi-marginal Sinkhorn machinery; it is their algorithmic result, not a theorem cited as someone else’s result. [Theorem 2 in the full paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.10704).

I expect this typed problem to be Class A for approximation, with fixed numbers of resource families and an explicitly supplied utility table. Its feasible region has \(\tau q_1q_2\) variables and linear marginal constraints; its objective is a weighted sum of logarithms of linear utilities. That is a polynomial-size convex optimization problem in the type supports. The theorem makes the continuous optimization technology especially recognizable to these authors, though it does not itself prove the typed algorithm or a bit-complexity bound for it.

The paper’s cited PPAD-completeness result for approximate Hylland–Zeckhauser equilibrium is not a second anchor here: it concerns a different mechanism and problem. This mirror covers only 1LF2G and the paper’s multi-good algorithm, not its other models.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 2 analyzes a regularized finite-agent problem, not this type-compressed input, and its per-iteration arithmetic count is not a total binary-input complexity bound for the unregularized problem. The paper provides no named hardness result for its Nash-bargaining models. So the theorem supports a well-motivated continuous question and an expected tractability route, rather than proving that route for the mirror. Further questions include how the approximation bound depends on the smallest positive type mass, whether the method extends cleanly to multiple or variable numbers of resource families, and whether applications requiring ex-post deterministic multi-way matchings need a separate integrality or rounding result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that Theorem 2 does not establish a complexity result for the typed society. It analyzes a regularized problem with \(n\) named agents, for fixed \(\zeta\); its per-iteration arithmetic bound does not give a running time in the mirror’s parameters \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\epsilon\), or a bound for removing the regularization. A typed version would need its own analysis. So Theorem 2 is evidence that the authors have relevant optimization machinery, but it is not itself a result about continuous populations.

The stronger objection is that Typed Two-Family Nash Assignment is an exact aggregation of a finite 1LF2G instance with duplicate utility rows. If the masses and supplies have denominator \(N\), with \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(\nu^r_a=s^r_a/N\), then \(x_{tab}/\mu_t\) gives each of the \(n_t\) clones the same fractional assignment. The finite objective divided by \(N\) is exactly \(\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t\). That makes the proposed mirror a quotient of the paper’s existing convex program, rather than a new allocation geometry.

But that objection cuts only so far: \(N\) can be exponentially large in the bit length of the rational masses, while the typed input has size governed by \(\tau\) and the utility table. That compression is precisely the high-multiplicity regime the programme is meant to study. A polynomial-size convex formulation also makes the problem look tractable, but an easy or familiar answer is not grounds here to reject a well-posed mirror.

I cannot make the universal negative case convincingly. The course-allocation scenario supplies plausible repeated types; the objective and constraints aggregate cleanly; and the mirror can ask a computational question about the population while retaining the paper’s Nash-welfare model. The theorem is a weak anchor for that question, and the paper’s multi-way setting leaves implementation questions, but neither defeats the better mirror. No identity dependence or vanishing limit supplies a fundamental obstruction. The honest negative case is weak.
