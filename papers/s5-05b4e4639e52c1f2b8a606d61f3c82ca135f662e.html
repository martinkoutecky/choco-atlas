Why no mirror

Theorem 1 gives a constructive finite-improvement route to a pure Nash equilibrium, so it has computational character. But in the proposed continuum, an individual deviation changes no mass, and replacing unilateral Nash stability with a Wardrop condition changes the problem the theorem addresses. The proposed equilibrium search is a possible follow-on, not a continuous analogue of this result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For a coalition structure \(C\), it defines \(\Phi(C)=\tfrac12\sum_i\widehat u_i(C)\), where \(\widehat u_i\) is the sum of \(i\)’s values for coalition members after each value \(v_{ij}\) is multiplied by \(1+\alpha\) when \(i\) and \(j\) are friends. The theorem proves that a player’s utility change from a unilateral move equals the change in \(\Phi\). Thus \(\Phi\) is an exact potential: a pure Nash equilibrium exists, and every sequence of strictly improving moves terminates. The paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar; Theorem 1 is its closest algorithmic anchor, but it gives no polynomial bound on convergence.

A plausible mirror is Block-Continuum SCASHG Equilibrium. Consider a large organization with many employees but a fixed, small set \(\mathcal T\) of recurring roles. A type \(t\) specifies an employee’s valuation \(v_{ts}\) of each role \(s\), and their symmetric friendship intensity \(q_{ts}\) with each role. Assume agents of a type are interchangeable under these descriptions and that ties mix uniformly across types. The organization has \(K\) team slots. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of employees of type \(t\), and let \(x_{t\ell}\) be the fraction of the whole workforce that is type \(t\) and joins team \(\ell\), with \(\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}x_{t\ell}=\mu_t\).

In this block-mixing regime, a type-\(t\) employee’s incentive from joining team \(\ell\), up to terms that do not depend on the chosen team, is

\[ u_{t\ell}(x)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal T}x_{s\ell}v_{ts}(1+\alpha q_{ts}). \]

The first part is the value of the teammates; the second captures the value an employee adds to friends’ coalition utilities. Define \(w_{ts}=v_{ts}(1+\alpha q_{ts})\). The problem given to a prover is: from rational \(\mu,v,q,\alpha\) and \(K\), produce a feasible \(x\) such that every type assigned positive mass to a team receives at least as much incentive there as in any other team. Formally, \(x_{t\ell}>0\) implies \(u_{t\ell}(x)\ge u_{tj}(x)\) for every \(j\). That is a type-symmetric Wardrop equilibrium. The potential to investigate is

\[ \Phi(x)=\frac12\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sum_{t,s\in\mathcal T}x_{t\ell}x_{s\ell}w_{ts}. \]

Because \(w\) is symmetric, a maximizing feasible \(x\) satisfies the equilibrium condition: its marginal potential for assigning type \(t\) to a team is precisely \(u_{t\ell}(x)\).

This is a population mirror, not a fractional-outcome one: employees still join whole teams; the mass variables record the proportions making each choice. The regime is \(N\gg\tau\), with many employees sharing each of a few complete role types. Fixed team slots suit an organization with recurring projects, while the block-mixing social graph is a deliberate restriction of the paper’s arbitrary graph model. The mirror covers the existence-and-potential result of Theorem 1, not the paper’s price-of-anarchy or price-of-stability bounds.

My expectation is tractable for equilibrium existence: the feasible mass assignments form a compact set and the continuous potential attains a maximum. That does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for producing an exact equilibrium. Finding one efficiently is a natural follow-up, as are bounds on how quickly discrete high-multiplicity equilibria approximate the continuum one.

The weakest point is the block-mixing assumption. An arbitrary friendship graph can make an employee’s exact friend list strategically relevant; averaging ties into \(q_{ts}\) loses that information unless the instance is genuinely exchangeable or the model restricts assignments to be type-symmetric. I think the mirror remains plausible as a study of the block-structured regime, but it should not be presented as a limit of every SCASHG instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper proves no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 proves an exact potential exists, so a pure Nash equilibrium exists and every sequence of improving moves terminates. It gives no running-time bound or classification for finding an equilibrium. The remaining results concern welfare ratios. That leaves no complexity result for a continuous mirror to continue.

The proposed block model is a reasonable high-multiplicity regime. A dense, deterministic block friendship graph can preserve the altruism term as population grows, and a fixed set of teams can make mass assignments a sensible description. So I would not dismiss it on the ground that people or friendships must remain individuated in every scenario.

In that regime, however, the finite improvement-path claim loses its content in the limit: an infinitesimal agent’s move changes no aggregate mass. Replacing it with a Wardrop condition and a potential functional gives a valid continuum game; compactness gives equilibrium existence. Finding an exact equilibrium would be a computational question, but it is a new question about solving a quadratic optimization or complementarity problem. Theorem 1 does not analyze that task, and its termination proof supplies no algorithmic bound for it.

The same issue remains in a broader model. Preserving arbitrary friendship neighborhoods requires types to retain the network detail that drives individual incentives, which can eliminate the intended compression. Averaging neighborhoods into type-level densities restores a compact model for block-structured societies, while changing the instances and equilibrium computation under study.

So the strongest negative case is that this paper offers a potential-game existence and convergence result, not a computational result of the kind the programme is set up to continuize. I would record the dense-block equilibrium model as a plausible follow-on, rather than a worthwhile mirror of a result in this paper. That conclusion has a real weakness: if the programme accepts computational equilibrium search as an anchor in its own right, the block model could merit study.
