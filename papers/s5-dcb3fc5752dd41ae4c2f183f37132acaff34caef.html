The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 11, proved in this paper in Appendix A.2; its \(k=1\) case is Theorem 1 in the main text. It says that, for two agents with additive, possibly unnormalized valuations and any fixed \(k\ge1\), deciding whether a complete allocation satisfying both EF-\(k\) and EQ-\(k\) exists is NP-hard. The proof reduces from PARTITION: ordinary goods encode the numbers, and dummy goods worth more than their total force the two agents to receive exactly equal-valued sets of ordinary goods. The theorem is proved here, not cited.

The mirror I would hand to a prover is Continuous EF-\(k\)+EQ-\(k\) Feasibility. Its input is a finite item catalogue \(M\), a finite set \(T\) of complete additive valuation types \(v_t\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), and a rational per-capita supply \(q_g\in[0,1]\) for each item \(g\). An allocation is a nonnegative mass \(\lambda_{t,S}\) for each type \(t\) and integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni g}\lambda_{t,S}=q_g \quad\text{for every }g\in M. \]
Thus each person receives a whole bundle; only the fraction of a type’s population receiving each bundle can be fractional. The question is whether such an allocation exists in which every pair of positive-mass outcomes \((t,S)\), \((t',S')\) satisfies both fairness guarantees: there is a set \(D_E\subseteq S'\) of at most \(k\) items with \(v_t(S)\ge v_t(S'\setminus D_E)\), and a set \(D_Q\subseteq S'\) of at most \(k\) items with \(v_t(S)\ge v_{t'}(S'\setminus D_Q)\). A solution is the finite list of bundles and masses \(\lambda_{t,S}\). This asks about fairness between people’s realized bundles, not fractional average bundles.

A natural regime is a large employer or campus allocating indivisible resources to cohorts with shared, complete valuation profiles. For the two-type version related to Theorem 11, let each type have mass \(1/2\), and let each item have supply \(1/2\) per capita: in a population of \(N\) people, each item then has \(N/2\) copies. The number of people can be very large while the type support remains \(\tau=2\). More generally, one might have millions of people and a modest number of types. This is the usual high-multiplicity interpretation: for \(N\) agents with \(n_t\) members of each type and \(c_g\) copies of each item, set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(q_g=c_g/N\). The discrete allocation is a solution whose masses lie on the \(1/N\) grid; the continuous problem allows arbitrary rational masses.

I would provisionally expect hardness to transfer (Class B): Theorem 11’s PARTITION structure lives in the items and their values, and every person in the mirror still receives an integral bundle. That makes the mirror a meaningful test of whether population mass alone removes the hardness. The prediction is not a transferred proof. The continuum can split a type across different bundles, which may dissolve the exact balancing obstruction; determining whether it does is one of the questions the mirror creates. Other natural questions are whether feasibility is tractable for fixed \(\tau\), and whether structured valuation domains make the bundle-support problem easier.

This preserves the paper’s underlying question—whether indivisible goods can be fully allocated while satisfying both envy and equitability relaxations—and changes the population to a distribution over types, with per-capita supplies scaled accordingly. I would expect the authors to recognize that as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime. The mirror covers deterministic EF-\(k\)+EQ-\(k\) feasibility for additive goods, especially Theorem 11 and its main-text \(k=1\) case; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s chores or randomized results.

The weak point is that the anchor has only two agents, and its reduction is driven by item values rather than population size. In the mirror, agents of the same type may receive different integral bundles, so the reduction’s exact partition need not survive. That limits what the anchor establishes about complexity. It does not make the mirror a different fairness problem: the same complete-allocation and individual-bundle comparisons remain in force, while the relaxation is specifically in how mass is distributed across people of each type.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 11 is a poor anchor for a continuous mirror. Its PARTITION reduction relies on a fixed stock of one-off goods: the \(3k\) dummy goods force the two agents to receive particular numbers of dummies, and each ordinary good must go wholly to one agent or the other. Those choices encode the subset sum. In a high-multiplicity version with positive per-capita supplies, each good becomes a category with copies scaling with the population. A type can then be split across bundle outcomes, and the reduction’s per-agent counting argument no longer applies. Keeping the original finite stock instead makes per-capita supply vanish as the population grows. So the paper does not specify a canonical supply regime for this anchor; the proposed model is a reasonable repeated-inventory extension, but the theorem itself does not motivate its particular supplies.

That objection does not defeat the best alternative. Theorem 4 already has a natural high-multiplicity regime: 31 valuation types, with mass \(83/113\) on one type and \(1/113\) on each other type, and per-capita supply \(1/113\) for each of the 341 goods. Its nonexistence argument lifts to that continuous instance. The repeated type has approval supply \(165/113\), so its average utility is at most \(165/83<2\); some positive mass must receive utility at most 1. EQ1 then caps every realized utility at 2. Since every four goods share an approving type, EF1 caps each bundle at three goods. But total goods supply is \(341/113>3\), a contradiction.

That is a credible continuous-population question about this paper’s central fairness guarantee. It is enough to make the universal negative case weak: I can challenge the proposed Theorem 11 mirror, but I cannot honestly claim that no useful mirror exists in any scenario. The 113-agent construction supplies a plausible one.
