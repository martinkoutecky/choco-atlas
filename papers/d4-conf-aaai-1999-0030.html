The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The proponent’s case concedes that, and the numbered propositions confirm it: they characterize dependence and stability, but state no algorithm, complexity bound, or hardness result for a computational problem. The paper does not specify the finite input representation needed to ask such a complexity question. The cited NP-completeness result about cooperation is from another paper and concerns a different problem; importing it would make the proposed mirror a study of that prior result, not a mirror of this paper.

The best rescue would be to ask how hard it is to find or recognize stable plans in a large society, using Propositions 6–9 as the starting point. But that requires choosing finite plan spaces, utility encodings, and which deviations or coalitions count. Those choices determine the computational problem; the propositions do not. Continuizing the population makes the gap sharper: in a standard atomless model, one person has zero mass, so a unilateral deviation cannot change aggregate outcomes. Replacing an individual deviation with a positive-mass cohort’s deviation could make power meaningful, but it changes individual stability into a coalition or population-level question. Keeping identity-specific power instead requires types rich enough to preserve the relevant identities and relations, which can defeat the small-support high-multiplicity premise.

So I would reject a continuous *mirror of this paper’s results*: none of its results is computational. That does not establish the stronger universal claim that no worthwhile continuous computational study could ever be built from its model. A separate study of continuum coalition stability might be valuable, but calling it a mirror would overstate what this paper contributes.
