The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no eligible numbered anchor: it states no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. The strongest positive direction is a continuous-population version of the paper’s social-welfare objective (1). A type would be an agent’s complete constraint-based utility function, including its constraint regions and weights. The instance would give a finite support of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and the shared issue domains \([0,X]^m\). The decision is one contract \(s\in[0,X]^m\); the goal is to return a contract maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t u_t(s)\), where \(u_t(s)\) is the sum of the weights of the type’s constraints satisfied by \(s\).

A plausible regime is a large negotiation among stakeholders whose complete utility profiles recur—for example, an industry or public consortium with many participants but relatively few constraint profiles. There, \(N\), the number of participants, could be much larger than \(\tau=|T|\), and mass represents each profile’s share of the participants. This is a credible mirror of the paper’s underlying welfare-maximizing contract problem. It does not, however, mirror the paper’s full private-utility bidding protocol.

For this candidate, I would expect the main difficulty to remain in searching the nonlinear contract space: the combinatorics live in the issue dimensions and their interdependencies. Any hardness result for the corresponding discrete welfare problem driven by those features should transfer through rational type masses. The paper itself proves no such hardness result, so that expectation is not an anchored classification. The further questions are whether the explicit constraint representation admits efficient exact or approximate optimization, and whether the paper’s sampling and deal-identification protocol has a meaningful version for populations with repeated types.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper studies negotiations with only \(2\) to \(5\) agents, and its computational contribution is a protocol for exploring large contract spaces. Replacing the agent list by masses does not itself simplify that contract search. So there is a plausible continuous-population question here, but under your anchor requirement this paper supplies no named result on which to build the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no eligible anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational complexity result. Its exhaustive-sampling observation and experimental comparisons are not such results. By the requested anchor standard, there is nothing here to mirror.

The best fallback is to continuize the objective in (1): assign mass \(\mu_t\) to each complete utility type and maximize \(\sum_t \mu_t u_t(s)\). That is well-defined, but it does not give a strong mirror of this paper’s contribution. The population enters only through the aggregate function \(F_\mu(s)=\sum_t\mu_tu_t(s)\). The task then is to optimize one weighted nonlinear utility over contracts. If the mediator knows the type functions, this drops the paper’s private-utility setting and becomes centralized optimization. If privacy is retained and the protocol is adapted, copies of the same type submit the same bids; they can be coalesced and their welfare weights combined. That is useful compression, but it leaves the paper’s main problem—the search over overlapping bids—largely intact.

A large consortium with recurring utility templates is a plausible setting, so I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile high-multiplicity optimization problem here. But that would be a new question suggested by the paper’s objective, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper establishes. The negative case is strongest on that distinction; it is weak if the bar is whether any sensible continuous-population question can be formulated.
