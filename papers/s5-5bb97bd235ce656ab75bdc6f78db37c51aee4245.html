The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many agent types with rational population masses, finite contract templates specifying eligible roles and coalition sizes, and strict type-specific preferences over feasible integral bundles satisfying scale economies, find a measurable allocation to feasible multilateral contracts such that each participant receives an integral bundle, the outcome is individually rational, and no positive-mass cohort can weakly setwise block it by coordinating over new contracts and bundle changes.

The model it lives in

An atomless, type-distributed multilateral matching market with mass balance for each type and role, allocations represented as measures over feasible finite coalition configurations, integral contract bundles for each participant, and weak setwise stability tested against positive-mass feasible deviations.

The objection that survived

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions does this continuous-type market admit a weakly setwise stable allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a plausible new research direction, but this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor it. Theorem 1, proved here, says that the Constrained Serial Dictatorship algorithm produces a weakly setwise stable outcome in a market with scale economies: CSD builds an outcome by letting agents in an exogenous order choose contracts that can be extended to an individually rational outcome; the paper proves the result is individually rational and constrained efficient, then uses scale economies to rule out weak setwise blocks. Theorem 2, also proved here, says CSD produces a setwise stable outcome under ordinal scale economies. These are constructive existence theorems, but neither gives a running-time bound or a complexity classification. CSD’s steps optimize over contract sets under arbitrary preferences, so I would not call either result “in P.” The paper has no named hardness result either.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large economy of recurring multilateral projects with many interchangeable participants in finitely many roles. A type would fully specify an agent’s preferences over contract bundles and their available contract roles; mass would be the fraction of the population of that type. Each participant would still receive an integral bundle of contracts, while the aggregate allocation could assign fractions of each type to different bundles.

One possible extension question is: given finitely many types with rational masses, finite contract templates, and strict preferences over feasible bundles, find a measurable assignment of participants to contract coalitions and bundles that is individually rational and has no positive-mass weak setwise block. It preserves multilateral contracts and coordinated blocking while making the population continuous. But it is a proposed mirror of Theorem 1’s existence question, not an algorithmic result established by this paper; the paper does not determine whether this problem is tractable, inherits discrete hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a constructive existence result: CSD produces a weakly setwise stable outcome under scale economies. Theorem 2 gives the parallel result for setwise stability under ordinal scale economies. But neither states a complexity bound, defines a computational input representation, or analyzes CSD’s running time. Its steps optimize over arbitrary strict preferences and test whether contract sets extend to an individually rational outcome. Calling CSD an algorithm does not establish that either theorem answers the programme’s question about tractability, hardness, or parameterized complexity. A continuous version that asks whether a stable outcome exists would therefore add an existence theorem, not mirror a computational result established here.

The proposed mass-market repair also changes what counts as a block. In the paper, a block is a coalition of particular agents signing particular contracts. A population distribution over preference types does not record which agents can form which coalitions or which contracts they can jointly sign. In an atomless model, a finite blocking coalition has zero mass; if the mirror counts only positive-mass blocks, it has changed stability. If it retains finite-coalition blocks, it needs identity-level information that the type distribution discards. The same issue reaches Theorem 2: setwise blocks allow particular agents to compromise on individually rational bundles, so aggregate preference masses alone do not specify the deviations.

A better model could stipulate anonymous project templates, finitely many roles, and a measurable assignment of each type’s mass to project coalitions and bundles. That is plausible for recurring ventures, and the authors might recognize it as their problem. But those coalition templates and feasibility rules are new primitives. They do the work needed to turn the paper’s identity-based contracts into an aggregate allocation model; neither CSD theorem supplies them or a complexity question for them.

There is neighboring continuous matching work: Hatfield and Kominers study multilateral contracts with continuously divisible quantities and a continuum-of-agents application, deriving stability results under concavity. That is analytic work with a different contract model, not a collision with a computational mirror of Huang’s results. [Hatfield and Kominers, “Multilateral Matching”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022053114000635)

So the case against is strongest as a scope argument: Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 do not anchor the computational questions ChoCo is meant to chart, and their aggregate versions require substantial new modeling choices. But I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The anonymous project-market version is plausible; this paper simply does not establish that it yields a meaningful computational problem.
