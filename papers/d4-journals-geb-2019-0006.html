The best rescue is still a plausible high-multiplicity scenario: a large professional association aggregates members’ rankings of candidates, represented by ranking types and their population shares. The continuous rule is well-defined. But its output depends on the intersection of rankings in the positive-mass support: an arbitrarily small positive share has the same effect on unanimous comparisons as a large one. Thus the continuous input collapses to a support question, rather than making the shares themselves part of a computational problem.

That support dependence also makes a natural intervention mirror degenerate. With reference ordering \(abc\), start with unit mass on \(cba\), so the rule selects \(cba\). Move mass \(x>0\) to type \(abc\). The support now contains both rankings, their only common comparisons are reflexive, and the rule selects \(abc\). If the transfer cost is proportional to mass moved, the cost is positive for every feasible \(x\), yet can be made arbitrarily close to zero. The minimum cost is not attained. Requiring a minimum cohort size would regularize this, but adds a threshold absent from the paper’s rule.

This is a negative case, not a proof that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror. A large association is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the evaluation problem remains well-posed. The case is weaker precisely there: its force is that the paper offers no numbered computational result, while its best computational candidate uses only positive support and its natural mass-intervention version has a continuum-specific degeneracy.
