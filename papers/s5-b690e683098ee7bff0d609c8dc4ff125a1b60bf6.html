The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\lambda\), and a finite-support normalized prior \(\pi\) over disutility profiles \(x(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}_+^T\) with \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X_t]=1\), determine the worst-case competitive ratio of weighted Bottom-Heavy cap-and-fill allocation against the optimal prior-dependent Fair-Share allocation, where \(\sum_t\lambda_t a_{t,\omega}=1\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[a_t(X)X_t]\le1\).

The model it lives in

Types \(t\in T\) carry population masses \(\lambda_t\); \(a_{t,\omega}\) is the per-capita allocation density. The API rule caps \(a_{t,\omega}x_t(\omega)\) at \(\bar{x}(\omega)=\sum_t\lambda_t x_t(\omega)\) and fills lowest-disutility types first. The benchmark minimizes \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\sum_t\lambda_t a_t(X)X_t]\) subject to feasibility and Fair Share, giving a finite LP.

The objection that survived

The exact bounds in Proposition 5 depend on equal-mass named-agent symmetry and the parameter \(n\); unequal type masses require a new weighted extremal analysis.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers bad division, the Bottom-Heavy API rule, and its competitive-ratio and Price-of-Fairness analysis; it leaves goods, Top-Heavy rules, envy-freeness, mixed goods and bads, and the paper’s distribution-specific asymptotics aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does weighted Bottom-Heavy dominate every weighted symmetric API rule satisfying Fair Share for every mass vector \(\lambda\)?
  • What is the exact value of \(\sup_{\pi}\mathrm{CR}_{\lambda}\), and when does it equal the finite-agent expression \(n/4+1/2+1/(4n)\)?
  • Which population parameter, such as \(|T|\), support size, or minimum positive mass, controls the continuous problem’s complexity?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a qualified Class A mirror for the paper’s division of bads. It is important to say first that the paper contains no named theorem proving NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), FPT, or another standard complexity classification. Its named results are structural optimization and approximation results. Thus this is a substantive mirror, but not a perfect ChoCo anchor under the strictest computational-anchor rule.

The natural regime is a large workforce repeatedly assigned undesirable jobs. There are \(n\gg\tau\) workers but only \(\tau\) complete worker types: for example, location–skill classes in a national repair service. Workers of the same type have identical normalized disutilities for every job category, identical costs and information, and are interchangeable. Let \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) and let \(\lambda_t\) be the population mass of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\). A job state \(\omega\) is drawn from a finite distribution \(\pi\), and reveals normalized disutilities \(x_t(\omega)\), with \(\mathbb E_\pi[x_t]=1\).

An allocation is a per-capita density \(a_t(x)\ge0\), meaning that type \(t\) receives total mass \(\lambda_t a_t(x)\) of the job. Feasibility is \(\sum_t\lambda_ta_t(x)=1\). The API restriction is that \(a\) may depend on the current disutility profile and the population composition, but not on the unknown prior \(\pi\). Fair Share for a bad is \(\mathbb E_\pi[a_t(X)X_t]\le1\) for every positive-mass type, and social cost is \(C_\pi(a)=\mathbb E_\pi[\sum_t\lambda_ta_t(X)X_t]\).

This is exactly the high-multiplicity scaling of the paper: if \(n_t=n\lambda_t\) agents have type \(t\), and each receives probability \(\phi_i\), set \(a_t=n\phi_i\). Then the paper’s constraint \(\mathbb E[\phi_iX_i]\le1/n\) becomes \(\mathbb E[a_tX_t]\le1\), while the social-cost objective is unchanged. The random job remains finite and random; the continuized object is the worker population.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2 (for bads), proved in this paper. It states that, for every \(n\ge2\), the Bottom-Heavy rule \(\phi^1\) dominates every other symmetric almost-prior-independent rule satisfying Fair Share. Dominance means that for every realized disutility vector \(x\), the Bottom-Heavy rule has weakly smaller total disutility, with strict improvement somewhere unless the rules coincide. The rule gives as much allocation as Fair Share permits to the lowest-disutility agents, gives the next cutoff group the residual amount, and gives nothing to higher-disutility agents. Its cap for agent \(i\) is \(\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\frac{\sum_{j\ne i}x_j}{x_i}\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous Bottom-Heavy API\(_\infty\). Given \(T\), rational masses \(\lambda\), and a realized profile \(x\), compute a feasible allocation density \(a(x)\) that is induced by a symmetric API rule satisfying Fair Share for every normalized prior and that minimizes \(\sum_t\lambda_ta_t(x)x_t\) pointwise among all such rules. Also decide whether the resulting rule pointwise dominates every other fair API rule.

The natural candidate is the continuum limit of Bottom-Heavy. Put \(\bar x=\sum_t\lambda_tx_t\), and impose the caps \(a_t(x)x_t\le\bar x\). Fill the lowest-disutility types to their caps until total allocated mass is \(1\), splitting the residual across the cutoff type. The caps guarantee Fair Share because \(a_t(x)x_t\le\bar x\) pointwise and \(\mathbb E_\pi[\bar X]=\sum_t\lambda_t\mathbb E_\pi[X_t]=1\). For finite \(T\), computing the allocation is a sorting-and-threshold problem, hence plausibly tractable in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The substantive theorem would be the continuum dominance statement, not merely the threshold computation.

A useful supporting anchor is Proposition 5 (for bads), items (ii) and (iii), also proved here. Item (ii) says that the competitive ratio of Bottom-Heavy satisfies \(n/4+1/2+1/(4n)\le \mathrm{CR}_n(\phi^1)\le n/4+5/4\), and is \(109\%\) when \(n=2\). Item (iii) says that the smallest Price of Fairness achievable by any prior-dependent fair rule is exactly \(n/4+1/2+1/(4n)\), equal to \(108\%\) for \(n=2\).

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Fairness-Performance\(_\infty\): given \((T,\lambda,\pi)\) with finite rational support, compute the optimal prior-dependent fair allocation, the Bottom-Heavy API cost, the unconstrained utilitarian cost, and the corresponding competitive-ratio and Price-of-Fairness quantities. The prior-dependent optimum is a finite LP with variables \(a_{t,\omega}\), constraints \(\sum_t\lambda_ta_{t,\omega}=1\) and \(\sum_\omega p_\omega a_{t,\omega}x_{t,\omega}\le1\). The expected direction is Class A for finite type and state descriptions; the interesting open question is whether the paper’s sharp worst-case bounds survive for arbitrary mass vectors \(\lambda\).

The authors should recognize these as their problem: the same revealed normalized disutilities, the same API information restriction, the same Fair Share constraint, and the same social-cost objective are preserved. What changes is that a large interchangeable workforce is represented by type masses rather than named individuals. This is not merely fractionalizing the outcome: the population itself is the continuous object.

The weakest point is also clear. The paper’s finite-\(n\) Bottom-Heavy theorem already has an explicit formula, so the proposed mirror may look like a compressed large-\(n\) restatement rather than a new complexity phenomenon. More seriously, there is no formal complexity anchor in the paper. Under a strict requirement that an anchor must assert \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or similar, the honest verdict is that this paper does not qualify. Under the broader reading that a named theorem solving a nontrivial allocation-design problem is enough, it supplies a credible, precise, and plausibly tractable continuous-population mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper fails ChoCo’s central gate before one reaches the modelling details: it contains no named computational result. Theorem 2 is a dominance characterization of division rules, and Proposition 5 gives exact worst-case ratios. Neither asserts membership in \(P\), hardness, approximation, parameterized tractability, or an algorithm for an input problem. A continuous formulation may therefore be mathematically legitimate, but it is not a computational mirror of a result in the programme’s sense.

Theorem 2 does admit a sensible high-multiplicity story. Let \(n_t=n\lambda_t\) identical workers have type \(t\), set \(a_t=n\phi_i\), and let \(x_t\) be their disutility. Then
\[ \sum_t\lambda_t a_t=1, \qquad \mathbb{E}[a_tX_t]\le 1. \]
The finite Bottom-Heavy cap converges to
\[ a_t(x)x_t\le \bar{x}, \qquad \bar{x}=\sum_t\lambda_tx_t, \]
and filling the lowest-disutility types up to these caps is a perfectly coherent continuum rule.

That concession defeats the obvious objections: multiplicity can be meaningful, and the per-capita rescaling is legitimate. But it also exposes the weakness of the proposed anchor. The resulting computation is a weighted sorting-and-threshold operation, essentially immediate once the cap is written down. The finite theorem’s substantive content is a symmetric functional characterization over named agents, not a computational bottleneck created by population size. If one preserves the theorem exactly by taking equal-mass clones, the continuum statement is a limiting reformulation of an explicit rule. If one allows unequal masses and asks for the genuinely weighted optimum, the paper’s symmetry argument no longer supplies the result: that becomes a new theorem about a new weighted model, rather than a mirror of Theorem 2.

The proposed mirror of Proposition 5 has the same problem. For finite type and state supports, the prior-dependent benchmark is the LP
\[ \min \sum_{\omega}p_\omega\sum_t\lambda_ta_{t,\omega}x_{t,\omega} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_t\lambda_ta_{t,\omega}=1, \qquad \sum_\omega p_\omega a_{t,\omega}x_{t,\omega}\le1. \]
Computing this benchmark is polynomial in the explicitly listed type and state dimensions, while evaluating the Bottom-Heavy rule is threshold computation. This is a reasonable baseline exercise, but it is not a complexity result extracted from Proposition 5. The paper’s \(n/4+1/2+1/(4n)\) bound concerns the number of separately named agents under full individual symmetry. In a continuum, one must newly decide whether to take a worst case over \(\lambda\), over finite-support priors, or over families with a lower bound on every type mass. Those choices are not determined by the proposition.

The lower-bound construction in Proposition 5(iii) makes the mismatch especially clear. Its \(m\) single-minded agents can be replicated into type classes, but then the relevant dimension is the number of distinct utility types, not the total population. Replication changes the finite-\(n\) fairness scale without introducing new utility structure. Thus the proposition does not naturally identify a population-continuity question; it identifies an extremal geometry question in the dimension of the utility vector.

None of this establishes that the high-multiplicity model is nonsensical. On the contrary, the workforce example is admissible, and a weighted continuous fair-division problem could be worthwhile in its own right. The negative conclusion must therefore be narrower but firm: under the stated ChoCo standard, this paper offers no computational anchor. The proposed mirrors are either direct scaling limits of closed-form finite-agent rules or newly invented weighted extremal problems whose computational significance is not supplied by the paper. If structural allocation theorems are admitted as sufficient anchors, the negative case becomes weak; under the programme’s explicit computational criterion, all three proposed anchors fail.
