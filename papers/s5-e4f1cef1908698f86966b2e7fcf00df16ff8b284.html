The case FOR (proponent)

Under the stated anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational result. Theorem 5, proved here, establishes the \(n/4\) bound for the critical threshold value and says the minimum-norm point \(p^\star\in Q(W)\) attains the displayed minimax bound. Theorem 6, also proved here, characterizes equality: \((2/n)\mathbf 1\) must lie in the convex hull of winning-coalition incidence vectors and \((1/2)\mathbf 1\) in the convex hull of losing-coalition incidence vectors. These are quantitative and geometric results, not runtime or hardness results. Conjecture 2 is a conjecture, and the cited prior work is not a named computational result proved in this paper.

The strongest positive direction I can offer is therefore an extrapolation, not a mirror anchored to a qualifying result. Consider a large electorate with a fixed finite set \(T\) of voting-power classes. Class \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); a coalition is a vector \(z\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\). Give the instance a monotone simple-game rule \(v(z)\), represented by a finite rule over rational mass vectors. Define
\[ Q(\mathcal W)=\{p\ge 0:\langle p,w\rangle\ge 1\text{ for every }w\text{ with }v(w)=1\}, \qquad \alpha_\infty=\inf_{p\in Q(\mathcal W)}\ \sup_{v(\ell)=0}\langle p,\ell\rangle. \]
The question is to compute \(\alpha_\infty\) and return an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal price vector \(p\), or decide whether \(\alpha_\infty\le r\) for a rational \(r\). Here \(p_t\) is the weight per unit mass of class \(t\), and the objective is the greatest weight assigned to any losing coalition.

A plausible regime is a large shareholder electorate with a handful of classes that share identical voting rights and game-relevant parameters. The number of people is large while \(|T|\) is small. Authors could recognize this as a fractional-population version of their critical-threshold optimization. If the winning and losing coalition regions admit efficient linear optimization, I would expect the problem to be tractable through separation-based LP methods. With a succinct general game rule, its complexity and the possibility of continuum-specific hardness would need separate study. Natural follow-up questions are whether the \(n/4\) bound has a normalized analogue, what an equality certificate looks like, and which rule representations make the optimization tractable.

The weak point is that the paper’s proof relies on indivisibility: a cover’s intersection with a winning coalition contains at least one player. With fractional mass, a positive intersection can be arbitrarily small, so the same vector need not meet the unit-weight constraint. Theorems 5 and 6 therefore do not carry over just by replacing player counts with masses. The typed threshold problem is a plausible research question, but this paper supplies no named computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 5 proves a universal bound, and Theorem 6 characterizes equality by convex-hull membership. Neither states a runtime, a hardness result, or an algorithmic classification. So there is no named computational anchor here; the computational programme cannot turn either theorem into a mirror merely by changing players into population masses.

The proposed continuous threshold problem is the strongest alternative, but it is an extrapolation from the paper, not a mirror of one of its results. It also needs a finite input model for the winning rule. A monotone rule on the continuum of mass vectors is not itself a finite computational instance. Different encodings—say, a quota rule, a formula, or an optimization oracle—would define different computational problems. The paper supplies no representation to choose among them.

There is also a real modelling fault line. In the finite game, a coalition can win because it contains a particular player. In a nonatomic population, “any positive mass of this type wins” allows winning coalitions of arbitrarily small mass, so no finite per-unit weight may satisfy the threshold constraints. A quota-based rule can avoid that collapse, but choosing such a rule narrows and changes the subject. It may yield a sensible research problem; the paper does not establish which version its authors would recognize as their problem.

That is the strongest negative case, but its limit matters: it does not show that every continuous threshold problem is uninteresting. A typed, quota-based version could be worth studying. What the paper fails to provide is a computational result that makes that version a continuous mirror of this paper.
