The continuous mirror question

Given criteria \(N\), ordered domains \(X_i\), categories \(C_1 \prec \cdots \prec C_p\), and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) with positive rational masses over labeled types \((x,k)\), decide whether there are nondecreasing frontier profiles \(b^1,\ldots,b^{p-1}\) and an upset \(\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^N\) such that the resulting U-NCS model assigns every \(x\) in \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) to its observed category \(k\).

The model it lives in

Households are indivisible units of mass, and each type is a criterion profile \(x\) paired with its observed category \(k\). The decision variables are the frontier profiles and the sufficient-coalition upset \(\mathcal{F}\); the objective is exact consistency on every positive-mass type, so the masses identify the population support but do not weight errors.

What the mirror covers

Covers exact consistency for U-NCS, with MR-Sort as a narrower counterpart; leaves noisy or error-tolerant fitting, robustness objectives, generalization, and the paper’s experimental performance comparisons outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the classical and parameterized complexity of exact U-NCS consistency as a function of \(|N|\), support size \(\tau\), category count \(p\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Can repeated household types enable algorithms or encodings that improve on solving the support-level SAT instance?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s learning problem. Picture a public agency sorting millions of households into ordered support tiers from standardized criteria such as income band, housing condition, and commute time. Many households share the same profile and the same reference tier. A type is the complete pair \((x,k)\): criterion profile \(x\) and observed category \(k\). Its mass is the fraction of households of that type. With binned criteria, millions of households and a much smaller support of \(\tau\) types is a plausible regime.

Lead mirror: Mass-U-NCS Consistency. An instance gives the criteria \(N\), ordered criterion domains, categories \(C_1\prec\cdots\prec C_p\), and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over labeled types \((x,k)\), with positive rational masses summing to one. The question is whether there are nondecreasing frontier profiles \(\langle b\rangle\) and an upset \(\mathcal F\subseteq 2^N\) such that the resulting U-NCS model assigns every positive-mass profile \(x\) to its observed category \(k\). A solution is such a pair \((\langle b\rangle,\mathcal F)\); the task is exact consistency, with no error objective.

Its anchor is Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Theorem 2, and Corollary 2, all proved in this paper. Theorem 1 encodes any U-NCS model that extends the given assignment as a satisfying assignment to \(\phi_A^{SAT}\); Corollary 1 says unsatisfiability therefore rules out representation. Theorem 2 decodes any satisfying Boolean assignment into nondecreasing frontier profiles and an upset of sufficient coalitions whose U-NCS model extends the assignment. Corollary 2 concludes that the assignment is representable in U-NCS if and only if \(\phi_A^{SAT}\) is satisfiable. This is a faithful formulation result, not a claim that the problem is in P or NP-hard.

Second mirror: Mass-MR-Sort Consistency. Use the same input and ask for frontier profiles, nonnegative criterion weights \(w_i\), and a nonnegative threshold \(\lambda\) such that every positive-mass type receives its observed category under MR-Sort, where a coalition is sufficient exactly when its total weight is at least \(\lambda\). A solution is the resulting \((\langle b\rangle,w,\lambda)\).

Its anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that an assignment is representable by MR-Sort if and only if the MIP-D formulation is feasible, and that a feasible solution decodes to MR-Sort parameters extending the assignment. MIP-D is a feasibility formulation: it uses zero objective and enforces a margin of at least one between the relevant sufficient-coalition votes and the threshold. This mirrors the paper’s narrower additive model, separately from its broader U-NCS contribution.

For both questions I would expect hardness to transfer, if discrete hardness of the corresponding consistency problem is established: any finite training set can be represented by giving its labeled types uniform positive masses, merging duplicate types if needed. The mass representation does not remove the combinatorics of fitting the model. The paper itself does not prove such a hardness result, so this is a forecast rather than an anchored complexity claim. The resulting questions are how complexity varies with the number of criteria, support size \(\tau\), categories, and input bit length, and whether repeated records yield useful high-multiplicity algorithms. The paper’s SAT formulation has variables for coalitions of criteria, so its encoding also makes dependence on the number of criteria a natural issue.

The authors should recognize these as their learning problems: the observed labels, model classes, and exact consistency requirement are unchanged. The household setting supplies a plausible population interpretation for the alternatives being sorted; the mirror does not replace the sorting question with a different outcome problem.

The soft spot is that mass mostly records frequency here. Because the task requires fitting every positive-mass type, changing the masses without changing the support does not change feasibility. The continuous version is therefore a high-multiplicity encoding of the learning set, not a new weighted-learning result, and the paper’s theorems do not show that this encoding changes the complexity. Its case is strongest when many households genuinely share standardized profiles and the support is far smaller than the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchors are real computational results, so I would not dismiss them as merely axiomatic. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Theorem 2, and Corollary 2 establish an exact SAT characterization of U-NCS consistency; Theorem 4 does the same for MR-Sort via MIP-D. The problem is that the proposed continuous versions add a measure that neither question uses.

For Mass-U-NCS Consistency, an assignment is consistent on a distribution \(\mu\) exactly when it is consistent on \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\). Once the positive-mass labeled profiles are listed, their masses and bit lengths disappear. The paper already treats the learning set as a set of constraints: duplicate examples add no new constraint, and its experiments vary the number of reference alternatives. So a distributional instance reduces directly to the paper’s instance by dropping the masses; conversely, any finite learning set can be given uniform masses without changing the question. In the proposed household setting, repeated binned profiles are plausible, but they only make duplicate records compressible. That is useful preprocessing, not a new continuous computational problem.

Mass-MR-Sort Consistency has the same issue. Theorem 4’s feasibility condition depends on whether every example is represented, not on how much mass it carries. Adding duplicate records or changing their frequencies leaves feasibility unchanged. A mass-sensitive repair—such as minimizing the misclassified population fraction or allowing error up to \(\varepsilon\)—would make the distribution matter. But that changes the paper’s exact, noise-free consistency question into weighted statistical learning. It may be a worthwhile extension, especially since the paper mentions noisy data as future work, but it does not mirror Theorem 4.

The honest limit of this case is that a large population of households with repeated standardized profiles is a plausible regime. I cannot rule out all such scenarios. My objection is narrower: for both named anchors, the continuous population measure contributes no information to the computational question. The only apparent rescue makes mass matter by changing the objective, and then the proposed mirror is a new learning problem rather than a continuization of the result the paper proves.
