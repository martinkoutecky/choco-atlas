The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is a computational follow-on, not a positive mirror anchored in this paper. Section 2 already models a unit-mass population with finitely many types: \(e(x,y)\) records the mass of type-\(x\) agents matched to type \(y\). The population is continuous in the paper itself, with equal mass assigned to each type. So copying that model as a continuous mirror would restate its starting point.

There are no named computational results to anchor the case. The closest are proved here, but they concern economic equilibria: Lemma 1 says that, with two types, an equilibrium featuring hyperphily makes high-type agents’ partner distribution first-order stochastically dominate low-type agents’. Proposition 1 characterizes when a hyperphily equilibrium exists; Proposition 2 characterizes all equilibria in the two-type model; Proposition 3 says that, with any finite number of equally represented types and \(\rho/(r+\delta)\) sufficiently small, an equilibrium exists if and only if it features hyperphily. None establishes that a computational problem is tractable or hard. The paper’s numerical fixed-point method also comes with no complexity result.

A plausible new question would be Finite-Type Stationary Equilibrium Computation: given a finite set \(X\), rational type masses \(\mu_x\) summing to one, rational symmetric production values \(f(x,y)\), and rational rates \(r,\delta,\rho\), find an equilibrium tuple \((e,d,\pi,S)\) for the paper’s on-the-match search model, or report that none exists. A solution must satisfy the mass constraints \(\sum_{y\in X\cup\{\emptyset\}}e(x,y)=\mu_x\), the steady-state equations, and the paper’s consistency, bargaining, and market-robustness conditions. There is no global objective: the bargaining step maximizes each match’s product of individual surpluses. This would be a recognizable computationalization of the authors’ model, especially if arbitrary rational type masses extend its equal-mass assumption. But it is a new algorithmic question, not a result the paper establishes; I would not assign it a tractability or hardness class from this paper.

So the positive case has a concrete nearby research question, but zero eligible anchors. Its weakest point is decisive: the paper already has a continuous population and supplies no named computational result whose discrete hardness or tractability could be tested in a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no eligible computational anchors here. Lemma 1 and Propositions 1–3 characterize equilibrium behavior and sorting; none gives an algorithm, runtime, hardness result, or approximation guarantee. In particular, Proposition 1’s conditions for hyperphily, Proposition 2’s two-type equilibrium classification, and Proposition 3’s result for small \(\rho/(r+\delta)\) are economic equilibrium results, not computational results about a discrete population whose complexity might change under continuization. The nested fixed-point method for the numerical examples has no complexity guarantee either.

The proposed Finite-Type Stationary Equilibrium Computation problem is the strongest follow-up, but it does not supply the missing mirror. The paper already models a unit-mass population, represents matches by masses \(e(x,y)\), and solves for a steady-state distribution. Replacing equal type shares with arbitrary rational masses and asking for an algorithm to compute equilibria makes that existing continuum model a computational object; it does not continuize the population. The authors might recognize the algorithmic question as relevant to their model, but recognition alone does not make it a population mirror. The relevant input size would be the number of types and the encoding of the primitives and masses, not the number of people.

A finite-agent stochastic counterpart, followed by a theorem connecting its equilibria to the paper’s mass equations, could be worthwhile research. So could complexity bounds for computing equilibria of the existing model. But both require adding a computational programme that this paper does not establish; neither is a continuous mirror of one of its computational results. The negative case is strongest on that distinction. It cannot rule out valuable new work on equilibrium computation; it can say that this paper offers no worthwhile *continuization* for ChoCo, because its central population object is continuous already and its named results do not make computational claims.
