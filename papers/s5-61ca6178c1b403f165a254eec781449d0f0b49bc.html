The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous version of Kemeny consensus. There is an anchor-rule caveat: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition that states a complexity classification for this problem. Its numbered propositions concern nonnegative parameter estimates and a conjugate prior. The relevant hardness claim appears unnumbered in §3.2: for the Mallows case, where all \(\theta_j\) are equal, finding
\[ \pi_0\in\arg\min_{\pi'}\sum_{i=1}^{N} d_K(\pi_i,\pi') \]
is NP-hard. The paper cites Bartholdi et al. (1989) for this claim; it does not prove it. That is the best substantive anchor, but it does not meet the requested numbered-result criterion.

The mirror is Continuous Kemeny Consensus. An instance consists of \(n\) alternatives and a support \(T\subseteq S_n\) of \(\tau\) complete rankings, each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The question is to return a ranking \(\pi^\star\) minimizing
\[ D_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,d_K(t,\pi), \qquad \pi^\star\in\arg\min_{\pi\in S_n}D_\mu(\pi). \]
Here a type is a complete ranking, its mass is that share of the population, and the decision is the consensus order. The objective is the expected number of pairwise disagreements with a voter drawn from the society.

A plausible regime is a very large survey that asks everyone to rank a fixed shortlist of policy proposals. With five proposals, there are at most \(120\) ranking types, even if hundreds of thousands of people respond. Those people are the agents; repeated exact rankings make \(\tau\ll N\) credible. This is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own voter-preference setting.

The paper’s hardness transfers, so I expect Class B. Given any discrete profile, tally each ranking \(t\) with multiplicity \(n_t\) and set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Then \(D_\mu(\pi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i d_K(\pi_i,\pi)\), so the optimizing ranking is unchanged. The translation is polynomial in the explicit profile size, with \(\tau\le N\) and mass bit lengths bounded by \(O(\log N)\). Thus an exact algorithm polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\) would solve the paper’s NP-hard consensus-ranking problem. The hardness is driven by the number of alternatives, not population multiplicity.

The authors should recognize this mirror: they already describe rankings as voters’ preferences and consensus ranking as an aggregation rule. This proposal covers the constant-\(\theta\) consensus-ranking result only. It does not claim to continuize the generalized Mallows model, joint estimation of \((\pi_0,\theta)\), or the conjugate-prior result. The natural follow-up is whether nonconstant stage weights \(\theta_j\), or joint estimation of \(\pi_0\) and \(\theta\), change the continuous problem’s complexity.

The weak point is that the paper’s anchor is unnumbered, and the continuous objective is the discrete Kemeny objective expressed using population proportions. A strict reading of the requested anchor rule therefore leaves no eligible anchor. The positive case rests on the mirror being a sensible high-multiplicity computational question, not on a numbered result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Under the required paper-specific anchor rule, the Kemeny case fails. The NP-hardness statement in §3.2 is unnumbered and attributed to Bartholdi et al.; it is prior work cited by the paper, not a complexity result established there. Propositions 1 and 2 concern parameter identifiability and a conjugate prior. SearchPi is the paper’s own named algorithm, but its discussion gives a worst-case search size and data-dependent pruning analysis, not a numbered complexity theorem. So the proposed continuous Kemeny problem is a sensible research question, but it is not anchored to a qualifying result of this paper.

The strongest attempt to rescue the anchor is to mirror SearchPi itself: given a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, fit \((\pi_0,\theta)\) by maximizing its expected log-likelihood. But this is exactly the paper’s likelihood with the empirical averages replaced by expectations under \(\mu\). The objective still depends on the population through the pairwise statistic \(Q\), which the paper already identifies as sufficient and studies over the convex polytope of feasible pairwise matrices. This makes the proposed extension especially close to the paper’s existing formulation; it does not supply a distinct, paper-established complexity result to mirror.

That is the negative case’s limit. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A large survey over a fixed shortlist of proposals is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and rational type masses preserve the Kemeny objective exactly. Nor does the fact that the continuous problem reduces to pairwise margins invalidate it: that may make the mirror computationally modest, but the programme allows direct high-multiplicity questions whose answers are familiar or unchanged. The defensible conclusion is narrow: this paper does not pass the strict source-anchor gate for the proposed mirror. The broader claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile continuous mirror is weak.
