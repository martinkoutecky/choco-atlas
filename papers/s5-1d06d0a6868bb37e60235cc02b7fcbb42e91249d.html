What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind this task asks for: no numbered theorem classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The exact Banzhaf computation time mentioned in the experiments is attributed to earlier work, not proved here as a named result. So there is no qualifying complexity anchor, and I would not present this paper as already containing a complexity landscape for a continuous mirror.

The closest anchor for a positive case is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: no weighted quota rule can guarantee perfect balance for every stake profile, where perfect balance means each voter’s Banzhaf power is proportional to their stake. That is an analytical impossibility result, not a complexity result. Still, it gives a natural question about what happens to the same balance criterion in a high-multiplicity regime.

My lead proposed mirror is Asymptotic Banzhaf Balance. An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of exact stake types, positive rational stake amounts \(a_t\), and rational population fractions \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. Type \(t\) means a voter with stake \(a_t\) and the same voting rights as every other voter of that type. Consider populations of size \(n\) with \(n_t/n\to\mu_t\), using the paper’s stake-weighted majority rule. For a voter of type \(t\), let \(b_{t,n}\) be the probability that a uniformly random coalition of the other voters is losing but becomes winning when that voter joins. The question is to compute the limiting type-level power shares \(p_t=\lim_{n\to\infty} n_t b_{t,n}/\sum_u n_u b_{u,n}\), and their balance error \(D=\sum_t|p_t-\rho_t|\), where \(\rho_t=\mu_t a_t/\sum_u\mu_u a_u\) is type \(t\)’s share of total stake. The coalition’s binary inclusion of each other voter is the counterfactual action in the Banzhaf definition; the society itself is the distribution \(\mu\).

For fixed finite \(T\), with no individual stake growing with \(n\), a local central limit argument predicts \(\sqrt n\,b_{t,n}\to \sqrt{2/\pi}\,a_t/\sqrt{\sum_u\mu_u a_u^2}\). Thus \(p_t=\rho_t\) and \(D=0\) in the limit. The unscaled individual Banzhaf indices vanish, so the \(\sqrt n\) scaling is part of the question. If placed in ChoCo’s trichotomy, this fixed-regime problem looks tractable: the limit has a closed form. It suggests the finite impossibility in Theorem 1 can disappear when many voters replicate a bounded set of stake types.

The regime is a large staking system with many users on a small menu of exact stake levels—for example, a protocol with standardized staking tiers. Here \(n\) is large while \(\tau=|T|\) stays small. That is plausible for such a protocol, but it is not a faithful description of the paper’s measured Catalyst addresses, whose stake amounts may be mostly distinct. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s balance question only; it does not continuize the paper’s Dirichlet variance analysis or its empirical study.

The main weakness is that this is a new asymptotic analytical question, not a continuous version of a named computational result in the paper. It also excludes regimes with a few whales whose individual stakes grow alongside the population, and a direct nonatomic Banzhaf definition would lose individual pivotality without the local fluctuation scaling. Those limits matter. The positive case survives narrowly: the paper studies how power balance changes with population size, and this mirror keeps its voting rule, Banzhaf notion, and balance objective while making the population itself the limiting object. Further questions include convergence rates for finite \(n\), growing type support, unbounded stake distributions, and quotas away from one half.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper is a poor anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme. Its strongest named result, Theorem 1, says that no weighted quota rule guarantees perfectly stake-proportional Banzhaf power for every stake profile. That is an impossibility statement, not a result about the complexity of computing or optimizing anything. The theorem on single-agent variance gives an analytical expression; the computational work is experimental, and the cited exact-computation bound is prior work. So the paper supplies no computational result whose complexity landscape a mirror could directly chart.

The proposed *Asymptotic Banzhaf Balance* is coherent, but it does not close that gap. Banzhaf power asks whether a particular voter turns a particular coalition from losing to winning. A nonatomic society has no positive-mass individual to test. The proposal restores one by lifting \(\mu\) to finite populations, retaining random coalition inclusion, then taking a scaled limit. That is a defensible asymptotic model, but its computational input is the limiting type distribution; it does not give the finite-instance complexity picture that ChoCo studies. A stronger finite high-multiplicity version—compute type-level Banzhaf indices or optimize a quota given type counts—would restore that picture, but it also restores the finite coalition structure. Existing high-multiplicity work supports the regime’s relevance; it is not a novelty objection. The issue is that this route studies a finite weighted voting game in a high-multiplicity encoding, rather than supplying a computational result in the continuous limit.

The same difficulty applies to the paper’s variance analysis. A continuous distribution over stake types can replace the finite list of users, but it does not by itself define individual pivotality. One must add a finite-population lift, a limiting influence notion, or a stochastic smoothing model. Each may lead to worthwhile mathematics. None is an obvious continuous computational counterpart of a result the paper proves.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, and it is narrow. The analyst’s fixed-support asymptotic question is sensible, keeps the paper’s power-balance objective, and could be worthwhile if ChoCo includes asymptotic analysis. A finite high-multiplicity Banzhaf problem could also be a promising project. I would not claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; the honest conclusion is that this paper offers no clear computational anchor, while the proposed mirror survives as a plausible but mainly analytical follow-on.
