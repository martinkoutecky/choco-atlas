The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended “CASE FOR” raises no pro-mirror anchor; it argues that there is none. That is the strongest point against a continuous mirror here: the paper reports exact calculations for one 27-country instance, including an enumeration of \(2^{27}\) coalitions, but states no complexity result for a problem over a family of inputs. There is therefore no named computational theorem, lemma, or corollary to mirror.

The strongest candidate for a new question would be a federation with many jurisdictions grouped by population type: choose a quota to bring each type’s relative Banzhaf power close to its square-root population target. But this requires changing the object under study. In the paper, citizens’ population sizes determine voting weights; the coalitions whose criticality is measured are coalitions of distinct governments. A distribution over citizen types does not describe those coalitions.

One could instead take a continuum of jurisdictions. Yet Banzhaf power asks whether a particular jurisdiction is pivotal among the other jurisdictions. In a continuum, each has zero mass. Recovering nonzero pivotality would require extra scaling assumptions and details about finite coalition counts or subset-sum structure that the limiting distribution need not retain. A smoother limit might yield an asymptotic power formula, but that would be a new analytic result; retaining the finite details would return to a finite or high-multiplicity problem. Either could be worth studying independently, but neither mirrors a computational result this paper establishes.

So the negative case is strong against an anchor-based ChoCo mirror, but weaker as a universal claim that no useful continuous question can arise in any scenario. The paper supplies no computational anchor, and its most plausible population continuizations either miss the council-level pivotality it studies or need a new model to preserve it.
