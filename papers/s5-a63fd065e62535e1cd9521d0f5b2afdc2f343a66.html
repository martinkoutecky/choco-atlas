The strongest unanchored direction is a finite-type version of their DYCE overlapping resource-allocation problem. Consider a large fleet of standardized batteries, EV chargers, or similar DERs, with many hundreds of thousands of units but relatively few complete operational types. A type would include every attribute the model uses: its schedule, cost, error and flexibility, product eligibility, and relevant grid or neighborhood role. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the fleet with that full profile. Given finite products and time intervals, choose \(\alpha_{t,p,h}\), the fraction of type \(t\)’s available capacity reserved for product \(p\) in interval \(h\), with \(\sum_p\alpha_{t,p,h}\le 1\). Each product’s coalition aggregates the resulting type-level contributions; its value is computed by an explicitly specified mass extension of DYCE’s contribution and target-fulfilment formulas. The problem is to find an allocation maximizing the sum of product-coalition values, and return that allocation and its value.

This is recognizable as their problem: the paper motivates scaling to very large DER populations and explicitly extends an agent’s contribution to allow resources to be split among simultaneous products in §3.2, equations (4)–(6). The continuous model makes that resource splitting and type-level aggregation the decision itself. Its complexity remains open: a restricted model with suitable concave, piecewise-linear values might admit a linear or column-generation formulation, while the paper’s full nonlinear values and coupled grid constraints provide no basis for claiming tractability or transferred hardness. Further questions include the pricing problem for coalition profiles and whether a decentralized DYCE process can realize an optimum of the aggregate model.

The main weakness is that DYCE’s central process operates among named agents: neighborhood and trust rules, individual grid positions, and decentralized negotiations all affect formation. Grouping agents into types could erase those distinctions or make the number of types too large. The model is therefore a plausible research direction grounded in the paper, but it is not a continuous mirror anchored in one of its computational results.

The strongest proposed mirror—allocating the capacity of finite types of standardized DERs among simultaneous products—is plausible. A type could include its schedule, costs, errors, flexibility, product eligibility, and grid role; the decision could allocate type mass across products subject to capacity constraints. That would be a legitimate computational question. But it would define a new aggregate optimization problem, such as a planner maximizing the sum of product values. The paper does not formulate that problem or show it is equivalent to DYCE’s decentralized, trust-based negotiations and coalition regrouping. Equations (4)–(6) allow contributions to multiple products; they do not supply the missing optimization problem or a complexity result for it.

Trying to preserve more of DYCE makes the mirror less clean: neighborhoods, trust, and negotiation state may distinguish agents and enlarge the type space. A symmetric, finite-type fleet could avoid that problem, but the resulting question would still be a new research direction grounded in the paper’s subject, not a mirror of one of its computational results.
