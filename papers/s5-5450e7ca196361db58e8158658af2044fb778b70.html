The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_B\) summing to \(1\), define \(\operatorname{PAV}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal T}\mu_B H_{|B\cap W|}\) for each \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\). Is there exactly one committee maximizing \(\operatorname{PAV}_{\mu}\)?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass nonatomic electorate whose types are approval sets \(B\in\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\), with rational masses \(\mu_B\); the decision variable remains a discrete size-\(k\) committee \(W\subseteq C\), scored by \(\sum_B\mu_B H_{|B\cap W|}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s uniqueness result, Theorem 3.1’s membership results, and Theorem 5.1’s counting result. It does not address winner-finding, score approximation, or representation-axiom results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the parameterized complexities of continuous PAV membership, uniqueness, and counting when parameterized by support size \(\tau\) or committee size \(k\)?
  • Which restrictions on approval-type support or ballot size make exact optimal-set questions tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is continuous PAV optimal-committee counting. Let \(C\) be the candidate set and let a voter type be an approval ballot \(B\subseteq C\). An instance gives the support \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\), with each type’s positive rational mass \(\mu_B\) encoded in binary and \(\sum_{B\in\mathcal T}\mu_B=1\), together with a committee size \(k\). The population can be read as a nonatomic unit mass of voters: a voter of type \(B\) receives PAV utility \(H_{|B\cap W|}\) from committee \(W\). Thus the per-capita score is
\[ \operatorname{PAV}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal T}\mu_B H_{|B\cap W|}, \qquad \operatorname{Opt}_{\mu}=\arg\max_{|W|=k}\operatorname{PAV}_{\mu}(W). \]
The decision remains a discrete committee choice. In the counting problem, the requested output is the number of distinct committees in \(\operatorname{Opt}_{\mu}\), not a mass or a fractional committee.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large union, university, or citywide election for a fixed slate of representatives using approval ballots. There may be hundreds of thousands of voters but far fewer distinct approval sets, because many voters submit the same ballot. Their type is exactly their approval set; identities have no role in PAV. The paper’s concern with retaining every tied optimum carries over unchanged.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that deciding whether a PAV election has exactly one optimal committee is \(\Delta_2=\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{NP}}\)-complete, and that the hardness already holds when there are at most two optimal committees. The mirror, Continuous-PAV-Uniqueness, asks, given \((C,k,\mathcal T,\mu)\), whether \(|\operatorname{Opt}_{\mu}|=1\). Its expected classification is also \(\Delta_2\)-complete, with hardness persisting in instances with at most two maximizing committees. That small-tie case is particularly apt: it makes the difficulty about deciding whether the population’s PAV objective leaves one exact outcome or two, rather than about navigating a large family of ties. The paper strengthens an earlier coNP-hardness result of Janeczko and Faliszewski; that earlier result is outside literature, while Theorem 4.1 is proved here.

Two more anchors support distinct questions. Theorem 5.1, also proved here, says that \(\CountPAV\), the function returning the number of optimal committees, is \(\#\!\cdot\!\mathrm{OptP}\)-complete under metric reductions. In particular, for every \(f\in\#\!\cdot\!\mathrm{OptP}\), the paper constructs an election \(\mathcal E_x\) with \(\CountPAV(\mathcal E_x)=f(x)+1\); when the source representation always has a feasible witness, the reduction can preserve the count exactly. Continuous-\(\#\)PAV asks for \(|\operatorname{Opt}_{\mu}|\) on the rational-mass instance above, written in binary. Its expected classification is \(\#\!\cdot\!\mathrm{OptP}\)-completeness under metric reductions. The additive one matters: every PAV election has at least one optimum, so the target cannot parsimoniously represent source inputs with zero optimal witnesses. The paper strengthens earlier \(\#\mathrm P\)-hardness by Janeczko and Faliszewski.

Theorem 3.1, proved here, says that both \(\SomePAV\) and \(\AllPAV\) are \(\Delta_2\)-complete: respectively, whether a designated candidate belongs to some optimal committee and whether it belongs to every optimal committee. Continuous-PAV-Some-Member asks whether some committee in \(\operatorname{Opt}_{\mu}\) contains designated \(c\); Continuous-PAV-All-Member asks whether every committee in \(\operatorname{Opt}_{\mu}\) contains \(c\). Each is expected to remain \(\Delta_2\)-complete. The paper strengthens Yang’s cited coNP-hardness for universal membership and proves the existential version’s classification as well.

The reason to expect these classifications in the mirror is direct. An explicit election with \(n_B\) voters of each ballot type becomes the rational society \(\mu_B=n_B/n\), where \(n=\sum_B n_B\). Its score is the original total PAV score divided by \(n\), so its maximizing committees—including all ties—are identical. The paper’s reductions use polynomially many explicitly listed voters, so aggregating and normalizing their ballots gives polynomial-size rational-mass instances. For the upper bounds, exact comparisons remain polynomial-bit computations: clear the input mass denominators and the harmonic denominators to obtain integer scores of polynomial bit length. Then the same NP-oracle comparisons, or the same optimal-witness counting formulation, apply. These are therefore Class B mirrors: the discrete hardness transfers into the continuous-population setting. I would not predict that continuization makes these three problems tractable.

The scope is deliberately narrow: the population becomes a distribution over approval types, while the candidate set, committee size, PAV utilities, and exact optimal-set questions stay as in the paper. There is no fractional committee or lottery. This is a good fit for the programme’s gate: the authors study precisely the PAV objective and the exact structure of its maximizing committees, and the mirror changes the voter representation to masses while preserving that object.

The weakest point is that, for this rule, the normalized mass score is simply the average of the original voter scores. Every rational mass profile can be cleared to integer multiplicities, though that expanded voter list may be exponentially long in the binary input size. So the mirror does not promise a new complexity phenomenon; its strongest claim is that optimal-set complexity survives in the compact high-multiplicity representation. That is still a meaningful question about the regime, and the paper’s use of repeated, anonymous approval ballots makes it especially credible here. Further questions include how these membership, uniqueness, and counting problems behave when parameterized by support size \(\tau\), committee size \(k\), or maximum ballot size, and whether any restrictions make exact ties easier to determine.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this mirror changes the input representation more than the underlying PAV problem. The paper’s score already depends only on ballot multiplicities: voter identities have no role. A rational distribution over approval types gives the same score ordering as an integer electorate obtained by clearing denominators. For the exact questions here, that conversion preserves every maximizing committee and every tie. Since the paper’s reductions use polynomially many voters, they also yield polynomial-size rational-mass instances. So the mirror adds no new mathematical behavior to these results; it asks whether they persist when a high-multiplicity electorate is given compactly.

That is a real reservation about novelty, but it does not defeat the anchors under ChoCo’s stated standard. Compact high-multiplicity input is itself in scope, and a Class B result is still a worthwhile mirror. Calling this “only a re-encoding” would effectively reject high multiplicity as a research regime, which the programme explicitly treats as evidence for a sensible mirror.

A second concern is that exact ties are fragile: for any pair of committees, their score difference is linear in the type masses, so ties generally lie on boundaries in the space of populations. A small change to the masses can break them. That makes uniqueness and optimal-committee counting questions about exact balance, and suggests a more robust population question might also be useful. But the paper deliberately studies exact optima, and balanced populations are plausible instances. Fragility does not make exact membership, uniqueness, or counting ill-posed.

The three anchors therefore survive their stronger formulations. For Theorem 3.1, mass normalization preserves whether a designated candidate appears in some or every optimum. For Theorem 4.1, it preserves the one-versus-two-optima distinction, including the reduction’s at-most-two case. For Theorem 5.1, it preserves the number of distinct optimal committees, including the additive-one construction. Rational masses also permit exact score comparisons with polynomial-bit arithmetic, so the paper’s upper-bound approach has a natural continuous counterpart.

I cannot make an honest case that defeats all three anchors. The best negative conclusion is that these are unusually direct Class B mirrors, with limited novelty beyond the compact high-multiplicity formulation. That is a reason to calibrate the expected contribution, not a reason to say there is no worthwhile continuous mirror.
