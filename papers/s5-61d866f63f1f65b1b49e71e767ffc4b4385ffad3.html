The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of belief types \(p_t\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), compute the equal-wealth parimutuel equilibrium price \(q\) for state \(1\) and a type-level allocation \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}_+^2\). Each type maximizes \(p_tx_{t,1}+(1-p_t)x_{t,2}\) subject to \(qx_{t,1}+(1-q)x_{t,2}\le1\), and market clearing requires \(\sum_t\mu_tx_t=(1,1)\).

The model it lives in

A two-state parimutuel market with a finite-support population distribution \(\nu=\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{p_t}\), per-capita equal wealth, and state supplies \((1,1)\). Types choose per-capita bundles \(x_t\); the output is the equilibrium price \(q\) and a market-clearing allocation.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 4’s two-state parimutuel price calculation and its market interpretation in Proposition 3. It does not cover the many-state reporting-game equilibrium correspondence or the Nash-implementation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove that the equilibrium price is the unique \(q\) satisfying \(\nu((q,1])\le q\le\nu([q,1])\), including endpoint and atom cases, and bound exact bit complexity for rational support and masses.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result that explicitly classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, or otherwise in a complexity class. The closest computational anchor is Proposition 4, proved in the paper: with two states and \(n\) individuals whose beliefs for state 1 are \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\), the equal-wealth parimutuel price is the median of the \(2n-1\) values
\[ p_1,\ldots,p_n,\frac1n,\frac2n,\ldots,\frac{n-1}{n}. \]
That gives an exact order-statistic computation, though the paper does not state a runtime bound. So this is a limited positive case, not a formal complexity result of the kind the brief prefers.

The mirror I find plausible is Two-State Continuum Parimutuel Price. Consider a large public portfolio-choice society with two uncertain states. A type \(t\) is a complete belief \(p_t\in[0,1]\), together with the common risk attitude, wealth, and other relevant attributes assumed by the model. The instance gives a finite support \(T\) and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is many people sharing relatively few belief types—for example, a large population considering the same environmental or health policy. Mass is the fraction of the population of each type.

Normalize each person’s wealth to one unit and the aggregate supply in each state to one unit. The question is to find the equal-wealth parimutuel equilibrium price \(q\) for state 1, together with type-level allocations \(x_t\in\mathbb R_+^2\), such that each type maximizes
\[ p_t x_{t,1}+(1-p_t)x_{t,2} \]
subject to \(q x_{t,1}+(1-q)x_{t,2}\le 1\), and the market clears:
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t=(1,1). \]
The price is the requested outcome; allocations can be included as a witness and may not be unique.

Proposition 4 suggests the continuum answer. Let \(\nu=\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{p_t}\) be the population’s belief distribution, and let \(\lambda\) be the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\). The limiting price \(q\) is the median of the equal mixture \(\tfrac12\nu+\tfrac12\lambda\). Equivalently, it is the unique \(q\) satisfying
\[ \nu((q,1])\le q\le \nu([q,1]). \]
The uniform component is the limit of Proposition 4’s phantom values \(j/n\). With finite support, sorting the belief types and scanning their cumulative masses gives an exact algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. This is a Class A mirror.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it covers the paper’s two-state parimutuel-price result, not its general implementation characterization or all of its strategic claims. Its weakest point is that the paper proves a finite order-statistic formula, not a complexity classification, and the continuum market problem does not automatically preserve the finite game’s individual incentives. It is therefore strongest as a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s price-aggregation subproblem; extending the strategic Nash-equilibrium interpretation to a nonatomic population would be a separate question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is the best anchor, but it is not a complexity result: it gives a closed-form median characterization, with no computational problem stated or complexity bound. The proposed mirror—compute a price from rational masses on belief types—is a sensible new query, but it is added to the paper’s result rather than a continuous version of a computational problem the paper studies. Extending it to more states does not fix that gap; it still asks for a market price, while the paper’s central strategic object is a finite-agent reporting game.

There is also a substantive break in the strategic interpretation. In a nonatomic reporting game, one person’s report has zero effect on the aggregate, so unilateral deviations cannot select the finite game’s equilibrium price. The proposed mixture-median price is a limit of finite parimutuel equilibria; it is not the outcome selected by Nash equilibrium in the nonatomic reporting game. Preserving influence would require finite-sized agents, while allowing positive-mass groups to deviate changes the players and the solution concept. The market-equilibrium formulation remains coherent, but it is a distinct object.

That is the strongest case against, and it has a clear limit: the market-price question is itself a plausible high-multiplicity problem closely tied to the paper’s Proposition 3. The type masses are meaningful, and the paper’s market interpretation gives the authors a reason to recognize it. Under a gate that accepts computational questions inferred from a paper’s characterization, this anchor survives. So the negative case is weak: it can challenge whether this is a mirror of a computational result, but it cannot honestly show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
