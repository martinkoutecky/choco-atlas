The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a new high-multiplicity computation problem built around the paper’s stochastic egalitarian constrained equal costs rule (SEEC). The paper does not, however, contain a named computational result of the kind your anchor requirement calls for.

The closest results are Theorem 6 and Theorem 7, both proved in the paper. Theorem 6 says that a solution has Local Symmetry—every unit increase in cost is shared equally among agents whose liabilities are not yet reached—if and only if it has the constrained equal costs form: an ex ante transfer plus the constrained equal awards allocation of realized cost against the remaining liabilities. Theorem 7 says there is a unique egalitarian solution with Local Symmetry, namely SEEC, whose ex ante transfers are chosen to make all agents’ disutilities equal. These are characterization and uniqueness results, not results that a problem is in P, hard, or encoded and computed. They therefore are not qualifying computational anchors.

A plausible follow-on is High-Multiplicity SEEC Computation. Consider a large firm with many divisions sharing a central service, where the total cost scales with the number of divisions. Each division has the same cost beliefs and preferences, and its type is its per-division liability \(\ell_j\). The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu_j\) over liability types, with rational masses summing to \(1\), support size \(\tau\), and many divisions relative to \(\tau\). For a concrete version, let per-division cost \(C\) be uniform on \([0,\bar c]\), and let disutility be expected cost. Require \(\sum_j\mu_j\ell_j\ge\bar c\) and \(\min_j\ell_j\ge\bar c/2\), the per-capita versions of the paper’s admissibility and \(V\)-sufficiency conditions.

The question would be: compute transfers \(t_j\) and the resulting allocation schedule, where \(\sum_j\mu_jt_j=0\), \(t_j\le\ell_j\), and
\[ x_j(c)=t_j+\min\{\lambda(c),\ell_j-t_j\}. \]
For each \(c\), \(\lambda(c)\) is chosen so that
\[ \sum_j\mu_j\min\{\lambda(c),\ell_j-t_j\}=c, \]
and the transfers must make every type face the same expected cost:
\[ \frac{1}{\bar c}\int_0^{\bar c}x_j(c)\,dc=\frac{\bar c}{2} \quad\text{for every }j. \]
A solution is an exact transfer vector and its induced schedule, represented by its breakpoints and pieces. The paper’s Theorem 7 makes this a plausible computationalization of SEEC: the firm’s divisions can be grouped by complete type, and rational masses compactly describe many repeated divisions. I would tentatively expect tractability from the ordered, piecewise structure of the allocation, but the paper proves no running-time bound for this task.

The regime is recognizable in the authors’ own multi-divisional-firm setting: a large organization with many divisions sharing the service, and a small number of liability classes repeated across divisions. This is a population mirror, not a claim that the paper’s random cost is already a continuous society. The paper keeps a fixed finite set of agents; its continuity is in cost uncertainty.

So the positive case is for computationalizing a central result of the paper, not for claiming that it already reports a computational result. That is also the weakest point: under the requested anchor rule there are zero eligible anchors, and the proposed problem adds the algorithmic question the authors did not study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is categorical: the paper contains no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 6 characterizes Local Symmetry as the constrained equal costs form; Theorem 7 identifies the unique egalitarian solution satisfying that property. The other numbered results likewise concern existence, characterization, or transfer comparisons. None states a complexity bound or studies an algorithmic problem. By the proposed anchor standard, neither theorem is a qualifying anchor.

The proponent’s stronger version is coherent: take many divisions from finitely many liability classes, represent the classes by rational masses, and compute a mass-weighted SEEC schedule and its transfers. This does make the population continuous. The type can be just the liability when preferences are common, as in the paper; weighted transfers and type-level allocations preserve the relevant constraints. The paper’s stochastic cost is not itself a continuous population, but that does not invalidate this proposed population model.

What it does not supply is a computational result from this paper to mirror. It creates a new task—computing a rule that the paper characterizes axiomatically. That task might be worth studying, but its complexity, whether easy or hard, cannot turn Theorem 7 into a complexity theorem. So it is the best attempted anchor, but it falls outside the stated requirement.

I would not claim a stronger objection based on identity, scaling, or a degenerate limit: those do not defeat this model. The negative case is strong under the anchor requirement and weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous follow-on exists. If “mirror” includes adding computation around an axiomatically characterized rule, the weighted SEEC proposal remains plausible, and I cannot honestly rule it out as worthwhile.
