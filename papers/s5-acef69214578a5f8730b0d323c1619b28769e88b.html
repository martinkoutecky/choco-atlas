The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The closest result is Theorem 1 (proved here): a recognition function satisfies cost-effective implementation, consistency, and top-agent proportionality if and only if it is the unique function \(p^*\) with
\[ p^*_1=\frac{\sum_t d_{1t}}{\sum_i\sum_t d_{it}},\qquad p^*_j=\left(\prod_{i<j}(1-g(i))\right)g(j), \]
where
\[ g(j)=\frac{\sum_t\min\{\sum_{i\le j}d_i-\sum_{i<j}x_i,\ c_j\}_t} {\sum_i\sum_t d_{it}-\sum_{i<j}\sum_t x_{it}}. \]
That is an axiomatic characterization of a mechanism, not a result about complexity or an algorithm’s running time. The other numbered propositions likewise concern equilibrium payoffs or axioms. Section 5 explicitly says the computational aspect was not explored and describes the allocation minimization as straightforward.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large cloud-services market with a small number \(\tau\) of provider profiles. A type would specify a provider’s service tier, per-provider task contracts, capacities, and cost function; mass would be the fraction of providers with that complete profile. The decision could assign task workload per provider type, with objective \(\sum_r \mu_r f_r(\sum_t x_{rt})\). That is a sensible continuous task-allocation model, but it does not supply the requested anchor: the paper’s main result concerns bargaining and recognition among ordered, named agents. Extending it to mass types would require defining how proposer recognition and unanimous acceptance work in that setting.

So the strongest honest positive case is only a research lead: investigate whether Theorem 1’s recognition rule has a well-defined cohort or population limit that still implements efficient task allocations. It is not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result, and I cannot give it a complexity-class prediction under your criteria. The weak point is the mechanism’s dependence on individually ordered agents and proposer recognition; the paper supplies no result showing that those features survive a population limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes the recognition function satisfying three axioms; Propositions 1–4 concern bargaining payoffs and axiomatic properties. None states a complexity bound or analyzes an algorithm. Section 5 explicitly leaves the computational aspect unstudied. A continuum characterization of \(p^*\) would therefore be a new mechanism-design question, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

The most promising proposed setting, a large market of similar service providers, runs into a problem on the theorem’s own domain. Theorem 1 applies on \(\tilde D\), where agents’ contracted task-type sets are disjoint, and the paper fixes the number \(m\) of task types as the number of agents \(n\) varies. With positive contracts, there can be at most \(m\) agents in this domain. Replicating providers with the same task profile creates overlapping contracts and leaves the domain of the characterization. Giving each provider’s jobs separate task-type labels restores disjointness, but makes the type description grow with the population. Grouping providers into cohorts avoids that growth only by changing the mechanism: each cohort becomes a strategic player with aggregate costs and capacities, and the paper’s individual announcement order, proposer recognition, and unanimous veto procedure no longer specify how its members act.

An atomless version has a related obstacle. In \(p^*\), recognition is assigned to individually ordered agents, and the first agent’s probability is their share of contracted tasks. In a continuum of individual providers, an individual’s share is zero. Individual deviations then have no effect on aggregate outcomes in the usual atomless model, while the bargaining stage still gives each individual a veto. Replacing individual recognition with a density or cohort-level recognition could address these features, but that requires new bargaining rules and axioms; the paper’s theorem does not determine them.

One could also ask for algorithms for cost-minimizing task allocation over a continuous provider population. That is a plausible research question about the surrounding scheduling model. The paper, however, says this optimization was not studied, and it identifies no computational theorem for it. On the evidence here, it is a new project rather than a mirror.

This case does not establish that no useful continuum mechanism could be built. Its weak point is that a well-designed cohort or atomless bargaining model might be worthwhile in its own right. The negative claim is narrower: the paper offers no computational anchor for that work, and its central recognition result depends on a finite, ordered-player structure that the natural high-multiplicity scenarios do not preserve.
