The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a continuous population of procurement participants. But the paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. Theorem 1 is its only numbered theorem; the convergence findings are experimental, not named complexity results. So there is no qualifying computational anchor under your rules.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says: if \(\lambda^\star\) is optimal for the Lagrangian dual and \(q^\star\) is optimal for the corresponding relaxation, then, assuming convex seller costs, concave buyer valuations, convex feasibility sets, stability of the market-clearing problem, and feasibility of \(q^\star\) for that problem, \(q^\star\) is socially efficient and \((q^\star,\lambda^\star)\) is a Walrasian equilibrium. That is a substantive economic result, but it does not establish a complexity bound.

If Theorem 1 is accepted as a non-complexity anchor, the lead mirror would be Continuous-Type Market Clearing. A plausible regime is a regional biomass procurement exchange with many small suppliers and industrial buyers. Suppliers share standardized profiles for product mix, capacity, and cost; buyers share profiles for valuation, processing technology, and blend constraints. A type includes the participant’s role and every profile detail used by the market. The mass \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the participant population; \(\tau\) is the number of profiles, with \(\tau\ll N\). Each type’s decision \(q_t\) is its per-participant quantity vector, and market totals are the mass-weighted sums. This preserves the paper’s economic question: allocate goods to maximize total welfare while balancing supply and demand.

For an explicit computational version, take a finite goods set \(L\), buyer and seller types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), bounded rational polyhedral feasibility sets \(D_t\), and rational piecewise-linear buyer values or seller costs. Find welfare-maximizing per-type quantities \(q_t\in D_t\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{t\in T_B}\mu_t q_t=\sum_{t\in T_S}\mu_t q_t, \]
and return a supporting price vector \(p\) such that each buyer type maximizes \(V_t(q_t)-p\cdot q_t\) and each seller type maximizes \(p\cdot q_t-C_t(q_t)\). The solution is the type-level allocation and supporting prices; prices need not be unique. For this explicitly represented piecewise-linear version, the expected classification is Class A: it is an LP whose size depends on the number and descriptions of types, goods, and linear pieces, rather than on the number of named participants. Theorem 1 motivates the price-supported output, but the LP argument—not a complexity result in this paper—supports that expectation. Further questions include whether the same result holds for broader function encodings and how many price-query rounds an iterative auction needs when participants reveal only best responses.

This mirror covers the paper’s socially efficient market-clearing problem and the economic outcome targeted by its subgradient and bundle auctions. It does not mirror the experiments comparing their convergence, nor the paper’s discussion of incentive compatibility. Its weakest point is the central one: the paper does not study computational complexity, and its model already has continuous goods while keeping participants finite. The population mirror is plausible and keeps the underlying allocation problem intact, but it cannot supply the requested discrete-hardness anchor or claim a complexity discovery by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the paper’s results: it has no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 1 is an economic duality statement: under convexity, stability, and feasibility assumptions, an optimal allocation and optimal multipliers form a Walrasian equilibrium. It gives no complexity bound, algorithmic classification, or input-size analysis. The subgradient and bundle procedures are described as algorithms, and their performance is compared experimentally, but the paper does not prove a worst-case computational result about them. Under ChoCo’s stated remit, there is no qualifying computational anchor.

The best version of the proposed market-clearing mirror is quite plausible: define buyer and seller types by their complete economic profiles, assign each type a rational population mass, and maximize mass-weighted welfare subject to mass-weighted supply-demand balance. That preserves the economic question. But it mirrors Theorem 1’s equilibrium characterization, not a computational result in the paper. A computational study would need to specify how values, costs, and feasible sets are encoded. The paper allows general continuous convex or concave functions; the proponent’s explicit polyhedral version supplies an encoding, but that makes a new computational question rather than uncovering one the paper answered.

One could also extend the paper’s auction experiments into a question about iteration or query complexity for type-level best responses. That would be a reasonable research direction, but the paper reports empirical convergence on generated instances, not a complexity result for those procedures. Turning its experiments into a worst-case analysis would add the result to be studied; it would not mirror a result already established there.

The negative case should not claim that a continuous population is implausible. A market with many suppliers and buyers sharing complete economic profiles is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and divisible goods do not make the population continuous by themselves. So the defensible conclusion is narrower: this paper offers no computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is not established; the type-level market-clearing question remains plausible, but it would be a new computational investigation rather than a continuization of the paper’s computational findings.
