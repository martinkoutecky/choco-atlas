The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is conditional, because this paper has no qualifying computational anchor.

Its named results are all proved here, but none asserts NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), approximation, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic running-time bound. Theorem 1 proves an impossibility for universal peer-prediction mechanisms; Theorem 2 strengthens it with costly observation; Lemmas 1–4 derive incentive inequalities; Theorem 3 compares spot-check probabilities; and Corollaries 1–2 instantiate that comparison for mechanism families. These are important mechanism-design results, but not computational-complexity results under ChoCo’s anchor rule. The literature claims mentioned in the introduction do not repair that absence.

There is nevertheless a natural continuous-population question hiding in the paper. I would formulate it as follows, while explicitly labelling it an unanchored extension rather than a compliant mirror.

Consider a large peer-grading or online-review population. A type \(t\in T\) specifies everything relevant about an evaluator: effort cost \(c_t^E\), the distribution of her high-quality signal, her low-quality signal technology, the objects she evaluates, and any other parameters used by the mechanism. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of evaluators of type \(t\). Thus \(n\gg |T|\): many students or reviewers share a small number of evaluation technologies and effort costs.

For each type, the action is a pure or mixed strategy choosing whether to incur effort and which signal-to-report map to use. If \(g\) is such a strategy, let \(x_{t,g}\) be the mass of type \(t\) using it, with
\[ \sum_g x_{t,g}=\mu_t. \]
The induced aggregate report frequencies determine peer rewards. The mechanism keeps the paper’s information restriction: it must be universal and cannot be designed using the signal distribution, even though that distribution is used to evaluate its incentives.

The lead problem would be Continuous Spot-Check Design. Given \(T\), \(\mu\), the signal kernels, effort costs, trusted-signal law, object assignments, and a specified spot-checking mechanism, compute the smallest \(p\in[0,1]\) for which truthful high-effort reporting is a dominant strategy for every positive-mass type. A solution consists of \(p\), together with the truthful strategy profile and a certificate that every typewise deviation has no higher expected utility.

Its closest named result is Lemma 1, proved here:

\[ p_{\mathrm{ds}}\,\mathbb E[y(s^h,s^t)]-c^E = p_{\mathrm{ds}}\,\mathbb E[y(g^l(s^l),s^t)]. \]

In the heterogeneous population version, the corresponding threshold is the maximum of the typewise thresholds,
\[ p_{\mathrm{ds}}^\infty(\mu) = \max_{t:\mu_t>0} \frac{c_t^E} {\mathbb E_t[y(s^h,s^t)]-\mathbb E_t[y(g_t^l(s^l),s^t)]}, \]
whenever the denominators are positive. For a fixed finite mechanism and rational input distributions, this is plausibly Class A: the population enters through finitely many rational expectations and typewise incentive inequalities. This is a genuine high-multiplicity model, since changing \(\mu\) changes the peer-report distribution and hence the unchecked rewards.

A second problem is Continuous Pareto Spot-Checking. Using the same instance, compute the smallest \(p\) for which the truthful high-effort profile Pareto dominates every no-effort equilibrium of the aggregate strategy game:
\[ U_t(\text{truthful},p)\ge U_t(x,p) \qquad \text{for every positive-mass type }t \]
and every equilibrium strategy distribution \(x\).

The closest named anchor is Theorem 3, proved here and restated in the appendix as Theorem 4. It states that, for any spot-checking peer-prediction mechanism, if the low-quality equilibrium exists and Pareto dominates the truthful equilibrium at \(c^E=0\) and \(p=0\), then
\[ p_{\mathrm{Pareto}}\ge p_{\mathrm{ds}} \]
for every \(c^E\ge0\). The natural continuous conjecture is the same inequality with \(p_{\mathrm{Pareto}}^\infty(\mu)\) and \(p_{\mathrm{ds}}^\infty(\mu)\), under typewise versions of the theorem’s assumptions. Exact computation of \(p_{\mathrm{Pareto}}^\infty(\mu)\) is a worthwhile open question: it may be tractable for fixed finite mechanism classes, but equilibrium quantification could also create continuum-specific difficulty.

The authors should recognise this as their problem: it retains their high-quality and low-quality signals, costly observation, trusted reports, universal mechanisms, peer-insensitive mechanisms, Pareto dominance, and the objective of minimizing ground-truth access. It does not fractionalize grades or outcomes. It merely replaces a large population of exchangeable evaluators by rational masses of evaluator types, which is a plausible regime for a massive course, crowdsourcing platform, or review site.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper never studies the computational complexity of either optimization problem. Thus these are credible continuous extensions of the paper’s incentive model, but not mirrors anchored in a named computational result. Under the programme’s strict gate, the correct verdict is therefore: natural continuous analogue, but no compliant positive mirror case for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong at the programme’s formal gate: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are impossibility results about equilibrium incentives; Lemmas 1–4, Theorem 3, and Corollaries 1–2 are incentive inequalities and equilibrium comparisons. None states a complexity class, an algorithm, an approximation guarantee, or a parameterized result. The paper does not even define a computational input whose complexity is being studied. The proponent therefore concedes the decisive point: its proposed mirrors are unanchored extensions, not continuous mirrors of computational results.

The proposed mirror of Lemma 1 is especially weak as a continuization. Lemma 1 concerns a peer-insensitive mechanism whose unchecked reward is the constant \(W\). Consequently, the threshold

\[ p_{\mathrm{ds}} = \frac{c^E} {\mathbb E[y(s^h,s^t)]-\mathbb E[y(g^\ell(s^\ell),s^t)]} \]

does not depend on the number of agents, their reports, or their population shares. In the heterogeneous version, taking the maximum over positive-mass types still depends only on the support of \(\mu\), not on the masses themselves. Two societies with radically different proportions of evaluator types yield the same threshold whenever they contain the same types. That is not a continuous population problem in ChoCo’s sense; the distribution has been replaced by a list of constraints.

One could make \(\mu\) matter by replacing dominant-strategy truthfulness with a mass-weighted welfare objective, but that changes the paper’s question. Alternatively, one could restore peer-dependent rewards, but then the result is no longer a continuization of Lemma 1: the constant unchecked payoff, which is exactly what makes the lemma work, has been removed.

Preserving the paper’s full model creates a second problem. A complete agent type must include not only effort cost and signal technology, but also the objects assigned to the agent, cross-object correlations, belief-report opportunities, and the mechanism’s role or sampling position. In the paper these features can be agent-specific. Treating them as types gives one type for nearly every individual, destroying high multiplicity. Collapsing them into exchangeable evaluator classes is possible, but then finite assignment patterns, common low-quality signals, random peer sampling, and the paper’s finite joint signal structure have all been replaced by a new mean-field model. That may be an interesting model, but it is not what Lemma 1 analyses.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 3 is stronger, but it still does not rescue the paper. Theorem 3 does not compute anything. It says that, under assumptions concerning one particular low-effort equilibrium,

\[ p_{\mathrm{Pareto}}\ge p_{\mathrm{ds}}. \]

The continuous proposal asks for the smallest \(p\) that dominates every no-effort equilibrium of an aggregate game. That is a newly invented equilibrium-computation problem, not the continuous form of a computational problem in the paper.

There is also no canonical population translation preserving the paper’s strategic object. In the finite game, one agent’s report can affect empirical frequencies, sampled peers, and rewards. In a nonatomic society, an individual deviation has measure zero and does not change those aggregates. If instead one defines equilibria over strategy masses \(x_{t,g}\), one has changed the strategic model from a finite peer-prediction game to a mean-field game. The paper’s coordination problem may then have a different equilibrium structure.

Pareto dominance makes the mismatch sharper. In the paper it is componentwise over named agents. A rare agent with an idiosyncratic cost or signal technology can block Pareto dominance. In the continuum, that agent disappears if its type has measure zero. Requiring dominance for every positive-mass type preserves the word “Pareto” but makes the answer depend mainly on support; weighting types by mass preserves continuity but weakens the paper’s Pareto criterion. Neither is a faithful high-multiplicity version of the stated theorem.

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 do not provide a fallback anchor. Their proofs compare two elicitable signal structures and add effort costs; apart from requiring at least three agents, they do not use population multiplicity or finite-agent computation. The same argument can be written for a continuum, but that would merely show that the impossibility survives a change of language. It would not create a computational question about a continuous society.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer would be easy, nor an objection based on existing high-multiplicity work. A high-multiplicity study of a genuinely computational peer-prediction problem could be worthwhile. The difficulty is that this paper does not contain such a problem. Its best possible continuizations either make the population distribution irrelevant, or introduce a new mean-field equilibrium model whose hard parts come from newly chosen signal, assignment, and equilibrium formalizations. That is a plausible research direction, but not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper under ChoCo’s gate.
