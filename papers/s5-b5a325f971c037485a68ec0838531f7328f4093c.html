The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(M\) of \(m\) indivisible items, an integer population scale \(N\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) on \(\tau\le N\) types, each type \(t\) being a complete monotone utility function \(u_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}^{\ge 0}\), find \(S\subseteq M\) such that \(|S|\le\min(\lfloor(m+N)/2\rfloor,m)\) and \(u_t(S)\ge u_t(M\setminus S)\) for every \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity Agree problem: the society is given by type masses \(\mu_t\), each type carries its monotone utility function, and every type of positive mass must agree; the items remain indivisible, and the size guarantee uses the population scale \(N\).

The objection that survived

The size bound depends on a separate population scale \(N\), while the distribution weights have no effect beyond support; the mirror is therefore effectively a finite Agree instance with repeated constraints.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 1.3 and the Agree guarantee it computes; it does not mirror the Kneser or Schrijver search results.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is the paper’s Agree problem. I would use one anchor: Theorem 1.3, proved in this paper by combining its Kneser algorithm with Proposition 6.3. It says that, given \(m\) items and \(\ell<m\) agents with oracle access to monotone utility functions, a randomized algorithm finds with high probability a set \(S\) agreeable to every agent, with
\[ |S|\le \left\lfloor\frac{m+\ell}{2}\right\rfloor, \]
in time \(m^{O(1)}k^{O(k)}\), where \(k=\left\lceil\frac{m-\ell}{2}\right\rceil\). The paper’s other main results concern Kneser and Schrijver search; they offer no comparably direct population mirror. Theorem 1.3 is an algorithmic anchor, rather than a hardness result.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mass-Agree\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite set \(M\) of \(m\) indivisible items, a positive integer population scale \(N\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\le N\) types. Each type \(t\) is a complete monotone utility function \(u_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q^{\ge 0}\), available by oracle; its population mass is \(N\mu_t\). Find a subset \(S\subseteq M\) such that
\[ |S|\le \min\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{m+N}{2}\right\rfloor,m\right) \quad\text{and}\quad u_t(S)\ge u_t(M\setminus S)\ \text{for every }t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu). \]
A solution is the subset \(S\) itself. This keeps the paper’s indivisible items, complement comparison, and unanimity requirement, while representing the stakeholder population by its type distribution.

A plausible regime is a large association or consortium choosing between two packages formed from a large catalogue of indivisible projects. Many stakeholders share a small number of complete preference profiles: \(N\) is large, \(\tau\ll N\), and the interesting case has \(N<m\). Mass records what fraction of stakeholders has each profile; any type with positive mass must be satisfied. The authors should recognize the underlying question: this is their Agree problem for a high-multiplicity society, with repeated agents grouped by identical utility.

The predicted direction is FPT in the gap parameter \(k=\lceil(m-N)/2\rceil\). If \(N\ge m\), return \(M\). Otherwise, put one copy of each supported type into an \(N\)-agent Agree instance and fill any remaining slots with copies of a supported type. Since the question requires agreement from every positive-mass type, this preserves exactly the feasible subsets, even when the rational masses do not correspond to integer agent counts. Theorem 1.3 then gives the stated size bound and a high-probability solution in \(m^{O(1)}k^{O(k)}\) time, with polynomial overhead to read the support and masses. This is an FPT guarantee, not a claim of polynomial time for every value of \(k\).

The mirror covers Theorem 1.3 and the Agree result it computes; it does not claim to continuize the Kneser or Schrijver graph problems. Further questions include whether the dependence on \(k\) can be improved, and what happens if the requirement is relaxed so that all but an \(\varepsilon\) fraction of population mass must agree.

The soft spot is that the masses \(\mu_t\) do not affect feasibility beyond whether they are zero: unanimity makes even a tiny positive-mass type decisive. The scale \(N\) still affects the size bound, but the distribution’s detailed weights do not. So this is a credible high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s Agree question, not a case where continuous weights unlock a new optimization method or dissolve hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The one anchor is Theorem 1.3, through the paper’s \(\Agree\) problem. The best mirror is the one the proponent gives: keep the finite item set, represent stakeholders by a distribution over complete utility types, require every positive-mass type to agree, and retain a total population scale \(N\) in the size bound. That is a coherent high-multiplicity version. The paper’s theorem even transfers to it by representing the supported types among \(N\) agents. I can’t honestly argue that no author would recognize this as their problem.

The strongest objection is that the distribution’s weights do no work. With the same support, changing the masses from \((1/2,1/2)\) to \((1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\) leaves the feasible sets unchanged: even an arbitrarily small positive-mass type has full veto power. Yet the size bound depends on \(N\), which is not determined by the normalized distribution. Keep \(N\), and the mirror is effectively a finite \(\Agree\) instance with duplicate constraints; discard \(N\), and the paper’s population-dependent size guarantee has no canonical continuous counterpart. This makes the proposed mirror population-indexed, but not meaningfully sensitive to population composition.

A weighted version that permits a small fraction of the population to disagree would make the masses matter, and could be a worthwhile problem. But it changes the paper’s exact unanimity guarantee, so it does not rescue this anchor as a mirror of Theorem 1.3. Likewise, a fixed catalogue with \(N\) growing eventually reaches the paper’s trivial regime \(N\ge m\); scaling the catalogue with the population avoids that, but adds another modeling choice.

So the negative case has one real reservation—mass-insensitivity—and no decisive obstruction. The strongest mirror survives it as a sensible high-multiplicity formulation. I would call the case against weak, not claim that this anchor has been defeated.
