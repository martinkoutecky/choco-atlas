The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high-multiplicity, atomless-population version of the paper’s core problem. The candidates remain discrete; only the electorate is continuized.

Take \(\tau\) voter types \(i\in[\tau]\), with rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Each type has a fixed approval set \(A_i\subseteq C\). Candidates with the same approval pattern across voter types are grouped into types \(R\subseteq[\tau]\), with supply \(c_R\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). An outcome is still an integral committee \(x=(x_R)\), where
\[ 0\le x_R\le c_R,\qquad \sum_R x_R\le k, \]
and type \(i\)’s utility is
\[ u_i(x)=\sum_{R\ni i}x_R. \]

A measurable coalition is represented by masses \(q_i\in[0,\mu_i]\). It can deviate to an integral committee \(y\) satisfying
\[ \sum_R y_R\le k\sum_i q_i. \]
It blocks \(x\) when \(q_i>0\) implies \(u_i(y)>u_i(x)\). The continuous problem is to find an integral committee with no such blocking coalition; in the lead version, find one that is also Pareto-optimal for all positive-mass types.

This is genuinely a population continuization: \(\mu_i\) is a fraction of society, not a count of named voters. It does not fractionalize candidates, committees, utilities, or deviations. Each member of a type receives an integral bundle and has integral utility; only population averages and coalition sizes are fractional.

The lead anchor is Theorem 6.2, proved in this paper. Its full content is: for every instance \((c,k,b)\) with at most five weighted voters, a Pareto-optimal committee in the core can be computed in polynomial time. Here the continuous instance is the one above with
\[ b_i=k\mu_i. \]
Thus the corresponding continuous question is:

Pareto-Optimal Continuous Core Committee, \(\mathrm{PCC}_5\). Given at most five voter types with rational mass vector \(\mu\), candidate-type supplies \(c_R\), and committee size \(k\), output an integral committee \(x\) that is in the population core and is Pareto-optimal among integral committees.

The expected classification is Class A: polynomial time. The paper’s algorithm computes an approximate fractional core allocation, rounds its utility vector down, uses normality of the committee-election monoid for at most five voter types, and then selects a Pareto-optimal integral witness. The action being computed is the actual committee \(x\), not the LP encoding used internally.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 5.2, also proved here: for every weighted instance with at most seven voters, the core is non-empty. Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous Core Existence, \(\mathrm{CCE}_7\). Given a rational population distribution \(\mu\) supported on at most seven approval types, candidate supplies \(c_R\), and committee size \(k\), determine and output an integral committee \(x\) for which no positive-mass coalition can afford an integral committee that strictly improves every participating type.

The answer is always yes. This is a positive existence result, not yet a polynomial-time computation theorem for the \(\tau=6,7\) cases. The paper proves it using the fractional Lindahl core, decomposition into minimal holes, exhaustive classification of the \(50\) six-type holes and \(21{,}818\) relevant seven-type holes, and utility reduction for one voter type.

The continuous interpretation is exact for high-multiplicity finite elections. If a population of \(N\) named voters contains \(N\mu_i\) voters of type \(i\), then the compressed weighted instance has budgets
\[ b_i=k\mu_i=k\frac{N_i}{N}. \]
Conversely, any rational \(\mu\) can be expanded into a finite electorate by choosing a common denominator. A partial continuous coalition does not create a discrepancy: if it contains positive mass of precisely the types in \(I\), enlarging it to all of those types only increases its budget and leaves the utility inequalities unchanged. Hence a continuous blocker exists exactly when a weighted type coalition blocks.

The regime is quite plausible. Think of a city choosing a fixed-size committee of projects from a public slate, with millions of residents belonging to five large stakeholder blocs having common approval patterns; or a national organization selecting a board from a fixed candidate pool, with approval ballots concentrated in a handful of institutional constituencies. The relevant comparison is \(N\gg\tau\), for example millions of voters and five or seven distinct approval types. This is much stronger than the paper’s literal “small jury” interpretation: the number of named agents is large, while the number of complete types remains small.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The paper already works with weighted voters, explicitly identifies the number of distinct approval sets as the operative parameter, and gives the alternative interpretation of voters as weighted decision criteria. The mirror preserves the full object of study: approval utilities, integral committees, proportional coalition budgets, strict blocking, and Pareto comparisons. It is not made easier by replacing the core with EJR, by allowing lotteries, or by making candidates divisible.

The expected boundary is also meaningful. For \(\tau\le5\), the mirror is computationally tractable. For \(\tau\le7\), existence is guaranteed but efficient exact computation remains less clear. For larger \(\tau\), the paper’s conclusion gives a concrete frontier: any empty-core example must have at least eight voter types, at least sixteen candidates, and committee size at least nine. The six-voter failures of monoid normality do not refute the mirror; they show that naive fractional-to-integral rounding fails even though the core can still exist. That is precisely the kind of continuum-specific boundary the programme wants exposed.

The weakest point is that this mirror is very close to an explicit model already present in the paper. Replacing \(b_i\) by \(k\mu_i\) is mathematically an exact reparameterization, and the paper’s voter-type theorem already transfers weighted results to large electorates with repeated approval sets. I would therefore not claim novelty merely from introducing \(\mu\). The positive case is instead that this is strong evidence that the high-multiplicity continuous regime is natural, and it leaves a genuine computational programme beyond the already solved corner: exact complexity for six and seven types, the unrestricted \(\tau\)-type population problem, and the point at which empty cores or computational hardness first appear.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case against both anchors is that the proposed “continuization” has already been carried out mathematically in the paper. This is not the objection that the answer happens to remain unchanged: the continuous instance, the blocking coalitions, and the algorithmic input are isomorphic to the paper’s weighted-voter instance.

For rational masses, set \(b_i=k\mu_i\). Conversely, every positive rational budget vector \(b\) with \(\sum_i b_i=k\) gives \(\mu_i=b_i/k\). A continuous coalition with masses \(q_i\in[0,\mu_i]\) can be enlarged to include every type with \(q_i>0\). Its utility inequalities do not change, because all members of a type are identical, while its budget only increases. Hence a continuous blocker exists exactly when a coalition of weighted voter types blocks. Pareto optimality is likewise unchanged, since it depends only on the integral utility vectors of the committees, not on the masses. The paper explicitly allows arbitrary rational weighted voters and explicitly transfers its results to instances with a bounded number of voter types.

Consequently, Theorem 6.2 does not acquire a new continuous problem under the proposed \(\mathrm{PCC}_5\) formulation. It is precisely the same theorem after the substitution \(b_i=k\mu_i\): same integral committee, same core condition, same Pareto comparison, same normality argument, and same polynomial-time algorithm. Calling the voters a large population rather than weighted types supplies a persuasive application story, but no new computational object. The fact that the algorithm outputs an integral committee does not change this; that is already exactly what the weighted theorem computes.

The same defeats Theorem 5.2. The proposed \(\mathrm{CCE}_7\) is just the paper’s weighted core-existence theorem with budgets \(b_i=k\mu_i\). The six- and seven-type classifications are already classifications of weighted, compressed populations. Their computational content concerns approval-pattern hypergraphs and integral-versus-fractional utility feasibility; it does not depend on whether the weight \(b_i\) is described as a voter budget or as \(k\) times a population mass. The promised boundary at seven types is therefore a boundary in the paper’s existing finite-type model, not a boundary discovered by continuization.

A stronger attempt would use an atomless space of agents rather than finitely many labelled types. That still collapses: with finitely many candidates and approval utilities, agents can have only finitely many approval sets. Pushing the atomless measure forward through the approval map yields exactly the finite mass vector \(\mu\). If one adds continuously varying budgets, valuations, locations, or other parameters, those become substantive types and the problem is no longer the paper’s approval-based core problem. Making candidates or committees divisible would be outcome-space continuization, explicitly outside the programme’s scope.

The honest limitation is that a new research programme for the weighted model with an unbounded number of types could still be worthwhile—especially exact computation beyond five types or the first empty-core instance. But that would be a continuation of the paper’s already weighted/high-multiplicity problem, not a continuous mirror of either named theorem. Thus neither anchor establishes an independent continuous-population question worth adding to the programme.
