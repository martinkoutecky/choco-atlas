The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in \(P\), hard, FPT, or similar. Its named results are mathematical characterizations, not complexity results.

The closest anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved here: the interval egalitarian solution is the only efficient, symmetric, uniformly Hurwicz-compatible solution on all interval games. A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large cooperative membership organization with many interchangeable members. A coalition could be represented by its mass, its worth by an interval-valued function \(W(q)\), and its allocation by a per-capita interval payoff. But extending Theorem 4.2 to that setting would be an axiomatic classification, not a computational problem the theorem analyzes. Calling it a tractability result would overstate the paper.

That extension also requires a substantive modeling choice: the paper permits worths to vary arbitrarily by coalition, while a mass-based mirror must make worth depend on coalition composition rather than the identities of its members. The scenario is conceivable, but the paper does not establish that this restriction preserves the problem its authors study.

The paper’s intervals represent uncertainty about coalition outcomes; its players remain finite. That outcome uncertainty is not itself a continuous population. So the honest conclusion is that the paper may motivate an axiomatic population-continuum extension, but it offers no named computational anchor for a ChoCo mirror. The strongest point for such an extension is the natural large-membership setting; the weakest, and decisive, point is that the computational question would have to be supplied from outside the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.2 classifies efficient, symmetric, uniformly Hurwicz-compatible solution concepts; Theorem 5.2 gives the corresponding classification under grand-coalition certainty. Proposition 3.2 and the core results are also mathematical statements about compatibility and set inclusion. None defines an algorithmic problem or proves a complexity bound. The paper asks which axioms a solution satisfies, not how hard it is to compute an answer from an instance.

The large-membership-organization scenario is plausible, but making it a continuous-population model requires worths to depend on coalition mass or type composition. The paper allows an interval worth for every individual coalition, including coalitions of the same size with different worths. Restricting that game to a mass-based function could be a sensible new model; it would still turn Theorem 4.2 into an axiomatic classification over that model. The high-multiplicity regime would not supply the missing computational question.

A stronger attempt might ask for algorithms to compute allocations, Shapley values, or core outcomes from a continuous worth function. But that requires choosing how the function is represented and what computational task to solve. Those choices create a new algorithmic project; they do not mirror a computational result in this paper. The authors might recognize the continuous cooperative-game extension as related work, but the paper gives no computational anchor for ChoCo.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s stated gate, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile neighboring project. The proposed mirror is best described as an axiomatic extension, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
