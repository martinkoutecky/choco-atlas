The continuous mirror question

For each rule \(R\in\{\mathrm{GreedyAV},\mathrm{GreedyCost}\}\), given projects \(P\), integer costs \(c\), budget \(B\), target \(p\) with \(c(p)\le B\), a fixed tie-breaking order, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(A\subseteq P\), find a minimum-cardinality deletion set \(X\subseteq P\setminus\{p\}\) such that \(p\) is funded by \(R\) on \(P\setminus X\). The rule orders projects by \(s_\mu(q)=\sum_{A\ni q}\mu_A\) for GreedyAV or \(s_\mu(q)/c(q)\) for GreedyCost, and tests affordability against the remaining integral budget.

The model it lives in

Approval sets \(A\subseteq P\) are the types, each with positive rational mass \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\); the instance has support size \(\tau\) and mass encoding length \(L\). The decision is an integral project deletion set \(X\), and the objective is to minimize \(|X|\), with project costs and budget remaining integral.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s constructive deletion approximation hardness and Theorem 2’s constructive deletion parameterized hardness for GreedyAV and GreedyCost. It leaves project addition, destructive control, and the paper’s other parameterized results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s full classification be stated sharply in \((m,\tau,L)\) for arbitrary rational ballot masses, including how binary mass encoding interacts with the original voter parameter?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large-electorate version of the paper’s constructive control by deleting projects. Picture a citywide participatory-budgeting vote with many thousands of residents and a short project slate. If there are ten projects, there are at most \(2^{10}\) approval types, so a society of \(100{,}000\) voters can be represented by at most \(1{,}024\) ballot types. A type is an approval set \(A\subseteq P\); its mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of residents casting that ballot. The electorate is given by its positive rational masses, with support size \(\tau\) and binary encoding length \(L\).

For each project \(q\), define its support share by
\[ s_\mu(q)=\sum_{A\ni q}\mu_A. \]
GreedyAV scans projects in descending order of \(s_\mu(q)\); GreedyCost scans them in descending order of \(s_\mu(q)/c(q)\). Both use the paper’s fixed tie-breaking order, and fund a project if it fits the remaining budget. Project deletion remains a discrete intervention: choose a set \(X\) of projects to remove, then run the rule on \(P\setminus X\). The population is the object made continuous; neither the projects nor the funded bundle become fractional.

This is very close to the authors’ problem. They already define support through approval ballots and use support, or support divided by cost, to determine the project order. For any rational \(\mu\), clearing denominators gives integer voter counts with exactly the same GreedyAV and GreedyCost outcomes: every score, and every score-to-cost ratio, is multiplied by the same factor. Thus the mirror is precisely a compressed high-multiplicity electorate. That is a natural input regime for a large PB vote, and the paper’s project-control question remains intact.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in the paper. For every \(\epsilon>0\), both GreedyAV-Opt-CCDC and GreedyCost-Opt-CCDC are NP-hard to approximate within \(m^{1-\epsilon}\), even with three voters. The reduction is from Restricted Exact Cover by 3-Sets: it builds set-project costs from powers of \(4\), plus guard projects, so that a yes-instance has a control solution of size \(N\), while a no-instance requires at least \(N^3\) deletions. The three voters’ ballots enforce the intended project order, regardless of tie-breaking. [Theorem 5 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25723).

The corresponding continuous question, which I would call Continuous Greedy PB Minimum Constructive Deletion, is: given \(P\), integer project costs \(c\), budget \(B\), a tie-breaking order, a target \(p\) with \(c(p)\le B\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval sets, find a set \(X\subseteq P\setminus\{p\}\) of minimum cardinality such that \(p\) is funded by GreedyAV—or, in the parallel variant, GreedyCost—on \(P\setminus X\). A solution is a minimum-size deletion set; an approximation returns a deletion set whose size is within the claimed factor of optimum. I expect hardness transfers: the paper’s three-voter instances become distributions over at most three types, and multiplying each voter class produces arbitrarily large electorates with the same masses. The paper’s approximation gap therefore persists in the continuous formulation. This is my strongest mirror because it gives a precise optimization problem tied directly to the paper’s performance-measure motivation.

A second, related anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in the paper: GreedyAV-CCDC and GreedyCost-CCDC are W[1]-hard when parameterized by the deletion limit \(r\), even with three voters. The proof reduces from Perfect Code, encoding graph neighborhoods in the base-\(b\) digits of project costs and using guard projects to enforce the control behavior. [Theorem 2 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25723).

Its continuous decision mirror is Continuous Greedy PB Constructive Deletion: given the same data plus \(r\), is there an \(X\subseteq P\setminus\{p\}\) with \(|X|\le r\) such that \(p\) is funded after deletion? A solution is such a witness set \(X\). Again I expect hardness transfers: the three-voter reduction is already a distribution over at most three approval types, and can represent a population of any size divisible by three. This anchor shows that the hardness survives even when support is tiny; parameterizing only by the number of voter types will not remove it. A useful next question is which restrictions on cost values or cost diversity recover tractability.

These mirrors cover the paper’s constructive project-deletion optimization and decision results under GreedyAV and GreedyCost. They do not claim to cover project addition, destructive control, or participatory budgeting as a whole. The main weakness is also the clearest boundary: the reductions’ hard combinatorics live in the project set and the encoded costs, while the electorate can have only three types. So this is a strong, recognizable high-multiplicity mirror, but the anchors put it in the “hardness transfers” class; they do not show that population continuization itself creates a new hardness phenomenon or yields an algorithmic gain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that this paper’s rules already act on an aggregate population description. Given a finite profile, let \(\mu_A\) be the fraction of voters who approve ballot \(A\). Then each project’s support is \(n s_\mu(q)\), so GreedyAV’s ordering is unchanged; GreedyCost’s score-to-cost ratios are all scaled by the same factor. The execution and every successful deletion set depend on \(\mu\), project costs, the budget, and tie-breaking—not on voter identities. Conversely, every rational \(\mu\) can be realized by clearing denominators and forming a finite profile. For the mirrors proposed here, the continuous input is therefore an aggregate re-encoding of the paper’s input, and the intervention remains the same discrete choice of projects.

That makes Theorem 5’s minimum constructive deletion mirror unusually thin. The better version is exactly the proposed citywide model: rational ballot masses, a minimum-cardinality project deletion, and an integral project slate. It is sensible, and it preserves the intended question. But the paper’s approximation reduction already uses only three voters. Turning those ballots into three rational masses leaves the construction and its gap intact. The continuous version thus appears to add a compressed representation of the same instances, rather than a new model whose complexity needs independent discovery. This is the best negative point I can make; because the masses are binary encoded, analyzing algorithms in \(m,\tau,L\) could still be useful.

Theorem 2’s decision mirror has the same weakness. Its three-voter reduction becomes a distribution with at most three ballot types, so the continuous input neither removes the reduction nor creates a distinct population-level control action. A stronger formulation could study constructive deletion directly on arbitrary rational distributions, but the paper’s rule still sees only the induced support shares and their ordering. That question is coherent, yet its connection to the paper is primarily an exact change of representation.

I cannot make this a strong case against every plausible scenario. Approval ballots give a natural finite type space, and large PB electorates can plausibly contain many voters of each type. The objectives do not track named voters, and the continuum does not degenerate. Under ChoCo’s stated boundary, existing high-multiplicity structure supports the mirror rather than disqualifying it. So the most defensible negative verdict is limited: these particular anchors may yield near-immediate continuous corollaries, but I do not have an honest fundamental obstruction to a worthwhile mirror of the paper.
