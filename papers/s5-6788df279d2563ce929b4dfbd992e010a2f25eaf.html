What the mirror covers

The propositions cover equilibrium and shift formulas for linear AI transformations; the empirical bias studies, network simulations, Grok audit, and legal analysis are not covered by the proposed optimization.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I can’t honestly give a positive mirror case that meets the brief’s anchor requirement. Its numbered propositions are proved in the paper, but they are analytical results about opinion dynamics, not results stating that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class.

The nearest candidates are Proposition 3.1, which gives a unique equilibrium and geometric convergence for the linear AI transformation; Proposition 3.2, which gives a closed-form expression for the equilibrium shift; and Proposition 3.3, which shows that with uniform stubbornness and a doubly stochastic influence matrix, the average equilibrium shift is a scaled version of the AI’s one-off bias, with the scale exceeding \(1\) when \(m(1-\lambda)>\lambda\). These are meaningful results, but the paper does not pose or analyze the complexity of computing an intervention or deciding a property of the equilibrium. Treating the formula in Proposition 3.1 as a theorem that an underlying problem is in P would overstate what the paper establishes.

There is a plausible follow-on mirror: in a large platform population, define types by innate opinion, stubbornness, AI adoption, and a shared influence role; let type masses interact through a fixed influence kernel; then ask for the minimum-cost allocation of AI exposure that moves the equilibrium average opinion past a target. A finite set of shared network roles could make this a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But the paper neither studies that optimization problem nor proves a named result about it. Its existing equations already make opinions continuous, while its society remains a finite network. So that candidate is a proposed new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 3.1–3.3 are substantial, but they characterize the dynamics analytically: Proposition 3.1 gives a unique equilibrium and a convergence bound; Proposition 3.2 expresses the equilibrium shift; Proposition 3.3 gives an amplification factor under uniform stubbornness and a doubly stochastic influence matrix. None analyzes the complexity of a computational problem. Their continuous opinion values do not make the population continuous: the model still has \(N\) distinct nodes in a finite network.

A stronger follow-on than the proponent’s sketch is conceivable. In a large platform population, types could record innate opinion, stubbornness, AI adoption, and one of finitely many network roles; a fixed influence kernel could describe how those roles interact. One could then ask for the minimum-cost allocation of AI exposure that moves the equilibrium average past a threshold. That can be a sensible high-multiplicity model. But it adds an intervention problem and an objective that this paper does not formulate or analyze. The propositions do not establish the complexity of choosing those masses. And if preserving the original network requires encoding each node’s distinctive influence relations, the types must retain increasingly detailed network information; fixing a small role set instead defines a special model.

The propositions can motivate analytic continuum versions—for example, replacing a finite influence matrix with a measure-preserving kernel. That would continuize the population, but the resulting equilibrium and amplification questions remain analytic. Neither that reformulation nor the proposed allocation problem supplies the missing computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong for the claim that this paper has a worthwhile computational continuous mirror under ChoCo’s stated standard: none of the proposed anchors qualifies. It does not prove that the allocation problem would be uninteresting as a separate research direction; it shows why that adjacent question should not be presented as a mirror of this paper’s results.
