The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many agent types and job types with binary-rational masses and strict rankings over acceptable opposite-side types, represent each type by an atomless cohort. Does there exist a measurable, measure-preserving partial bijection between the two populations such that no feasible rival matching is preferred by more total population mass and no rival is preferred by more agent mass; if so, find one maximizing matched mass? Each person compares their own assignments in the two matchings, with unmatched last and equal assignments abstaining.

The model it lives in

Use atomless cohorts for each agent and job type. A feasible matching is a measurable partial bijection along acceptable type pairs; its type-pair masses are marginals, while popularity comparisons use the assignments of the same individuals across matchings. The objective is total matched mass.

The objection that survived

Type-flow marginals do not encode how the same individuals are assigned across two matchings, so the proposed finite representation and polynomial-time route remain unestablished.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's existence decision and maximum-size objective as existence and maximum matched mass; it does not mirror the paper's other technical claims as separate results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can popularity and agent-side popularity be characterized by finite constraints over the type support while preserving same-person comparisons?
  • What is the exact complexity in the number of types, acceptable pairs, and mass bit length?
  • How does this atomless problem relate to discrete instances with repeated types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1 (Main Theorem), proved in this paper: given a marriage instance \(G=(A\cup B,E)\) with strict preferences, a linear-time algorithm decides whether a fully popular matching exists; if one exists, it returns a maximum-size fully popular matching. Here “fully popular” means popular among all vertices and also \(A\)-popular, where the second comparison counts only votes from \(A\).

I would mirror that result with Mass-Fully-Popular Matching. An instance has finite sets \(I\) and \(J\) of agent types and job types, acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\), strict rankings of neighboring types, and binary-rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\ge 0\), normalized so \(\sum_i\mu_i+\sum_j\nu_j=1\). Let \(\tau=|I|+|J|\). Each type represents a nonatomic cohort: its members share the same relevant preferences, and members of one type are interchangeable to members of the other side.

A matching is a measurable one-to-one partial pairing of the agent and job populations along acceptable type pairs. Write \(x_{ij}\) for the mass paired from type \(i\) to type \(j\); feasibility requires \(x_{ij}\ge0\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\mu_i\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\nu_j\). Although \(x\) can be fractional, it represents integrally matched individuals: atomless cohorts can be split by mass, and each individual is matched at most once.

For any two matchings \(M,N\), let \(\Delta_A(M,N)\) be the mass of agents who prefer their assignment in \(M\) minus the mass who prefer their assignment in \(N\); define \(\Delta_{A\cup B}\) the same way over both populations. Unmatched is last, and anyone with the same assignment in both matchings abstains. Call \(M\) fully popular if, for every feasible rival \(N\), both \(\Delta_A(M,N)\ge0\) and \(\Delta_{A\cup B}(M,N)\ge0\). The problem is: decide whether such an \(M\) exists and, if so, return one maximizing \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\), with a finite type-flow encoding and an implementation as a measurable matching.

The expected direction is Class A, provisionally: the finite support suggests a mass-aware extension of the paper’s stable-matching and popularity machinery, or a finite flow and LP formulation. The target complexity would be polynomial in \(|I|+|J|+|E|\) and the mass bit length \(L\). Simply expanding the masses into individuals does not establish that bound: binary-rational masses can require exponentially many copies. Further questions include whether a finite exact flow characterization exists, whether optimum masses have polynomial-size rational encodings, and how the mass problem relates in both directions to discrete instances with repeated types.

This is a plausible mirror because it retains the paper’s underlying matching question and its two head-to-head tests, including the special status of side \(A\); maximizing matched mass mirrors its maximum-size objective. The continuum is in the population. It does not replace individual matching with lotteries: each person still receives at most one job. A plausible regime is a large placement market with recurring applicant profiles and position profiles, where many people share exact rankings over a small set of counterpart types.

The main vulnerability is the paper’s strict rankings over individual vertices. My mirror assumes rankings over anonymous types. If rankings among named people are essential, those identity-specific distinctions may force the number of types \(\tau\) to grow with the population. The positive case therefore rests on a particular, credible regime—markets where preferences are based on cohort attributes and individuals within a cohort are interchangeable—not on all matching markets. The paper’s cited NP-hardness result from FKPZ19 is background, not a numbered result proved here, so I do not use it as a second anchor or claim this mirror covers that hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives a linear-time decision algorithm and returns a maximum-size fully popular matching. Nor can I dismiss the proposed market regime. Large placement markets with recurring applicant and job profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity setting.

The strongest objection is that the proposed finite type-flow description does not, by itself, determine the paper’s voting comparisons. Popularity counts how the *same individuals* fare under two matchings. Type flows record each matching’s marginal assignments, but not how those assignments line up across alternatives.

For example, take one agent type of mass \(1\), three job types of mass \(1/3\), and a common agent ranking \(b_1 \succ b_2 \succ b_3\). Let \(M\) assign a third of the agents to each job type. A rival \(N\) can have exactly the same type flows while cyclically reassigning the individuals: those with \(b_1\) under \(M\) get \(b_3\) under \(N\), those with \(b_2\) get \(b_1\), and those with \(b_3\) get \(b_2\). Then two thirds of agents prefer \(N\), even though the flow matrices for \(M\) and \(N\) are identical. So the mass-flow variables cannot certify \(A\)-popularity unless the model also represents the joint, person-by-person assignments.

There are two repairs, but neither makes the proponent’s proposed polynomial flow formulation follow automatically. One can fix atomless identity spaces for each type, define matchings as measurable assignments on those spaces, and compare the same people across every pair of matchings. That is a coherent problem, but popularity then depends on the alignment of assignments, and a finite exact representation of that condition still needs to be established. Or one can preserve the paper’s strict rankings over named neighbors; then those names and rankings are part of a type’s complete description, and the proposed compression to a few types ranked only by counterpart type changes the preference model.

This leaves a real negative case against the *stated finite-flow mirror*: its key voting condition is not captured by its advertised variables, and repairing that gap may alter the computational problem. But I cannot honestly turn that into the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The fixed-identity, atomless version is plausible and remains within the population-continuization idea. The objection weakens the proponent’s tractability proposal; it does not defeat the anchor or rule out a worthwhile continuous question.
