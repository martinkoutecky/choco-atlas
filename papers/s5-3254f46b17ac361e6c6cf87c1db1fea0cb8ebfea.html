The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a positive case for continuizing the paper’s central aggregation problem, but the paper has no qualifying named computational result to anchor it to. Theorem 1 gives the unique axiomatic distance formula; Lemmas 1 and 2 support that characterization. None classifies an aggregation problem as tractable or hard. The paper defines PAS-ADAM and gives a mixed \(0\)-\(1\) formulation in Appendix C, then reports solving instances with LINGO, but it states no complexity result. I would not present Theorem 1 as one.

The most credible *candidate mirror*, then, is an unanchored continuization of PAS-ADAM itself. Consider a large public consultation on a fixed slate of projects. Each participant submits a complete ranking and marks which projects are acceptable. A type is one complete preference-approval structure \((R_t,A_t)\); mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants with that type. In a regime with many participants and a moderate number \(\tau\) of distinct types, the input is the supported types and their binary-rational masses. The output remains a valid preference-approval structure \(q=(R,A)\): the ranking and approval set are still discrete. For fixed \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), ask for any minimizer of

\[ \min_{q=(R,A)\in\mathcal R(X)} \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t \sum_{x\in X} \left( \alpha\left|P_{R_t}(x)-P_R(x)\right| +(1-\alpha)\left|I_{A_t}(x)-I_A(x)\right| \right). \]

The instance consists of the alternatives, the supported PAS types, their masses, and \(\alpha\); a solution is a minimizing group PAS, with its objective value. This is precisely the paper’s total-distance aggregation objective with repeated identical participants compressed into type masses. The paper’s venture-investment and funding-review examples have only five reviewers, so I would motivate the high-multiplicity regime with a much larger recurring consultation over a standardized slate. Participants who submit the same ranking and approvals are interchangeable for this objective, and \(\tau\) could be far smaller than the population. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their group aggregation problem with a population representation, rather than a change to the outcome space.

My tentative expectation for this candidate is Class A, through structure in the weighted footrule-style ranking objective and the approval mismatch term. But that is a conjecture, not a result of the paper: the requirement that the output be a valid PAS couples rankings and approvals, and the mixed-integer formulation alone does not establish tractability. The next questions would be whether a polynomial algorithm exists in the number of alternatives, support size, and bit length; whether minimizers or approval sets are unique; and how the optimum changes with \(\alpha\).

The central named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every pair of preference-approval structures, Axioms 1–7 uniquely determine their distance as the sum, over alternatives, of \(\alpha\) times the absolute difference in ranking positions plus \(\beta\) times the absolute difference in approval indicators. This gives a well-defined objective for the candidate mirror, but it does not assert a complexity classification for the aggregation problem, so it is not a valid computational anchor under your criteria. My weakest point is therefore decisive: this is a plausible continuous version of the paper’s main optimization problem, but there is no named hardness or algorithmic result here whose fate in the continuum can be tested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes the distance function from axioms; Lemmas 1 and 2 support that characterization. PAS-ADAM is an optimization problem, and Appendix C gives a mixed-integer formulation, but the paper establishes no algorithmic or hardness classification. So Theorem 1 is not a computational anchor.

The proposed PAS-ADAM mirror is still a credible problem. A large consultation over a fixed slate could have many participants and a moderate number of distinct ranking-and-approval types. Their type masses define the objective directly. The paper’s five-person committee examples do not rule out that regime, and the type definition handles identical participants cleanly. I do not see a sound argument that this scenario is nonsensical.

The best objection to that mirror is about its likely contribution: every rational-mass instance can be scaled to an integer electorate with the same minimizing group structure. The output remains a discrete PAS, and the population masses become weights in the paper’s existing objective. The mirror therefore preserves the same combinatorial aggregation problem; it adds a high-multiplicity input representation without introducing fractional decisions or an evident new continuous-optimization structure. That may make it a less promising route to the programme’s optimization toolbox, but high-multiplicity complexity is itself within the programme’s scope.

So the negative case is narrow: there is no theorem-level computational finding to mirror, and the proposed mirror may offer limited new optimization structure. I cannot honestly extend that into the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The large-consultation version survives as a sensible high-multiplicity question, even though this paper supplies no complexity result for it.
