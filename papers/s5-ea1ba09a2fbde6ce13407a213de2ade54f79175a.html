The continuous mirror question

Given two two-sided typed markets with the same finite type supports and binary-rational masses, where the second market differs only by merging two adjacent preference tiers in one type’s order, decide whether the maximum total matched mass among weakly stable fractional matchings is equal in the two markets.

The model it lives in

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite sets of agent types with rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), each side having total mass one. A feasible matching is a matrix \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) whose row and column sums do not exceed the corresponding masses. It is weakly stable if no acceptable type pair has positive mass on both sides that is unmatched or assigned to a strictly less preferred type. Compare the maximum value of \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\) before and after merging two adjacent tiers in one type’s preferences.

What the mirror covers

This mirrors Theorem 1’s Is Size Same tie-merge sensitivity question. It leaves aside the paper’s instance-generation framework and its other generators’ guarantees and limitations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is equality of the two maximum stable matched masses polynomial-time decidable in the type support sizes and mass bit lengths, or is it hard?
  • Can a discrete hardness reduction preserve the optimum under fractional matching, or does fractional stability yield a tractable method?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the decision problem behind the paper’s first generator, rather than the task of generating instances.

Anchor. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, concerns Is Size Same: given SMTI instances \(I\) and \(\widetilde I\), where \(\widetilde I\) is obtained by merging two adjacent ties in one person’s preference list, decide whether \(\operatorname{opt}(\widetilde I)=\operatorname{opt}(I)\). The theorem says that if a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm solves Is Size Same, then \(P=NP\). This is the paper’s named hardness anchor for the cost of checking whether a tie merge preserves the optimum. The paper also cites the NP-hardness of MAX SMTI from earlier work, but does not give it as a numbered result proved here.

A continuous version would model a large two-sided assignment market. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite sets of types on the two sides. A type is a complete weak preference order over acceptable types on the other side, including which types are unacceptable. Each type \(a\in A\) has rational mass \(\mu_a\), and each \(b\in B\) has rational mass \(\nu_b\); take total mass one on each side. The instance lists only types with positive mass, so its support size is \(\tau\).

A matching is a matrix \(x_{ab}\geq 0\), with row and column sums bounded by the corresponding type masses. Its value is \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\). Call it weakly stable if there is no pair of acceptable types \((a,b)\) for which positive mass of type \(a\) is unmatched or assigned to a type it strictly likes less than \(b\), and positive mass of type \(b\) is unmatched or assigned to a type it strictly likes less than \(a\). Let \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,\nu)\) be the largest value of a weakly stable matching.

The problem Continuous Same Optimum under Tie Merge takes such an instance and a second one with the same type sets and masses, differing only in that two adjacent preference tiers have been merged in one type’s order. It asks whether the two values of \(\operatorname{OPT}\) are equal. A solution is YES or NO, with the optima defined by the mass-matching problem above. The preference change applies to the whole mass of that type, as a type-level change should. The instance uses binary rational masses; its natural complexity parameters include the numbers of types on both sides and their mass bit lengths.

This regime is plausible for a large centralized assignment market: for example, many applicants and positions grouped into a modest number of cohorts with the same acceptable categories and preferences over program or job types. The number of people can then be far larger than the number of distinct types, and mass captures the fact that changing a cohort’s preference tier is not a one-person intervention. It is also a recognizable extension of the paper’s question: does coarsening preferences change the maximum size of a stable assignment? The co-modification is that the ranking is over counterpart types and assignments can split mass; that is what makes this a population-continuum question.

My expectation for this continuous decision problem is Class C, as a conjecture: fractional assignments change the feasible stable outcomes, so Theorem 1’s discrete hardness does not by itself transfer. But the weak-stability condition still has a disjunctive choice for each pair of types—at least one side must have no mass willing to block—and that leaves room for hardness driven by the type structure. The central follow-up is whether that structure admits a polynomial optimization or separation method, or whether the disjunction yields a continuum-specific hardness proof. A second important question is whether some discrete reduction can preserve the optimum despite fractional matching; that would establish hardness transfer instead.

The mirror covers only the paper’s tie-merge sensitivity question. It does not cover the construction of a complete generator or claim that every result in the paper has a continuous counterpart. Its weakest point is the change in stability semantics: a type-level fractional matching can have stable mass allocations with no corresponding integral stable matching of the finite instance. That prevents using Theorem 1 as evidence that the proposed continuous problem is hard. It does not, however, make the question arbitrary: the paper’s own target is precisely the effect of merging preference tiers on maximum stable matching size.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against has to concede that Theorem 1 is a genuine computational anchor: the paper names Is Size Same and proves that a polynomial-time solver would imply \(P=NP\). So the objection cannot be that the paper has no computational result to mirror.

The concern is what that result is *for*. Is Size Same is a check inside one proposed method for generating SMTI benchmark instances with known optima. The paper’s practical question is whether that generator can produce useful test instances. Comparing the maximum stable mass before and after a cohort’s preferences are coarsened would be a coherent new problem, but it would be a narrow sensitivity question about a modified matching market. It would not, by itself, carry over the paper’s instance-generation problem or answer whether useful benchmarks with known optima can be generated.

There is also a real modeling gap. In the paper, preferences rank named people. A mass matrix between types can represent the relevant blocking information only if agents treat members of each counterpart type as interchangeable. Otherwise, it loses who outranks whom, and therefore which pairs can block. Preserving those identity-specific preferences can make support grow with the population; replacing them with preferences over counterpart classes gives a sensible cohort-matching model, but changes the preference domain and stability structure.

The proponent’s stronger version—moving a positive fraction of a cohort into a tied-preference type and asking how maximum stable mass responds—addresses the objection that one type’s entire mass changes at once. It is also well-posed and plausible for some labor or placement markets. That makes a categorical rejection hard to defend. My negative case is therefore about priority, not admissibility: this is a comparatively bespoke mirror whose connection to the paper’s main contribution depends on treating its generator subroutine as the object of interest. I do not see a fundamental reason no worthwhile scenario exists, so the universal negative claim is weak.
