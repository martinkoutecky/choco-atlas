What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest assessment is that this paper has no eligible computational anchor, so I can’t give a defensible continuous mirror of one of its named results under your criteria.

The closest results are characterizations of incentive properties. For example, Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that on a local domain, DSIC is equivalent to G-LOBIC plus elementary monotonicity; on rich connected domains without restoration, positive responsiveness joins that equivalence. Theorem 3 gives a related characterization for unanimous rules on the single-peaked domain. Theorem 1, cited from Majumdar and Sen (2004), says that on the unrestricted domain with at least three alternatives, unanimity and G-OBIC are equivalent to DSIC. These are mathematical results about social choice functions, not claims that a problem is tractable, hard, or captured by an algorithm or encoding.

There is a superficially promising population interpretation: let types be single-peaked rankings and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of a large electorate holding each ranking. But the paper’s agents have individual preferences, and each agent evaluates a mechanism using beliefs over the other agents’ finite preference profiles. Those beliefs are not empirical type shares. In a nonatomic population, one person’s report would not change the aggregate distribution; making a positive mass of agents able to deviate would instead introduce a coalition-control problem that the paper does not study. That construction might motivate new work, but it does not mirror Theorem 2 or Theorem 3 closely enough to serve as their continuous analogue.

So there is no prover-ready continuous problem per anchor to state without inventing a different problem. The paper’s probability distributions and restricted preference domains do not make its society continuous, and its numbered results do not classify computational complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent identified: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 characterizes when axioms imply DSIC; Theorem 3 gives a related characterization on the single-peaked domain; Theorem 1, which the paper cites, characterizes unanimous G-OBIC rules on the unrestricted domain. They do not classify an algorithmic problem or its complexity. The paper also gives no representation of a social choice function as an input, so there is no running time or input size to carry into the continuous model.

The best population version of Theorem 2 would represent an anonymous election by a distribution over rankings and its rule by a function of that distribution. But its DSIC and LOBIC conditions compare one agent’s truthful report with that same agent’s deviation while everyone else stays fixed. In an atomless society, one agent has zero mass, so the distribution is unchanged by either report; the incentive condition becomes vacuous. Giving a deviator positive mass makes the question one of block manipulation. Keeping finite agents preserves the original incentive question, but also preserves a finite-agent model rather than supplying a continuous-population mirror.

Theorem 3’s strongest candidate has the same problem. Weak elementary monotonicity tests a particular unilateral adjacent swap at a top-2 profile, and unanimity and Pareto efficiency are used across exact preference profiles. A positive-mass version would test a different kind of deviation and require a new characterization. Theorem 1 has no better route: its generic-prior condition distinguishes probabilities of different subsets of finite profiles, while a population distribution records type masses rather than those profile events. Recasting these conditions over distributions would create an axiomatic question about continuum mechanisms, not a computational question anchored in this paper. The fact that Theorem 1 is prior work does not itself count against a mirror; its axiomatic character does.

A large electorate with repeated ballots could motivate a worthwhile follow-on project—for example, choosing an explicit representation for anonymous rules and studying the complexity of evaluating them. But this paper supplies neither that representation nor a computational problem for it. The negative case is therefore strong for a mirror of this paper, while weaker as a universal claim that no nearby project could be valuable: a newly formulated continuum incentive theory might be interesting, but its value would not come from continuizing one of these results.
