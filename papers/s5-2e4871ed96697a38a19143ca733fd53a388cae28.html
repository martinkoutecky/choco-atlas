The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a research extension, not a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result. The paper has no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result such as membership in P, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability.

The closest result is Proposition 5, proved in the paper. Under MONDAI\(_k\), it characterizes the feasible satisficing thresholds as
\[ S(x_0)=\{(\theta_{1:k},\theta_{k+1:p}) : \theta_{1:k}\leq\Theta_{1:k}(\theta_{k+1:p},x_0)\}, \]
where \(\Theta_{1:k}\) is computed along the trajectory generated by the feedback rule that chooses the least action satisfying the outsiders’ thresholds. This is an analytic characterization of a feasible set, not a complexity or algorithmic result with a finite input model and running-time claim. So it cannot serve as one of the requested anchors.

A plausible extension would treat residents affected by a sustainability policy as a population: a type is a group sharing the same indicator response, \(\mu_t\) is that group’s population fraction, and everyone faces the same policy path through the dynamics. One could ask which population-weighted sustainability thresholds are achievable, or optimize a welfare function over them. That is close to the paper’s threshold and social-welfare discussion, but the mass weighting and population of types are additions. In the paper, the \(p\) stakeholders are distinct dimensions of the bargaining problem; duplicating stakeholders with identical indicators adds no mass-dependent structure. The paper also leaves the final social choice among Pareto outcomes outside its scope.

Thus I would not submit a continuous computational problem as a mirror of this paper’s results. The stakeholder-to-population analogy is conceivable, but it does not produce a qualifying anchor, and making mass matter would require extending the problem the paper actually studies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent offers no qualifying anchor: the paper has no theorem, lemma, or proposition about computational complexity. Proposition 5 is the closest candidate—it characterizes the satisficing set under MONDAI\(_k\)—and Proposition 4 gives a feedback rule. But both are mathematical results for arbitrary nonlinear dynamics over an infinite horizon. The paper specifies no finite input representation or running-time claim. Turning either into a complexity question would first require adding those ingredients, so the resulting problem would be an extension, not a continuous mirror of a computational result here.

The strongest population-based extension would model residents by types that share policy responses or indicator profiles, with each type’s mass given as a fraction of society. That could be sensible for climate or resource policy, and might lead to useful computational questions. But it does not follow from the paper’s stakeholder model. Its \(p\) coordinates are distinct sustainability issues with potentially incomparable units, not interchangeable people. If we keep those \(p\) issues, duplicating a stakeholder does not make mass matter; using type frequencies to weight their outcomes adds a new social-welfare criterion. If we instead give every type its own bargaining coordinate, we need new choices about which thresholds count as guaranteed, how to compare a continuum of outcomes, and how veto power works. Those choices define a different problem.

The negative case is strongest against a *mirror of this paper*, not against every adjacent research idea. A continuous population of policy-affected residents could be worth studying, but it would need its own model and computational specification. The honest limit is that I cannot rule out such a worthwhile extension in every scenario. It does not rescue a mirror of this paper’s results: there is no computational result here for continuization to mirror.
