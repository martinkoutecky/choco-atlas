The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. So it has no anchor of the kind your rubric asks for, and I cannot honestly present a compliant positive case based on one.

The closest named computational result is Algorithm 4, “Create Institutions Preferences Using Indifference.” It orders applicants for each study by academy first (local before nonlocal), then relative rank, absolute rank, and disjunction size; it randomly orders applicants within each resulting group and concatenates those groups into the study’s preference list. The paper presents this as a procedure, but gives no complexity classification or theorem about the underlying matching problem.

If Algorithm 4 is accepted as a looser anchor, a plausible mirror is a continuous APB matching problem. An applicant type would comprise the academy and the complete partial-preorder preferences used by the procedure. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu_t\) over those types; each study has a capacity measured as a fraction of the population. Apply the paper’s optimistic completion and priority rules to each type. A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{t,s}\), where \(x_{t,s}\) is the share of type \(t\) assigned to study \(s\), respecting type mass and study capacities, with no blocking pair: no positive mass of applicants can move to a study they prefer while displacing lower-priority mass or using an available seat. The fractional assignment represents different members of a cohort receiving whole seats; it does not give an individual a fractional seat. Within an exact priority tie, mass can be split at the cutoff, representing the aggregate effect of the paper’s random ordering.

The natural regime is a large regional or national nonselective admissions system with many applicants but substantially fewer distinct combinations of academy and preference order. That is plausible given the paper’s APB setting, but the paper supplies no evidence that the number of such exact types is actually small. If accepted, I would expect this mirror to be tractable through a type-level version of deferred acceptance, and would ask whether it preserves the discrete mechanism’s stable outcomes and how its fractional cutoff relates to random tie-breaking.

The mirror retains the paper’s central concern—how applicants’ expressed preferences determine admissions priorities and reduce random selection. Its weakest point is that the proposed mirror is an extension of a named algorithm, not a result the authors prove or classify; moreover, the required small-support regime is plausible but unsubstantiated. The paper’s cited polynomial Gale–Shapley procedure is background attributed to earlier work, not a numbered result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The firmest objection is that this paper gives no named complexity result to continuize. Algorithm 4 constructs each study’s applicant priority list; the paper does not classify its complexity or define a computational problem around it. Gale–Shapley is background credited to earlier work. The proponent acknowledges this, then treats Algorithm 4 as a looser anchor. That may motivate a new project, but it does not supply the kind of result this programme is meant to mirror.

The proposed population model is nevertheless plausible. Applicants could be typed by academy and complete preference preorder, with study capacities scaled to population and random ties represented by split mass. APB’s large applicant cohort makes repeated types conceivable, though the paper gives no data on exact support size. And because the paper’s random tie-breaking treats tied applicants symmetrically, an assignment rate for a type could represent their ex ante exposure; it would be wrong to claim that mass necessarily erases the relevant concern.

The stronger objection is that this model adds the computational question the paper never asks. It must specify a stability notion for mass assignments, how ties and cutoffs work, and what is to be computed. “Find a stable assignment” is a natural question, but it is not a complexity result of this paper. Continuum matching work already provides nearby models with a continuum of students and deferred-acceptance analogues, while typed-agent work studies algorithmic stable-matching problems. Those are not exact collisions with the APB variant, and existing high-multiplicity work is support for the regime, not a reason to dismiss it. They do show that a worthwhile project would need a sharper APB-specific question than simply computing a stable mass assignment. ([continuum matching model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881); [typed stable matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04109))

So the best negative case is narrow: this paper is a poor anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme, because its contribution is a preference-processing proposal and its named algorithm has no complexity claim to mirror. The broader claim—that no plausible scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror—is not established. A large admissions cohort with recurring preference types is credible, and the resulting mass-matching question may be worth studying on its own. I would reject the proponent’s claim that this paper supplies a compliant computational anchor, but I would not claim to have defeated every possible continuous mirror of its matching model.
