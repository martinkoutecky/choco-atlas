The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a one-anchor case. The paper contains no numbered theorem asserting a computational class such as \(P\), NP-hardness, or FPT. Its computational statements are in the introduction and cited from elsewhere: MNW is polynomial-time computable for binary valuations, while general additive MNW is hard. Those are not anchors proved in this paper.

The best anchor is therefore Theorem 3.2, proved here:

For every fixed \(n\ge 2\), under binary valuations, every allocation rule satisfying EF1, strategyproofness, neutrality, minimal completeness, and IDU must maximize Nash welfare.

Here “maximize Nash welfare” means maximizing first the number of agents receiving positive value and then the product of their positive utilities; ties may be broken arbitrarily. This is not itself a complexity theorem, but it identifies the underlying problem whose continuous computational version is worth asking.

My lead mirror is Continuum Binary-MNW Allocation. Consider a large population of households receiving repeated, standardized indivisible goods—say meal packages, medical devices, or school-resource slots. A type \(t\) is a complete approval set \(P_t\subseteq G\): all households of that type value exactly those goods at \(1\), and all other goods at \(0\). The population is described by masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), where \(\tau=|T|\ll n\). Each good \(g\) has per-capita supply \(q_g\), so an \(N\)-agent realization has approximately \(Nq_g\) copies. Each individual still receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

An instance is therefore

\[ (G,T,\mu,q), \]

with rational \(G\), approval types \(P_t\), type masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_g\). Let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq P_t\). Feasibility requires

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq P_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

for every type \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t}\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}=q_g \]

for every valued good \(g\), with unvalued goods left unallocated. The utility of a type-\(t\) agent receiving \(S\) is \(|S|\).

The question is to compute a feasible \(x\) maximizing, lexicographically,

\[ \left( \sum_{t}\sum_{\varnothing\ne S\subseteq P_t}x_{t,S}, \quad \sum_{t}\sum_{\varnothing\ne S\subseteq P_t}x_{t,S}\log |S| \right). \]

The first coordinate is the mass receiving positive utility. The second is the normalized logarithm of the Nash product: in an \(N\)-agent replica,

\[ \frac{1}{N}\log\!\left(\prod_{i:u_i>0}u_i\right) \]

converges exactly to the second expression. Thus this is not a softened welfare objective; it is the high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s MNW problem.

I would expect this problem to be Class A. It is a configuration LP with exponentially many bundle variables, but its pricing problem appears polynomial. For a fixed type, the reduced cost of a bundle has the form

\[ \sum_{g\in S}a_g+\log |S|+\text{constant}. \]

For each cardinality \(k\), the best bundle consists of the \(k\) approved goods with largest adjusted coefficients; scanning \(k=0,\ldots,|P_t|\) solves the pricing problem. Thus column generation or separation should yield a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length, subject to the usual exact-arithmetic treatment of the logarithmic coefficients. The binary restriction is doing real work here, just as it does in the paper’s cited polynomial algorithms.

The continuous theorem-level analogue of Theorem 3.2 would say that any rule over these distributional instances satisfying continuum EF1, neutrality, minimal completeness, IDU, and an appropriate finite-replica form of strategyproofness must output a solution of Continuum Binary-MNW Allocation. The paper’s authors should recognize this as their problem: the types are precisely approval profiles, the bundles remain indivisible for every individual, and the only continuization is of the population and its repeated supply.

I would not use Theorem 4.2 as a second anchor. It is restricted to exactly two agents, so it does not by itself provide a meaningful many-agent regime. Nor would I stretch Proposition 3.4 or the tie-breaking theorems into computational anchors.

The weakest point is the incentive-axiom bridge. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass, so ordinary strategyproofness becomes nearly vacuous. The honest formulation must therefore require strategyproofness on every rational finite replica, or impose a carefully chosen cohort-deviation condition. That is a genuine co-modification of the theorem, not merely notation. A second weakness is that the mirror needs repeated goods or per-capita supply: if the paper’s goods are all unique and remain finite while the population grows, almost everyone receives nothing and the limit is uninformative. For standardized repeated goods, however, the high-multiplicity regime is natural and the underlying MNW question remains recognizably theirs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s anchor gate: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 3.2 is an axiomatic characterization. Its conclusion that a rule “maximizes Nash welfare” is not a complexity classification, algorithm, approximation guarantee, or parameterized result. The claims that binary-valued MNW is polynomial-time computable, and that general additive MNW is hard, are background citations rather than results proved here. Thus the proposed continuous optimization problem is not a mirror of a computational contribution of the paper; it is a new problem motivated by the paper’s rule.

The literal high-multiplicity limit also collapses. With a fixed set \(G\) of \(m\) indivisible goods and \(n\to\infty\), at most \(m\) agents can receive positive utility. Hence the positive-utility mass is at most \(m/n\to0\), and the normalized logarithm of the Nash product also tends to \(0\). The population disappears from the objective.

The proponent’s strongest repair is to introduce per-capita supplies \(q_g\) and infinitely many standardized copies. That is a sensible high-multiplicity fair-division model, and I would not reject it merely because supplies were scaled. But it no longer mirrors the theorem’s axiomatic content in a canonical way. In an atomless population, one individual’s report changes neither \(\mu\) nor the aggregate allocation, so strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Requiring strategyproofness on every rational finite replica avoids vacuity only by returning to an infinite family of discrete mechanisms. The continuum LP does not encode or determine those \(O(1)\)-agent incentive constraints: two replica mechanisms can have aggregate allocations differing by \(O(1/N)\), hence the same continuum limit, while one satisfies strategyproofness and the other does not. Replacing unilateral deviations by positive-mass cohort deviations would instead turn the theorem into a group-strategyproofness statement, which is a different axiom.

EF1 has the same problem. The paper’s EF1 is ex post and pairwise, concerning concrete integral bundles. The proposed variable \(x_{t,S}\) records only how much mass of each type receives each bundle. One must additionally choose whether EF1 is required for every bundle in the support, for expected utilities, or after some measurable decomposition into individuals. These are materially different notions; expected or mass EF1 is not the paper’s EF1. IDU is likewise not a direct type-level property once a positive-mass type is split across several bundles: “the agent did not receive \(g\)” has no single aggregate counterpart.

The pricing argument does show that a configuration-LP problem based on repeated binary-valued goods may be a legitimate Class A research problem. The scan over bundle cardinalities is plausible. But that tractability would be a new theorem about a newly specified high-multiplicity allocation model, not a continuous computational consequence of Theorem 3.2. The paper supplies neither the computational anchor nor the canonical continuum versions of the axioms needed to connect the two.

So the honest negative verdict is strong under ChoCo’s eligibility standard: no worthwhile *mirror of this paper’s computational results* exists, because the paper has no such result to mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim about fair division itself. A carefully developed repeated-supply MNW model could be worthwhile independently; it simply should not be credited as a continuization of this paper’s contribution.
