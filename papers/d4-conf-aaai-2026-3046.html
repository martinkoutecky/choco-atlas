The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result in the usual sense: no theorem classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or similar. The strongest positive case is a thin algorithmic reading of Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper. It says that, under the paper’s graph assumptions, the belief system converges to a unique stable consensus, independent of initial beliefs; every agent’s limiting belief is \(x^*=\theta+\varepsilon(1-\theta)\), strictly above \(\theta\) when \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(\theta<1\). The theorem is an analytic result, not a stated complexity bound. Treating its explicit formula as an algorithmic anchor is an inference.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large online community divided into cohorts that share the same prior, information-channel bias, and pattern of exposure to other cohorts. There may be millions of members but relatively few such types. A type is a cohort’s complete learning and interaction profile; its mass is its fraction of the community. A type-to-type influence matrix describes how each cohort weights information from the others. This scenario makes the network itself a type-level object, which is a substantial co-modification of the paper’s individual-agent graph.

I would call the mirror Continuous Distrust Equilibrium Computation. An instance gives a finite support of types with rational masses \(\mu_i\), a common truth parameter \(\theta\), a channel bias \(\varepsilon\), a rational type-level mixing matrix satisfying the paper’s connectivity assumptions, and initial type-level beliefs. The task is to return the limiting belief profile \(x^*\) for the population dynamics, represented by one belief per type. The output profile is the decision variable; the source has no welfare or intervention objective to preserve. The question concerns the underlying equilibrium computation, not an encoding.

For the homogeneous channel and learning assumptions in Theorem 4.2, the claimed answer is \(x_i^*=\theta+\varepsilon(1-\theta)\) for every type \(i\). Thus the mirror is tractable: its answer has a closed form and can be computed in time polynomial in the type support and input bit length. That tractability is an inference from the theorem, not a complexity result proved by the paper. It covers the convergence and consensus claim, not the paper’s experiments or its broader claims about platform interventions. It also yields natural follow-up questions: whether convergence and computation persist with type-dependent channel biases, and how much intervention is needed to bring the mass of agents above a pessimism threshold below a specified level.

The weakest point is that this is a computational wrapper around an analytic theorem. The paper studies a finite-agent graph, and the continuous version depends on assuming that a small number of cohorts and their mixing matrix adequately represent that graph. More seriously, the proof describes a time-varying operator whose contraction coefficient approaches \(1\); the fixed-point theorem for a single contraction does not by itself establish the claimed convergence. So this is a plausible population mirror of the paper’s central question, but a continuous computational-social-choice result would need a sound convergence argument and a more substantive computational question than evaluating the displayed formula.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.2 is a claim about the long-run behavior of a belief process, not a complexity result about an algorithmic problem. Recasting it as “return the limiting belief profile” adds an input/output wrapper, but the paper itself asks neither how to compute an optimum nor how the problem’s complexity changes with its parameters. Its other results are likewise about belief dynamics, with simulations for support; none supplies a computational task in the sense ChoCo is meant to map.

The strongest population scenario is a large platform divided into cohorts with identical priors, information channels, and interaction patterns. That could be a sensible continuous population model, and a type-level mixing matrix could preserve cohort interactions. But preserving the paper’s unrestricted network is the catch: agents’ interaction patterns are part of their state. On a generic graph, an exact type partition may have \(\tau=N\); a compact cohort model requires the extra assumption that the network admits a suitable quotient. If that assumption holds, the resulting task is to analyze a finite-dimensional consensus system on \(\tau\) types. That may be worthwhile network dynamics research, but it does not expose a computational social choice problem.

A stronger proposal might optimize platform interventions—for example, allocating fact-checkers or changing channel biases to limit the mass of pessimistic cohorts. The paper’s conclusion gestures toward interventions, but it specifies no action set, costs, constraints, or formal policy objective. Supplying those ingredients could create a substantive computational problem, yet it would be a new control problem built around the paper’s topic, not a mirror of one of its results. If the type space were made genuinely continuous instead, the natural question would become analysis of an operator on a continuum of agents, again outside the programme’s computational focus.

The proponent’s case is weakest where it treats equilibrium computation as an inference from Theorem 4.2. Even setting aside whether the proof fully establishes its time-varying convergence claim, the theorem’s stated contribution is convergence and consensus, not a computational classification. So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s scope: no result here gives the programme a computational question to continuize. It is not airtight in the broader sense. A cohort-based continuous model is plausible, and authors could recognize its equilibrium analysis as related work; the objection is that this would be a mirror of analytic social dynamics, not a worthwhile continuous computational-social-choice mirror of this paper.
