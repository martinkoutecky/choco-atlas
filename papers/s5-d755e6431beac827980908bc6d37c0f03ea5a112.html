The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying anchor: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Section 4.4 describes learning utility weights through linear-program feasibility, and Section 5 reports oracle experiments, but neither is a named computational result. So there is no honest anchor to restate, and no anchor-backed continuous question to hand to a prover.

The strongest positive direction I can see is an unanchored mirror of the paper’s “which” level. Imagine a regional response fleet with many ambulance agents but only a few standardized configurations: the number of agents \(N\) is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of types. A type specifies the ambulance’s equipment, capacity, information access, criteria, and shared fleet policy. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the fleet of that type. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, though the paper’s experiments with two ambulances do not establish it.

Call the problem Continuous Fleet Preference Fit. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\), a finite set of criteria oriented so lower is better, and, for each type \(t\), decision episodes \(j\). Each episode lists candidate actions \(A_{tj}\), rational criterion vectors \(q_{tja}\), and the action \(a^*_{tj}\) chosen by an expert. The task is to return fleet weights \(w\) in the probability simplex and nonnegative slacks \(\xi_{tja}\) minimizing weighted disagreement:

\[ \sum_t \mu_t \sum_j p_{tj}\sum_{a\ne a^*_{tj}}\xi_{tja} +\lambda\lVert w-w^0\rVert_1, \qquad \xi_{tja}\ge w\cdot(q_{t,a^*_{tj}}-q_{tja}), \]

where \(p_{tj}\) are rational episode weights, \(w^0\) is an initial policy, and \(\lambda\ge0\) controls how much to preserve it. The slacks measure how much the learned policy fails to rank the expert’s choice ahead of each alternative. A solution is an optimal rational \(w\) and corresponding slacks. This is an LP, so I would expect this formulation to be tractable (Class A). It covers the paper’s additive utility learner in Sections 4.3–4.5, including the contradiction problem raised in Section 4.5; it does not cover the decision tree, simulation, or GIS components. Further questions include exact zero-disagreement feasibility and how to update the fit as new interventions arrive.

This is only a candidate mirror, not a result the paper proves. The soft spot is substantial: the paper learns a policy for an ambulance from a particular expert’s sequential interventions. The fleet-wide weighted fit adds a shared policy and an aggregate loss, neither of which is established as the authors’ problem. It is a reasonable population version of their preference-learning objective, but the absent named anchor and the change from individual learning to fleet-level fitting prevent a stronger claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary in the paper establishing the complexity of a computational problem. Section 4.4 describes a linear program for updating one ambulance’s utility weights; Section 4.5 identifies inconsistent expert choices as an unresolved learning issue; Section 5 reports experiments. Those are not computational results with a stated complexity landscape. On the requested anchor-based test, there is nothing here to mirror.

The proposed Continuous Fleet Preference Fit is the strongest candidate, but it changes the object being learned. The paper keeps a separate preference model for each ambulance and updates it from that ambulance’s sequence of expert interventions. In the exact-fit formulation, assigning masses to repeated constraints would not change feasibility. To make masses matter, the proposal pools interventions into a fleet-wide disagreement objective. That makes the number of ambulances determine how much each preference counts—a sensible choice for some new training objective, but not one the paper poses. In particular, the paper treats disagreements as evidence about an individual expert’s changing, possibly inconsistent preferences, not as votes to aggregate across a fleet.

The best setting for the proposal would be a large fleet trained to follow a shared policy using many comparable expert-guided episodes. That is plausible, but it still leaves a fork. If the policy and complete decision context are shared, population mass adds little to the paper’s exact feasibility problem. If contexts, information, preferences, or histories differ, treating them as one type loses details the learning task uses; keeping them distinct can make the support grow with the fleet. One could instead pool their data under a common policy, but that is the new fleet-level objective doing the work. And if the episodes are repeated simulations rather than ambulance agents, their distribution is a training-data model, not a continuous society of agents.

So the negative case is strong against calling this a continuous mirror of a result in this paper: there is no qualifying result, and the proposed LP changes individual preference learning into population-level preference aggregation. It is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A shared-policy, high-multiplicity training problem could be reasonable; the paper simply does not establish it as its own computational problem.
