Why no mirror

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the main unanimity allocation theorem and its three-member corollary; it leaves the simple-majority comparison, dismissal and reallocation remarks, and experience discussion aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the extreme-asymmetry characterization extend exactly to these rational type and committee masses?
  • What exact scaling and rounding relationship connects the continuous optimum to finite allocations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result that explicitly classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its strongest relevant result is the unnumbered “Theorem”, proved in the paper. It is an optimization characterization, not a printed complexity claim: for fixed committee sizes \(N_1,\ldots,N_Z\), with independent experts of abilities \(p_i\in(1/2,1)\), the allocation maximizing average probability of a correct decision under unanimity is optimal if and only if it is extremely asymmetric. In that arrangement, experts are ordered by ability and assigned from highest to lowest to committees from smallest to largest. The authors say this means one need only rank the experts and allocate them accordingly. That gives a sound algorithmic anchor, though I would not describe it as a theorem stating “in P.”

A plausible mirror is Continuous High-Multiplicity Unanimous Committee Allocation. Fix a constant maximum committee size \(q_{\max}\). An instance gives a finite set \(T\) of expert types, each with a rational ability \(p_t\in(1/2,1)\) and rational population mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). It also gives committee sizes \(q\in Q\subseteq\{3,\ldots,q_{\max}\}\) and rational committee masses \(\kappa_q\ge 0\), with \(\sum_q q\kappa_q=1\). Type means the full relevant description here: experts share an ability and have the same independent-signal behavior. Mass is the fraction of experts—or, for committees, the normalized number of committees.

For each size \(q\) and multiset \(a\) of \(q\) types, let \(x_{q,a}\) be the mass of committees with that composition. Require
\[ \sum_{a:\,|a|=q}x_{q,a}=\kappa_q \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{q,a}a_t x_{q,a}=\mu_t\ \text{for every }t. \]
A committee of composition \(a\) is correct with probability
\[ F(a)=\frac12\left(\prod_{t\in T}p_t^{a_t} +1-\prod_{t\in T}(1-p_t)^{a_t}\right). \]
The problem is to find a feasible allocation \(x\) maximizing
\[ \frac{\sum_{q,a}x_{q,a}F(a)}{\sum_q\kappa_q}. \]
A solution is the optimal value and an allocation attaining it. This keeps the paper’s unanimous decision rule, independence assumption, and average-correctness objective; it makes the population and committee counts continuous.

The regime is a large institution assigning a substantial roster to many recurring, finite-size review panels—for example, a national agency running many independent adjudication panels. Experts fall into a small number of skill or experience bands, while the number of experts and panels is large. Thus \(\tau=|T|\) can stay modest as the population grows. It is the repeated supply of experts and panels that makes mass a natural assignment variable; the committees themselves remain finite.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. For rational inputs, the masses can be scaled to integer counts and viewed as a sufficiently large finite allocation instance. The paper’s theorem then supplies the rank-and-allocate structure; in the mirror, one sorts the skill types and fills the smallest committees from the highest-ability mass onward. Since \(q_{\max}\) is fixed, the composition variables also have polynomially bounded count in \(\tau\). The continuous formulation makes the high-multiplicity allocation explicit, but the paper’s result already points strongly toward its solution.

The weakest point is the scale choice: this continuizes the supply of experts across many finite committees, rather than making the membership of each unanimous committee itself a continuum. That is deliberate. If committee size also grew without bound, then for \(p_t<1\) the all-correct acceptance probability would shrink toward zero, and the paper’s accuracy objective would change character. A natural follow-up is to prove an explicit rounding or convergence bound between the continuous optimum and finite high-multiplicity instances. Another is to ask how much of the rank-order structure survives when skill levels are correlated or proposals have different values.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed LP may add little computational content: the paper’s theorem already says to rank experts by ability and assign them in order to committees from smallest to largest. But that is an objection about how much remains to prove, not whether the continuous question is worth asking. Under the programme’s stated standard, a mirror with a known or simple answer still counts. I cannot use this to defeat the anchor.

Nor is this a paper with no computational result to mirror. Its named theorem characterizes the exact optimizer of a finite allocation problem. It does not state a running time, but the paper itself says the allocation needs only the experts’ ranking. Calling the result purely axiomatic would miss what it establishes.

The proposed finite-type model is coherent with the paper. An expert’s ability \(p_t\) determines the behavior relevant to the objective; for a committee composition \(a\), the correctness probability is \(F(a)\). The variables \(x_{q,a}\) preserve each committee’s integral size and composition while allowing the *number of committees* and the supply of expert types to scale. With fixed \(q_{\max}\), the number of type multisets is polynomial in \(\tau\). The paper’s restriction that at most \(N-2\) experts share an ability does not rule out high multiplicity: it permits skill groups whose sizes grow linearly with \(N\). A multi-band population can therefore fit the paper’s regime.

The other obvious objections also fail. Committee size stays finite, so unanimity does not degenerate as it could if committee size grew without bound. The objective depends on committee accuracy, not on tracking named experts. And an atomless distribution of abilities offers an alternative mirror if one wants to avoid large groups of exactly equal abilities, though that would require specifying how the distribution is represented computationally.

So the honest negative case is weak. The mirror may yield less new complexity theory than the proponent suggests, but the supplied rules do not let that concern defeat a well-posed optimization question. This anchor survives; I cannot make the requested universal case against it.
