The continuous mirror question

Given \(\theta_L < B < \theta_H\), a discount factor \(\delta\), and a finite rationally encoded distribution \(P\) over issue states \(x \in [0,1]\), where each state has society \(\mu_x=(x,1-x)\) over low-cost and high-cost jurisdictions, choose a quota \(q \in [0,1]\). If \(x \ge q\), all jurisdictions are instructed to participate, and any refusal makes the collective action ineffective. Maximize \(U(q)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[\mathbf{1}[x\ge q](x(B-\theta_L)+(1-x)(B-\theta_H))]\), subject to \(\theta_H-B \le \frac{\delta}{1-\delta}U(q)\) whenever the quota passes an issue with \(x<1\); quotas that pass only at \(x=1\) are the unanimity outcome.

The model it lives in

A federation whose jurisdictions are indistinguishable within two cost types, represented on each issue by mass distribution \(\mu_x\). Future issue compositions \(x\) are drawn from finite rational support \(P\). The decision variable is the fraction quota \(q\); the objective is expected per-jurisdiction utility, with the paper's individual compliance incentive retained through an explicit veto rule.

The objection that survived

A single jurisdiction's veto remains payoff relevant despite its zero mass; the case does not establish that this unanimity primitive would be accepted in every atomless federation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4's optimal self-enforcing quota. It leaves aside the comparative statics and extensions in Propositions 5–9, including correlation, transfers, and changing membership.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite-support \(P\), does the two-rule structure of Proposition 4 always hold, with the first-best threshold \(q^*=(\theta_H-B)/(\theta_H-\theta_L)\) when enforceable and unanimity otherwise?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no result in this paper that qualifies as a computational-complexity anchor. Its numbered propositions characterize equilibria, welfare, and optimal voting rules; none classifies a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. So I can’t honestly provide a continuous problem *per qualifying anchor*.

The closest result is Proposition 4, proved in the paper. It says that for some critical discount factor \(\bar\delta\), the optimal self-enforcing voting rule is the first-best quota \(q^*=\left\lceil \frac{\theta_H-B}{\theta_H-\theta_L}N\right\rceil\) when \(\delta\ge\bar\delta\), and unanimity \(q=N\) when \(\delta<\bar\delta\). That is a structural characterization, not a complexity result. It is the strongest lead for a mirror, but it does not pass your anchor requirement.

If I were allowed to offer a research lead anyway, it would be Continuous Self-Enforcing Quota Design. Consider a large federation of jurisdictions with two issue-specific cost types, \(\theta_L<B<\theta_H\). On an issue, let \(x\) be the mass of low-cost jurisdictions; the society is \(\mu_x=(x,1-x)\). Future issues draw \(x\) from a finite rationally encoded distribution \(P\), preserving the paper’s uncertainty and correlation across members. Choose a quota \(q\in[0,1]\): if \(x\ge q\), all jurisdictions are instructed to participate. The objective is to maximize per-jurisdiction expected utility
\[ U(q)=\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\!\left[\mathbf 1[x\ge q]\bigl(x(B-\theta_L)+(1-x)(B-\theta_H)\bigr)\right]. \]
For any quota that passes an issue with \(x<1\), a high-cost jurisdiction must comply; retain the paper’s assumption that one refusal makes the collective action ineffective. Require \(\theta_H-B\le \frac{\delta}{1-\delta}U(q)\) for that compliance. Quotas that only pass when \(x=1\) are the unanimity outcome and need no such constraint. The requested output would be an optimal quota and its value.

This preserves the paper’s institutional-design question: choose a voting quota that maximizes shared expected utility subject to self-enforcement. I would expect this finite-support version to be tractable by sorting the support shares, checking the resulting quota intervals, and testing the compliance condition. The analogue of Proposition 4 suggests a choice between the first-best threshold \(q^*=(\theta_H-B)/(\theta_H-\theta_L)\) and unanimity, though that would need to be proved for the specified input model. A natural follow-up is whether transfers or changing membership alter that structure.

The weakest point is the continuum limit itself. The paper’s enforcement force depends on a single member’s defection making the action fail. In an ordinary atomless model, one member has zero mass, so that pivotality disappears if outcomes depend only on aggregate mass. The proposed mirror must preserve the paper’s “any refusal” implementation condition even for an individual of zero mass. That is mathematically expressible, but it is a substantial modeling choice an author or referee might reject. And even if they accept it, this remains a mirror of Proposition 4’s economic characterization, not an anchored continuation of a computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. The proponent says as much: Proposition 4 is a characterization of equilibrium voting rules, not a theorem about complexity or algorithms. The paper never poses a computational problem whose high-multiplicity version could be compared with the original. That is a decisive objection to treating this as an anchored ChoCo case.

The proposed quota problem does not fill that gap. With a finite rational support for \(P\), the objective and self-enforcement condition change only when \(q\) crosses a support value. An algorithm can check the resulting \(O(r)\) intervals, where \(r\) is the support size. That is a newly formulated optimization problem, not a continuous counterpart of a computational result in the paper. Its simplicity is not itself a reason to reject it; the issue is that the paper gives no discrete computational problem or complexity claim for it to continue.

There is also a flaw in the proposed formalization: a single member’s refusal does not change an atomless society’s distribution \(\mu_x\). So “any refusal makes the action ineffective” cannot be represented by the mass input alone. A better model could make benefits excludable and let nonparticipants opt out while the action proceeds for participants. The paper’s discussion of impure collective action makes that a plausible repair, and a large federation of similar jurisdictions could make the high-multiplicity regime sensible. But that would be a new model of the paper’s economic question, not a mirror of a computational result.

So the case against is strong for no computationally anchored mirror of this paper. It is not strong enough to establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous model could be built: the repaired excludable version is a credible research lead. I would not claim more than the evidence supports.
