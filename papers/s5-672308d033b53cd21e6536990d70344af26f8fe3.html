The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu\in\Delta^{\tau}\), additive cost vectors \(c_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{q}\), rational per-capita chore supplies \(r\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{q}\), and a fixed workload bound \(k\), let \(\mathcal{B}_k=\{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{q}:\|b\|_1\le k\}\). Compute masses \(\lambda_{t,b}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_b\lambda_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}\lambda_{t,b}b_j=r_j\), such that every supported integral bundle satisfies support-wise \(\mathrm{EF1}\), and no same-worker measurable reassignment of integral bundles with the same supplies weakly lowers every worker’s cost while strictly lowering the cost of positive mass.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity configuration model in which complete additive cost-vector types carry masses \(\mu_t\), chore classes have per-capita supplies \(r_j\), and \(\lambda_{t,b}\) assigns type mass to integral configurations; the task is to find any \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and \(\mathrm{PO}\) assignment.

The objection that survived

The proposed mirror is not a transfer of the paper’s polynomial-time guarantee: constant \(\tau\) types do not imply a constant number of named agents, and the paper’s forest-based perturbation argument may fail with repeated type rows.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the computational question corresponding to Theorem 1.3 in a constant-type, high-multiplicity regime. It leaves the paper’s pure existence results, weighted extension, and its particular finite-agent proof techniques without a direct transfer.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(\tau\) and \(k\), is an \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and \(\mathrm{PO}\) assignment computable in time polynomial in \(q\) and the input encoding length?
  • Can the support-wise \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and same-worker \(\mathrm{PO}\) conditions be handled by a configuration LP or separation oracle?
  • Does a type-level perturbation recover the paper’s non-degeneracy method, or does the continuous problem exhibit continuum-specific hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s recurring-workload interpretation, not a divisible-chore market.

Let \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) be worker types. A type \(t\) is the complete additive cost vector \(c_t=(c_{tj})_{j\in Q}\) over chore classes \(Q=\{1,\ldots,q\}\); entitlement, if relevant, is also part of the type. The society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta^\tau\). Chore supplies scale with the population: \(r_j\) is the number of copies of chore class \(j\) per unit population mass. A worker still receives an integral bundle. Formally, for a finite bundle alphabet \(\mathcal B\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q\), let \(\lambda_{t,b}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) workers receiving bundle \(b\). Feasibility is

\[ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\lambda_{t,b}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\lambda_{t,b}b_j=r_j \]

for every \(t\) and \(j\). Thus \(\lambda\) is fractional only at the type level: every individual worker receives an integral bundle \(b\).

For \(b\neq 0\), write \(h_t(b)=c_t\cdot b-\max\{c_{tj}:b_j>0\}\), and put \(h_t(0)=0\). The exact continuous EF1 condition is that \(h_t(b)\le c_t\cdot b'\) for every pair of supported bundles \(b\in\operatorname{supp}(\lambda_t)\) and \(b'\in\operatorname{supp}(\lambda_u)\), for all types \(t,u\). This says that every worker can eliminate envy toward every other worker by removing at most one chore from their own integral bundle.

PO is defined against another feasible measurable assignment of integral bundles, coupled to the same workers, that weakly lowers every worker’s cost and strictly lowers the cost of a positive mass of workers. fPO allows the competing assignment to give workers fractional bundles. This preserves exactly the paper’s distinction: actual allocations remain indivisible, while fractional allocations are used only as efficiency comparisons.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1.2, proved in this paper rather than cited elsewhere. It states that, with additive cost functions, an EF1 and fPO allocation of indivisible chores always exists. The corresponding continuous problem is:

Given rational type masses \(\mu\), additive cost vectors \(c_t\), per-capita chore supplies \(r\), and a finite integral bundle alphabet \(\mathcal B\), find a feasible mass assignment \(\lambda\) satisfying the support-wise EF1 condition above and such that no feasible fractional assignment Pareto dominates it.

This is recognisably the same question. The agents have merely been grouped by identical cost vectors, while chore copies and population mass are scaled together. A finite instance with \(K\mu_t\) workers of type \(t\) and \(Kr_j\) copies of chore \(j\) is the rational high-multiplicity realization. Conversely, a rational \(\lambda\) can be implemented exactly by taking \(K\) large enough. The continuum is therefore not an unrelated market model: it is the limit representation of repeated finite chore-allocation instances.

I expect this mirror to remain feasible, and to be Class A whenever the bundle alphabet is explicitly represented or has a controlled configuration oracle. The natural formulation is a configuration LP over \(\lambda_{t,b}\), with the nontrivial part being the support-selection version of EF1. If the bundle alphabet is implicit and chore classes are unrestricted, that pricing/support problem may itself become continuum-specific hard; but that would be a meaningful boundary of the mirror, not evidence that the mirror is ill-posed.

The second useful anchor is Theorem 1.3, also proved here. It states that, with additive costs and a constant number of agents, an EF1 and PO allocation can be computed in polynomial time. The theorem’s proof assumes positive integer costs and uses the paper’s perturbation argument together with the cited rich-family result, Theorem 5.1; The theorem itself is the paper’s result.

Its continuous counterpart is the following computational problem. Fix a constant number \(\tau\) of worker types and a fixed per-worker workload bound, for example bundles of exactly \(k\ge2\) chore copies, so that \(\mathcal B=\{b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q:\sum_j b_j=k\}\). Given \(\mu\), \(r\), and the additive type costs \(c_t\), compute a feasible \(\lambda\) that is EF1 and PO. The output is the mass of each type receiving each integral configuration, not a fractional chore allocation to an individual.

I would expect this fixed-\(\tau\), fixed-workload version to be tractable: \(|\mathcal B|=O(q^k)\), so the configuration space is polynomial for fixed \(k\), and the paper’s constant-agent enumeration suggests replacing the number of named agents by the number of genuinely distinct types. This is a direct computational question about the high-multiplicity regime, not merely an existence restatement.

The weakest point is precisely that last transfer. The paper’s algorithm relies on a non-degenerate agent–chore graph being a forest. If many agents are collapsed into one type, identical cost rows create degeneracies and the forest argument does not carry over verbatim. A new type-level perturbation or configuration argument would be needed. I would therefore present Theorem 1.2 as the robust mirror and Theorem 1.3 as the promising but technically open algorithmic extension.

The supplied paper contains no named NP-hardness theorem of its own for the target fair-allocation problem, so there is no honest hardness anchor to prefer. The cited NP-hardness of Nash social welfare concerns a different objective and should not be used as one. Theorem 1.4 would give a weighted version of the same mirror by adding \(\alpha_t\) to the type description, but I would not count it separately: it is a natural extension, not an independent anchor.

This case therefore covers the paper’s EF1\(+\)fPO existence result and its constant-agent EF1\(+\)PO algorithmic result. It does not claim that the paper has already studied continuous populations. Its divisible-allocation LP is machinery for fPO inside a finite-agent model; the proposed mirror makes the population itself continuous while retaining indivisible bundles for every individual.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proponent has confused a legitimate high-multiplicity reformulation with a new computational consequence of this paper.

Theorem 1.2 is not a computational theorem. In the proposed finite-bundle model, for rational \(\mu\), \(r\), and \(\lambda\), choose \(K\) clearing all denominators. The instance with \(K\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Kr_j\) copies of chore class \(j\) is an ordinary finite instance. The paper’s Theorem 1.2 supplies an EF1 and fPO allocation, which aggregates directly to the proposed \(\lambda\). Thus the continuous existence statement is essentially inherited from the discrete theorem; it does not expose a new continuous phenomenon. Asking to compute \(\lambda\) would be worthwhile perhaps, but that is a new algorithmic problem not established by Theorem 1.2.

Theorem 1.3 transfers even less directly. Its parameter is the number \(n\) of named agents, not the number of cost types. The proof depends on an \(n\)-dimensional weight simplex, agent-specific perturbations, and a non-degenerate agent–chore graph that is a forest. With \(K\) repeated workers, \(n=K\), so the polynomial bound becomes useless as \(K\) grows. Collapsing them to \(\tau\) types creates duplicate cost rows and destroys the non-degeneracy/forest argument. A type-level perturbation changes the instance, while an agent-level perturbation is incompatible with an atomless population of identical types.

The proposed rescue—constant \(\tau\), fixed bundle size \(k\), and \(|\mathcal B|=O(q^k)\)—is a new restricted configuration problem. The polynomial size of \(\mathcal B\) does not make it tractable: EF1 is a support-selection condition, a disjunction over which configurations receive positive mass, and PO is another global dominance constraint. The paper provides no algorithm for that problem. If \(k\) is not fixed, the configuration space becomes exponential; if it is fixed, the result is no longer a close mirror of the paper’s arbitrary-bundle theorem.

There is also a modelling fork. Keeping the paper’s finite chore set while making the population continuous makes per-capita supply vanish, leaving almost everyone with the empty bundle and making EF1 largely vacuous. Scaling chore supply avoids that degeneration, but it replaces the paper’s allocation of a finite set of chores by a recurring-workload or scheduling model with repeated chore classes. That model may be sensible, but its substantive content comes from the new workload construction, not from the paper’s results.

This is a real negative case against the proposed anchors: Theorem 1.2 yields an inherited existence corollary, while Theorem 1.3’s algorithm does not survive the passage from agents to types. But the universal conclusion is not honestly secure. The recurring-workload model is coherent, preserves integral bundles for individuals, and poses a genuine configuration-level computational problem. So the strongest defensible verdict is not “no continuous mirror exists,” but “the paper does not itself provide a compelling computational mirror; any worthwhile one would be a new high-multiplicity fair-scheduling project rather than a continuization of its proved algorithm.”
