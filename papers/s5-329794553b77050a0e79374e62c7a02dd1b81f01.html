What the mirror covers

The proposed allocation-only extension covers the Talmud awards formula in Proposition 1 and the outcome named in Theorems 1 and 2, but leaves their equilibrium existence, uniqueness, and strategic implementation claims uncovered.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive setting is a mass-claims bankruptcy: many holders of standardized claims share a small number \(\tau\) of claim sizes, with \(n\gg\tau\). A type is a claim size \(c_t\), and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of claimants. The estate can be expressed per claimant as \(e\). This makes the creditor population—not the already divisible estate—the continuous object. It is a plausible regime for mass claims, though the paper itself does not develop it.

The paper’s named results do not provide a qualifying computational anchor. Proposition 1, proved here, gives a two-creditor formula for Talmud awards. Theorem 1, proved here, establishes that for every finite bankruptcy problem the game \(G(c,E)\) has a Nash equilibrium with outcome \(T(c,E)\). Theorem 2, also proved here, says \(T(c,E)\) is the unique Nash-equilibrium outcome. These are formula, existence, and uniqueness results; the paper does not state a complexity classification or analyze an algorithm. So, under the requested anchor rule, there is no continuous problem I can honestly attach to a named computational result.

A plausible *research direction*, but not a compliant anchor, is Finite-Support Continuum Talmud Allocation. Given claim types \(t\in T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), claims \(c_t\), and a per-claimant estate \(e\leq\sum_t\mu_t c_t\), compute the award \(a_t\) to each claimant of type \(t\). For \(e\leq\frac12\sum_t\mu_t c_t\), set \(a_t=\min\{c_t/2,\lambda\}\), choosing \(\lambda\geq0\) so \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=e\). Otherwise set \(a_t=c_t/2+\max\{c_t/2-\lambda,0\}\), again choosing \(\lambda\geq0\) to meet that equation. This looks tractable by sorting the type-level breakpoints, but it computes the allocation rule; it does not mirror the paper’s strategic implementation result.

That distinction is the weak point. In \(G\), individual creditors announce vectors and permutations, one becomes coordinator, and two negotiate. A type-level mass distribution does not directly preserve those individual strategic roles or deviations. The paper supplies no continuous game or computational result to bridge that gap. The allocation endpoint has a plausible population mirror; the strategic justification that the authors actually prove does not yet have a convincing continuous computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 is a formula for two creditors; Theorems 1 and 2 establish existence and uniqueness of a Nash-equilibrium outcome. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result. The proponent acknowledges this, so their three named results do not supply a surviving computational anchor.

The proposed mass-claims setting is sensible: with finitely many claim types, rational population shares, and the estate scaled per claimant, one can ask for each type’s award. That is a coherent continuous-population allocation question. But it extends the Talmud rule itself; it does not mirror the paper’s strategic justification. Treating the allocation as a computational problem would add an algorithmic question the paper never addresses.

A stronger attempt would continuize the game. That runs into a structural break. The game selects a coordinator from a finite set, lets that creditor choose a named negotiating partner, and relies on individual deviations in the proposal and permutation stages. In an atomless population, a single creditor has zero mass, so those bilateral actions cannot change aggregate awards in the way the proof requires. Making each claim type a representative player, or letting a positive-mass bloc negotiate, could preserve strategic moves—but then the actors and bargaining institution have changed. The paper gives no basis for choosing that new game or treating it as the limit of \(G\).

So the routes divide: preserve the mass-allocation model and leave behind the result the paper proves, or preserve strategic implementation by adding a new bargaining structure that the paper does not study. That is a strong case against a ChoCo computational mirror of this paper. It is not proof that no valuable continuous bankruptcy research exists; the proposed allocation extension could be worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is strongest when confined to the paper’s contribution and the programme’s computational remit.
