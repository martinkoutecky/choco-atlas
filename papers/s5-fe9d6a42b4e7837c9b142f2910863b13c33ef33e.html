The continuous mirror question

For a unit-mass population with finitely many ranking types and rational masses, whose rankings are privately held by its members, what is the minimum worst-case value of \(\int_0^1 b_\Pi(r,x)\,d\lambda(x)\) among deterministic protocols that determine the exact IRV winner with the paper’s tie-breaking rule? In particular, is this per-unit-mass communication cost \(\Theta((\log m)^2)\)?

The model it lives in

Use an atomless unit-mass population with ranking types in \(S_m\) and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\), hidden across the agents rather than supplied to the authority. The authority may broadcast free queries to measurable cohorts defined by their public transcript; members send finite binary replies, and cost is the worst-case integral of bits sent.

The objection that survived

The protocol model should specify whether cohorts are chosen by prior public replies or by agent address, since those choices preserve different amounts of the labeled-voter information.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 for IRV; it does not cover Theorem 2’s single-peaked restriction or Theorems 3 and 4 on IRV-Average and STV.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact per-unit-mass communication complexity equal \(\Theta((\log m)^2)\) in a specified cohort protocol model?
  • How does the optimum depend on support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\)?
  • Does single-peaked IRV have a matching \(\Theta(\log m)\) per-unit-mass bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is an IRV mass-elicitation communication problem. A type is a complete ranking of the \(m\) candidates. A unit mass of agents is represented by a measurable map \(r:[0,1]\to S_m\), whose induced society is \(\mu_t=\lambda(\{x:r(x)=t\})\). Require \(\mu\) to have finite support and binary-rational masses. Each agent knows their own ranking; the authority does not know \(\mu\) and must determine the IRV winner, using the paper’s fixed tie-breaking rule.

A protocol may send queries for free, as in the paper, and receive finite binary replies from measurable cohorts of agents. Let \(b_\Pi(r,x)\) be the number of bits sent by agent \(x\). The continuous problem is to find a deterministic protocol that returns the exact IRV winner for every such population, minimizing worst-case per-unit communication:

\[ \mathrm{CC}_{\mathrm{mass}}(m) =\inf_\Pi\sup_r\int_0^1 b_\Pi(r,x)\,d\lambda(x). \]

The instance is the distributed population: its mass vector is held across the agents rather than supplied to the authority. The protocol’s solution is an exact winner-determining protocol, and its cost is total voter-to-authority bits per unit mass. This is a continuous-population elicitation problem, not the trivial task of computing IRV after \(\mu\) has already been given to the authority.

The anchor is Theorem 1, which states that IRV’s communication complexity is \(\Theta(n(\log m)^2)\). The paper proves the new lower bound using a fooling set; the matching \(O(n(\log m)^2)\) upper bound comes from the Progressive Preference Revelation protocol, credited to Conitzer and Sandholm (2005). Its direct mass analogue has initial cost \(\log m\) per unit mass to reveal top choices. When \(j\) candidates remain, the eliminated candidate has at most \(1/j\) of the mass, so querying those agents’ next choices costs at most \((\log m)/j\). Summing gives an \(O((\log m)^2)\) protocol. I would ask a prover whether \(\mathrm{CC}_{\mathrm{mass}}(m)=\Theta((\log m)^2)\). The paper’s per-voter lower bound and its regime \(n=\ell m!\), with arbitrarily many copies of each ranking, make the same lower bound plausible in the high-multiplicity limit. In the programme’s A/B/C language, I expect the discrete lower bound to carry over, though this is a communication-cost analogue of Class B, not an NP-hardness claim.

The regime is large-scale ranked-choice elections or online consultations where many agents hold the same ballot and the number of agents greatly exceeds the number of distinct rankings used. The paper’s lower-bound construction has \(\tau=m!\), so it does not establish a lower bound for small-support populations. Useful follow-up questions are whether the optimum improves with \(\tau\) or mass bit length \(L\), and whether the single-peaked result in Theorem 2 has a corresponding \(\Theta(\log m)\) per-unit communication bound.

The weak point is central: Theorem 1’s fooling set varies which *labeled voter* holds each ranking while keeping the ranking counts fixed. The aggregate \(\mu\) forgets those rearrangements. Thus the lower bound does not transfer automatically. This mirror keeps addressable agents as part of the distributed elicitation model, as they are in the paper; if ChoCo requires the authority to receive only \(\mu\) as input, the communication question disappears. I think the authors would recognize the distributed version as their problem’s continuous-population analogue, but it is a narrower, communication-focused fit than a mass-transfer or optimization mirror. It covers Theorem 1 alone; I would not stretch it to Theorems 2–4.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only anchor, and it is a real communication-complexity result. The strongest objection is that its input is not the continuous society \(\mu\). Every profile in the paper’s fooling set has the same count of each representative ranking, so all those profiles induce exactly the same \(\mu\). The lower bound comes from the fact that a protocol must learn which *named voter* holds which ranking: swapping one voter between two profiles can change the winner. It is a lower bound about discovering an assignment of ballots to people, not about computing on the aggregate society.

That leaves a fork for the proposed mirror. If the authority is given \(\mu\), it can run IRV on the weighted rankings without asking voters anything. If \(\mu\) is hidden and the authority can query individuals or cohorts, the input must include the identity-indexed assignment \(r(x)\), plus rules for addressing agents and aggregating their replies. Those details are not encoded by a finite-support distribution over ranking types. Treating each address as part of an agent’s type retains the distinction the high-multiplicity model was meant to collapse; omitting it loses the information the protocol uses. In particular, the paper’s one-voter hybrids have mass \(1/n\), while a single point in an atomless population has mass zero. Its fooling-set argument therefore gives no positive per-unit lower bound in the continuous model.

The mass-elicitation proposal could still be a worthwhile *new distributed-computation model*. But it needs an explicit account of how the authority selects cohorts and learns their replies. If exact aggregate responses are available at no additional cost, they act as an idealized aggregation oracle; if replies remain individually addressable, the model retains the identity structure absent from ChoCo’s \(\mu\)-input. On the programme’s stated input model, neither version is a direct continuous mirror of Theorem 1.

I would therefore argue against this anchor as a ChoCo mirror, while keeping the negative case qualified. The paper’s focus on voter burden makes a distributed mass-elicitation question plausible, and its authors might recognize that as their problem. I cannot honestly rule out that repaired version; the objection is that it changes the input and communication model, not that the resulting question would be uninteresting.
