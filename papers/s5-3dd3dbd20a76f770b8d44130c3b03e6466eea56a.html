The continuous mirror question

Given finite item kinds \(G\), finite complete binary valuation types \(T\) with value-oracle access, rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(0\le \rho_g\le1\), compute a finite-support histogram \(x_{t,A}\ge0\) over \(t\in T\) and integral bundles \(A\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,A:g\in A}x_{t,A}=\rho_g\), and \(v_t(A)\ge v_t(B\setminus\{g\})\) whenever \(x_{t,A}x_{t',B}>0\), for all \(t,t'\) and \(g\in B\); alternatively minimize unallocated item mass and decide whether the optimum is zero.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity course or seat allocation: type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\), each agent receives an integral subset of recurring item kinds \(G\), each kind has per-capita supply \(\rho_g\), and \(x_{t,A}\) records the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(A\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a Class-A extension anchored by the paper’s main result.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, not merely cited: for every finite instance with \(n\) agents, \(m\) indivisible items, and normalized monotone binary valuations—each marginal value is \(0\) or \(1\), without assuming additivity or submodularity—there exists a complete EFX allocation, and such an allocation can be computed in polynomial time. “Complete” means every item is allocated; EFX means \(v_i(A_i)\ge v_i(A_j\setminus\{g\})\) for every pair \(i,j\) and every \(g\in A_j\).

I would not use the propositions or lemmas as separate anchors: they are structural ingredients of the proof. Theorem 3.1 is the paper’s genuine computational result.

My lead mirror would be Continuous-Binary-EFX\(_\infty\). An instance has a finite set \(G=\{1,\ldots,m\}\) of item kinds, a finite set \(T\) of complete binary valuation types, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\) for the item kinds. Each type \(t\) has a valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) satisfying \(v_t(\varnothing)=0\), monotonicity, and \(v_t(A\cup\{g\})-v_t(A)\in\{0,1\}\).

The per-capita supply is the necessary high-multiplicity modification. For example, \(G\) may be course types and \(\rho_g N\) may be the number of seats in course \(g\) for a cohort of \(N\) students. Every individual still receives an integral bundle \(A\subseteq G\); only the population distribution over bundles is continuous.

The decision variable is \(x_{t,A}\ge0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(A\). It must satisfy \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,A:g\in A}x_{t,A}=\rho_g\) for every item kind \(g\). The EFX requirement is imposed support-wise: whenever \(x_{t,A}>0\) and \(x_{t',B}>0\), every agent of type \(t\) must satisfy \(v_t(A)\ge v_t(B\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in B\).

The problem is to output such an \(x\), or equivalently to minimize total unallocated item mass and decide whether the optimum is zero. A solution is a finite support list of triples \((t,A,x_{t,A})\). The interesting computational question is whether one can find it in time polynomial in \(m\), \(|T|\), and the rational input length, without expanding the denominator-cleared electorate. If repeated copies of one item kind should be allowed in a bundle, the same formulation uses integral count-vector bundles and removes one copy in the EFX test.

This is a credible scenario rather than an artificial cloning exercise. Consider a large university or employer with thousands of students or employees but a relatively small number of recurring valuation profiles: students in the same programme and cohort may have exactly the same binary valuation over course bundles, while course seats are repeated resources. The relevant type includes the entire valuation function, so this does not erase idiosyncratic preferences that matter to the problem; it groups only genuinely indistinguishable agents. A regime with \(N\) large and \(|T|\ll N\) is precisely the high-multiplicity regime.

The finite-to-continuous dictionary is exact for rational data. Clearing denominators produces \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(N\rho_g\) copies of item kind \(g\). Aggregating any finite EFX allocation gives \(x\); conversely, a rational \(x\) can be expanded into a finite allocation. Thus this is not cake cutting and not fractional ownership: \(x\) is a population histogram of integral bundles. The paper’s discussion of infinitely divisible resources is therefore background, not a novelty collision.

I would expect this mirror to belong to Class A, but as an open computational extension rather than as an immediate corollary of Theorem 3.1. The paper’s proof is unusually promising for aggregation: envy, pre-envy, source agents, cycle elimination, and safe bundles depend on valuation comparisons and binary one-item changes, not on the names of individual agents. That suggests replacing the \(n\)-vertex envy graph by a graph of positive-mass type–bundle states and replacing individual reassignment by mass transfers and cycle rotations.

The key unresolved issue is compression. Clearing denominators can create an exponentially large finite electorate, and the paper’s welfare-increase argument does not automatically give a polynomial number of aggregate updates when transferred masses may be very small. The support-wise EFX condition is also not simply a linear constraint until the bundle support is known. A successful proof would need a polynomial-size support certificate, a compact aggregate version of \(U_0/U_1\), or a configuration/column-generation method. If that fails for arbitrary succinct non-submodular valuations, the problem could become continuum-specifically hard; that is a meaningful boundary question rather than a defect in the mirror.

The weakest point is the repeated-item modification. With one fixed finite set of items and an atomless population, almost everyone receives the empty bundle, so the original indivisible-goods problem becomes degenerate. Scaling item supply with population is therefore necessary, but it changes the resource ontology from unique items to recurring item kinds. I would present this explicitly as an author-recognizable high-multiplicity extension, supported by the paper’s own school-choice, course-allocation, and computational-resource applications—not as a literal restatement of its finite instances.

Further questions include whether the continuous EFX optimum is always zero for arbitrary rational \(\mu\) and \(\rho\), whether a polynomial-size aggregate version of the paper’s algorithm exists, and whether arbitrary succinct binary valuations create a genuine Class-C barrier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper’s theorem is not naturally a theorem about a continuous population at all. Theorem 3.1 is a genuine computational anchor, but its objects are a finite set of named indivisible items and individual bundles.

If the item set \(M\) remains fixed while the population becomes atomless, every item can be assigned to at most one agent. The recipients therefore form a null set, and almost every agent receives \(\varnothing\). Under an almost-everywhere interpretation, EFX becomes trivial; under an everywhere interpretation, the allocation is not even a meaningful measurable population allocation. Thus the literal population limit destroys the problem.

The proposed repair—per-capita supplies of recurring item kinds—is sensible in some applications, but it is not merely continuization. It requires a new valuation model. With one copy of each kind per bundle, agents are subject to a capacity constraint absent from the paper. With repeated copies allowed, the original set function \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) must be replaced by a valuation on multisets or count vectors. There is no canonical extension: copies might be substitutes, complements, or independent goods, and these choices affect both binary marginality and EFX. Clearing denominators therefore does not automatically give an equivalent finite instance of the paper’s problem.

There is also a more fundamental difficulty with EFX itself. In the proposed support-wise formulation, the mass \(\mu_t\) has no role in the fairness inequalities. A type with arbitrarily small positive mass constrains every other positive-mass bundle just as much as a type comprising half the population. If one instead uses an almost-everywhere formulation, those constraints disappear when the type becomes null. Hence the continuous model has a sharp support discontinuity: either it collapses to a finite support-feasibility problem, or it ceases to preserve the paper’s agent-by-agent fairness guarantee. This is not merely a difficulty proving the theorem; it is a poor population-level semantics for EFX.

The computational anchor is also underspecified. The paper allows completely arbitrary binary set functions but gives no representation model for them. A truth table, value oracle, and succinct circuit description produce different complexity questions. The proposed configuration formulation inherits this ambiguity, while its EFX constraints are disjunctive support constraints rather than ordinary linear constraints. Consequently, the claimed “continuous LP” is not yet a well-defined computational problem without introducing substantial new modelling choices.

A repeated-course or repeated-seat interpretation is nevertheless plausible. It could yield a worthwhile high-multiplicity study, especially concerning compressed representations of allocations. But that would be a new capacity-constrained fair-allocation problem with a chosen valuation-on-kinds model, not a robust continuous mirror of the theorem as stated. Thus the negative case defeats the literal mirror and exposes serious non-canonicity in the repaired one, but it is not airtight: the course-allocation scenario remains a credible author-recognizable mirror, so claiming that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario would overstate the evidence.
