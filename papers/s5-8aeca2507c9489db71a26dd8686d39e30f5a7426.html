The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited: this paper has no named computational complexity result of the required kind. It states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The closest structural result is Observation 3, established in this paper. It says that an alternative is admissible under each of three Difference Principle specifications—basic maximin, pessimistic ex ante welfare, and ex post maximin—if and only if it is maximin-admissible for every individual in the original-position transformation. That is a useful anchor for a model, but it is not a computational anchor under your criteria.

The most credible continuous question built from it is Continuous Difference-Rule Choice under Ignorance. An instance gives finite alternatives \(A\), states \(S\), and a finite support \(T\) of types. A type is a complete welfare profile \(w_t(a,s)\) across every alternative and state; the society is a distribution with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). The decision is which alternative to choose. Return all alternatives maximizing
\[ \min_{t:\mu_t>0,\ s\in S} w_t(a,s). \]
This keeps the paper’s ignorance model—there are no state probabilities—and applies its least-advantaged criterion to the population distribution.

A plausible regime is a large population choosing among tax or institutional policies under several possible economic states. Citizens with the same welfare consequences under every policy and state share a type; perhaps millions of citizens fall into a few dozen or hundred such profiles. That is high multiplicity in the relevant sense. The paper’s authors could plausibly recognize this as their Difference Principle choice problem with citizens grouped by complete welfare profile. Since the criterion cares about the worst-off, it depends on which types have positive mass, not their precise shares. That is faithful to maximin, though it limits what the continuum contributes.

The mirror is tractable by direct enumeration: evaluate the minimum for each alternative and return the maximizers, in \(O(|A||S|\tau)\) comparisons, with polynomial bit complexity for rational welfare inputs. It covers the Difference Principle results summarized by Observation 3. For a finite electorate grouped by profile, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives the same recommendation. The continuous formulation therefore supplies a clean high-multiplicity representation, but the algorithmic conclusion is an extrapolation, not a result proved in the paper; the computation is already simple.

This case does not cover the paper’s four Lexical Difference Principle rules or its result that three cannot be grounded in an original-position argument. Extending those rules would raise a further question: can one define a principled continuum version that handles welfare distributions across types and states while agreeing with the finite-population rule? The paper supplies no answer to that modeling or computational question.

My weakest point is that the candidate question is a straightforward formalization of one of the paper’s choice rules, not a mirror of a named computational result. It also uses the mass values only to identify present types. So the strongest honest positive claim is that the paper admits a sensible continuous-population formulation of its maximin Difference Principle. It does not support the stronger, requested case for a computational mirror anchored in the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Observation 3 is the strongest anchor, but it is a structural equivalence, not a computational result. It relates three formulations of the Difference Principle to maximin choice in the original-position transformation. The paper asks which social choice rules are justified; it does not formulate a computational problem or analyze an algorithm.

The proponent’s proposed society is nevertheless a sensible population model. A type can be a citizen’s full welfare profile across alternatives and states, with its mass recording the fraction of citizens with that profile. That preserves the Difference Principle. But in this rule, mass matters only through whether a type is present: any positive mass counts equally toward the worst-off. This gives a faithful continuous-population formulation, but it does not supply a computational question from the paper. Asking a computer to return the maximizing alternative adds an algorithmic task; it does not mirror a computational result the authors established.

A stronger lexical version could retain type masses and compare the resulting welfare distributions. But the paper’s finite leximin rules do not specify how to order continuous distributions, and the choice among quantile-based or essential-infimum formulations would add a modeling decision. Even granting a natural extension, it would continue the paper’s normative analysis, not turn its axiomatic results into complexity results. The non-groundability result has the same limitation: it is about which individual choice rules can justify certain social rules, not about computation.

One could instead ask how much population change would reverse a recommendation. That would need a model of allowed changes and their costs. Here, types encode citizens’ welfare consequences under every policy and state; changing a type would alter that counterfactual profile. The paper defines no such intervention. Adding one might be a worthwhile new project, but it would not be a mirror of this paper’s results.

So the strongest negative case is against a computational mirror, not against continuous population modeling altogether. The honest weakness is that a continuous population interpretation of the Difference Principle is quite plausible, and its authors might welcome it. But the paper offers no computational result for that interpretation to mirror.
