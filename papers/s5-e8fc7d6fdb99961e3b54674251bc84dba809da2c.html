The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\) schools, a finite set \(T\) of complete normalized utility types \(v^t\in[0,1]^M\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\bar q_j\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j\bar q_j\ge 1\), compute an assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t(v^t\cdot x_t)\), where each type receives a lottery \(x_t\in\Delta^M\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le\bar q_j\) for every school \(j\) and \(v^t\cdot x_t\ge v^t\cdot x_s\) for every pair \(t,s\in T\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type population with mass \(\mu_t\) for each normalized utility vector \(v^t\), per-capita school capacities \(\bar q_j\), and one lottery \(x_t\) per type; maximize per-capita utilitarian welfare subject to capacity feasibility and exact envy-freeness between types.

What the mirror covers

Covers the linear-welfare envy-free optimization of Theorem 3(i) over a finite set of utility types; leaves the incentive results, pseudomarket computation, ordinal benchmark, and empirical preference estimation aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the finite-type formulation approximate continuous type distributions while controlling welfare and envy?
  • What finite-to-continuum identity holds when type masses or per-capita capacities cannot be matched exactly by integer replicas?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a single tractability mirror: EF-Welfare\(_\infty\), the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s welfare-maximizing envy-free assignment. The paper has no numbered NP-hardness result to use as an anchor, so this is a case for a meaningful continuous computational problem, not for discrete hardness disappearing.

The anchor is Theorem 3(i), “Convex-Program Structure,” proved here and formalized in Lean 4. It says that the envy-free mechanism’s feasible assignments form a convex polytope because its probability, capacity, and envy constraints are linear in the assignment. With a concave welfare objective, the mechanism is a convex program and every local maximizer is global; with strictly concave welfare, the maximizing expected-utility vector is unique. The paper computes this mechanism, and under linear welfare it is an LP. My mirror uses that linear-welfare case, where the complexity claim is precise.

A plausible regime is a large school district with many students but a relatively small set of recurring, complete cardinal-preference profiles over a fixed set of schools—for example, thousands of students whose normalized school-utility vectors come from a few dozen shared profiles. A type is one such vector \(v^t\), including every characteristic the mechanism uses; in this paper’s mechanism, that is the student’s normalized utilities for the schools. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the district. School capacities scale with population: \(\bar q_j\) is the number of seats per student at school \(j\). This is a population continuization: the lottery assignment is already part of the paper’s model, while the capacities become per-capita quantities.

Precisely, an instance consists of \(M\) schools, \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one and rational utility vectors \(v^t\in[0,1]^M\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\bar q_j\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j\bar q_j\ge1\). Choose a school lottery \(x_t\in\Delta^M\) for each type, maximizing per-capita utilitarian welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t(v^t\cdot x_t)\), subject to capacity feasibility \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le\bar q_j\) for each school and envy-freeness \(v^t\cdot x_t\ge v^t\cdot x_s\) for every pair of types \(t,s\). The output is an optimal lottery for each type and the resulting welfare. Each unit of mass receives one school; \(x_t\) describes the type’s lottery, or equivalently the proportions of that type assigned to each school.

This is an LP with \(\tau M\) variables and \(O(\tau^2+M+\tau)\) constraints, so for rational input it is solvable in polynomial time in \(M,\tau\), and the input bit length. That is the mirror question about the underlying assignment problem; the LP is its formulation. For finite populations whose type counts and capacities scale to these proportions, averaging assignments within each type gives the type lotteries above, and repeating those lotteries for each member gives a finite assignment with the same per-capita welfare and envy constraints. Thus the finite and continuous optima coincide on those replicated instances. The paper’s assignment model also permits feasible stochastic assignments to be implemented as lotteries over deterministic assignments.

The authors have good reason to recognize this as their problem: it keeps their cardinal preferences over assignment lotteries, utilitarian welfare, capacity constraints, and exact no-envy condition, while replacing individual counts with type masses. It also matches their large-market framing. The mirror covers Theorem 3(i) and the computation of the envy-free mechanism, not the paper’s incentive theorems, pseudomarket equilibrium computation, ordinal benchmark, or empirical preference estimation. It is a Class A question. Since the finite assignment problem is itself an LP in this setting, the gain is principally a type-compressed formulation and a complexity description in \(\tau\), not a claim that population continuization makes a hard problem easy.

My weakest point is the regime: exact equality of complete cardinal-utility vectors may be a demanding assumption. The paper’s Seattle preference draws do not establish that a real district has only a few such types; with continuously varying utilities, the support could be large or effectively unbounded, and this finite-support LP would not by itself settle the continuous-support case. The case survives because finite type distributions are a sensible large-market regime for this very mechanism, but it should not be sold as a description of the Seattle data.

The mirror also suggests concrete next questions: how the type-compressed formulation extends to \(\varepsilon\)-envy-freeness; whether continuous type distributions admit finite-dimensional approximations with welfare and envy guarantees; and how the exact finite-to-continuum identity changes when type counts or capacities cannot be matched exactly by integer replicas.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that its useful compression depends on repeated *exact* cardinal utility vectors. Seattle’s preference draws are student-specific points in continuous identified sets, so the paper gives no evidence that a district has only a few dozen recurring types. If most students have distinct normalized utility vectors, then \(\tau\) is close to \(N\): rewriting the assignment in masses saves little, and the envy constraints still grow quadratically in the number of types. The more ambitious version, with a continuous distribution of utility vectors, is an infinite-dimensional optimization problem. To make its computational complexity precise, one would first need to specify how that distribution and the allocation rule are encoded and how envy constraints are represented or approximated. The paper’s finite LP does not settle those choices.

That objection narrows the mirror’s likely relevance, but it does not defeat the better version. A district using a finite menu of cardinal reports, or a market with recurring preference profiles, is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for this mechanism. The paper itself allows finite type distributions in its large-market incentive analysis. For that regime, Theorem 3(i) and the paper’s LP formulation give a direct, exact mirror: use one lottery \(x_t\) per type and impose
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_{tj}\le \bar q_j, \qquad v^t\cdot x_t\ge v^t\cdot x_s \quad\text{for every }t,s. \]
This has \(\tau M\) assignment variables and polynomially many constraints. It preserves the paper’s cardinal preferences, welfare objective, capacity limits, and exact envy-freeness while making population mass the input.

So “no named computational result” is not a defensible objection here: the paper names the convex-program structure and computes the mechanism. Nor does its continuous-population incentive analysis already answer the computational question. The negative case can fairly say the finite-type mirror is mechanically obtained and its empirical regime is unsubstantiated. It cannot honestly establish that no scenario makes the mirror worthwhile. Theorem 3(i) remains a viable anchor, so my case against the universal claim is weak.
