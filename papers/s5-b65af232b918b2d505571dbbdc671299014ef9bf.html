The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes when payoff types are strategically distinguishable; Theorems 2 and 3 and Corollary 1 characterize when robust virtual implementation is possible. They are substantive results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The paper gives no input-size model or algorithmic complexity analysis. So there is no named computational result here on which to base the required continuous problem and predict its complexity.

The most plausible adjacent direction would use a large market with a finite set of complete payoff types: \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of agents of type \(\theta\), and the planner seeks a mechanism that robustly implements an allocation rule on these type distributions. That could be a natural question in its own right, but it is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper’s agents are a fixed finite set of strategic roles, and its example with \(\theta_i\in[0,1]\) makes each agent’s *type space* continuous; it does not make the population continuous. Its lotteries make outcomes continuous, which is also not the programme’s target.

The positive case is therefore weak: the paper studies strategic behavior under uncertainty about others’ types, a setting that could motivate a high-multiplicity extension. But producing a credible population model would require substantial new work, and the paper supplies no computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a ChoCo mirror is strongest at the anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes strategic distinguishability; Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 characterize robust virtual implementability; Theorem 3 gives a construction. None formulates a computational problem or states a complexity result. One could ask how hard it is to compute pairwise inseparability or check robust measurability from a finite utility table, but that would be a new computational question inspired by the characterization—not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed large-market framing does not supply the missing anchor, and it changes the object being studied. Here, \(\theta_i\) is a possible payoff type for a particular strategic agent, while beliefs over other agents’ types and all higher-order beliefs are unrestricted. A population distribution \(\mu\) over types would instead describe prevalence or impose a common aggregate state. That replaces the paper’s robustness question with a different one. The paper’s example with \(\theta_i\in[0,1]\) makes an individual’s type space continuous; its lottery space makes outcomes continuous. Neither makes the population continuous.

Replicating agents is the strongest attempted rescue: create many copies of each role and ask about the limit. But aggregation then needs extra structure. If the outcome depends only on the type masses, a single agent’s report has no effect in the continuum, so the original individual incentive and type-distinguishability questions no longer carry over directly. If the mechanism retains individual consequences so those questions still matter, the model needs scalable allocations and feasibility constraints absent from the paper’s fixed outcome space and finite set of strategic roles. That could be a worthwhile continuum mechanism-design project, but it is a substantial new model rather than a mirror of these results.

The negative case is not an impossibility claim: large-market versions of robust mechanism design may be sensible. Its weakness is that no universal argument rules out every such formulation. But the proponent’s proposed scenario does not clear the programme’s gate: it offers no continuous version of a named computational result, and the better population-level formulation changes the paper’s central strategic and informational object.
