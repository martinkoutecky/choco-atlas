The case FOR (proponent)

My best positive case is a type-mass fair kidney-exchange problem, with Proposition 2 as the closest named anchor. There is an important qualification: the paper has no numbered theorem establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or another complexity class. Propositions 1–3 prove asymptotic optimization and price-of-fairness claims instead. So if “anchor” strictly requires a complexity result, the paper has no qualifying anchor; the case below is the strongest computational mirror those named results support.

Proposition 2, proved in this paper, bounds the asymptotic price of its new fairness constraint. In the Section 4 random-graph model, the price is almost surely at most
\[ \max_{b_1,b_2,r}\max\left\{ \frac{\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,1}\mu_{r,0}-\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,0}\mu_{r,1}} {(\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,1}+\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,0})\mu_{r,0}}, \frac{\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,0}\mu_{r,1}-\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,1}\mu_{r,0}} {(\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,1}+\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,0})\mu_{r,1}} \right\} \]
as \(N\to\infty\). Here \(\mu_{b_1,b_2,r,a}\) is the population share of pairs with donor blood type \(b_1\), patient blood type \(b_2\), sensitization level \(r\), and protected-group value \(a\); \(\mu_{r,a}\) aggregates those shares over blood types. The bound is zero when the blood-type distributions are balanced across protected groups within each sensitization level. This is a named result about the value of a fair allocation, not a runtime bound.

The corresponding continuous question is: given a finite set of complete pair types \(t=(b_d,b_p,r,a)\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a compatibility kernel between types, a fixed cycle or recourse limit, and a utility for each feasible type-labelled exchange pattern, find the maximum-utility mass allocation subject to the paper’s conditional fairness constraints. For variables \(x_q\geq 0\) giving mass assigned to exchange pattern \(q\), let \(a_{tq}\) count how many places that pattern uses of type \(t\), and let \(y_t=\sum_q a_{tq}x_q\). The constraints are
\[ y_t\leq\mu_t,\qquad \left| \frac{\sum_{b_d,b_p} y_{(b_d,b_p,r,0)}}{\mu_{r,0}} - \frac{\sum_{b_d,b_p} y_{(b_d,b_p,r,1)}}{\mu_{r,1}} \right|\leq l_r \]
for each sensitization level \(r\). Maximize \(\sum_q u_qx_q\); return the optimal exchange masses and their value, alongside the unconstrained optimum so the price of fairness can be computed. This asks the same allocation question as the paper, expressed in masses of patient-pair types. It does not replace population continuity with fractional kidneys: each infinitesimal unit of mass is still assigned to an integral exchange pattern.

A plausible regime is a large, multi-centre exchange pool repeatedly drawing from a stable population, with \(N\) far larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported blood-type, sensitization, and protected-group cells. A type must also capture every modeled feature that affects compatibility and utility. Compatibility can be represented by a type-pair kernel; the dense independent-edge assumptions in the paper are what make it plausible that a type-level allocation describes the large-pool optimum. Proposition 1, also proved here, supports that bridge: for any \(\epsilon_N\downarrow0\), it shows that the optimum of the unconstrained packing problem and the optimum with the paper’s subgroup-priority constraints differ by \(o(N)\) almost surely. That proposition is likewise an asymptotic value statement, not a complexity theorem.

For fixed cycle length at most three and an explicit list of supported types, the proposed mass problem is an LP over type-labelled cycle patterns, with polynomially many patterns in \(\tau\); the fairness constraints are linear. I would therefore expect this mirror to be Class A under those assumptions. That is a prediction about the proposed continuous problem, not a complexity result proved by the paper. The mirror covers the paper’s fair utility-maximizing allocation and its asymptotic price analysis; it does not cover the selection-probability prediction procedure.

The soft spot is the compatibility network. Real HLA compatibility can be sparse, heterogeneous, and specific to individual pairs. If capturing it faithfully makes almost every pair a separate type, then \(\tau\) grows with \(N\) and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. The positive case is strongest in the dense, stable-type regime the paper’s asymptotic analysis uses; it should not be sold as a mirror for every operational KPD pool.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither anchor is a computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to mirror. Proposition 2 bounds the asymptotic price of fairness; Proposition 1 bounds an asymptotic loss in packing value. Neither states a complexity classification or a runtime guarantee. The paper does propose a column-generation procedure, but it gives no complexity result for that procedure. So a type-mass optimization problem may be a good new question, but it is not the continuous counterpart of a computational result these propositions establish.

Proposition 1 is the closest anchor, and it cuts both ways. Its supplementary proof constructs a coarse-type LP and shows that, under the paper’s random-graph assumptions, the finite graph optimum approaches that LP’s value. That is already a discrete-to-continuous identity, which is a meaningful result. It does not, however, chart the complexity of a continuous fair-allocation problem. Adding conditional fairness constraints to the type-mass LP would be a natural extension, but it would ask a new question rather than mirror Proposition 1. Proposition 2’s price bound likewise concerns the value of fairness, not the computational difficulty of finding a fair allocation.

The proposed LP also faces a real modeling tradeoff. With a small type list, a cycle-length limit of three, and compatibility captured by type-level rules, the mass problem is coherent and compact. But that abstraction is stronger than the paper’s real compatibility data: preserving detailed HLA compatibility and other features may require many types, potentially close to one per pair. The paper’s dense random-edge model supports the small-type formulation, so this does not rule out a plausible high-multiplicity regime; it limits how far that case extends to operational KPD.

I therefore cannot honestly defeat the best version of the mirror on the claim that no sensible continuous population model exists. A type-mass KPD problem is plausible, and the paper’s asymptotic analysis helps motivate it. The negative case is narrower: under ChoCo’s stated focus on computational questions, these anchors do not establish a worthwhile computational mirror. Whether the broader type-mass problem merits study is not settled by that objection.
