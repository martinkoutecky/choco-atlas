The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible population mirror here, but no result in the paper qualifies as a computational-complexity anchor: it states no result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest results are Proposition 4 and Proposition 5, both proved in the paper. Proposition 4 says that any pairwise-stable assignment via swapping in the joint-venture model must use the assortative matching. Proposition 5 says that stable assignments exist; in the M-optimal one, the technology-side payoff vector is componentwise minimal and has \(s_j^*=\sum_{j'=j}^{n-1}(X_{j'+1,j'}-X_{j'+1,j'+1})\) for \(j<n\), with \(s_n^*=0\), while the marketing-side payoffs are \(r_j^*=X_{jj}-s_j^*\). These are structural and existence results, not complexity classifications.

The strongest positive case is a new high-multiplicity question about that assignment problem. Imagine a large market with many marketing and technology businesses, grouped into a fixed number \(n\) of complete ability-and-cost types. Each type has mass \(1/n\); the number of businesses grows while the type support stays fixed. A mass \(x_{ij}\) of type-\(i\) marketers is matched with type-\(j\) technologists, so each business still gets one partner. The matching is represented by a flow, not by individually naming every business.

One precise candidate is Mass-Swap Stable Joint-Venture Assignment. Take rational costs \(c_{ij}\) generated by the paper’s ability-based cost function, with its complementarity conditions, and market parameters \(\delta>0\) and \(\bar\alpha\). Scale the demand intercept with market size. For a unit-mass matching flow \(x\), let \(C(x)=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}c_{ij}\). The paper’s joint-venture profit formula then has the large-market limit
\[ v_{ij}(x)=\left(\frac{\bar\alpha}{\delta}+\frac{C(x)-c_{ij}}{2}\right)^2, \]
assuming parameters make the profit expression positive throughout the feasible range. Given the type masses and parameters, the problem is to return a stable flow \(x\) and type-level transfer schedules \(r_i,s_j\), or report that none exists; when one exists, return an M-optimal solution whose \(s\)-vector is componentwise least among stable solutions. Feasibility requires the type payoffs to sum to \(v_{ij}(x)\) on matched type pairs. Stability tests whether any pair of types can profitably form a pair through an infinitesimal swap, evaluated at the aggregate state \(x\). This is a computational question about the population and its matching, not about making outcomes fractional.

That regime is plausible for a large market with repeated cohorts of firms sharing the same technology, marketing capacity, and relevant costs. The type count can be small while each type has many members. It also uses the paper’s own object—the joint-venture assignment with aggregate externalities—and Proposition 5 gives a concrete target: existence and computation of a one-side-optimal stable assignment. Proposition 4 suggests a route toward tractability through sorting. I would tentatively expect a tractable result under the paper’s strict complementarity assumptions: for a guessed aggregate cost, the matching resembles a finite assignment problem, with self-consistency in the single aggregate statistic \(C(x)\) left to resolve. That is a research conjecture, not a result proved by the paper.

The mirror covers the oligopolistic joint-venture assignment results, especially Propositions 4 and 5. It does not cover the pairs-contest results, intrinsic preferences, heterogeneous match qualities, or farsighted dynamics.

The weak point is substantial: as the market grows, an individual pair’s swap changes the aggregate cost by \(O(1/N)\), so its effect on other firms’ profits disappears in the limit. The proposed stability notion preserves aggregate externalities in the market state, but an individual deviation becomes price-taking. Requiring a positive mass of businesses to move together would retain a first-order externality effect, but would change pairwise stability into a coalition deviation. The demand scaling is also a co-modification needed for a nondegenerate limit. Thus this is a plausible continuous research question that the authors might recognize, but it is not an anchored complexity result under the stated test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 4 restricts which matching can support stability; Proposition 5 proves existence and characterizes the M-optimal payoffs. Neither asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of computing a stable assignment. The proposed task—return a stable flow and transfers—is a new computational problem attached to those results. That could be worthwhile, but its connection to the paper’s results is weaker than the proponent’s framing suggests.

The large-market joint-venture setting is plausible: repeated cohorts of firms with the same abilities and costs are a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The difficulty is preserving what makes Propositions 4 and 5 distinctive. In the finite model, a swap changes the aggregate cost of production and thereby changes every firm’s profit. With \(N\) firms, swapping two pairs changes the average cost by \(O(1/N)\). In the atomless limit, an individual swap leaves the aggregate state exactly unchanged. The proposed stability test therefore evaluates a new venture at fixed \(C(x)\): it is a price-taking assignment problem with an aggregate consistency condition. That is coherent, but it does not retain the paper’s defining interaction between a pair’s deviation and the externality it creates.

There are two natural ways to try to retain that interaction. Let a positive mass of firms swap, and the externality changes, but the deviation is then a coalition deviation whose size is part of the model. Let the deviating mass shrink to zero, and its effect on per-firm payoffs vanishes. Either choice moves away from the paper’s pairwise stability notion. Proposition 4’s swapping argument and Proposition 5’s output-matrix characterization consequently lack a direct continuous counterpart in the proposed model.

The fixed-state version may still merit study. With finite type support it has a finite assignment flow and a scalar consistency condition \(C=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}c_{ij}\); its computational structure may be interesting. But that is the best version of the anchor, and it is a new nonatomic equilibrium problem rather than a complexity question already posed by the paper. The negative case is strongest on that distinction. It remains weaker as a universal claim: large cohorts are credible here, and the new problem might be worth studying even without a close mirror of either proposition.
