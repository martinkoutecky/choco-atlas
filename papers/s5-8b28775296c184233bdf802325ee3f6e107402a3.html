The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. The nearest candidates are both proved here, but neither asserts a complexity classification or algorithmic result. Proposition 1 says that, under assumptions A1 and A2, the optimal coalition is a minimal majority exactly when \(v_{(n+3)/2}\le -W_B(n-1)/4\), and is universal exactly when \(v_n>-2W_B/(n+1)\). Corollary 2 says that, under the paper’s maintained assumptions, if every voter’s preference intensity in \(v\) is at least their intensity in \(v'\), then \(k^*(v,W_B)\ge k^*(v',W_B)\). These are structural and comparative-statics results, not results that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. So neither can honestly serve as a required anchor.

The strongest substantive mirror I can see, though, is the optimization underlying those results. Consider a large membership referendum in which two factions compete to buy votes. A type is a member’s monetary preference intensity \(v_j\) for \(x\) over \(y\); the society is given by rational masses \(\mu_j\) on \(\tau\) such types, with \(\tau\) small relative to the membership. This is high multiplicity in the paper’s sense: members of one type are interchangeable for the model. Normalize B’s budget to \(w_B\) per member, and assume A can afford a winning schedule.

Here is a continuous problem that could be handed to a prover. Given rational \(v_1\ge\cdots\ge v_\tau\), masses \(\mu_j\) summing to one, and \(w_B>0\), assume at most half the society has \(v_j\ge0\) and \(\max_j v_j<2w_B\), paralleling the paper’s assumptions that ensure a flooded coalition and nonnegative leveling bribes. Choose \(z_j\in[0,\mu_j]\), the mass of type \(j\) that A bribes, and a common transfer \(t\) such that, writing \(q=\sum_jz_j\),

\[ \min \sum_{j=1}^{\tau} z_j(t-v_j) \quad\text{subject to}\quad q>\tfrac12,\qquad t(q-\tfrac12)\ge w_B. \]

The solution is the chosen masses and transfer, hence A’s minimum per-capita expenditure and winning schedule: members in the chosen masses receive \(t-v_j\), while the rest receive zero. With a level schedule, B must recruit more than \(q-\tfrac12\) additional votes from A’s bribed coalition; each costs \(t\), so the constraint prevents B from buying a majority within its budget. For fixed \(q\), A chooses the highest-\(v_j\) types first. The problem then reduces to minimizing the convex, piecewise-rational function \(q w_B/(q-\tfrac12)-H(q)\), where \(H(q)\) is the total preference intensity of the highest-\(v_j\) mass \(q\). I would expect this mirror to be tractable by sorting the support and solving a one-dimensional convex optimization problem.

The setting is plausible for the authors: it preserves the sequential competitive vote-buying problem, the monetary preference intensities, and the parties’ budgets, while replacing named voters by masses of interchangeable voters. Banks explicitly contrasts his finite-voter analysis with the continuum model of Groseclose and Snyder, so this population mirror is close to the paper’s own conceptual neighborhood. That prior continuum work supports the plausibility of the regime, though it also means the broad continuum question is not new.

The mirror covers the paper’s optimal expenditure and coalition-size analysis, including the comparative-statics idea in Corollary 2; it does not preserve Proposition 1(a)’s exact finite-population condition for a *minimal* majority. In a continuum, there is no smallest mass strictly greater than one half. That loss of one-person granularity is the weakest point of the mirror, alongside the more fundamental limitation that the paper gives no named computational-complexity anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Banks gives no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 characterizes when the optimal finite coalition is a minimal majority or universal; Corollary 2 gives comparative statics. Neither states an algorithmic problem or a complexity result. The paper’s optimization over coalition size is real, but turning it into an input problem about rational masses and support size would be an analyst’s new research question, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed large referendum is nevertheless a credible high-multiplicity setting, and its mass optimization is a sensible model. The loss of a smallest majority in the continuum does not defeat it: coalition mass and the corresponding winning condition are natural quantities. Nor can I dismiss it just because it may have an easy algorithm. So the objection is one of fit to this programme’s stated target, not an underlying modelling flaw.

The paper also identifies its finite analysis as a counterpart to Groseclose and Snyder’s continuum model. That history makes the proposed mass problem less distinct as a model, but it is not by itself a valid collision: the existing analysis is not a computational-complexity classification, and outside answers do not make a well-posed question unworthy.

So I would reject the paper as an anchored computational mirror under the stated standard: none of the proponent’s named results qualifies, and the best rescued candidate is a newly formulated computational question. The negative case is weak on the broader claim, though.
