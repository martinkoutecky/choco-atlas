What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive scenario I can see is a large legislature or recurring voting body with many representatives but a small number \(\tau\) of interchangeable caucus types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of representatives of type \(t\), and let \(w_t\) encode their seat or vote weight. A decision could be a measurable partition into an active bloc and the other blocs, with plurality determining the winner; the quantity of interest would be each type’s power. That resembles the paper’s Ecuador example, which tracks power as legislative benches change.

But that scenario does not supply a qualifying anchor. Proposition 2.1, proved in Alonso-Meijide et al. (2017), says that a set of pairwise incomparable embedded coalitions determines a unique simple game whose minimal winning embedded coalitions are exactly that set. It is a structural result, not a computational claim. Definitions 3.4 and 3.5 introduce the new Colomer–Martínez and Holler–Colomer–Martínez indices, and the tables report their values for finite assemblies; neither is a numbered algorithmic or complexity result.

There are further modeling difficulties: the paper’s indices count minimal winning embedded coalitions, while a continuum of divisible representatives has no natural counting measure over such coalitions, and minimality itself may change under divisibility. Choosing a measure or redefining power would create a new index question rather than a clear mirror of a result in this paper.

So I cannot honestly name a lead anchor or give a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor*: the paper contains no numbered computational result of the required kind. A continuous power-index model may be an interesting extension, but it is not a result-anchored ChoCo mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The sole numbered result in the paper, Proposition 2.1, says that a finite set of pairwise incomparable embedded coalitions determines a unique simple game. It is an order-theoretic structural statement, not a complexity or algorithmic claim. Definitions 3.4 and 3.5 introduce indices; the Ecuador tables apply them to six political groups. Neither gives a computational result. The authors’ call for efficient computation is a direction for future work, not a result with a continuous counterpart. So Proposition 2.1 does not supply the required computational anchor, and the indices and tables do not supply one either.

The strongest attempted repair is a large legislature with many representatives in a small number of interchangeable types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But the paper’s players are parties or legislative benches, and its objects are partitions of those players. Replacing them with representatives changes who can form coalitions and how a bench may split. If representatives instead become divisible mass that can be divided among blocs, the set of embedded coalitions becomes a space of measurable subsets and partitions. The paper’s indices depend on counting minimal winning embedded coalitions; the population distribution does not provide a corresponding measure over that space. In a nonatomic plurality model, blocs can be refined into arbitrarily small pieces, so there is no finite analogue of the paper’s finest partition of the outsiders. The count-based formulas for DP, PG, CM, and HCM therefore do not carry over directly.

One could define a measure on coalition configurations or a new density-based notion of minimal winning coalition. But the choice would determine what the continuous power index means; it is not induced by the population masses or specified by the paper. Keeping types indivisible avoids that issue, but leaves a finite game over types rather than a continuum of coalition mass. Either route could motivate new research, but neither yields a clear continuous computational mirror of a result in this paper.

That is a strong negative case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror, not a proof that no worthwhile continuous power-index question could ever be formulated. Its main weakness is precisely that someone could propose a principled measure over coalition configurations and make a new computational project of it. The paper itself gives no basis for choosing such a measure or result to anchor that project.
