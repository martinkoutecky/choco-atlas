The continuous mirror question

Given projects \(A\) with costs \(c\), budget \(l\), and supported feasible approval sets \(S\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_S\) summing to \(1\), define \(r_\mu(X)=\sum_{S\supseteq X}\mu_S\). Set \(m_\mu(\emptyset)=0\), \(m_\mu(\{a\})=c(a)\), and, for \(|X|\ge 2\), set \(m_\mu(X)=\max\{(r_\mu(X)-\prod_{a\in X}r_\mu(\{a\}))c(X),\max_{a\in X}(-\sum_{Y\subsetneq X:\,a\in Y}m_\mu(Y))\}\), with \(u_{M,\mu}(X)=\sum_{Y\subseteq X}m_\mu(Y)\). Given a rational threshold \(Q\), determine whether there is a bundle \(B\subseteq A\) with \(c(B)\le l\) and \(\sum_S\mu_Su_{M,\mu}(S\cap B)\ge Q\).

The model it lives in

Approval sets \(S\) are voter types with rational masses \(\mu_S\); the bundle remains indivisible, with decisions \(x_a\in\{0,1\}\) and \(\sum_a c(a)x_a\le l\). Maximize \(\sum_S\mu_Su_{M,\mu}(S\cap\{a:x_a=1\})\), where \(u_{M,\mu}\) is computed from the mass-based co-occurrence rates.

Observed co-occurrence can reflect budget constraints or correlated preferences rather than causal synergy; this challenges the interpretation of the utility, but not the defined mass optimization problem.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the sum-welfare hardness of Proposition 7 and the minimum-welfare branch of Proposition 8. It leaves the paper’s utility-function and aggregation-rule axioms, the product branch, and the branch-and-bound algorithm and experiments outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Proposition 7’s hardness persist when the synergy utility is restricted to fixed \(k\)-additivity?
  • Which parameterizations by project count, support size, or budget admit useful algorithms for the mass-based sum objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s existing model. In a large municipality, many residents may submit the same knapsack ballot, while the number of distinct submitted ballots is much smaller than the population. A type is an exact approved project set \(S\); its mass \(\mu_S\) is the fraction of residents submitting that ballot. Project costs and the budget are shared, as in the paper. The funded bundle remains indivisible: only the population becomes continuous.

Given projects \(A\), costs \(c\), budget \(l\), and supported ballot types \(\mathcal T\), take rational masses \(\mu_S>0\) summing to \(1\), with \(c(S)\le l\). Define the mass-based occurrence rate \(r_\mu(X)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal T:X\subseteq S}\mu_S\). Apply the paper’s Möbius utility construction using these rates: \(m_\mu(\emptyset)=0\), \(m_\mu(\{a\})=c(a)\), and for larger \(X\), set \(m_\mu(X)\) to the maximum of \(\bigl(r_\mu(X)-\prod_{a\in X}r_\mu(\{a\})\bigr)c(X)\) and \(\max_{a\in X}\bigl(-\sum_{Y\subsetneq X:\,a\in Y}m_\mu(Y)\bigr)\). Then \(u_{M,\mu}(X)=\sum_{Y\subseteq X}m_\mu(Y)\). This is the paper’s empirical co-occurrence utility with fractions in place of counts, including its monotonicity correction.

My lead is Mass-Synergy Sum-PB. Given that instance and a rational threshold \(Q\), ask whether there is a bundle \(B\subseteq A\), \(c(B)\le l\), with \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal T}\mu_S u_{M,\mu}(S\cap B)\ge Q\). Equivalently, find a feasible bundle maximizing that population-average satisfaction.

The anchor is Proposition 7, proved in this paper. It shows that PB-Max-\(\sum-u\) is strongly NP-hard even with unit-cost projects, both for \(u_M\) and for utility functions where a pair selected together at least once has strictly greater utility than an equally often approved pair never selected together. The paper’s Clique reduction equalizes project approval counts, so the pair co-occurrence signal drives the choice. This is a direct fit: the mass version measures the same joint and marginal approval frequencies, and the objective is the expected utility of a randomly selected resident. For a finite profile with \(N_S\) voters of type \(S\), set \(\mu_S=N_S/N\). The rates, and thus the utilities, are unchanged; the continuous objective is exactly the paper’s finite sum divided by \(N\). The reduction’s combinatorics lie in the projects and their pair relationships, so I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. A polynomial algorithm in the number of projects, support size, and mass bit length would solve those discrete instances.

A second question comes from Proposition 8, also proved here. Its full result is that, for unit-cost projects and any utility with \(u(\emptyset)=0\) and \(u(X)>0\) for every nonempty \(X\), both PB-Max-\(\min-u\) and PB-Max-\(\prod-u\) are strongly NP-hard, and neither has a polynomial-time \(\delta\)-approximation for any \(\delta>1\), unless \(P=NP\). The paper reduces Set Cover: elements become voters, sets become projects, and a voter approves the projects for sets containing her element. With budget \(K\), positive minimum or product utility is achievable exactly when the selected projects cover every element. The proof is given in this paper; it also discusses prior max-min hardness due to Sreedurga et al.

I use the minimum-utility branch for Mass-Cover Max-Min PB. On the same mass-based instance, with unit project costs and \(u_{M,\mu}\), ask whether a feasible bundle has \(\min_{S\in\mathcal T}u_{M,\mu}(S\cap B)\ge 1\). Since \(u_{M,\mu}\) is monotone and each singleton has utility \(1\), this asks exactly whether every positive-mass ballot type gets at least one approved funded project. The Set Cover reduction carries over by assigning each element type mass \(1/|\mathcal U|\); duplicate ballots can simply be combined into one type. The restriction in Proposition 8’s proof ensures each ballot fits the budget. I again expect hardness transfers: the mass minimum is the finite minimum over the support, and every type in the reduction has positive mass. The product result is also relevant, but a continuous product needs an explicit convention—naturally a weighted geometric product—so I would treat it as a follow-up rather than quietly identify it with a product over a continuum.

These are questions the authors should recognize. Their utility already depends on the society’s ballot frequencies, and their aggregation rules already measure collective satisfaction. Replacing voter counts by rational masses makes the same statistical and optimization question meaningful for a large population with repeated ballot types. It does not continueize outcomes or relax the indivisibility of projects.

The weakest point is that this may look like type compression more than a new limit theory: every rational mass profile can be expanded into a finite electorate, and in worst-case instances the support size can grow with the number of voters. That limits any claim of automatic tractability. But it does not undermine the mirror: the high-multiplicity regime is plausible for large PB processes, and the exact finite-to-mass correspondence is precisely why these anchors provide a clear test of whether hardness survives.

The scope here is Proposition 7 and the minimum branch of Proposition 8. Further questions include whether the hardness persists under fixed \(k\)-additivity, which parameters—project count, support size, or budget—allow useful algorithms, and whether a weighted geometric-product version retains Proposition 8’s inapproximability.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both proposed mirrors are exact population reweightings of the paper’s existing problems, so they may add little beyond a compressed input format. But that objection does not meet this programme’s standard: an exact discrete-to-mass correspondence can itself be a useful result, and the paper’s optimization problems remain well-posed when the input is a distribution over ballot types. The fact that some real instances may have large support weakens the case for practical compression; it does not rule out the large-community, few-project regime.

Proposition 7 survives the attempted attack. For a finite profile with \(N_S\) voters of ballot type \(S\), setting \(\mu_S=N_S/N\) preserves every occurrence rate, hence the Möbius utilities. The mass-weighted objective is the original sum divided by \(N\). The reduction also yields rational masses with polynomial-size encodings. A better mirror could define the population directly by those ballot masses rather than start from a finite electorate, but the same computational question remains. Calling this “just a reparameterization” is not a valid objection under the stated programme.

Proposition 8’s minimum branch survives too. In the Set Cover construction, assign positive mass to every element ballot type. Then \(\min_{S:\mu_S>0}u_{M,\mu}(S\cap B)\geq 1\) exactly when every type receives an approved funded project, so the cover reduction still applies. A zero-mass type is outside the society’s support; an arbitrarily small positive mass still matters to a minimum objective, as it does to finite max-min fairness. If the product branch is included, a weighted geometric product likewise has positive value exactly when every positive-mass type has positive utility.

There is a legitimate concern that observed co-occurrence does not establish causal project synergy: budget constraints or correlated preferences can produce the same ballot distribution. But that challenges the interpretation of the paper’s utility function, not whether its computational problem has a continuous-population mirror. I cannot honestly defeat either anchor. The negative case is weak precisely where the proposed mirrors are strongest: the paper’s rules already depend on ballot frequencies, and repeated ballot types in a large participatory-budgeting process are a plausible high-multiplicity regime.
