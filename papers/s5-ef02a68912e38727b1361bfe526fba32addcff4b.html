The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of types \(t=(P_t,\rho_1(t),\ldots,\rho_m(t))\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita school capacities \(q_s\) summing to \(1\), and a threshold \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), compute a feasible assignment \(x_{t,s}\) (including the outside option) by a mass version of \(k\)-TA. Each type’s mass proposes in preference order; each school processes proposals by priority score and may replace held mass only when the proposer’s score exceeds the held mass’s score by more than \(\kappa\), splitting mass as needed and using a fixed rule for ties. Does the resulting assignment guarantee that no positive mass assigned below a preferred school faces either an unfilled seat there or assigned mass whose priority score is lower by more than \(\kappa\)?

The model it lives in

Types combine preference rankings with shared, school-specific priority scores; the society is rational mass \(\mu_t\) over these types, capacities scale with population, and assignment variables \(x_{t,s}\) may split a type’s mass across schools. The mechanism applies \(k\)-TA’s proposal and displacement rule to mass, with priority gaps and the threshold normalized to \(\rho_s(t)-\rho_s(t')\) and \(\kappa\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result: none of its propositions says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So it has no anchor of the kind your brief asks for. The best nearby constructive result is Proposition 2, proved in this paper: for every threshold \(k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\), the \(k\)-Threshold Acceptance mechanism returns a matching with no very justified envy. In full, no agent prefers a school to her assignment while either that school has a vacant seat or she outranks an assigned student there by more than \(k\) ranks. The paper gives a proof following the argument for Deferred Acceptance.

A plausible continuous question based on that proposition is Mass Threshold Acceptance. Take a large school district with many students but relatively few joint preference and priority strata. A type \(t\) records a strict preference ranking \(P_t\) and a priority score \(\rho_s(t)\in[0,1]\) at each school \(s\), with higher scores meaning higher priority. The society is rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); school capacities \(q_s\) are fractions of the population. Let \(\kappa\in[0,1]\) be the tolerated priority gap, corresponding to \(k/(n-1)\) in a finite market.

The decision variable is an assignment of mass \(x_{t,s}\) from each type to each school, and \(x_{t,\emptyset}\) to the outside option. Each unit of mass receives one school or the outside option, while the aggregate mass of a type may split across assignments. The question is: given \((\mu,P,\rho,q,\kappa)\), compute an assignment selected by aggregate \(k\)-TA that is feasible and \(\kappa\)-very-justified-envy-free. In each round, unassigned mass proposes to its next acceptable school; a full school displaces its lowest-priority held mass only when the new proposer’s score exceeds that mass’s score by more than \(\kappa\). The objective is to return the mechanism’s matching; the required output properties are feasibility and the stated fairness condition.

The motivating regime is a large urban school system in which many students share preference patterns and priority categories: \(N\) students, but a much smaller number \(\tau\) of joint strata. Mass records population shares, and capacities scale with the population. This is a recognizable high-multiplicity matching problem, though the paper does not study this regime.

I expect this finite-stratum version to be tractable: each student’s mass can advance through at most \(m\) preference choices, and each school’s displacement cutoff can move through only the finitely many priority scores in the input. A useful result would give a running time in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length, and prove that the aggregate procedure preserves Proposition 2’s guarantee. Further questions include whether arbitrary feasible \(\kappa\)-envy-free assignments can be found efficiently, and whether one can improve a mass-\(k\)-TA outcome while keeping the fairness guarantee.

The weakest point is the priority model. In the paper, priorities are strict rankings of individuals. If exact individual rank matters for envy, then it belongs in an agent’s complete type, and \(\tau\) may grow with \(N\). The finite-stratum mirror instead treats priority as a shared score or category, with the threshold measured in normalized score gaps. That is plausible for markets whose operative priorities are categorical, but it co-modifies the paper’s strict-rank model. And because Proposition 2 has no runtime claim, this is a new algorithmic question suggested by the paper, not a continuous version of a reported complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational-complexity result to continuize. Proposition 2 proves a fairness guarantee for the specified \(k\)-TA mechanism; Propositions 1 and 3–5 concern existence, incentives, and efficiency. The simulations report outcomes. None gives a complexity bound or studies an optimization or decision problem. The proposed task—compute an assignment by aggregate \(k\)-TA—is a new algorithmic question prompted by a new model, not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper. That is a poor fit for ChoCo’s stated gap.

There is also a real modeling cost. Under the paper’s exact definition, a type includes an agent’s rank in each school’s strict priority order. Since each rank belongs to only one agent, those complete types cannot repeat: \(\tau=n\). To get a high-multiplicity regime, one must replace individual ranks with shared priority categories or scores. That can be sensible, but priority advantage in the paper is a difference in positions, not a difference in scores. A mass version therefore needs a new interpretation—perhaps the mass between two priority strata—and rules for ties and partial displacement. The proponent’s score model may be a good extension, but it is not a direct continuation of Proposition 2’s strict-rank result.

The stronger version would ask for a welfare-maximizing \(\kappa\)-envy-free assignment over a finite set of preference and priority strata, perhaps using the paper’s discussion of constrained efficiency. That is a coherent computational question, and school districts with repeated preference patterns and priority categories are a credible setting. I cannot honestly rule out that this would be useful research. But it is a new optimization problem built from the paper’s setting, rather than a complexity question anchored by one of its results.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit: this paper offers ChoCo no computational result to mirror, and its most natural aggregate mechanism question requires a meaningful redesign of the priority model. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The proposed school-market regime is plausible, and the universal negative case is weak precisely there.
