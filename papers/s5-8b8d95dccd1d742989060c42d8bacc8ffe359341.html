What the mirror covers

The proposed population computation mirrors the Shapley value-based committee rule; it does not mirror the paper's axiomatic characterizations, rule properties, or comparisons.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 3, proved here, says the Shapley committee rule is the unique evaluative rule satisfying the exhaustive property, consistency, equal treatment for equals, and neutral alternative. Theorem 4 gives a second axiomatic characterization. Theorem 1 (Shapley, 1953) and Theorem 2 (Einy and Haimanko, 2011) characterize the Shapley value; Proposition 1 is another characterization, Proposition 2 gives special-case coincidences with approval voting, and Proposition 3 lists rule properties. None asserts a complexity classification such as hardness, polynomial-time solvability, or fixed-parameter tractability. Treating one as such an anchor would overstate the paper.

Still, the rule has a natural population-level computational question. A type would be a voter’s complete generalized-approval ballot \(E_t\): the set of nonempty groups of alternatives that voter approves. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the listed types, with rational masses summing to one. For each group \(S\), define its approval share by \(\delta_\mu(S)=\sum_{t:S\in E_t}\mu_t\), and set \(\delta_\mu(\emptyset)=0\). Compute each alternative’s Shapley score
\[ \Phi_i(\delta_\mu)=\sum_{S\subseteq C\setminus\{i\}}\frac{|S|!(m-|S|-1)!}{m!}\bigl(\delta_\mu(S\cup\{i\})-\delta_\mu(S)\bigr), \]
then return a size-\(k\) committee of the \(k\) highest-scoring alternatives, resolving a tie at the cutoff by any valid choice. The committee is the decision; the paper specifies no separate objective over committees.

A plausible regime is a large citywide participatory-budgeting consultation: many residents assess the compatibility of project combinations, but residents share a comparatively small number \(\tau\) of complete approval patterns. The mass of a type is its fraction of residents. This fits the paper’s generalized-approval model and its stated interest in participatory budgeting. The shared-pattern assumption is substantial, but it is exactly what makes a high-multiplicity instance credible.

For a finite electorate with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) divides every coalition’s approval count by \(n\). Shapley scores scale by the same positive factor, so the selected committee is unchanged. Thus the population formulation preserves the paper’s rule on empirical profiles and gives a per-capita version for rational type distributions. This is a prospective mirror of the rule underlying Theorem 3, not a mirror of a computational result asserted by that theorem.

On the paper’s explicit representation—each type’s approvals over the possible groups are listed—the scores can be calculated directly from the coalition values. That suggests tractability polynomial in the explicit input size. It does not establish polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\): the ballot description itself can have size exponential in \(m\). A useful follow-up would be to ask whether Shapley rankings can be computed efficiently when ballot types have compact descriptions.

The weakest point is decisive for the requested anchoring: the paper contributes axioms and comparisons, not a complexity landscape. The continuous-population problem is recognizable and precisely stateable, but the paper supplies no named hardness result whose dissolution or persistence would make it a continuization result in ChoCo’s sense.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with what the proponent concedes: this paper states no computational result. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 characterize a rule axiomatically; Proposition 2 gives special-case coincidences with approval voting. None classifies the complexity of computing, manipulating, or testing the rule. So there is no complexity result here for continuization to explain or extend.

The population model itself is credible. In a large participatory-budgeting consultation, residents can share complete generalized-approval ballots, and their masses can represent population shares. I would not reject the mirror on multiplicity grounds. But the proponent’s proposed anchor—computing the rule characterized by Theorem 3—does not open much of a computational landscape.

For each coalition \(R\), let \(e_R\) be the game that is \(1\) at \(R\) and \(0\) elsewhere. The population game decomposes as \(\delta_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{R\in E_t}e_R\). By Shapley linearity, each approved group contributes directly to every alternative’s score: for \(|R|=r\), its contribution is \(\mu_t(r-1)!(m-r)!/m!\) if \(i\in R\), and \(-\mu_t r!(m-r-1)!/m!\) if \(i\notin R\). Thus the scores and top-\(k\) committee can be computed in \(O(mQ)\) rational operations when the approved groups are listed, where \(Q=\sum_t|E_t|\). With the paper’s full coalition table as input, direct computation is polynomial in that table’s size. A bound polynomial only in \(m,\tau,L\) would require a succinct encoding for arbitrary approval families; the paper gives no such encoding. Any harder problem would depend on the newly chosen representation.

One could also add a population robustness question, such as the least mass reweighting that changes the top-\(k\) committee. With a finite set of allowed type changes and specified costs, the scores are affine in the masses, so this reduces to linear programming. That could be useful work, but it adds a perturbation model and costs that the paper never studies. If the model makes those changes difficult to describe, the new encoding or cost structure is where the difficulty comes from.

So the best case against is not that this paper lacks a plausible continuous population. It has one. It is that the stated rule’s computation is direct in the paper’s explicit representation, while the paper contributes axiomatic characterizations rather than computational questions. A compact ballot language or a carefully motivated robustness or manipulation model could produce worthwhile ChoCo work, but that would be an extension beyond the results offered as anchors. The negative case is strong against treating this paper as a continuization target; it cannot honestly establish that no such extension could ever be worthwhile.
