The continuous mirror question

Given a known population scale \(N\), an online stream of cohorts over finitely many availability and entry-position types \(t\), and rational cohort fractions \(\mu_t\), can a causal mechanism choose one available vehicle to lead and settle transfers on each segment \(J\) of constant availability so every vehicle’s normalized net chore burden is \(|J|/A_J\), where \(A_J=\sum_{t:\,J\subseteq[a_t,d_t)}\mu_t\), while attaining the Payment Transfer mechanism’s zero-rotation efficiency? On each such segment, followers pay \(u|J|/(N A_J)\), and the leader receives the total; the leader’s net burden and each follower’s payment, multiplied by \(N/u\), should give each vehicle accumulated burden \(\int_{a_t}^{d_t} ds/A(s)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution over vehicle availability and entry-position types, with rational masses, homogeneous follower utility \(u\), and known scale \(N\). The mechanism tracks the selected individual leader and transfers by segment; its objective is original SOCD efficiency subject to equal normalized per-vehicle burden. A sweep over cohort arrival and departure events is expected to run in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

The objection that survived

A type-mass input alone does not identify the individual leader whose account must be tracked; the proponent’s \(N\)-scaled burden addresses normalization but leaves that selection detail unspecified.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s Payment Transfer guarantee. It leaves the distributed impossibility result and the repeated-game and single-game load-balancing mechanisms aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a cohort representation with a tracked leader preserve exact per-vehicle equity causally when future cohorts remain unknown?
  • Does the event-sweep guarantee extend to finite sets of utilities, costs, and entry positions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is for Theorem 2, proved in this paper’s Appendix B. It says that the Payment Transfer mechanism is both efficiency-optimal and equitable. In each segment with a fixed set of \(n_{\mathrm{seg}}\) vehicles, every follower pays the leader \(u|\mathrm{seg}|/n_{\mathrm{seg}}\). The mechanism allows vehicles to join at any position, and makes no rotations: a change of leader occurs when the leader leaves or a new vehicle joins at the front. The payments make each participant’s equivalent share of the chore equal to its ex-post proportional share, while avoiding efficiency losses from rotations. This is a mechanism guarantee, not a named result about NP-hardness or running time.

I would define the continuous problem as Continuous Mass Payment SOCD. An instance is an online stream of arrival cohorts. A type \(t=(a_t,d_t)\) specifies a vehicle’s availability interval; vehicles of that type have the same follower utility \(u\) and the same costs. Each cohort arrives with a rational mass, and its departure time is known on arrival. The eventual society has distribution \(\mu\) over the finite support \(T\), with \(|T|=\tau\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is a large automated fleet on standardized routes: many vehicles share a small number of entry and exit windows, so the population scale \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\). Batch arrivals relax the paper’s no-tied-arrival assumption.

The question for a prover is: given this online cohort stream, construct a causal leader-and-payment mechanism that keeps one vehicle leading in each realized convoy, maximizes efficiency, and gives every vehicle its ex-post proportional chore burden. A solution is the leader-selection rule and a transfer ledger. At time \(s\), let
\[ A(s)=\sum_{t:\,a_t\le s<d_t}\mu_t \]
be the active fraction of the population. On a segment \(J\) of length \(|J|\) where availability is constant, there are \(N A_J\) active vehicles. Each follower pays \(u|J|/(N A_J)\); the leader receives the total paid by the followers. In chore-time units, a follower’s burden is its payment divided by \(u\), or \(|J|/(N A_J)\). The leader’s burden is its lead time minus its receipts divided by \(u\), also \(|J|/(N A_J)\). Thus the normalized burden \(N\) times each vehicle’s burden accumulates to its continuous proportional share
\[ \int_{a_t}^{d_t}\frac{ds}{A(s)}. \]
The mechanism needs only the currently available mass. It can be implemented by sweeping the arrival and departure events and settling transfers segment by segment.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: with finite support and rational data, the sweep is polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The result is an explicit mass version of the paper’s accounting identity; it does not need column generation or a new optimization theorem. Natural follow-ups are whether the same guarantee survives heterogeneous utilities, positive rotation costs, or cohorts with continuously varying availability windows.

The scenario is plausible because large fleets can have many vehicles running a small set of standardized routes and schedules. The important condition is that availability windows really form a small set of complete types; if nearly every vehicle has a distinct schedule, then \(\tau\) grows with \(N\) and the high-multiplicity regime disappears.

My weakest point is that Theorem 2’s fairness proof relies on individual payment accounts and a selected physical leader, while the continuous input records type masses. The large-fleet interpretation and its finite-population recovery rule make that bridge concrete, but an author might still regard it as accounting over repeated finite convoys rather than a new population-continuous problem. I would keep the scope to Theorem 2: the paper’s distributed impossibility and repeated-game results need different mirrors, and this paper offers no named computational-complexity classification to transfer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a weak anchor for ChoCo’s particular aim. It proves that a specified payment rule is equitable and efficiency-optimal; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The rule already says what to do: divide the road into segments, set each follower’s payment from the number of vehicles on that segment, and transfer the total to the leader. Its proof is a segmentwise accounting identity.

The proposed cohort mirror preserves that identity almost verbatim. On a segment \(J\), replace the vehicle count \(n_{\mathrm{seg}}\) by the cohort count \(N A_J\). The equality between the leader’s receipts and each follower’s saving then carries over, and a sweep through the arrival and departure events produces the ledger. Even adding entry position or finitely many vehicle classes to the type would make the input more detailed, but would not create the sort of pricing, optimization, or algorithmic bottleneck that motivates the programme. The continuous question would mostly recast an existing rule in cohort notation.

There is also a genuine modeling wrinkle. The mass distribution records how many vehicles are available by type, but Theorem 2 assigns the chore to one particular vehicle and its payments to one account. In an atomless population, that leader has zero mass, so the aggregate model loses the individual burden the fairness claim concerns. The proponent’s \(N\)-scaled per-vehicle burden and finite-\(N\) recovery rule are defensible ways to preserve it. But they retain a population scale and a distinguished individual alongside the continuous distribution; they do not get the fairness claim from the mass society alone.

I would not argue that convoy fleets lack a plausible high-multiplicity regime: standardized routes and schedules provide one. Nor would I use the paper’s discrete agents as evidence against a continuous version. My narrower case is that, even in that regime, this anchor offers little computational research for ChoCo: the continuous extension is a direct sweep of a rule whose guarantee is already an elementary identity. A richer heterogeneous model could be worthwhile, but the paper explicitly leaves its fairness criteria open there, so that would be a new problem rather than a stronger mirror of Theorem 2.

This negative case is strongest on fit with ChoCo’s computational agenda, and weak on the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. If the programme counts a clean cohort-level restatement as a useful result, this anchor can survive; I do not see a sound argument that the continuous model is incoherent.
