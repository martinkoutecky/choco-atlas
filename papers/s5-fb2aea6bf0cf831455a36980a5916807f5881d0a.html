What the mirror covers

The proposed LP covers allocation and Walrasian-price computation for type masses; it does not cover the finite-agent incentive constraints in Theorem 4.1 or Proposition 3.1, or the paper's two-agent payment constructions.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named result in this paper that classifies a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So it has no anchor that meets your strict complexity-result requirement. The strongest nearby anchors are mechanism-existence results, not complexity theorems. My positive case rests on those, while treating the computational question below as a proposed continuous mirror rather than a result proved in the paper.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in Section 4. It says that with heterogeneous capacities and more goods than the smallest capacity, no mechanism can be simultaneously welfare-maximizing, incentive compatible, envy-free, and free of positive transfers. Equivalently, every efficient IC and EF mechanism must pay an agent on some valuation profile.

A plausible regime is a large allocation market in which many agents share a small set of complete valuation-and-capacity types: for example, recurring cohorts of buyers with the same value for each product category and the same capacity limit. Let the finite support be \(T\), with type \(t=(c_t,v_t)\) holding mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Capacity is public, as in the theorem’s public-capacity setting. Goods are categories with many identical copies, supplied at rational per-capita amounts \(q_j\). In the discrete counterpart with \(n\) agents, category \(j\) has about \(nq_j\) copies. Here \(\tau=|T|\) can stay small as \(n\) grows.

My lead mirror is continuous heterogeneous-capacity allocation with truthful Walrasian prices. Its input is \(T\), the rational type masses \(\mu\), rational per-capita supplies \(q\), and the valuations and capacities. It asks for nonnegative item prices \(p_j\) and a mass assignment \(x_{t,b}\), where \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^s\) is an integral bundle with \(\sum_j b_j\le c_t\). The assignment must allocate all mass of each type, respect supplies, and assign positive mass of type \(t\) only to bundles maximizing
\[ \sum_j b_j\bigl(v_t(j)-p_j\bigr) \quad\text{over}\quad b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^s,\ \sum_j b_j\le c_t. \]
Any good with positive price must be fully allocated. Payments are \(p\cdot b\). Thus the question is to compute, for each reported population distribution, a welfare-maximizing market-clearing allocation and prices that implement it as a direct mechanism. A solution includes the prices and mass assignment, and the mechanism rule that runs this computation on every report distribution.

In the nonatomic model, one agent’s report does not change the population distribution or prices. Given those prices, a truthful report gets a bundle maximizing that agent’s true utility; a different report can only select another feasible bundle, so it cannot improve utility. This gives truthfulness. Market-clearing prices make recipients’ bundles utility-maximizing, which gives envy-freeness, including when a lower-capacity agent compares against a feasible subset of another bundle. Nonnegative prices give no positive transfers, and the empty bundle gives individual rationality.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. For the replicated-category model, the aggregate welfare problem is the rational LP
\[ \max \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j=1}^s v_t(j)y_{tj} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_j y_{tj}\le c_t\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t y_{tj}\le q_j,\qquad y_{tj}\ge0. \]
It has \(O(s\tau)\) variables and constraints. Its dual gives item prices; the aggregate assignments can be decomposed into masses receiving integral bundles. Thus the proposed computation is polynomial in the support size, number of categories, and input bit length. The paper itself supports this direction: Theorem 2.4, proved there, shows capacitated valuations are gross substitutes, and the paper invokes Gul and Stacchetti for Walrasian equilibrium existence. Neither result is a complexity classification.

A second, narrower mirror is homogeneous-capacity Walrasian allocation with truthful reports. The anchor, Proposition 3.1, is proved in the paper: for homogeneous capacities \(c\ge2\), no incentive-compatible mechanism always produces Walrasian prices; the proof gives a witness at \(c=2\). Restricting the continuous problem above to a common capacity \(c\ge2\) asks for exactly the price-and-allocation computation that the finite result rules out. The same LP and price-taking argument predict a tractable continuous answer.

The key limitation is also the weakest point: atomlessness removes an individual’s ability to affect prices. That is precisely why the finite impossibilities may disappear, but it changes the strategic setting that produced them. The paper’s authors might recognize the resulting object as their allocation market in a price-taking, high-multiplicity regime; they might also regard the truthfulness question as weaker than finite-agent dominant-strategy truthfulness. The mirror therefore covers the paper’s mechanism-existence results on truthful, efficient, envy-free allocation and Walrasian pricing—not a named complexity result, and not its other payment constructions. Further questions are whether the price-taking mirror admits useful finite-\(n\) approximation guarantees, and how much incentive regret remains when one agent’s report changes the market by \(1/n\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper’s headline results are about strategic mechanisms, while the paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 4.1 rules out a mechanism that is simultaneously efficient, incentive compatible, envy-free, and free of positive transfers. Proposition 3.1 rules out an incentive-compatible mechanism that produces Walrasian prices. Neither defines an algorithmic problem or classifies its complexity. The proposed equilibrium LP is a coherent computational question, but it is a new market-clearing problem rather than a computational mirror of either result.

For Theorem 4.1, the strategic condition depends on an individual report affecting the mechanism’s outcome and payments. In an atomless population described only by \(\mu\), one individual’s report leaves the distribution unchanged. Truthfulness then means that, at given prices, the individual chooses a bundle that maximizes their own utility. That is a sensible price-taking property, and the LP may compute the associated allocation and prices. It removes the finite-agent strategic constraint at the center of Theorem 4.1. Giving a cohort positive mass so its report can move prices makes the cohort a strategic bloc; retaining finite agents keeps their \(1/n\) influence and the original exact incentive problem. Either choice changes the theorem’s strategic unit or its limit.

Proposition 3.1 has the same gap. Its claim concerns a direct mechanism that maps finite-agent valuation profiles to Walrasian prices while remaining dominant-strategy incentive compatible. Computing prices for a reported distribution does not test that claim when an individual report cannot move the distribution. A stronger nearby question would study finite-\(n\) incentive regret when prices are computed from a continuum approximation. That could be useful, but it needs an approximation notion and assumptions on valuations and price selection; it is a new asymptotic mechanism-design question, not Proposition 3.1’s pricing-existence question.

Theorem 2.4 does not close the gap. It establishes gross substitutes for each capacitated valuation and supports Walrasian equilibrium existence. Its statement does not depend on population multiplicity. The aggregate LP may make a useful population-computation model, but the gross-substitutes theorem itself supplies no computational anchor for a continuous mirror.

This is the negative case’s limit: the proposed atomless equilibrium computation is well-posed, and repeated buyer types with per-capita supplies are plausible. I cannot honestly rule out its value to the programme as an adjacent application of continuous optimization. The stronger objection is narrower: it does not preserve the strategic questions in the paper’s named impossibility results, and those results provide no computational problem for a direct continuization.
