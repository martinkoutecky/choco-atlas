The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over interval types \(I_t=[\alpha_t,\beta_t)\) whose union is \([0,1)\), consider sequences of populations with type counts \(n_t(N)\) satisfying \(n_t(N)/N\to\mu_t\). Scale the cake and each type’s interval to \([0,N)\) and \(N I_t\), respectively. Among sequences of direct mechanisms that, for every finite instance in the paper’s domain, produce envy-free, truthful allocations partitioning the cake with each agent’s bundle contained in their reported interval, determine the infimum of \(\limsup_{N\to\infty} K_N/N\), where \(K_N\) is the number of cuts; compute or approximate this rate from \(\mu\) and the interval endpoints.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over supported interval types describes a growing society, with the cake supply and valuation intervals scaled per capita. The solution is a sequence of finite direct mechanisms, each retaining the paper’s individual truthfulness and envy-freeness conditions; typewise bundle laws describe limits, while the objective records the sequence’s asymptotic cuts per agent.

The objection that survived

The mirror depends on finite mechanisms and scaled individual bundles: it is an asymptotic implementation problem, and the stated coherent limit does not specify a topology or typewise envy condition that fixes the resulting laws and rates.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 16’s solid-interval, envy-free, truthful mechanism through an asymptotic cut-rate question; it leaves the structural lemmas, possible improvements to the cut bound, and the proposed broader valuation classes aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the asymptotic cut rate and associated typewise bundle laws be computed or approximated in time polynomial in \(\tau\), endpoint bit length \(L\), and requested precision?
  • Does the rate depend on how the type counts \(n_t(N)\) are rounded toward \(\mu\)?
  • Can the paper’s bound yield a rate of \(2\), and can any truthful envy-free sequence achieve a smaller rate?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous, high-multiplicity version of the paper’s solid interval cake-cutting problem. Imagine a very large batch of workers dividing a divisible workload: each worker values the portions in one contiguous time window uniformly, and many workers share the same window. A type is that complete interval valuation \(I_t=[\alpha_t,\beta_t)\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of workers with that type. The regime is \(N\) large and the number \(\tau\) of distinct windows modest. The society is the distribution \(\mu\), not the cake’s divisibility.

The anchor is Theorem 16, proved here. It says Mechanism 1 finds, in \(O(n^3)\) time, an envy-free and truthful allocation \(A_N=(A_i:i\in N)\) for a solid interval profile, with \(A_i\subseteq C_i\), \(\bigcup_i A_i=C\), and at most \(2n-2\) cuts. This is a particularly good anchor because small cut count is central to the paper, and the theorem gives both a construction and an explicit bound.

I would give the prover this problem: Mass-Interval Envy-Free Truthful Cut Division. An instance is \(C=[0,1)\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over rational interval types \(I_t\), with positive rational masses summing to one and \(\bigcup_t I_t=C\). The task is to construct a direct, truthful allocation rule for such societies and report its minimum asymptotic cut rate: the infimum of \(\kappa=\limsup_{N\to\infty}K_N/N\), where \(K_N\) is the number of cuts in an allocation for a population of size \(N\) whose type proportions approach \(\mu\). The rule must make every finite allocation a partition into ordinary bundles, envy-free and truthful for individual interval reports, with each bundle contained in its recipient’s interval. The target suggested by the theorem is \(\kappa\le 2\).

The continuous output records type-level averages, while retaining the individual bundles needed to state fairness and incentives. For a finite allocation with \(n_t=N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), let \(\Lambda_t^N\) be the empirical distribution of their scaled bundle measures \(N\,\mathbf{1}_{A_i}(x)\,dx\). A continuous solution is a coherent limit of these laws as \(N\) grows. Its type average \(x_t=\mathbb E_{\Lambda_t}[\nu]\) may be fractional even though every finite-population agent receives an actual union of intervals. The scaling keeps per-capita utility and cut rate meaningful as individual bundles shrink. The limiting laws must preserve envy-freeness across types; they must also come from finite mechanisms that satisfy the paper’s individual truthfulness condition. Thus the problem is not just to find a fractional cake allocation for a fixed population: the society distribution is the input, and the rule must respond truthfully to reports.

I expect this to be Class A, though the paper does not prove the continuous result. Its density argument has an immediate mass analogue, \(\rho(X)=\operatorname{len}(X)/\mu(X)\). The discrete perfect-matching step suggests a fractional matching or interval-flow problem over the supported types. With \(\tau\) modest, that points toward a finite LP or flow computation whose complexity depends on \(\tau\), endpoint bit length \(L\), and requested precision—not on \(N\). The concrete question is whether one can compute the limiting rule, or approximate it, in time polynomial in those parameters, with cut rate at most \(2\).

This mirrors only Theorem 16’s solid-interval, truthful, envy-free small-cut result. The paper’s own algorithm supplies finite approximants with \(K_N\le 2N-2\) and runtime \(O(N^3)\), but it does not establish convergence, a direct algorithm on \(\mu\), or a runtime bound in \(\tau\). Further questions include whether the limit is independent of how type counts are rounded, whether the best cut rate is below \(2\), and how closely a computed continuous rule approximates finite implementations.

The authors would likely recognize the mirror: it keeps their valuation domain, strategic reports, fairness condition, and cut objective intact. The weakest point is that their theorem is about individually allocated pieces, and its truthfulness proof is finite-population. A type-average limit alone could erase envy between particular bundles or make truthfulness vacuous for an infinitesimal agent. Requiring the limit laws to arise from truthful, envy-free finite mechanisms addresses that issue, but makes convergence and efficient computation genuine parts of the new problem. If the authors reject such an asymptotic mechanism as their continuous question, the case weakens substantially.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The serious anchor is Theorem 16. It gives a named computational result, and the paper’s workload example admits a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many tasks can share the same acceptable time interval. I can’t make a convincing case that no such population exists, or that the theorem has nothing to mirror.

The strongest objection is that the theorem’s central objects resist a direct continuum limit. With a fixed cake \(C=[0,1)\), a measurable partition among an atomless population gives almost every individual a bundle of measure zero. Envy and truthfulness then become vacuous under the paper’s utility definition. The proponent’s rescaling, \(N\mathbf{1}_{A_i}(x)\,dx\), tries to preserve per-person utility, but its output is a limit of finite allocations. The cut rate is also a property of those finite implementations: it is not determined by a type-level average allocation, and it need not survive as a statistic of a weak limit.

A stronger mirror could make that implementation sequence the object of study: for each rational type distribution \(\mu\), find the minimum \(\limsup K_N/N\) over sequences of finite mechanisms that remain envy-free and truthful, with rules for rounding type counts and for convergence of the individual bundle laws. That formulation preserves the paper’s substance better than a simple fractional allocation LP. But it asks for an asymptotic implementation theory whose state includes finite-population incentives and cut microstructure. If those details are dropped, a mass allocation may retain average utilities while losing the individual comparisons and deviations that Theorem 16 guarantees. If they are retained, the proposed continuous answer is an added structure on a sequence of discrete mechanisms, not a direct computational result on \(\mu\).

That is the best negative case I see, and it is limited. A well-specified asymptotic implementation problem could be worthwhile in its own right, and the paper’s workload motivation makes the setting credible. I would not claim this anchor is defeated; the case against weakens precisely if ChoCo treats scaled, finite-lifted mechanisms as an acceptable continuous object.
