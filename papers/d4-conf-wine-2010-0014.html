The continuous mirror question

Given \(m=3\) candidates, a target \(p\), an explicit finite set \(T\) of complete antisymmetric preference-table and cost types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and pair-flip costs, choose nonnegative transfers \(x_{t,u}\) from initial type \(t\) to final type \(u\) minimizing \(\sum_{t,u}\gamma(t,u)x_{t,u}\), subject to mass conservation and \(p\) being a \(\mathrm{Copeland}^{\alpha}\)-winner of the final profile \(N_{\mu'}(a,b)=\sum_t\mu'_t\mathbf{1}[t:a\succ b]\).

The model it lives in

A continuous-population \(\mathrm{Copeland}^{\alpha}\)-microbribery model in which complete table and cost profiles are types with masses \(\mu_t\), transfers \(x_{t,u}\) split mass between initial and final types, and the objective is total per-unit flip cost. For \(m=3\), pairwise-outcome regimes yield finite transportation LPs; the discrete Partition obstruction disappears when the required mass can be divided.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Proposition 4.3 and Corollary 3.6. It leaves the other scoring-rule, Copeland, maximin, weighted microbribery, Appendix, and swap-bribery results unclaimed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For arbitrary \(m\), what is the complexity of continuous \(\mathrm{Copeland}^{\alpha}\)-microbribery with explicit or implicitly represented table types?
  • What formal high-multiplicity dictionary relates weighted block costs to per-capita mass costs, including rounding guarantees back to finite elections?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a two-anchor mirror, led by the paper’s hardness result.

The natural regime is a large population of individuals who share a small number of complete behavioural types. In the campaign setting, a type consists of a ranking, the cost curve for moving the preferred candidate \(p\), and any other parameters used by the rule. The mass \(\mu_t\) of a type is the fraction of the population with that description. Campaign cost is proportional to the mass reached. This is particularly plausible here because the paper itself says that a “voter” may represent a group reached by the same promotional activity, and explicitly motivates weighted voters through groups of voters or search terms with identical treatment.

My lead anchor is Proposition 4.3, proved in this paper. It states that, for every rational \(\alpha<1\) and every \(K\), there is no polynomial-time \(K\)-approximation algorithm for weighted Copeland\({}^\alpha\)-microbribery unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The proof uses only three candidates and reduces from Partition. Thus the result is not merely a consequence of having many candidates: its hardness comes from binary voter weights and the requirement that an indivisible collection of weighted voters be split exactly.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous Copeland\({}^\alpha\)-microbribery. An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(C\), a preferred candidate \(p\), a finite set \(T\) of complete preference-table types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and type-dependent pair-flip costs. A type is a complete antisymmetric pairwise-preference table together with its flip-cost function, so two agents are of the same type only when they are indistinguishable for the whole problem. A solution chooses nonnegative masses \(x_{t,t'}\) transferred from each initial type \(t\) to final type \(t'\). The transfer cost is \(\gamma(t,t')x_{t,t'}\), where \(\gamma(t,t')\) is the minimum cost of changing table \(t\) into \(t'\). The final population induces pairwise masses
\[ N_{\mu'}(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu'_t\,\mathbf 1[t:a\succ b], \]
and hence normalized Copeland\({}^\alpha\) scores. The task is to find a minimum-cost mass transfer making \(p\) a Copeland\({}^\alpha\)-winner.

This is the same microbribery question—same candidates, same pairwise operations, same costs, same winner condition—with voter multiplicity made divisible. For the three-candidate regime used in Proposition 4.3, the continuous problem is exactly solvable in polynomial time for explicit \(T\): enumerate the finitely many possible pairwise outcomes and solve the resulting transportation LPs.

The paper’s Partition reduction makes the point sharply. Its weighted voters aggregate into three continuous types of masses \(1/2,1/4,1/4\):
\[ a\succ b\succ p,\qquad p\succ a\succ b,\qquad p\succ b\succ a. \]
Only the \(a\)-versus-\(b\) relation in the first type may be flipped, at zero cost. In the discrete reduction, one must choose a subset of the weighted \(a\succ b\succ p\) voters whose weights sum to exactly half. In the continuous mirror, one simply transfers mass \(1/4\) of that type to \(b\succ a\succ p\). Then every pairwise contest ties, all candidates receive Copeland score \(2\alpha\), and \(p\) wins at cost \(0\). The Partition hardness therefore dissolves for exactly the reason continuization is supposed to expose: the combinatorics lived in indivisible population blocks, not in the agenda or the voting rule.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A in the anchored three-candidate regime. For unbounded \(m\), the complexity of continuous Copeland microbribery remains a worthwhile further question: the winner condition has disjunctive pairwise structure, and the type universe can be exponentially large. It is also worth asking precisely which restrictions restore hardness—indivisible blocs, fixed campaign charges, or constraints forbidding a type’s mass from splitting.

My second anchor is Corollary 3.6, also proved here. It states that, for every scoring rule \(\vec\alpha\), there is an algorithm \(\mathcal B^w\) which, given a weighted instance of weighted \(\mathcal R_{\vec\alpha}\)-shift-bribery, outputs a successful shift-action of cost at most \(2\opt(I)\) in time polynomial in \(|I|\) and \(|\vec\alpha|\). This is a direct algorithmic result for the paper’s central campaign-management problem.

The continuous counterpart is:

Continuous scoring-rule shift campaign. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a target \(p\), a scoring vector \(\vec\alpha\), ranking types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and nondecreasing per-unit cost curves \(\pi_t(k)\). If \(p\) is in position \(r_t\) in type \(t\), let \(y_{t,k}\) be the mass of that type shifted upward by exactly \(k\) positions, for \(0\le k<r_t\). The constraints are
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{r_t-1}y_{t,k}=\mu_t,\qquad y_{t,k}\ge0. \]
The objective is
\[ \min \sum_{t\in T}\sum_k \pi_t(k)y_{t,k}, \]
subject to \(p\) maximizing the aggregate scoring-rule score after the shifts. A solution is the mass-allocation matrix \(y\), together with the induced post-campaign society.

This is not a softened campaign problem: \(p\) is still the same target, only upward shifts are allowed, the same scoring vector is used, and the same type-specific prices apply. The sole relaxation is that a type’s mass may be divided among different shift amounts, which is precisely the continuous-population interpretation.

For an explicit support \(T\), this problem is an LP with \(O(|T|m)\) variables and linear winner constraints, so I expect an exact polynomial-time algorithm. It is therefore a particularly clean Class A mirror. If the ranking types and costs are supplied implicitly over all \(m!\) rankings, the natural next question is a pricing or separation problem; that is a genuine continuation of the paper’s computational question rather than an unrelated fractional-election model. The discrete algorithm of Corollary 3.6 then gives a useful downward bridge: rational masses can be rounded to a finite electorate, with approximation or additive-error guarantees depending on the denominator and the number of types.

This case deliberately does not claim to mirror every result in the paper. It covers Proposition 4.3 and Corollary 3.6 emphatically; I would not stretch it to the unnumbered possible-winner claim about swap bribery, or claim that all maximin and Copeland variants automatically inherit the same LP structure.

The weakest point is the lead anchor. Proposition 4.3 concerns microbribery in the paper’s irrational-voter model, and its reduction uses zero-cost flips and pairwise tables that need not come from transitive rankings. A referee might regard that as a technical model rather than a realistic campaign. Also, if the real-world action is a fixed indivisible advertising bloc, fractional transfer would be inappropriate. The answer is that the paper itself studies microbribery, explicitly allows weighted groups, and the continuous question preserves its complete type descriptions and operations; it changes only the granularity of the population. The scoring-rule shift mirror is consequently the more robust positive case, while the Proposition 4.3 mirror is valuable precisely because it shows a named hardness result whose population-integrality component disappears under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirrors is semantic rather than computational: both change the paper’s campaign model more substantially than the proponent admits.

For Proposition 4.3, the weighted voters are indivisible blocks. Their weights \(a_i\) encode the Partition instance, while the microbribery price is paid once for flipping a block’s table. The proposed continuous model replaces those blocks by one type of mass \(1/2\), then splits off mass \(1/4\) at zero cost. That deliberately destroys the numerical structure responsible for the theorem. It is therefore not a continuous version of the weighted microbribery instance in the ordinary campaign sense; it is a relaxation in which fractional pieces of an advertising bloc may be treated independently.

The weakness is even clearer at three candidates. The entire construction collapses to three table types, and the continuous optimum is obtained by a constant-size transportation LP. This is a valid fractional relaxation of the reduction, but hardly a substantive continuous theory of Copeland microbribery. If one instead preserves the general unbounded-candidate problem, Copeland’s winner condition is a disjunction over pairwise outcomes, while arbitrary preference tables give \(2^{\binom m2}\) possible types. The proponent has not shown that the resulting problem is an LP or polynomial-time solvable; their Class A claim is established only for a tiny gadget.

That objection nevertheless does not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s rules. The disappearance of Partition’s indivisibility is precisely the kind of phenomenon continuization is meant to expose. A type consisting of a complete preference table and its costs is legitimate, and the three-candidate problem is still a named computational question. The fact that its first answer may be easy is not a valid objection.

Corollary 3.6 has a similar modelling vulnerability. In the paper, \(\pi^i(k)\) is a fixed price for shifting voter \(i\), and a weighted voter is not literally \(w_i\) independent people who each pay that price. In the proposed LP, however, shifting mass \(y_{t,k}\) costs \(\pi_t(k)y_{t,k}\). That changes a fixed campaign or advertising charge into a per-capita charge. If the intended campaign cost is paid once for reaching a type, fractional mass allocation requires activation variables and is no longer the stated LP.

But this also fails as a universal objection. The paper explicitly motivates voters as groups reached by the same promotion and search terms with different popularity. A per-capita campaign model is a perfectly sensible high-multiplicity regime: identical agents share the same ranking and cost curve, and total cost scales with the mass reached. Under that interpretation, the proposed formulation is exactly the natural mass relaxation. Its LP may be routine, but routine tractability is still a legitimate Class A result.

Thus the negative case cannot honestly rely on lack of multiplicity, identity-dependent objectives, degeneration, or prior continuous treatment. The paper itself supplies the group interpretation, and both anchors concern named computational results. The best criticism is that the Copeland anchor is narrow and that the scoring anchor must state its per-capita cost semantics explicitly. Those are caveats about scope and overclaiming, not reasons why no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. I would not sign the universal negative verdict: Corollary 3.6, at minimum, survives as a credible continuous-population question.
