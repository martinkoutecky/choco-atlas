The continuous mirror question

Given socioeconomic categories \(1,\ldots,k\), explicit mass-preserving transfer vectors \(T=\{t^1,\ldots,t^m\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^k\) satisfying Theorem 1’s Hilbert-basis condition, and rational population distributions \(\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Q}_+^k\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_i\nu_i=1\), decide whether \(\mu-\nu\in\operatorname{cone}(T)\), equivalently whether \(T\lambda=\mu-\nu\) for some \(\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_+^m\).

The model it lives in

Treat the paper’s ordered socioeconomic categories as population types carrying masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_i\). The decision variables \(\lambda_j\) are amounts assigned to the permitted transfer directions \(t^j\); feasibility of \(T\lambda=\mu-\nu\), \(\lambda\ge0\), decides whether the distributions are ordered and gives a rational linear-feasibility problem.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s comparison criterion for transfer sets satisfying its Hilbert-basis condition, including its use in applications; it leaves transfer systems without that premise and the paper’s other analysis outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For transfer sets without the Hilbert-basis condition, what is the sharp bound on the smallest replication factor \(q\) that turns a rational conic witness into an integral transfer sequence \(q(\mu-\nu)=Tz\), \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_+^m\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s core task of comparing two distributions under a supplied transfer order. The paper’s actual inputs are finite count vectors, \(x,y\in\mathbb{D}_n^k\), over \(k\) ordered socioeconomic states; its order is defined by whether \(x-y\) belongs to the integer cone generated by the allowed transfers. So the population is not already continuous just because the paper uses the real cone as a tool: the objects being compared still have integer counts. [The paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-025-01620-y)

My single anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It gives an equivalence for a transfer set \(\mathcal T=\{t^1,\ldots,t^m\}\subseteq\mathbb Z^k\) that contains a Hilbert basis of its cone, with \(r=\operatorname{rank}(T)\leq\min\{k-1,m\}\). For \(x,y\in\mathbb{D}_n^k\), it says: (i) \(x\succeq_T y\); (ii) \(x-y\) is a nonnegative rational combination of a linearly independent subset of the transfers; (iii) a simplicial subcone generated by such a subset also orders \(x\) and \(y\); and (iv) \(\operatorname{rank}(T)=\operatorname{rank}([T\ \ x-y])\) and there exists a generalized inverse \(G\) of \(T\) with \(G(x-y)=\theta\in\mathbb{Q}_+^m\). The article works out algorithms for finding the relevant simplicial cone or a separating vector from this characterization. Theorem 1 is not a P or NP-hardness theorem; it is the computational formulation result that the prompt allows as an anchor. [Theorem 1 and its computational use](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-025-01620-y)

I would call the mirror Continuous Transfer-Order Comparison. An instance gives \(k\) ordered socioeconomic categories, an explicit list of integer, mass-preserving transfer directions \(T\), and two rational population distributions \(\mu,\nu\in\mathbb Q_+^k\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_i\nu_i=1\). Assume the transfer system satisfies Theorem 1’s Hilbert-basis condition. The question is whether
\[ \mu-\nu\in\operatorname{cone}(T), \]
equivalently, whether there is a vector \(\lambda\in\mathbb Q_+^m\) such that \(T\lambda=\mu-\nu\). A yes-instance has \(\lambda\) as a certificate; a no-instance can be certified by a separating linear functional. There is no objective to minimize: the decision is whether the two distributions are ordered. The coefficients \(\lambda_j\) are the amounts of population assigned to each permitted transfer direction.

A natural regime is a country-scale health or social-welfare comparison: millions of residents grouped into a fixed small set of categories, such as the five self-assessed-health states used in the paper’s example. A type is one such category, complete for the comparison because the supplied order uses no other information about individuals in it. Mass is the fraction of the population in each category; each resident still occupies an ordinary category. Here the number of people is millions while the type space has five categories (or a similarly small fixed set). The paper itself motivates comparisons on distributions of this kind. [The distribution model and health example](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-025-01620-y)

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A) when \(T\) is explicit: it is rational linear-feasibility, solvable in polynomial time in the encoded instance size. That is an inference from the LP formulation, not a complexity classification proved by the paper. The high-multiplicity bridge is unusually clean here. Given rational \(\mu,\nu\), clear a common denominator \(N\) to obtain finite-population count vectors \(x=N\mu\) and \(y=N\nu\). Under Theorem 1’s condition, its equivalence identifies the discrete comparison with the continuous cone test. Conversely, normalizing a finite count-vector instance gives a rational continuous instance. Thus the equality of the two tests is a result to explain, not a reason to dismiss the mirror as a restatement.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s comparison criterion for transfer systems satisfying its Hilbert-basis premise. It does not claim to continuize every result or application in the paper. In particular, the paper’s treatment of transfer sets without a Hilbert basis shows that the premise excludes cases of interest: there can be integer differences in the real cone that are not in the discrete cone generated by the listed transfers. That is the main limitation and a natural follow-up question: for such systems, when does the continuous comparison become exact after replicating both populations, and how large must that replication factor be? The other soft spot is that the underlying task is a normative distribution comparison, not a costed intervention problem. Still, the question here fixes the transfer order and asks for an exact computational comparison between large populations. That makes it a credible population-continuum mirror of the paper’s central result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a complexity result. It characterizes when the paper’s finite distribution vectors are ordered: cone membership, a nonnegative rational combination of transfers, a simplicial subcone, and a generalized inverse are equivalent descriptions. It does not state the complexity of a decision, search, or optimization problem. The paper’s computational discussion uses linear programming to implement the criterion, but the proponent’s anchor turns a structural characterization into a new complexity question. That is a plausible follow-up, but it is not a mirror of a computational result established by the paper.

The proposed mirror is otherwise unusually credible. Comparing country-scale health distributions over five categories is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; the relevant types can be complete, and the order does not track individual identities. The better version is exactly the one proposed: for rational distributions \(\mu,\nu\), ask whether \(\mu-\nu\in\operatorname{cone}(T)\). But choose a common denominator \(N\) and set \(x=N\mu\), \(y=N\nu\). Then the continuous instance is the paper’s finite-vector instance, and the cone test is unchanged by scaling. Under Theorem 1’s Hilbert-basis condition, the paper already supplies the equivalence to a rational cone test and discusses LP-based implementation.

That makes the proposed work look less like a new computational landscape and more like applying the paper’s existing criterion to normalized population data. Outside the Hilbert-basis condition, a continuous cone test would discard the integer-semigroup distinctions the paper studies; questions about when replication repairs that gap concern those arithmetic distinctions.

This is a limited negative case, though. The programme explicitly allows a discrete-to-continuous identity to count as a result, so the exact correspondence cannot by itself disqualify the mirror. I can argue that the anchor is not a computational-complexity theorem and that the best mirror adds little new computation; I cannot honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists here.
