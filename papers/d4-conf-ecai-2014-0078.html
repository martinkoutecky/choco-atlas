The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying anchor in this paper. Definitions 1–10 specify the P&S formalism and its semantics, but none asserts a computational result such as tractability or hardness. H1–H3 are empirical hypotheses; their confirmation is reported through survey results, not as numbered computational theorems or propositions. So there is no named computational result here to restate as a continuous problem.

The nearest positive angle would treat respondents as a population: types could encode their domain knowledge and response profile, mass could represent the fraction of respondents of each type, and the question could be whether the population’s modal answer agrees with P&S status. But that recasts the paper’s empirical evaluation as an aggregate survey question. It supplies neither a computational problem the paper studies nor a named result to mirror, so I would not present it as a valid ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I agree with the proponent that the paper has no named computational result to mirror: Definitions 1–10 give a formalism and its semantics; H1–H3 are empirical hypotheses, and the reported findings are survey results. That is a strong reason against a ChoCo mirror, though it does not by itself rule out an interesting adjacent project.

The best version of the respondent-population idea is quite plausible: in a large public consultation, define each type by its domain knowledge and full response profile, and let \(\mu\) describe the mass of each type. One could ask what fraction accepts each position, or whether the aggregate judgment agrees with P&S status. This avoids objections about whether people can be grouped: repeated profiles are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and mass is a natural way to report response shares. But it still does not mirror a computational problem in this paper. The paper studies whether individuals’ judgments correspond to formal status; aggregating supplied response masses is a different task, and the paper’s survey already reports aggregate response shares. The computational work, if any, lies in evaluating the argument theory, not in having a continuous audience.

A stronger computational proposal might ask how much mass must be persuaded, or how an explanatory interface should change, to shift the majority judgment. That could be worthwhile research on argumentation and campaigning. But it adds an intervention objective and its costs or susceptibility model, none of which this paper studies or proves anything about. It is an adjacent problem, not a continuous mirror of a result here.

So the respondent angle is the strongest candidate, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor. I cannot rule out every worthwhile project one could build around large audiences and argumentation; I do not see one that this paper itself gives ChoCo a result to continuize.
