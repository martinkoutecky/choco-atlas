What the mirror covers

Theorem 4 and the other persuasion results establish analytical implementation, equilibrium, and robustness claims; none supplies a computational anchor for a population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most plausible high multiplicity regime is a large electorate of shareholders or legislators with many agents but relatively few preference and information classes. A type could specify an agent’s state-contingent utilities and signal technology; mass would be the fraction in each class. A sender’s decision would be an exchangeable information structure, and the goal would be to make a specified policy win in each state.

The closest result to anchor that story is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that, given exogenous private signals satisfying condition (20) and a strictly increasing aggregate preference function \(\Phi\), for every state-contingent policy \((x(\alpha),x(\beta))\), there are independent expansions of the voters’ signals and equilibria such that the probability of electing \(x(\omega)\), conditional on each state \(\omega\), converges to \(1\) as the electorate grows. This is a strong existence result about persuasion in a large society, and the population scale is central to it.

But Theorem 4 is not a computational result of the kind the ChoCo programme requires: it classifies no complexity and supplies no algorithm for computing an information structure or equilibrium. Theorem 2 and the propositions on commitment, detail-freeness, and equilibrium dynamics are likewise analytical results. I therefore cannot honestly offer a qualifying anchor or a continuous computational problem per anchor.

There is also a substantive obstacle to turning Theorem 4 into a static continuum-voter problem. Its persuasion mechanism relies on voters conditioning on being pivotal. In an atomless population, one voter’s action does not change the majority outcome, so that incentive and the associated pivotal inference disappear. Preserving them requires a finite-electorate limit, with information structures that may themselves vary with electorate size. That is a promising asymptotic persuasion question, but it is not yet a continuous-population computational mirror. The high-multiplicity setting is plausible; the missing computational anchor and the loss of pivotal incentives are the weakest points.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 4 is its strongest candidate, but it establishes asymptotic equilibrium existence: for each target policy, some sequence of signal structures and equilibria implements that policy as the electorate grows. Theorem 2 does the same for a sender with no exogenous voter information. Propositions 1–3 add existence and equilibrium-selection properties. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic task with a finite input encoding. That is the main weakness in the proposed case for a ChoCo mirror.

The best attempt to construct one would be: given a population distribution over preference and signal classes, find an information structure that implements a target policy, perhaps minimizing the number of signals or some measure of signal cost. The difficulty is that the paper’s persuasion mechanism depends on finite voters’ pivotal incentives. A voter’s action matters when the other votes tie, and the posterior after a signal includes the likelihood of that event:
\[ \Pr(\mathrm{piv}\mid \omega_j;\sigma,n) =\binom{2n}{n}q(\omega_j;\sigma)^n(1-q(\omega_j;\sigma))^n. \]
In an atomless population, an individual changes the vote share by zero. The pivotal incentive disappears, and conditioning on a tie does not restore it. Moreover, the constructions vary with \(n\), using rare signal events whose probabilities shrink with the electorate. Their force comes from comparing the resulting finite-\(n\) pivotal likelihoods. Those quantities do not become a mass-transfer problem in the continuum.

A finite-\(n\) optimization of signal design could be well posed and potentially interesting. Its input would include a finite electorate or its count distribution, and its computation would retain the pivotal likelihoods. A minimum-signal or minimum-cost version would add an objective the paper does not study. Both are reasonable new projects; neither is a computational question produced by replacing the population with a continuum. The paper’s existing formulation also takes \(G\) to be an arbitrary continuous density, so a complexity analysis would need a new finite encoding, such as rational masses on finitely many preference classes.

The proposed shareholder or legislative scenario makes repeated classes plausible, but it does not resolve the central obstacle: the continuum version loses the strategic incentive that drives Theorems 2 and 4. With a finite number of classes and positive mass per class, agents still have no individual influence on the outcome. Theorem 4, Theorem 2, and Propositions 1–3 therefore fail together as anchors for a static continuous-population computational problem. The media results rely on the same pivotal mechanism.

This is a strong negative case against these anchors, though it does not prove that no useful continuous model of persuasion can be built. A model that retains finite-electorate pivotality and studies signal-design complexity could be worthwhile. The honest objection is narrower: it would be a finite-population computational extension of this paper, while the paper’s defining mechanism gives no clear computational problem in an atomless population.
