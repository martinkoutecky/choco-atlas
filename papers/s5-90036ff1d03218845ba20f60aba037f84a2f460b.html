The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. It contains no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a problem is tractable, hard, or in a parameterized complexity class.

The nearest result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: for anonymous, strategy-proof, responsive voting rules with positive participation costs and the stated participation assumptions, every truthful Nash equilibrium of the simultaneous game and every truthful subgame-perfect equilibrium of the sequential game has exactly one participant. That participant has either the lowest or the highest peak. This is an equilibrium characterization, not a result about computational complexity. Theorem 2 likewise characterizes equilibria for the symmetric median rule; Corollaries 1 and 2 give further equilibrium and implementation impossibilities. None supplies the required computational anchor.

A tentative population model would give each type a peak \(p\) and cost \(c\), let \(\mu(p,c)\) be its population share, and let \(q(p,c)\in[0,1]\) be the participating share. The outcome would be determined by a continuum extension of the voting rule over participating mass, and the question would be which participation functions and outcomes form an equilibrium. But this has a serious mirror problem: the paper’s result turns on individual participation changing the outcome. In a nonatomic population, one person has zero mass and ordinarily cannot be pivotal, so positive participation costs and the paper’s “abstain when the outcome is unchanged” condition point toward universal abstention. Keeping pivotal voters as positive-mass blocs would preserve more of the game, but would weaken the case that the population itself has been continuized. The paper also explicitly frames its setting as small committees.

So I would not offer that tentative model as an anchored positive case under your criteria. The paper’s continuous outcome variable does not make its society continuous, and its named results do not provide the computational anchor needed for this programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s central point is the strongest negative ground: there is no computational result here to anchor a mirror to. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibria under assumptions on the voting rule; Theorem 2 characterizes equilibria for the symmetric median rule. Corollaries 1 and 2 are further equilibrium and implementation claims. None states a complexity result or frames an algorithmic problem. One could newly ask for an algorithm to compute equilibria, but that would not mirror a computational result of this paper.

The best candidate question would be: given a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types—peaks, costs, and any other relevant characteristics—what participation shares \(q_t\) form an equilibrium under a mass version of the voting rule? For independent, nonatomic agents, an individual’s participation or abstention changes no mass and therefore cannot change the outcome. A participant can save a strictly positive cost by abstaining; a nonparticipant cannot affect the outcome by joining and would incur that cost. Thus the only equilibrium participation profile is \(q_t=0\) for every type. The paper’s assumption that a person may benefit by participating alone has no counterpart: a single person is not a positive-mass participant in the limit. This same loss of pivotality removes the strategic basis of Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and both corollaries, including under the probabilistic median rule.

A stronger proposed mirror might let everyone of a given type choose together, so a type’s mass can be pivotal. That preserves a nontrivial game, but it makes each type a coordinated bloc. The paper’s agents decide individually; sharing a peak and cost does not make them a coalition. This version therefore changes the strategic unit and asks about a weighted-bloc game. Keeping individuals pivotal instead requires finite voter atoms or finite-size tie effects, which preserves the feature the continuum limit removes.

The negative case has a real limit: one cannot rule out every plausible bloc-based model, and such a model might motivate a separate computational project. But it would need new assumptions and a new computational question. For the paper’s individual participation problem, the natural population limit erases pivotality, while the proposed repairs change the model. That is the strongest case against spending this programme’s effort on a continuous mirror of its results.
