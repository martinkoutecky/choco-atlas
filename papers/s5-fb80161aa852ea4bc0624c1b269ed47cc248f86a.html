The case FOR (proponent)

I find no valid computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 proves a necessary condition on preference domains for unanimity and local strategy-proofness to imply dictatorship; Theorem 2 gives a sufficient condition under the additional L-tops-only assumption; and Theorem 3 proves a dictatorship result for global strategy-proofness. These are axiomatic results, not claims about complexity or algorithms. Proposition 3 is a cited reduction from Aswal et al. between voter counts, also not a computational result.

A continuous-population version of those dictatorship questions might be coherent mechanism-design research, but it would continuize the society to ask an axiomatic question. It therefore does not supply a mirror for ChoCo’s computational programme. Since there is no qualifying anchor, I can’t state a continuous problem per anchor as requested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I agree with the proponent that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 gives a necessary condition on domains; Theorem 2 gives a conditional sufficiency result; Theorem 3 concerns strategy-proofness. They classify when axioms force dictatorship. None asks for an algorithm or establishes a complexity bound. The proponent raised no result-specific anchors, so there is nothing to defeat one by one.

The stronger reason a continuous population would not help is that the paper’s central object is the preference domain \(D\), not a society distribution. Its conclusions depend on which rankings are allowed and which are adjacent. Assigning those rankings masses would add data the theorems do not use. One could invent complexity questions about recognizing domain properties or finding counterexample rules, but those would be new problems; making the population continuous would not give them their computational content.

The obvious population limit also strains the property being studied. Dictatorship means that one fixed voter determines the outcome. In an atomless society, an individual has measure zero, so a rule based only on the population distribution cannot preserve that individual’s decisive role. Retaining a distinguished voter as an atom preserves the dictatorship question by keeping that voter individualized; defining incentive constraints for positive-mass groups instead changes the axioms and asks a new question. That could be interesting mechanism-design work, but it is not a computational mirror of these results under ChoCo’s stated scope.
