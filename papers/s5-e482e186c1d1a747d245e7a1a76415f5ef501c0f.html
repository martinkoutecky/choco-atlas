What the mirror covers

No result is covered: Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 give axiomatic characterizations, while Theorems 2 and 3 concern mechanism characterization and revenue optimality.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the market setting. A large public-housing cohort, for example, could have many applicants but relatively few complete preference types: each type is a full preference over house categories and payments, and \(\mu_t\) is its share of applicants. A continuum model would need per-capita supplies \(q_a\) of each house category, with an allocation and payment distribution for each type. That preserves the paper’s unit demand, income effects, equal treatment, and no-wastage concerns. The setting is plausible, though the paper’s cited examples do not establish that applicants actually fall into few exact types.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that on the classical preference domain a mechanism is strategy-proof, individually rational, equal-treatment-of-equals, no-wastage, and no-subsidy if and only if it is a minimum Walrasian equilibrium price mechanism. That is a strong economic characterization, but it is not a computational result: it gives no algorithm, running time, or hardness classification for computing an allocation or price. Corollary 1 is another axiomatic equivalence. Theorem 2 is a characterization attributed to Holmström and Morimoto–Serizawa; Theorem 3, attributed to Kazumura et al., concerns revenue optimality. Neither supplies a computational anchor either.

So the honest count of eligible anchors is zero. One could pose a new continuum question about whether those five axioms uniquely characterize a minimum-price Walrasian rule with type masses and scaled supplies. That would be an analytical mechanism-design question, not the computational mirror ChoCo asks for. The positive case is that the paper’s underlying market is naturally population-based; its weakness is that none of its named results establishes a computational question to continue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes which mechanisms satisfy five axioms; Corollary 1 gives another axiomatic equivalence. Theorem 2 is an efficiency characterization, and Theorem 3 concerns revenue optimality. None states a computational problem or establishes its complexity. The proponent’s count of eligible computational anchors—zero—is right.

The housing-market scenario is credible evidence that a high-multiplicity model could make sense. With preference types and supplies scaled per capita, one could ask whether analogous axioms characterize a continuous-market rule. That would be an analytical mechanism-design question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. One could instead ask how to compute a minimum Walrasian equilibrium for a typed population, but that would require choosing a finite representation for the paper’s unrestricted classical preferences and defining the computational task. Those choices would create a new algorithmic problem; they would not continue a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against a mirror of this paper, though weaker as a claim that no worthwhile adjacent project exists. The market setting may support such a project. It cannot supply the missing computational anchor.
