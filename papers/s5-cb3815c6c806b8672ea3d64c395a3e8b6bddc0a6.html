The continuous mirror question

Given finite type and good-class sets \(T\) and \(G\), rational utilities \(u_{tg}\in\{a_t,b_t\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\ge0\) with \(\sum_gq_g=1\), compute \(p\) and \(z\) such that \(p_g\ge0\), \(\min_{g\in G}p_g=0\), \(z_{tg}\ge0\), \(\sum_g z_{tg}=1\), \(\sum_t\mu_tz_{tg}=q_g\), \(\sum_gp_gz_{tg}\le1\), and \(z_t\in\arg\max\{\sum_gu_{tg}y_g:\ y_g\ge0,\ \sum_gy_g=1,\ \sum_gp_gy_g\le1\}\) for every \(t\).

The model it lives in

A bivalued high-multiplicity HZ market with type masses \(\mu_t\), good-class supplies \(q_g\), per-type lottery variables \(z_{tg}\), prices \(p_g\), and the objective of computing any equilibrium rather than optimizing welfare.

The objection that survived

The weighted matching, vertex-cover, and price-raising algorithm for rational \(\mu_t\) and \(q_g\) is not actually derived from Theorem 15, and strong polynomiality may not survive the compressed representation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the bivalued equilibrium algorithm in Theorem 15 and also supports a broader type-compressed general HZ equilibrium problem based on Theorem 27; it leaves the irrational-equilibrium example and structural bundle lemmas as supporting material.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the matching, vertex-cover, and DPSV components of Theorem 15 be extended to rational \(\mu_t\) and \(q_g\) in time polynomial in \(\lvert T\rvert\), \(\lvert G\rvert\), and the encoding length?
  • Does the general type-compressed HZ equilibrium problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm, or is it FIXP-hard or continuum-specifically hard?
  • What exact finite-to-continuous correspondence holds when rational \(\mu\) and \(q\) are expanded into repeated agents and repeated good copies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity Hylland–Zeckhauser market, with the paper’s probabilistic allocation mechanism retained but the population represented by masses.

Take a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types and a finite set \(G\) of good classes. A type \(t\) includes its entire cardinal utility vector \(u_t=(u_{tg})_{g\in G}\), not merely a ranking. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of agents of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(q_g\) be the per-capita supply of good class \(g\), with \(\sum_gq_g=1\). The natural regime is a large course-allocation, housing, or school-seat market: perhaps millions of applicants, dozens of seat classes, and only \(\tau\) recurring utility types.

For a type \(t\), let \(z_{tg}\) be the probability share of good \(g\) assigned to one member of that type. Let \(p_g\) be the price of one unit of good \(g\). Aggregate allocation is \(x_{tg}=\mu_tz_{tg}\). A continuous HZ equilibrium consists of \(p,z\) satisfying \(p_g\ge0\), \(\min_g p_g=0\), \(\sum_gz_{tg}=1\) for every \(t\), \(\sum_t\mu_tz_{tg}=q_g\) for every \(g\), and \(\sum_gp_gz_{tg}\le1\). In addition, each \(z_t\) must solve the HZ individual problem \(z_t\in\arg\max\{\sum_gu_{tg}y_g:y_g\ge0,\ \sum_gy_g=1,\ \sum_gp_gy_g\le1\}\). The task is to compute any such equilibrium; there is no additional welfare objective.

My lead problem is Bivalued Continuous HZ Equilibrium. Its input is the above market, with the restriction that for every type \(t\), all utilities belong to \(\{a_t,b_t\}\), where \(0\le a_t<b_t\). Its solution is an equilibrium \(p,z\) satisfying exactly the conditions above.

This mirrors Theorem 15, proved in the paper: for the bivalued-utilities case of the HZ scheme, there is a strongly polynomial-time algorithm computing equilibrium allocations and prices. The theorem is not merely cited. The paper proves it by reducing each bivalued utility vector to a \(0/1\) vector using Lemma 14, then solving the unit case through matching, minimum vertex cover, and the simplified DPSV price-raising procedure.

The continuous version is plausibly Class A. The high-multiplicity market replaces the paper’s individual-agent matching graph by a capacitated type–good graph: \(\mu_t\) is type mass and \(q_g\) is good capacity. The paper’s matching and Fisher-market components should become weighted matching/flow and price-raising computations whose size depends on \((\tau,|G|)\), not on the number \(N\) of duplicated agents. With rational masses, the expected target is polynomial time in \((\tau,|G|,L)\), and perhaps strong polynomiality under an appropriate capacity representation.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is exact for rational data. Choose \(N\) clearing the denominators of \(\mu\) and \(q\); create \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_g\) interchangeable copies of good class \(g\). A type-level equilibrium is precisely the symmetric aggregate form of the resulting finite HZ market. Thus this is not a new welfare problem or a relaxation of HZ’s individual optimality constraints: it is HZ with repeated agents and repeated good copies compressed into masses.

A second, broader mirror is General Continuous HZ Equilibrium, defined by the same input and equilibrium conditions but with arbitrary rational utility vectors \(u_t\). This is anchored in Theorem 27, also proved in the paper: the HZ scheme belongs to FIXP. The paper constructs a polynomial-size straight-line program using \(+,-,\times,/,\min,\max\) over a compact domain of prices and allocations, and proves that every fixed point is an HZ equilibrium.

For the continuous version, the natural conjecture is that the same construction can be compressed to one allocation vector per type, with price updates using aggregate excess demand \(\sum_t\mu_tz_{tg}-q_g\). Its expected status is a Class C candidate: membership in FIXP should survive, while FIXP-hardness may persist even when \(\tau\) and \(|G|\) are small. The paper’s Theorem 18, though not itself a complexity anchor, supports this suspicion: it gives a four-agent, four-good instance with two disconnected equilibria and irrational prices and allocations. That suggests the difficulty lies in nonconvex equilibrium geometry, not merely in having many named agents.

The authors should recognise both questions as their problem. HZ was explicitly designed for large matching markets, and the proposed mirror changes precisely the population representation: a finite market with many repeated utility vectors becomes a distribution \(\mu\), while the price mechanism, budget constraint, size constraint, and market-clearing condition remain intact. The paper’s existing fractional lotteries are not the mirror by themselves; the new object is the continuous population and its compressed complexity measured in \(\tau\) rather than \(N\).

The weakest point is that Theorem 15 itself proves the unweighted, named-agent algorithm, not the weighted type-capacity version. Turning its matching and DPSV arguments into a genuinely polynomial algorithm for rational masses requires a real proof, and exact general HZ equilibria may require algebraic rather than rational output. Even so, the bivalued mirror is a credible tractability question, while the general mirror gives a natural boundary problem: determine whether population compression yields a polynomial algorithm or whether HZ remains hard for continuum-specific equilibrium reasons.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with a mismatch in the proposed first mirror. In the paper, the \(n\) goods are individually indexed, and a type must specify utilities for every one of them. If only the population is continuized, repeated agents still face \(n\) named goods; in a generic instance, their complete utility vectors remain distinct, so \(\tau\) grows with \(n\). The proposed replacement by good classes and capacities \(q_g\) is therefore not merely population continuization. It adds interchangeability on the goods side and turns the problem into a capacitated HZ market.

This does undercut the claim that Theorem 15 itself already establishes the proposed continuous result. Its proof uses unweighted matching, vertex covers, and price raising; none automatically proves the rational-capacity version. Moreover, strong polynomiality is no longer the natural claim when \(\mu_t\) and \(q_g\) are rational. At best, the proponent has identified a plausible weighted generalization whose algorithm still needs to be proved.

That objection is not enough, however. A large course, school-seat, or housing market with genuinely interchangeable copies is a perfectly natural HZ instance. Expanding rational masses into repeated agents and repeated goods gives an exact finite HZ market, while the compressed formulation is precisely its high-multiplicity representation. Capacitated matching and flow make the bivalued extension technically plausible. Thus the first anchor survives as a worthwhile question, even if the proponent overstated what Theorem 15 proves.

The same is true, more strongly, of Theorem 27. The paper’s fixed-point construction is almost tailor-made for type compression: retain one allocation vector \(z_t\) per utility type, use the same per-type best-response circuit, and replace the price excess-demand term by
\[ \sum_t \mu_t z_{tg}-q_g. \]
After removing zero-supply goods and choosing a polynomial-bit price bound, this gives a polynomial-size straight-line program. Expanding the population could be exponentially large, so this is not formally inherited by expansion, but the direct compressed construction is straightforward. It is a genuine computational question whether the resulting problem is tractable, FIXP-hard, or continuum-specifically hard.

Theorem 18 does not rescue the negative case. Its four-agent example can itself be represented by four types of mass \(1/4\), with each good having per-capita supply \(1/4\); the two disconnected irrational equilibria remain. That shows the difficult geometry is already present at constant type complexity, so it weakens the claim that population multiplicity is the source of HZ’s difficulty. But it does not make the continuous question ill-posed or already solved. It remains a valid boundary case for the programme.

So the negative case can show that the proponent has not proved a population-only analogue of Theorem 15, and that the general FIXP mirror may be more a compressed reformulation than a new mechanism. It cannot honestly show that no worthwhile mirror exists. The capacitated bivalued problem is a credible high-multiplicity question, and the type-compressed general equilibrium problem is an even clearer computational mirror. The universal “no scenario” verdict is therefore not defensible.
