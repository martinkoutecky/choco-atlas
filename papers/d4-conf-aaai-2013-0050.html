The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a rational background distribution \(b\) over rankings, strategic mass \(\delta>0\) with \(\sum_\rho b_\rho+\delta=1\), preferred candidate \(p\), and threshold \(q\), choose one common report \(\sigma\in\mathcal R(C)\) for the strategic mass. Is \(\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal R(C)}\Pr_{\nu^\sigma}[p\text{ wins under RV-Borda}]\ge q\), where \(\nu^\sigma_\rho=b_\rho+\delta\mathbf 1[\rho=\sigma]\) and a voter is sampled by mass before selecting their highest-ranked Borda co-winner?

The model it lives in

Types are strict rankings \(\rho\in\mathcal R(C)\), with fixed mass \(b_\rho\) and a distinguished strategic cohort of mass \(\delta\) choosing one report \(\sigma\). The decision objective is to meet threshold \(q\) for the probability that the mass-sampled ballot ranks \(p\) highest among the Borda co-winners.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s Borda manipulation result through homogeneous strategic blocs; it leaves the other voting rules, tie-breaking comparisons, and empirical results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does allowing the strategic cohort to split its mass among multiple reports change the complexity?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper: the non-deterministic manipulation problem for Borda with random-vote tie-breaking is NP-complete. The instance has \(n-1\) fixed votes, a preferred candidate \(p\), and a probability threshold \(t\); the question is whether one remaining vote can be cast so that \(p\) wins with probability at least \(t\). The paper proves this by a reduction from MINSAT.

A continuous counterpart is RV-Borda Bloc-Mass Manipulation. Let \(C\) be the candidates and \(\mathcal R(C)\) the strict rankings of \(C\). An instance gives a rational background mass distribution \(b\) over reported rankings, a rational strategic mass \(\delta>0\) with \(\sum_\rho b_\rho+\delta=1\), a preferred candidate \(p\), and a rational threshold \(q\). The strategic mass is a homogeneous cohort: its members share a sincere ranking with \(p\) first and commit to one common reported ranking \(\sigma\). The resulting society is \(\nu^\sigma_\rho=b_\rho+\delta\mathbf 1[\rho=\sigma]\). Its Borda score is \(\mathrm{sc}_{\nu^\sigma}(c)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R(C)}\nu^\sigma_\rho\bigl(m-\operatorname{pos}_\rho(c)\bigr)\), and its co-winner set \(W\) consists of the candidates with maximum score. A random vote means sampling a member by mass and electing that ballot’s highest-ranked candidate in \(W\). Thus \(p\)’s winning probability is \(\sum_{\rho:\operatorname{top}_\rho(W)=p}\nu^\sigma_\rho\). The question is whether some report \(\sigma\) makes this probability at least \(q\); a solution is such a ranking.

A plausible regime is a large party or membership election where many people share each of a relatively small set of complete rankings, and a coordinated caucus shares preferences and submits one common strategic ballot. The mass of each type is its fraction of the electorate. For any rational mass instance, a sufficiently large common denominator gives a finite realization with \(N\) agents and \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of supported rankings; the strategic cohort also grows with \(N\).

The hardness connection is exact. From a Theorem 9 instance with \(n\) voters, set \(\delta=1/n\) and let \(b_\rho\) equal the number of fixed votes of ranking \(\rho\), divided by \(n\). Every report has the same co-winners as in the discrete election, since all Borda scores are scaled by \(1/n\); the random-vote probability is the same fraction of votes. Replicating the whole profile gives arbitrarily many members per type while preserving the mass instance. The continuous problem is also in NP: a report ranking is a certificate, and its Borda scores and winning probability can be computed from the listed rational masses. I therefore expect this problem to be NP-complete, a Class B mirror: the reduction’s combinatorics concern alternatives and rankings, and the discrete problem embeds directly.

This case covers Theorem 9 only. It leaves open how the complexity changes if a cohort can split its mass among several reports, or under restrictions such as bounded \(\tau\), or if the task is to find the minimum strategic mass needed to reach a threshold.

The weakest point is the move from one manipulator to a coordinated cohort that shares a report. The paper proves Theorem 9 for one voter; the bloc version is a high-multiplicity extension, and its plausibility depends on whether the authors accept coordinated homogeneous voters as the relevant strategic unit. The exact embedding and the mass-based interpretation of random-vote tie-breaking are what make this extension a credible mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is Theorem 9, and the strongest objection is that its manipulator is one individual while the proposed mirror gives a positive mass of voters a coordinated common report. In a nonatomic society, one person has zero mass: changing their ballot cannot change Borda scores or the random-vote distribution. Preserving the manipulator’s influence therefore means modeling a bloc, or keeping a distinguished atom in a hybrid continuum-and-atom model. Neither is literally the paper’s single-voter problem.

That objection is a real modeling caveat, but it does not defeat the mirror. A coordinated caucus with a shared interest is plausible, and the mass-transfer definition preserves both the Borda winner condition and the probability induced by sampling a voter. For rational masses, the instance also has an exact finite realization; replicating that profile gives arbitrarily many voters of each type. The programme explicitly treats such high-multiplicity formulations as supporting evidence, not as a collision or a scope violation.

Letting the strategic bloc split its mass among different reports would be a further, recognizable coalition-manipulation question. The shift from one manipulator to coordinated mass is substantial, but it does not make the computational question ill-posed or obviously unworthy. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor: the negative case against the paper is weak, and the proposed mirror survives the strongest objection available.
