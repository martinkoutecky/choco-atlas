The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s many-worker risk-exponent design. The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result. Its useful anchor is an optimization result: Theorem 2, proved here by adapting Tsitsiklis [6]. It says that, under sub-Gaussian private observations, there is an optimal common belief \(q^\star\) for the fusion agent and all local agents. Using the paper’s likelihood-ratio decisions and belief update, that common-belief policy attains the optimal fusion risk exponent as \(N\to\infty\), even when compared with all decision rules considered there. The exponent is

\[ \beta^\star = \max_{q_1} C\!\left( \operatorname{Bern}\!\left(p_{\hat H_1\mid H}(1\mid 0;q_1)\right), \operatorname{Bern}\!\left(p_{\hat H_1\mid H}(1\mid 1;q_1)\right) \right). \]

So the theorem optimizes how informative the local agents’ binary decisions are about the shared hypothesis, as measured by Chernoff information. The paper’s Corollary 2, also proved here, gives the Gaussian specialization: for \(Y_i=H+Z_i\), \(Z_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), setting every belief to \(c_{MD}/(c_{FA}+c_{MD})\) asymptotically minimizes the fusion agent’s Bayes risk, with exponent \(C(\operatorname{Bern}(Q(0.5/\sigma)),\operatorname{Bern}(Q(-0.5/\sigma)))\), approximately \(0.0793\) when \(\sigma=1\).

Here is a continuous-population problem matching that result. Call it Continuous Annotator Risk-Exponent Design. An instance has a binary hypothesis with rational prior \(\pi_0\), positive rational error costs \(c_{FA},c_{MD}\), and a Gaussian signal model with rational \(\sigma^2>0\). A society of mass \(1\) consists of one worker type: its members share the same signal law, costs, and decision policy. Consider high-multiplicity realizations with \(n\) workers, each of mass \(1/n\), independently observing the same item conditional on its true label. The single fusion agent has its own private signal, an initial belief \(q_0\), and uses the paper’s likelihood-ratio rule, interpreting the workers as having belief \(q_0\). The society’s decision variable is the worker type’s common belief \(q\).

The problem is: choose \(q_0,q\in(0,1)\) to maximize

\[ \beta(q_0,q) = \liminf_{n\to\infty} -\frac{1}{n}\log R_n(q_0,q), \]

where \(R_n\) is the fusion agent’s true Bayes risk when workers use \(q\) and the fusion agent uses the paper’s rule with belief \(q_0\). A solution returns an optimal pair and the exponent. In this Gaussian case the paper’s result supplies the answer: \(q_0=q=c_{MD}/(c_{FA}+c_{MD})\), with the exponent above. I expect this restricted problem to be tractable; the result characterizes the optimum explicitly, though the paper does not state a bit-complexity bound for computing numerical approximations.

The scenario is a large labeling task in which many annotators judge the same binary item after comparable training. A type is a complete worker specification for the model: signal distribution, error costs, and shared policy. Mass is the fraction of annotators in that cohort. The regime of interest has many workers and very small type support; the lead instance has support size \(\tau=1\). The fusion agent remains a single hub, just as in the paper. This is a population mirror, rather than a continuization of the binary outcome or of uncertainty: the hypothesis and signal noise remain probabilistic as in the original model.

The match is unusually direct: the paper already studies the large-\(N\) regime for statistically identical local agents. That makes the high-multiplicity society sensible to the authors, though it also limits what this case can claim. The paper’s Theorem 2 and Corollary 2 give an answer for the symmetric asymptotic regime; they do not establish a complexity classification for general continuous societies.

The main further questions are whether the result extends to several worker types with rational masses, whether type-specific beliefs can improve the exponent, and how to compute the optimum for general sub-Gaussian signal laws. My weakest point is that the strongest mirror is essentially the paper’s existing asymptotic result expressed in population-mass language. It establishes a credible continuous-population problem and a solved tractable case, but it does not open a new hardness-versus-tractability boundary on its own.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is Theorem 2, with Corollary 2 as its Gaussian specialization. Neither states a computational complexity result: Theorem 2 characterizes a risk exponent by maximizing Chernoff information, and Corollary 2 gives the optimal belief and exponent for Gaussian noise. The proposed one-type “Continuous Annotator Risk-Exponent Design” asks for that same asymptotic optimization. Its society has mass \(1\), but the decision variable is the shared belief \(q\), not a change to the population distribution. In the Gaussian case, the paper already supplies the answer.

There is also a real limit issue behind that close match. If workers are replaced by exact masses of decisions, then conditional on \(H\), the mass voting \(1\) is just the corresponding decision probability. When those probabilities differ between hypotheses, the fusion agent can distinguish them perfectly, and the risk becomes zero. The nonzero exponent comes from rare fluctuations in the finite sample of \(n\) workers. Keeping those fluctuations through a large-deviation model preserves the question, but leads back to the Chernoff exponent that Theorem 2 already analyzes. The mass description alone loses the quantity the result studies.

The strongest improvement would be a society with several worker types—different signal laws, costs, or training histories—with rational masses and type-specific beliefs. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and could lead to a worthwhile new study. But it adds heterogeneity absent from the paper’s common-signal, common-cost model. To make it a computational problem, one would also need to specify finite encodings for signal laws and policies, and identify a coupled optimization task. The paper’s unrestricted sub-Gaussian assumption does not provide those encodings; its specified Gaussian case is already solved for the symmetric regime. Adding quotas or a budget might create a sharper algorithmic problem, but that would be a new model rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against the offered anchor: its continuous version is either the paper’s solved asymptotic result or a deterministic mass limit that erases the risk exponent. It is weaker against a carefully designed heterogeneous-cohort extension. I cannot honestly rule out that extension as worthwhile, but it would need to stand on its own rather than claim an unsolved continuous mirror of this paper.
