Why no mirror

Theorems 1 and 2 establish incentive properties of the geometric median; neither says that an object can be computed or that computing it is hard. The proposed infinitesimal factor is a statable follow-on computation, but it is not a computational result of this paper to mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed factor question draws on the isotropic manipulation analysis in Theorems 1 and 2, using a finite-support society and an infinitesimal strategic mass. It leaves out the collusion result, the skewed-preference and skewed-median results, and the experiment.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow, and there is a formal limitation: this paper has no named theorem asserting that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its named results concern incentive guarantees. I can make a plausible mirror of those guarantees as an algorithmic question, but I cannot present either theorem as a complexity result.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: under Assumption 1—a smooth, full-dimensional distribution of reported vectors in dimension \(d\ge 5\), with finite first moment—the geometric median is asymptotically \(\mathrm{SKEW}(H_\infty)\)-strategyproof, where \(H_\infty\) is the Hessian of the distribution’s average-distance loss at its geometric median. In the paper’s terms, for sufficiently many other voters, with high probability and uniformly over the strategic voter’s target and report, the truthful distance is at most \(1+\mathrm{SKEW}(H_\infty)+\varepsilon\) times the best achievable distance. Theorem 1, also proved here, supplies a useful boundary: even when the other voters’ vectors span at least two dimensions, there is no finite \(\alpha\) that makes the geometric median \(\alpha\)-strategyproof for every profile. Its proof uses four repeated vector types whose geometry makes the attainable set increasingly skewed.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large platform collecting users’ ideal policy vectors for content moderation or recommendation. A type is an ideal vector together with the same distance-based preferences and reporting model. Millions of users may fall into a comparatively small catalogue of policy archetypes, with each type’s mass its population share. That preserves the paper’s underlying question: how much can strategic reporting move a geometric-median decision away from a user’s target? It also makes the population distribution—not fractional outcomes—the continuous object.

My lead mirror is the Infinitesimal Geometric-Median Manipulation Factor. An instance is a finite-support society \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\delta_{x_i}\), with rational masses and rational vectors in \(\mathbb Q^d\). Assume its geometric median \(g\) is unique, lies outside the support, and has positive-definite Hessian
\[ H_\mu=\nabla^2\!\left(\int \|z-x\|_2\,d\mu(x)\right)\bigg|_{z=g} =\sum_i\mu_i\frac{I-u_i u_i^\top}{\|g-x_i\|_2}, \qquad u_i=\frac{g-x_i}{\|g-x_i\|_2}. \]
The question is to compute the largest limiting multiplicative gain for a strategic mass \(\eta\) as \(\eta\to0\), taking its target to be \(g+\eta a\). Truthful and manipulated societies are respectively \((1-\eta)\mu+\eta\delta_{g+\eta a}\) and \((1-\eta)\mu+\eta\delta_s\); the action is the reported vector \(s\), and the objective is distance from the resulting geometric median to the target. The output is the supremum, over targets \(a\) for which truthful loss is positive, of truthful loss divided by the minimum loss attainable by a report.

In the first-order model, the attainable scaled outputs form \(E_H=\{y:\|H_\mu y\|_2\le1\}\), while the truthful scaled output \(y_H(a)\) minimizes \(\tfrac12 y^\top H_\mu y+\|y-a\|_2\). Thus the factor is
\[ \Gamma(\mu)=\sup_{a\notin E_H} \frac{\|a-y_H(a)\|_2}{\operatorname{dist}(a,E_H)}. \]
The tangent geometry gives \(\Gamma(\mu)=1+\mathrm{SKEW}(H_\mu)\), expressible through the extreme eigenvalues as
\[ \Gamma(\mu)= \frac{\lambda_{\max}(H_\mu)+\lambda_{\min}(H_\mu)} {2\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(H_\mu)\lambda_{\min}(H_\mu)}}. \]
So I expect this mirror to be tractable, at least as an approximation problem under natural conditioning promises: compute the geometric median and its local curvature, then the extreme eigenvalues. A careful bit-complexity result for arbitrary rational inputs would need to account for near-singular curvature and support points close to the median. Theorem 2 motivates the distribution-specific bound; this finite-support computation and its complexity are the proposed mirror question.

Theorem 1’s four-point family also explains why the universal version has no finite bound. Put mass \(1/4\) on each of \((-X,-1),(-X,1),(X,-1),(X,1)\). Its median is \(0\), and its Hessian is proportional to \(\operatorname{diag}(1,X^2)\). As \(X\) grows, the local manipulation factor grows without bound. This is a useful boundary for the mirror, though the paper’s finite-profile theorem is not itself a complexity classification.

The weakest point is the move to an infinitesimal strategic mass. A single voter has vanishing mass in a continuum, so the mirror rescales targets toward the population median to retain the incentive comparison. That local question is closely tied to the paper’s own asymptotic analysis, but an author or referee could reasonably regard it as a new sensitivity problem rather than the continuous form of individual manipulation. The paper already studies a distributional limit analytically; the affirmative case therefore rests on the added computational question over a rational, type-compressed society. This scope covers the isotropic geometric-median incentive results in Theorems 1 and 2, and does not claim a mirror for the paper’s Byzantine-resilience result or its skewed-preference extensions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest objection is the programme’s first gate: there is no computational result here to classify. Theorem 1 rules out a uniform incentive guarantee; Theorem 2 proves an asymptotic incentive bound; Theorem 3 extends that analysis. None gives the complexity of computing a best report, deciding whether a profitable manipulation exists, or solving another algorithmic problem. The paper’s mention of algorithms for computing geometric medians is background, not a result of this paper. A computation inspired by these theorems could be worthwhile, but it would be a new algorithmic question rather than a mirror of a computational result.

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, but the proposed factor \(\Gamma(\mu)\) changes what is being computed. The paper already studies an i.i.d. distribution and its population limit analytically. The proposed finite-support society generally does not meet Theorem 2’s smooth-density assumption, so its Hessian formula and the claimed equality \(\Gamma(\mu)=1+\mathrm{SKEW}(H_\mu)\) need a separate justification; the theorem itself gives an asymptotic upper bound, not that tightness result. Even granting the derivation, the proposed task is to compute local curvature at the geometric median and then its extreme eigenvalues. With suitable conditioning promises, that looks like standard geometric-median optimization followed by spectral computation. It may be useful for evaluating the bound, but it does not expose a computational bottleneck in the paper’s strategyproofness problem.

Theorem 1’s four-point construction is the best evidence for a high-multiplicity setting: it repeats four vector types, so it would be wrong to dismiss it on grounds that multiplicity is implausible. Its difficulty is the disappearing manipulator. As the population grows, one voter has mass \(1/V\). For a fixed target \(t\ne g\), where \(g\) is the population geometric median, truthful and manipulated outcomes both approach \(g\), so their distance ratio tends to \(1\). At \(t=g\), truthful loss is zero. The nontrivial gain in the construction instead uses a target \(t_V\) approaching \(g\) at order \(1/V\). For a smooth distribution of user targets in dimension \(d\), the expected number of voters within that distance is on the order of \(V^{1-d}\), which vanishes for \(d\ge2\). The proposed infinitesimal factor preserves the worst case by scaling the target with the vanishing mass; it is therefore a local sensitivity question about a specially tuned target, not manipulation by a persistent type in the population.

A positive-mass coalition with a shared target avoids that degeneration and could motivate an algorithmic problem. But it changes the unit strategic voter in Theorems 1 and 2 into a coordinated group with a specified collective objective. Proposition 1’s collusion result concerns robustness to displacement, not the group’s incentive to improve a shared utility. That coalition question might be worth studying on its own; it does not make the case-for’s two anchors computational mirrors of these theorems. The negative case is weakest precisely there: this paper’s discussion of collusion gives such a new problem some footing, so I would not claim that no worthwhile follow-on problem can be invented.
