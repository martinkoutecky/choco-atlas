The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper’s mechanism has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large membership organization funding a repeated public project, with members falling into a small number of valuation and endowment types. A type’s mass would be its share of the membership. Members of each type could choose conditional contribution messages, and the mechanism could compare contributions and thresholds on a per-capita scale. That preserves the paper’s central strategic question: whether conditional contributions can support efficient provision.

But the paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. Its closest result is Theorem 1.7, proved here: it characterizes the outcomes of recurrent classes of the non-binary Conditional Contribution Mechanism under UBRD as exactly the Pareto-optimal allocations that are strict* Pareto improvements over zero contribution. That is a game-theoretic characterization, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. Theorem 1.5 gives a similar characterization for the binary mechanism; Theorem 1.12 characterizes recurrent-class outcomes for non-linear valuations under an existence assumption. None supplies a qualifying computational anchor.

A possible follow-on question would be to define a continuum version of the repeated mechanism and ask which per-capita contribution allocations can arise as stable outcomes for a rational distribution over valuation types. I would not present that as a mirror of Theorem 1.7 under this task’s rules: the paper does not define continuum UBRD or compute with such a model.

The main weakness is substantial. The paper studies small finite groups, including experimental groups of five. In an atomless population, a single member’s message has no effect on aggregate contributions, which may remove the pivotal incentives that make conditional contributions work. So the setting suggests a plausible research direction, but the paper offers no named computational result to anchor the required continuous problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I’d grant the proponent’s large-membership example. Members could plausibly fall into a small number of valuation types, so the five-person experiments are no reason to reject a high-multiplicity regime. The difficulty is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1.7 characterizes recurrent-class outcomes under UBRD; Theorem 1.5 does the same for the binary mechanism; Theorem 1.12 gives a core characterization under an existence condition. These are results about strategic stability, not complexity or algorithms. The experimental findings are empirical. The mechanism’s outcome for a submitted profile is already obtained by finding a maximal feasible contribution level. One could invent a problem about computing stable outcomes, but the paper neither poses that problem nor identifies a computational obstacle in it.

The strongest candidate is a continuum version of Theorem 1.7. Its central difficulty is that individual pivotality disappears in an atomless population. With contributions and public-good returns defined per capita, one member has zero mass and changing their message leaves aggregate provision unchanged. If they currently contribute a positive amount, switching to a message that always sets their contribution to zero preserves the aggregate while saving their private cost. That is a strict better response, and the zero-contribution message is unexploitable. Thus positive contributors leave under the natural individual-level extension of UBRD. The finite-population mechanism can stabilize contributions because an individual’s message may affect the feasible outcome; the atomless version loses that incentive.

Scaling public-good returns with population size does not restore an individual’s mass in the continuum. Treating an entire valuation type as the deviating unit would restore an aggregate effect, but it makes a type cohort a coordinated coalition. That changes the strategic unit and the dynamics, rather than carrying the paper’s UBRD into a continuous society. Theorem 1.5 has the same pivotality problem in the binary case.

Theorem 1.12 offers a more plausible route because it invokes the core, where coalitions are already central. One could ask whether a distribution of valuation types admits a core allocation and how to find it. But a continuum coalition that changes positive mass is not an individual UBRD deviation; it supplies a new dynamic model. With finitely many types, a natural formulation would use type-mass variables, and the paper gives no sign of the pricing or complexity barrier that would make this a ChoCo problem. Theorem 1.13’s existence result for arbitrarily cheap incentives likewise supplies no algorithmic question.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror: its named results are noncomputational, and the most direct continuum version of its main dynamic result loses the incentive that sustains provision. The universal claim is less secure. A new computational study of continuum core allocations could be worthwhile, but it would be a follow-on problem constructed from the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper actually contains.
