The paper’s Theorems 1 and 2 are its strongest possible anchors, but both characterize correspondences by axioms. A large population of residents with single-peaked location preferences is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, so I would not reject the mirror on multiplicity grounds. The obstacle is what happens to the theorems. For Theorem 1, one could ask how strategy-proofness and peaks-onliness characterize rules over a distribution of peaks. For Theorem 2, one could ask the analogous question with voter-sovereignty and efficiency. Those are still axiomatic characterization questions. A continuous limit might lead to quantile or phantom-voter descriptions, but that would not put the paper’s results into ChoCo’s computational programme.

There is also a real modeling problem with incentives in a nonatomic society. If a rule takes only the population distribution as input, one person’s report changes that distribution by zero, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Treating a positive-mass group as the deviator could restore a consequential change, but that is a group-incentive question; the paper studies individual strategy-proofness, and Example 2 shows its correspondences need not be group strategy-proof. Adding a costed mass-transfer objective could produce a computational problem, but the paper supplies no such objective. That would be a new problem inspired by its rules, not a computational mirror of either theorem.

The continuous interval of alternatives does not itself amount to a continuous society, and I would not count it as a collision. But replacing the finite electorate with a continuum would move the paper’s axiomatic questions onto a new population model; it would not reveal a computational question the paper already studies. So the strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is narrow but firm: plausible scenarios exist, yet neither named theorem anchors a computational mirror without adding a different problem.
