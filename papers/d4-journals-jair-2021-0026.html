The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper: it gives no hardness classification, running-time bound, or algorithm for a decision problem. The closest honest anchor is its structural optimization result, so this is a positive case for a *continuous extremal-optimization mirror*, not a strong ChoCo complexity case.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\beta\in[0,1]\), the maximum distortion of the expected winner is attained by an election whose voters occupy just two locations, \(x_b\in B\) and \(x_d\in D\). The authors use that reduction to optimize the distortion in Section 3.1. This is a useful structural result about the underlying distortion problem, but it does not establish an algorithmic complexity bound.

A precise mirror is Continuous Expected-Winner Distortion. Its input is a rational participation parameter \(\beta\in[0,1]\) and a rational accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\). A type is a voter’s location \(x\) on the line, with candidates at \(0\) and \(1\); a society is a probability measure \(\mu\) over locations with finite first moment. Its mass at \(x\) is the fraction of the population there. A type’s participation probability is \(p_\beta(x)=\left(\frac{\bigl||x|-|x-1|\bigr|}{|x|+|x-1|}\right)^\beta\), with the paper’s convention that \(p_0(x)=1\). Expected vote masses are the integrals of these participation probabilities over voters preferring each candidate, and social costs are \(C_\ell=\int |x|\,d\mu(x)\) and \(C_r=\int |x-1|\,d\mu(x)\). The task is to approximate the supremum, over such societies, of the distortion of an expected winner. A solution gives an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate bound on that supremum and an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal society.

The two-location form is the key: the paper’s theorem suggests that a prover should be able to use a society \(\mu=q\delta_{x_b}+(1-q)\delta_{x_d}\), with \(x_b\in B\) and \(x_d\in D\). For example, with \(\ell\) the expected winner and \(r\) socially optimal, the relevant constraints are \(q p_\beta(x_b)\ge(1-q)p_\beta(x_d)\) and \(C_r\le C_\ell\); the objective is \(C_\ell/C_r\). This is a population mirror rather than a continuum of outcomes: the mass shares replace voter counts, while location still determines preference, participation, and cost.

The regime is a large electorate concentrated in two geographically or ideologically uniform cohorts. Each cohort may contain many voters, while the number of distinct types is two. That is plausible for a stylized election with two concentrated voter groups, and the complete type is location because the model uses it to determine both distances and turnout probability. Replacing sums by integrals is natural for the paper’s expected-vote and social-cost calculations. The authors would likely recognize this as the population analogue of their expected-winner distortion question.

I would expect the resulting optimization to be tractable in the continuous-optimization sense: Theorem 3.1 compresses the search to a few real variables. But the paper’s reduced program is nonconvex, and it gives no polynomial-time algorithm or bit-complexity result. So the Class A prediction is a conjecture for the mirror, not a result of the paper. A useful follow-up is to determine whether the two-location reduction extends to arbitrary probability measures and then give a certified approximation algorithm for rational \(\beta\).

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s expected-winner distortion result, not the paper’s expected distortion over random election outcomes: in a continuum, individual turnout randomness washes out, so that latter question needs a finite-population or convergence formulation.

The weak point is substantial: the paper already optimizes over voter locations, and its displayed two-group optimization treats group shares as real-valued—for \(\beta=1\), its stated optimizer uses share \(1/(2+\sqrt{2})\). If that is intended as the exact model rather than an asymptotic calculation, the proposed mirror may largely restate the paper’s existing optimization. That limits the novelty and computational fit, even though the two-cohort population interpretation itself is sensible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t see an airtight case against every possible mirror. Two large groups of voters at fixed locations are a sensible high-multiplicity regime: location determines the preferences, participation probability, and costs the paper uses. That is not a modelling objection.

The stronger objection is that the anchor, Theorem 3.1, is a structural distortion result, not a computational result. It reduces the worst case to two voter locations. Section 3.1 then optimizes over those locations and the groups’ relative sizes, even giving a real-valued optimizer for \(\beta=1\). A continuous society supported on two locations replaces the groups’ counts with masses and reproduces essentially that same low-dimensional optimization. The paper’s finite-election formulation and the continuous mass formulation are distinct, but the proposed mirror does not expose a new complexity landscape: its global input is just \(\beta\), while its natural computation is already the paper’s optimization.

A stronger mirror might take a finite-support society as input and ask for its expected-winner distortion. That is a sensible population model, but the task reduces to summing each type’s mass times its participation rate and social cost, then comparing the candidates. If the task instead asks for the probability of the actual winner, the infinite-population limit removes the individual turnout randomness whenever expected vote masses differ. Retaining that randomness requires a finite population scale; it gives a plausible high-multiplicity computation, but its substance is finite-size turnout fluctuations. The paper already studies their concentration in Theorem 4.1.

So the negative case is strongest on fit with ChoCo’s computational programme, not on whether a continuous society is sensible. Theorem 3.1 has a clean mass interpretation, and proving the finite-to-continuous identity could be mathematically worthwhile. The case against is that this paper supplies no named computational problem for that mirror to classify, and its natural extremal version is already expressed through real-valued group masses and a two-location reduction. A computational study of finite-size turnout probabilities remains the main plausible adjacent direction; I would not claim that it is impossible, only that it is not a compelling new continuous mirror of this paper’s result.
