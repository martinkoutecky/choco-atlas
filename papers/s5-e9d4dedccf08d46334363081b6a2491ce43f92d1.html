The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) candidates and a target \(q\), decide whether there is a finite labeled binary voting tree \(\Theta\) such that, for every rational distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \([n]\) with nonzero pairwise margins, \(d^+_{T_\mu}(\Theta(T_\mu)) \ge q\), and construct such a tree when one exists.

The model it lives in

Types are strict rankings of \([n]\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\); each society induces a tournament by pairwise majority. The decision variable is a finite voting tree \(\Theta\), and the objective is to maximize its minimum winner out-degree over all such societies.

The objection that survived

The tree depends only on pairwise-majority signs, so support size and margin information play no role; this leaves open whether the authors would view the formulation as an electorate-level problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s worst-case Copeland guarantee for voting trees. One caveat: the paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar. The closest anchor is a proved construction result, rather than a complexity classification.

Take a society whose types are strict rankings of the candidates \([n]\), and whose rational mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with ranking \(t\). For each pair \(i,j\), put \(i\to j\) when more than half the mass ranks \(i\) above \(j\); assume no pair is tied. This gives a tournament \(T_\mu\). A plausible regime is a very large electorate with relatively few distinct rankings: for fixed \(n\), there are at most \(n!\) types, and McGarvey’s theorem, as noted in the paper, lets polynomial-size profiles realize every tournament.

Here is a precise continuous problem: Continuous Mass Copeland Tree Design. Given \(n\) and a target \(q\), find a finite voting tree \(\Theta\), with leaves labeled by candidates and repetitions allowed, such that for every rational society \(\mu\) with strict pairwise majorities,
\[ d^+_{T_\mu}\bigl(\Theta(T_\mu)\bigr)\ge q. \]
Equivalently, optimize the minimum Copeland score of the tree’s winner over all such societies. The decision variable is the tree; the objective is the winner’s out-degree in the society’s majority tournament.

The anchor is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper: for \(n\) candidates there exist voting trees \(\Omega_n\) whose minimum winner out-degree over all \(n\)-vertex tournaments is at least \((\sqrt{2}+o(1))\sqrt n\). In the mass version, this says that one tree guarantees that score for every continuous society. This is an exact translation: every society induces a tournament, and every tournament can be induced by a rational voting profile. The authors should recognize the mirror because their tree takes precisely that pairwise-election aggregate as input.

I expect the design problem to sit on the hardness-transfer side of the programme’s trichotomy: changing the representation to masses does not remove any tournament input, so any hardness of the corresponding tournament-design problem would carry over. Theorem 1.1 itself supplies a constructive guarantee, but it does not establish a complexity classification for the broader optimization problem. Natural follow-ups are whether a support bound \(\tau\) improves the guarantee, and what the optimal guarantee and smallest tree size are.

The weak point is substantial: the tree sees only whether each pairwise mass crosses \(1/2\). Once \(T_\mu\) is formed, the magnitudes of the masses disappear. So this is a credible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ problem, but it does not create a new LP or a new complexity landscape; a referee might regard it as an exact population realization of the tournament problem. That makes it a modest positive case, not evidence that continuization helps this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.1 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but its mass version has no computation over a society in it. For rankings \(t\), define the pairwise margin \(D_{ij}(\mu)=\sum_t\mu_t(\mathbf 1[i\succ_t j]-\mathbf 1[j\succ_t i])\). The proposed guarantee depends on \(\mu\) only through the signs of these margins: once they form tournament \(T_\mu\), the tree sees only \(T_\mu\). The objective is constant within each region of the mass simplex having the same margin signs. And, by McGarvey’s theorem, every tournament is induced by some rational society. Thus “maximize the worst-case guarantee over societies” pulls Theorem 1.1 back through a map onto its original input domain. No mass, voter count, or support parameter enters the tree-design problem.

That is a structural mismatch, not an objection that the continuous answer happens to be the same. The paper studies functions on tournaments; it does not study an electorate, manipulate its type masses, or classify the complexity of a population-level task. Its named results are constructions and guarantees for voting trees, rather than complexity classifications. Calling the universal tournament guarantee a continuous-society question supplies a possible origin for the input, but the society is discarded before the computational question begins.

The strongest repair would restrict the adversary to societies supported on at most \(\tau\) rankings, then ask what guarantee a tree can achieve. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but it changes the problem: the adversary now ranges over a restricted set of majority tournaments, rather than the tournaments in Theorem 1.1. A margin-sensitive mass-perturbation question could also be interesting, but it would be a new control or robustness problem, not a mirror of this theorem. Neither repair makes the paper’s original tree-design result computationally depend on a continuous population.

So I would reject the proposed unrestricted mirror as a worthwhile continuization: it adds societal notation without adding a population-level computational question. The negative case is weaker against the broader claim that *no* worthwhile mirror exists. A support-bounded profile variant is plausible, and I cannot rule out that the authors would recognize it as a useful new regime.
