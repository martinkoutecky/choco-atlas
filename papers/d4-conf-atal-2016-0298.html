The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), complete ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), satisfaction function \(\gamma\), an initial committee \(W_1\), and canonical deterministic tie-breaking, repeatedly compute a maximum-satisfaction assignment \(x_{t,c}\), replace each representative by a candidate maximizing satisfaction over its assigned mass, and recompute the assignment; determine the first terminal pair \((W,x)\) and the complexity of doing so in the mass bit model.

The model it lives in

The decision variables \(x_{t,c}\) assign mass of type \(t\) to candidate \(c\), maximizing \(\Gamma_\mu(x)=\sum_{t,c}x_{t,c}\gamma(\operatorname{rank}_t(c))\) for a fixed committee; Chamberlin–Courant permits arbitrary representative masses, while Monroe imposes \(\sum_t x_{t,c}=1/k\) for each selected \(c\). The continuous analogue runs the paper's local representative-update process on these mass assignments.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a one-anchor case centred on the paper’s clustering algorithm.

The anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: “If the satisfaction function used by \(R\) is polynomially bounded, then the clustering procedure runs in polynomial time,” where \(R\) is either Chamberlin–Courant or Monroe. The procedure starts from a committee, computes an optimal assignment of voters to that committee, repeatedly replaces each representative by the candidate giving the highest satisfaction to its current cluster, recomputes the optimal assignment, and stops at a fixed point. The paper’s NP-hardness statements are unnumbered background claims citing earlier work, so I would not present them as additional anchors.

The continuous mirror I would propose is Mass-Lloyd-\(R\) Representation, with this as the lead mirror.

An instance consists of candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a finite set \(T\) of complete ranking types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a non-increasing satisfaction function \(\gamma\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with ranking \(t\). The decision variable is \(x_{t,c}\), the mass of type \(t\) represented by candidate \(c\).

For Chamberlin–Courant, a feasible assignment satisfies \(\sum_{c\in W}x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), where \(W\subseteq C\) and \(|W|=k\). For Monroe, the continuous balanced condition is \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,c}=1/k\) for every \(c\in W\). This is the natural continuum counterpart of the paper’s requirement that every selected representative serve either \(\lfloor n/k\rfloor\) or \(\lceil n/k\rceil\) voters.

The score is \(S(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{c\in W}x_{t,c}\gamma(\operatorname{rank}_t(c))\). Starting from a specified committee \(W_1\), compute an optimal fixed-committee assignment \(x^1\). For every \(c\in W_i\), form its mass cluster \(x^i_{\cdot,c}\) and choose a candidate \(a\) maximizing \(\sum_t x^i_{t,c}\gamma(\operatorname{rank}_t(a))\). The resulting candidates form \(W_{i+1}\), after which an optimal assignment \(x^{i+1}\) is computed. Fixed deterministic tie-breaking replaces the paper’s random choices. The required output is the first terminal pair \((W,x)\) reached by this process: \(x\) is optimal for \(W\), and every representative in \(W\) is locally optimal for the mass assigned to it.

This is genuinely a population continuization. Candidates and committees remain integral, and every unit of population receives one integral representative. Only the bookkeeping of many indistinguishable agents becomes fractional: \(x_{t,c}\) records how a large type population is divided among representatives. A rational finite election with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) is recovered by scaling, up to the usual \(O(1/n)\) rounding issue for Monroe balance.

The regime is particularly plausible in the paper’s own product-selection interpretation. Imagine millions of customers, perhaps \(n\) in the millions, choosing among \(m\) products, with \(k\) products to be offered. Customers are grouped into perhaps \(10^2\)–\(10^3\) behavioural or demographic segments, each segment having a common ranking over products. For Chamberlin–Courant, a product may serve arbitrary mass; for Monroe, each selected product serves approximately \(1/k\) of the market. This is a stronger high-multiplicity setting than the paper’s experiments with \(n=100\), but it is exactly the sort of business-planning application the authors present.

I would expect the fixed-committee part to be Class A. For Chamberlin–Courant it decomposes by type, and for Monroe it is a transportation or flow problem. The full terminal-state problem is more interesting: my prior is that it may be continuum-specifically hard, although this is a conjectural direction rather than a result of the paper.

The reason is that Proposition 1’s finite proof relies on an integer-valued potential whose range is bounded by the explicit number of voters and the polynomially bounded satisfaction scores. With binary-encoded rational masses, score differences can be exponentially small in the input length, and there can be exponentially many distinct committees. Thus the paper’s polynomial-time argument does not automatically survive high-multiplicity compression. Proving a polynomial acceleration would give a Class A result; proving exponentially long trajectories or PLS-type hardness would give a natural Class C boundary.

The main further questions are whether the terminal state can nevertheless be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length; whether the continuous process admits an efficient approximate fixed point; and whether its output has a provable approximation guarantee for the globally optimal continuous Chamberlin–Courant or Monroe score.

My weakest point is that Proposition 1 is a relatively small algorithmic result, not the paper’s central winner-determination claim, and the exact continuous runtime question is not already answered by the proposition. If the referee insists that a mirror must target exact winner determination, this case is weaker. But as a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own clustering procedure, it is precise, recognisable to the authors, naturally motivated by their applications, and capable of exposing a genuinely new computational phenomenon.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed anchor does not carry the paper’s polynomial-time result into the standard continuous bit model.

Proposition 1 is genuinely numbered and computational, so the objection cannot simply be that the paper has no qualifying result. But its proof relies on an integer-valued potential. With \(n\) explicitly listed voters, every strict improvement raises \(\Gamma\) by at least \(1\), and \(\Gamma\le n\max_i\gamma(i)\). Hence the number of iterations is polynomial in the numeric population size.

For rational masses, the potential is instead

\[ S(x)=\sum_{t,c}x_{t,c}\gamma(\operatorname{rank}_t(c)). \]

Two successive values may differ by \(2^{-L}\), where \(L\) is the mass encoding length. The same argument therefore gives only a bound polynomial in the common denominator, potentially exponential in the input length. Thus Proposition 1 does not establish polynomial-time termination for Mass-Lloyd-\(R\). If one measures complexity in the denominator, the algorithm is merely the finite algorithm run on an election expanded to that many voters; the supposed continuous gain has disappeared.

This is a real weakness in the proposed anchor, but it does not defeat the mirror. It converts the question into a legitimate high-multiplicity or possibly continuum-specific local-search problem: is a terminal state computable efficiently from compressed masses, or only by a pseudo-polynomial trajectory? That is precisely the sort of boundary question the programme is meant to expose. The fact that the paper does not answer it is not a reason the question is unworthy.

The proponent’s formulation also needs more tie-breaking than stated. Optimal assignments for a fixed committee need not be unique, so the mass clusters \(x_{\cdot,c}\), and consequently the next committee, may depend on which optimal flow is returned. A canonical flow rule would repair this, just as deterministic candidate tie-breaking does. This is a modelling gap, not a fundamental obstruction.

A stronger negative attempt would replace the heuristic by exact continuous winner determination. For Chamberlin–Courant, the objective becomes

\[ \max_{|W|=k}\sum_t \mu_t\max_{c\in W} \gamma(\operatorname{rank}_t(c)), \]

and for Monroe the fixed-committee assignment is a capacitated transportation problem. One could argue that this is only a weighted representation problem: the masses are sufficient statistics, the fixed-committee subproblem already decomposes or reduces to flow, and the remaining combinatorics is the choice of \(k\) candidates. But that is not a valid objection under the programme’s rules. It is still a genuine high-multiplicity population model, and the paper’s product-selection application gives a convincing regime with millions of customers and relatively few preference segments.

Nor do identity, multiplicity, or degeneracy provide an escape. The objective is additive over voters, so individual identities are irrelevant; large customer or electorate segments make type mass natural; and Monroe’s \(1/k\) quotas have an obvious per-capita interpretation. Existing high-multiplicity work would support rather than undermine this case.

So the best negative conclusion is narrow: Proposition 1 should not be presented as already yielding a polynomial continuous algorithm, and the exact terminal-state problem may be a poor mirror if the intended output is only the paper’s heuristic trajectory. But the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists cannot honestly be sustained. The anchor survives as a well-posed high-multiplicity question, with a plausible possibility of a genuinely new bit-complexity or Class C phenomenon.
