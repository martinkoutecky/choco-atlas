The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s strict school ranking, per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and original and adjusted priority orders over types, compute a feasible mass assignment \(x_{ts}\geq 0\), including the outside option, with \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\). Require individual rationality, non-wastefulness, and no positive-mass blocking pair under the intersection of the two priority profiles. Call a type improved if some type it outranks under the original profile outranks it under the adjusted profile. The output should be weakly stable and, for every group \(G\) containing all improved types, admit no other weakly stable assignment that Pareto improves \(G\), where improvement is witnessed by typewise couplings of old and new assignments that make every matched unit weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off.

The model it lives in

An atomless population is given by finitely many types and rational masses; each type shares a school ranking and priority category. Types may split across schools, but each unit of mass receives one school or the outside option. Priorities are orders over types, with the two profiles intersected as in the paper; group welfare comparisons use typewise couplings to express individual Pareto improvement.

The objection that survived

The paper says strict priorities are essential to its characterization and warns that its main results may fail with ties; no argument here establishes group-optimality for the proposed mass assignments.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s group-optimality guarantee for the EADA outcome. It does not claim to mirror the other numbered results or the weighted-average priority application.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can such a group-optimal weakly stable mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length?
  • Does the mass assignment arise as a limit of finite cohorts with repeated types, and can each assignment be implemented by measurable subsets of each type?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for any group \(I'\) containing every improved student, the EADA outcome
\[ EA\bigl((\succ^O\cap\succ^A),\succ^A\bigr) \]
is \(I'\)-optimally weakly stable: it is individually rational, non-wasteful, and fair under the intersection of the original and adjusted priorities, and no other matching with those properties Pareto improves the outcome for \(I'\). The theorem gives a guarantee for the output of a named algorithm, though the paper does not classify the problem by computational complexity or give a running-time bound. So this is an algorithmic-outcome anchor, not a P-versus-NP result.

A credible high-multiplicity setting is centralized public-school admissions with large cohorts of applicants who share a school ranking and a priority category. For example, many applicants may have the same application pattern and the same category under both the original score policy and an adjusted affirmative-action policy. The type is that shared preference-and-priority profile; its mass is the fraction of applicants of that type. In this regime, the number of applicants can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of active types.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Group-Optimal Weak Stability for Mass School Choice. An instance gives a finite set of schools \(S\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s strict ranking of \(S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_s\), and original and adjusted priority rankings over types. Let
\[ x_{ts}\geq 0 \]
be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\), with \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\). Each person still receives one school or the outside option: \(x_{ts}\) says what fraction of a type receives \(s\), not that anyone receives a fractional seat.

Call an assignment weakly stable if it is individually rational, has no unfilled seat that a positive mass of applicants would prefer to their assignments, and has no blocking pair under the common priority comparisons \(\succ^O\cap\succ^A\). A type is improved if some type it ranks below under the original priorities is ranked below it under the adjusted priorities. A solution is a weakly stable mass assignment \(x^*\) such that, for every group of types containing all improved types, no other weakly stable mass assignment Pareto improves that group. For a type’s split assignments, Pareto improvement means that its new distribution over schools can be coupled with its old one so every member of the type is weakly better off and some positive mass is strictly better off. This makes the group comparison meaningful while preserving the paper’s ordinal welfare criterion.

I expect this finite-support problem to be tractable, perhaps through a mass version of EADA or a flow method, with complexity in \(|S|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. That is a conjecture for the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 1. The algorithm’s guarantee is the part being mirrored; proving a polynomial-time result for the mass problem is the new computational question. It also generates useful follow-ups: does the answer agree with limits of large finite cohorts, and can a mass solution be implemented by assigning measurable subsets of each type?

The soft spot is real: the paper assumes strict linear priorities over individuals, whereas repeated agents in one type cannot be strictly ordered against one another while remaining indistinguishable. The mirror therefore treats members of a type as tied and incomparable within that type, while retaining the original and adjusted comparisons across types. That is a co-modification needed to make high multiplicity possible, but it departs from an assumption the paper says matters to its fairness characterization. The case rests on authors accepting that type-level priority model as a natural mass version of their intersection-priority idea. The proposed mirror covers Theorem 1’s group-optimality guarantee only; it does not claim to mirror every result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only anchor, and it is a welfare theorem rather than a computational result. It proves that an EADA outcome is Pareto-undominated among weakly stable matchings for specified groups. The paper gives the EADA construction, but states no complexity question, runtime bound, hardness result, or algorithmic classification. The proposed mirror therefore starts from a guarantee about an allocation rule and adds a new search problem; it does not continue a computational question the paper asks.

The strongest version of that mirror is plausible: large admissions cohorts could contain repeated preference and priority-category profiles, with capacities scaled per capita and each type’s mass divided among schools. A group comparison based on whether the mass assignments can be coupled to make every member weakly better off is a reasonable way to define Pareto improvement. I would not reject it on the grounds that school choice cannot have high multiplicity.

The difficulty is preserving this paper’s model while obtaining that multiplicity. Its priorities are strict orders over named students, and whether a student is improved depends on pairwise reversals in those orders. Under strict priorities, two distinct students cannot be identical in their complete priority relations. Retaining those relations therefore leaves essentially one type per student. Grouping students into shared priority types requires replacing strict orders with tiers or ties. The paper explicitly says strictness is essential to Proposition 1 and warns that its main results may fail under ties. The mass model also replaces groups of named students with groups of types, and replaces individual Pareto comparisons with a coupling-based welfare criterion. Those may be sensible choices, but they create a new theorem about a new model.

So the best case against this anchor is that it lacks the computational target ChoCo is meant to mirror, while the proposed version needs substantial changes to the paper’s priority and welfare objects. That case is weak if an algorithmic welfare guarantee counts as a sufficient anchor: the admissions scenario and mass formulation are credible, and the resulting question could be worthwhile on its own.
