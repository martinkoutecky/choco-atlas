The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The supplied case for a mirror names no anchor; it concludes that none can be stated. The paper’s Theorems 1–3 characterize full implementability through Bayesian monotonicity and incentive compatibility. None gives a computational result. That leaves no named algorithmic problem here for a continuous-complexity mirror to continue.

The strongest proposed rescue is a large exchange economy with a finite set of trader types and rational masses, asking whether a given social choice set is fully implementable. That population regime is plausible, and high-multiplicity work would support it. But the paper supplies no finite encoding for its arbitrary utility functions, feasible allocations, or social choice sets, and no computational task over such inputs. Defining one would require choices about those representations and about equilibrium and outcomes in an atomless economy. Theorem 3 does not determine those choices; it provides a structural characterization and a mechanism construction.

That proposed decision or mechanism-synthesis problem could be worthwhile on its own. Its motivation would come from a new computational formulation of continuum mechanism design, rather than a computational result in this paper. A continuum also makes the implementation notion less automatic: a single agent’s deviation has zero mass, while treating a whole type as a deviating bloc changes the game.

So the case against a mirror rests on the missing computational anchor, not on a claim that large exchange economies lack meaningful type masses. It cannot prove that no related project could be valuable. It does show why this paper does not, on its own, furnish a worthwhile continuous mirror under ChoCo’s computational brief.
