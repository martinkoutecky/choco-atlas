What the mirror covers

No surviving mirror covers Theorem 1 or Theorem 2; the proposed type-level feasibility model covers aggregate allocation feasibility but not their individual strategic guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result, so it supplies no eligible anchor for a continuous-complexity mirror. Theorem 1, proved here, establishes that the \(\min\max\) utility level is a Fair Guarantee in the non-atomic model and that Divide and Choose implements it; the first divider can secure \(\mathrm{Maxmin}\), while other agents cannot guarantee more than \(\min\max\). Theorem 2, also proved here, establishes that Bid and Choose and Moving Knife rules guarantee levels between \(\min\max\) and \(\mathrm{Maxmin}\) for monotone preferences. These are substantive fair-division results, but neither classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard, nor gives a running-time analysis. The paper also leaves utilities very general, without a computational input representation.

A plausible *new* problem suggested by the paper would be type-continuous guarantee feasibility. Consider a large cooperative distributing a per-capita supply \(\omega\) of divisible goods or tasks. Its members fall into \(\tau\) complete preference types \(t\), with rational population shares \(\mu_t\); each type has a succinctly represented utility \(u_t\). Given target guarantees \(g_t\), ask whether there are cross-sectional bundle distributions \(P_t\) such that every member of type \(t\) receives a bundle worth at least \(g_t\), and aggregate resource use is feasible:
\[ P_t\bigl(\{z:u_t(z)\ge g_t\}\bigr)=1 \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P_t}[z]\le \omega. \]
A solution is a feasible assignment of bundles across each type’s population. This makes the population continuous while keeping individual allocations meaningful through per-capita supplies. Large worker or resident cooperatives with repeated valuation profiles are a plausible regime where \(N\gg\tau\).

That is a research direction, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Its weakest point is substantial: the paper’s mechanisms rely on finitely many individually acting agents, while this formulation changes the allocation problem and does not preserve the strategic guarantee of Theorem 1. Also, the paper already makes the manna non-atomic; that is continuity of the resource space, not of the population. So the honest positive case is that its fair-guarantee framework could motivate a population-continuous computational problem—not that this paper has established, or even analyzed, one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves a fair-guarantee and an implementation by Divide and Choose; Theorem 2 proves guarantees for Bid and Choose and Moving Knife rules. Neither studies the complexity of a computational problem, and the paper gives no finite input representation for its broad domain of continuous utilities. Those theorems can motivate new questions, but they do not supply complexity results for a mirror to preserve or re-examine.

The best population version of Theorem 1 would give each preference type a mass and ask whether everyone in that type can receive a bundle meeting some utility threshold. But that changes the object being guaranteed. In the paper, an individual secures utility by choosing or dividing against the actions of the other finite agents. In a nonatomic population, one individual has zero mass and cannot play that role. Making each type’s entire mass act as a single divider restores the mechanism only by turning the type back into one agent. The aggregate feasibility problem may be sensible, but it is not a population version of the theorem’s strategic guarantee.

Theorem 2 has the same problem. Its guarantees depend on individual agents stopping a clock or choosing shares in sequence. A mass of identical agents has no single stopping action unless the model adds a representative or a group coordination rule. Either addition changes the mechanism whose guarantee the paper proves. Calling the manna non-atomic does not solve this: the paper already has a continuum of resources, while its participants remain finite and individually acting.

The proposed type-continuous feasibility problem is coherent, but it looks like an adjacent allocation problem rather than a mirror of either theorem. With linear utilities and per-capita supplies, its constraints reduce to a finite linear program. With suitably represented concave utilities, they lead to standard convex feasibility. For general continuous utilities, the paper supplies no encoding from which an algorithm or running time could be defined; the difficulty would depend on how one represents each type’s utility superlevel set. Allowing lotteries can matter for nonconvex preferences, but then the relevant computational object is the convex hull of each type’s acceptable bundles. Any hardness or tractability would largely come from the chosen utility representation and optimization oracle, not from the guarantee results in this paper.

I would therefore reject this as evidence that the paper has a worthwhile continuous mirror: neither named theorem survives as a theorem about a continuum population, and the suggested replacement changes the question. The negative case is weaker against the broader claim that *some* useful ChoCo problem could be built from this setting. A large cooperative with repeated preference types is plausible, and its aggregate guarantee feasibility might be worth studying on its own. But that possibility supports a new research direction, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
