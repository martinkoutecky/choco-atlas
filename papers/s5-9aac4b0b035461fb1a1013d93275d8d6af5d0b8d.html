The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity continuum version of EFX feasibility, led by Theorem 1. I should state one limitation plainly: the paper contains no named result asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, W[1]-hardness, FPT, or a comparable complexity classification. Theorems 1–3 and the lemmas are existence and structural results. Thus this is a genuine continuous mirror of the paper’s mathematical problems, but not yet a qualifying ChoCo complexity anchor.

My lead problem is Continuum-Integral EFX Feasibility. There is a finite set \(S\) of agent types, with masses \(\mu_s\) summing to \(1\), and a finite set \(G\) of chore types, with per-capita supplies \(\lambda_g\). Type \(s\)'s additive cost for chore type \(g\) is \(c_s(g)\). An allocation is a finite-support distribution \(\rho_{s,B}\), where \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\) is an integral bundle: \(\rho_{s,B}\) is the mass of type-\(s\) agents receiving \(B\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_B \rho_{s,B}=\mu_s \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{s,B}\rho_{s,B}B_g=\lambda_g \]
for every \(s\) and \(g\). No individual receives fractional chores; only the mass of agents receiving each integral bundle is fractional.

For \(B\neq\varnothing\), define
\[ \tau_s(B)=\sum_g c_s(g)B_g-\min_{g:B_g>0}c_s(g), \]
and set \(\tau_s(\varnothing)=0\). The question is whether there exists such a \(\rho\) for which, whenever \(\rho_{s,B}>0\) and \(\rho_{s',B'}>0\),
\[ \tau_s(B)\le \sum_g c_s(g)B'_g. \]
This is exactly EFX, quantified over the support of the continuum allocation.

The regime is unusually convincing here. In the paper’s construction, there are only two agent types, \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), while the number of agents and chores grows. For Theorem 1, with \(t_1=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), \(t_2=\lceil n/2\rceil\), \(s=2t_2+1\), \(q=s+2\), and \(r=s(q+1)/2\), the continuum instance has masses \(\mu_{T_1}=t_1/n\) and \(\mu_{T_2}=t_2/n\), chore supplies
\[ \lambda_A=(n-1)/n,\qquad \lambda_B=\lambda_C=s/n, \]
and costs
\[ c_{T_1}(A)=c_{T_2}(A)=r,\quad c_{T_1}(B)=c_{T_2}(C)=1,\quad c_{T_1}(C)=c_{T_2}(B)=q. \]
Thus the number of agents is growing while the number of agent and chore types stays at \(2\) and \(3\). This is precisely a high-multiplicity regime, not a story invented after the fact.

The named anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every \(n\ge4\), there exists a tri-valued additive chore instance with \(n\) agents and no EFX allocation. The construction uses two agent types and three chore groups. There are \(n-1\) universally expensive chores \(A\), and two groups \(B,C\) of \(2\lceil n/2\rceil+1\) chores. Agents of type \(T_1\) see \(A,B,C\) at costs \(r,1,q\), while agents of type \(T_2\) see them at \(r,q,1\). The theorem also establishes tightness in the relevant dimensions: two chore types always admit EFX, and one agent type always admits EFX.

The continuous question above asks whether the same construction, interpreted as masses and per-capita chore supplies, admits an EFX allocation. I expect the obstruction to persist. For rational masses and supplies, a finite-support \(\rho\) is exactly the normalized bundle-count description of a high-multiplicity discrete allocation; conversely, a rational \(\rho\) scales to a finite allocation. Hence this mirror is not a fractional relaxation that quietly gives one agent pieces of chores. It preserves the indivisible-bundle combinatorics while removing irrelevant agent names.

The expected classification is therefore structural persistence rather than continuum-specific hardness: the paper’s nonexistence phenomenon should transfer. For the fixed two-agent-type, three-chore-type restriction, one might hope for a tractable finite-pattern or flow characterization of EFX feasibility; for unrestricted type systems, the complexity of deciding whether an integral-bundle continuum EFX allocation exists is a natural open question. The paper itself does not prove either classification.

A second, also plausible mirror uses Theorem 2, proved here. It asks for a continuum EFX allocation that is also Pareto-optimal. The input is the same kind of \((\mu,\lambda,c)\) instance, with Pareto optimality quantified over measurable reallocations that weakly reduce the cost of almost every agent and strictly reduce it for positive mass. Theorem 2’s family has two agent types, chore groups \(A,B,C\), costs in \(\{1,r\}\), \(r>\lceil n/2\rceil+1\), and supplies
\[ \lambda_A=(n-1)/n,\quad \lambda_B=(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1)/n,\quad \lambda_C=(\lceil n/2\rceil+1)/n. \]
The theorem says that EFX allocations exist, but every EFX allocation is Pareto dominated. Its continuous counterpart should preserve exactly that separation: EFX survives, while EFX plus PO fails. This is an especially good mirror because it tests whether the continuum changes the interaction between fairness and efficiency, rather than merely restating a fractional-allocation model.

The weak point is that the theorem family’s values \(q\) and \(r\) depend on \(n\) and grow with it, so it does not immediately yield one fixed limiting instance with fixed cost parameters. Also, if one allowed chores to be split among an individual agent, the obstruction could disappear—but that would be continuizing the items, not the population, and would be the wrong mirror. Subject to retaining integral bundles and treating \(q,r\) as instance parameters, the high-multiplicity interpretation remains strong: the paper’s own counterexamples already have exactly the small type space and repeated-agent structure that continuization is meant to expose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is stronger than the proponent admits: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 is a nonexistence theorem, Theorem 2 an incompatibility theorem, and Theorem 3 an existence theorem. None states a complexity classification, an algorithmic running time, an approximation guarantee, or a parameterized result. The algorithmic material in Section 5 is part of an existence proof, not a computational theorem. Literature claims about algorithms and hardness are not results proved by this paper.

Theorem 1 nevertheless gives the best apparent rescue, but its proposed continuum is either an exact restatement or a degenerate limit. For each \(n\), the masses and supplies are merely normalized counts:
\[ \mu_{T_1}=\frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{n},\qquad \mu_{T_2}=\frac{\lceil n/2\rceil}{n}, \]
and
\[ \lambda_A=\frac{n-1}{n},\qquad \lambda_B=\lambda_C=\frac{2\lceil n/2\rceil+1}{n}. \]
With integral bundles, rational mass allocations scale back exactly to the paper’s finite allocations. Thus, at every finite \(n\), the “continuous” problem removes agent names but adds no new computational object.

The actual \(n\to\infty\) limit is not a stable tri-valued instance. The supplies converge to \(1\), but \(q=s+2\) grows linearly and \(r=s(q+1)/2\) quadratically. Since multiplying all costs by a common factor does not change EFX, normalization does not repair this: normalizing by \(q\) makes the smallest cost tend to \(0\) and the largest tend to infinity; normalizing by \(r\) collapses both smaller levels to \(0\). The proof’s contradiction also depends on the exact integer relation \(r\equiv s+1\pmod q\), on precisely \(n-1\) universal chores, and on one exceptional bundle without such a chore. Its mass is \(1/n\), which disappears in the atomless limit. The obstruction is therefore not a robust continuum phenomenon; it is finite arithmetic encoded in rational masses.

One could instead fix positive costs and supplies and ask for EFX feasibility over arbitrary type distributions. That would be a legitimate fair-division problem, but it would be a new problem rather than a continuization of Theorem 1. The paper provides neither its formulation nor any computational result about it. A future tractability or hardness theorem could be worthwhile, but its value would come from the new theory, not from this paper’s result.

Theorem 2 is weaker still as a continuous anchor. Its threshold \(r>\lceil n/2\rceil+1\) grows with \(n\), and the proof’s efficiency gap is only one unit of total cost:
\[ \sum_i c_i(X_i)\ge (r+1)n+1. \]
After normalization, the excess over \(r+1\) is only \(1/n\). The Pareto witness is likewise constructed by selecting a particular named agent \(i\). At fixed \(n\), that agent can be represented by mass \(1/n\), giving an exact high-multiplicity restatement. In the genuine limiting population, however, the strict improvement has vanishing mass. A continuum Pareto relation requiring strict improvement for a positive-mass set would no longer see the finite witness.

To obtain a nonvanishing incompatibility, one would have to thicken the exceptional group, alter the supply of \(A\), and rebuild the proof so that a positive fraction of the population is inefficient. That may be interesting, but it is plainly a new theorem. The proponent’s proposed continuum PO statement does not establish it.

Theorem 3 cannot rescue the paper under the programme’s gate. Its proof relies on exactly four named vertices, a finite \(M_2\) multigraph, and case-specific bundle manipulations. Replacing those four agents by four positive-mass types produces a new support-selection/configuration problem; retaining four agents produces no continuous population at all. Again, the paper proves existence, not the computational complexity of either formulation.

So the proponent has shown that EFX admits a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation. That is a real point in its favour, and existing repeated agent types should not be treated as a collision. But the two proposed anchors either normalize the finite theorem exactly or lose its witness in the limit, while the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile future paper on nondegenerate continuous EFX and Pareto efficiency; I can say that such a paper would be new ChoCo research, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
