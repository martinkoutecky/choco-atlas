The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), budgets \(w_t\), explicitly represented concave degree-one homogeneous utilities \(u_t\), homogeneous constraints \(A_t x\leq 0\), and per-capita supplies \(\bar s_j\), assuming an equilibrium exists, compute a common price vector \(p\) and type bundles \(x_t\) such that \(x_t\in\arg\max\{u_t(x):p^\top x\leq w_t,\ A_t x\leq 0,\ x\geq 0\}\), each type spends its budget, and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=\bar s_j\) for every good \(j\).

The model it lives in

A mass-weighted ADMM with type-level decision variables \(x_t,y_t\), objective \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t\log u_t(x_t)\), type feasibility \(A_t x_t\leq 0\), consensus \(x_t=y_t\), and aggregate supply constraints \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{tj}=\bar s_j\); the capacity dual variables give the common prices.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers ADMM equilibrium computation for homogeneous linear constraints and the homogeneous-constraint equilibrium-existence test; it leaves the non-homogeneous fixed-point and ADMM convergence questions open.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove a mass-weighted type-level ADMM convergence guarantee and bound the work needed for an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium in \(m,\tau\), input bit length, and \(\varepsilon\).
  • Formalize the finite-replication correspondence and approximation transfer for rational type masses, including markets with multiple optimal bundles for a type.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s market with homogeneous linear constraints. Take many buyers in a recurring procurement market for divisible goods. A type records everything the model uses about a buyer: their utility, budget, and bundle constraints. For example, two buyers belong to the same type only if they have the same preferences and budget and face the same proportionality rules. A mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers of type \(t\), not a count. This is plausible when a large market has millions of accounts but relatively few repeated contract and preference profiles. The goods remain divisible, as in the paper; the population is what becomes continuous.

For \(m\) goods, let the support contain \(\tau\) types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Type \(t\) has budget \(w_t\), concave degree-one homogeneous utility \(u_t\), and constraints \(A_t x\leq 0\), \(x\geq 0\). Let \(\bar s_j\) be the supply of good \(j\) per buyer. A type’s variable \(x_t\) is its representative buyer’s bundle, so total consumption per buyer is \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t\). A market equilibrium is a common price vector \(p\) and bundles \(x_t\) such that each type maximizes \(u_t(x)\) subject to its budget and constraints, spends its budget, and the market clears: \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=\bar s_j\) for every good. In a finite replication with \(N\) buyers, there are \(N\mu_t\) buyers of type \(t\) and \(N\bar s_j\) units of good \(j\). The aggregate model records the same market using \(\tau\) types rather than \(N\) named buyers.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, “Convergence of ADMM for bp-sop-adm,” proved here using a cited general ADMM convergence result. It states that if an equilibrium exists and each buyer has a concave, degree-one homogeneous utility, the paper’s ADMM algorithm converges to an equilibrium price vector at rate \(O(1/k)\), where \(k\) is the number of iterations. This is a convergence theorem, not a polynomial-time classification.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Homogeneous-Constrained Fisher Equilibrium. Given the rational type masses, per-type budgets and homogeneous constraints, per-capita supplies, and explicitly represented utilities, find equilibrium prices and one optimal bundle for each type. The solution must satisfy type-level individual optimality, budget exhaustion, and aggregate market clearing as above. The expected outcome is tractable in the paper’s continuous-optimization sense: an ADMM scheme can operate on type variables, with the objective weighted by \(\mu_t w_t\) and aggregate supply weighted by \(\mu_t\). Establishing the exact type-level convergence statement and its dependence on \(m,\tau\), and input precision would be part of the proof. A further question is whether the iteration and per-iteration costs can be bounded in those parameters independently of the number \(N\) of replicated buyers, and how the finite and continuous approximate equilibria relate.

A second, distinct anchor is Corollary 2, “Convex Program to Test for Existence of Non-negative Equilibrium Price for Homogeneous Linear Constraints,” proved here. It says that when all extra constraints are homogeneous, a non-negative equilibrium exists if and only if all capacity constraints are tight at an optimum of the paper’s convex program with capacity equalities replaced by inequalities. In the type model with linear utilities \(u_t(x)=a_t\cdot x\), that program is
\[ \max_{(x_t)}\ \sum_t \mu_t w_t\log(a_t\cdot x_t) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\leq\bar s_j,\qquad A_t x_t\leq0,\quad x_t\geq0. \]
The problem is Continuous Non-negative Equilibrium Existence: decide whether a non-negative equilibrium exists; a “yes” solution can include an optimizer whose aggregate capacity constraints are all tight, from which the paper’s result obtains equilibrium prices. The expected outcome is again tractable via convex optimization. Further questions include how to certify the tightness test at a stated precision and whether the result extends to non-homogeneous constraints, where the endogenous budget perturbations enter.

Both mirrors preserve the paper’s market question: each type still optimizes under a common price, and supplies must clear. The market’s divisible outcomes do not themselves make this a population-continuum model; the change is that the society is represented by masses of complete buyer types. I would expect the authors to recognize that as a continuum-agent form of their equilibrium problem. The mirror covers their homogeneous-constraint existence and ADMM results, not the non-homogeneous case, for which the paper reports numerical convergence but leaves theory open.

The weak point is substantial: neither anchor gives a standard complexity classification in \(m,\tau\), and bit length, and Theorem 5’s \(O(1/k)\) claim does not by itself specify finite-precision iteration costs. Also, the result depends on exact type identity and on homogeneous constraints; relaxing those assumptions may change the problem. I would not claim the continuum population model is novel. The positive case is narrower: the paper has named algorithmic results on an individually specified finite market, and their type-aggregated continuous versions are sensible computational questions. The paper contains no named hardness result, so this case has no hardness anchor from which to ask whether population-continuity dissolves discrete hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this mirror is exact aggregation of the paper’s existing finite market. Give each buyer a type containing their utility, budget, and constraints, and let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s fraction of the market. A society with rational masses can be replicated by finitely many buyers, and a finite market can be grouped by identical buyer records. With homogeneous constraints, averaging identical buyers’ feasible bundles preserves feasibility; convexity lets the grouped market represent an optimum. After normalizing supplies per buyer, its social program is precisely
\[ \max \sum_t \mu_t w_t\log u_t(x_t) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\leq \bar s_j,\qquad A_t x_t\leq 0. \]
That is a useful dictionary, but it leaves little new market structure to investigate: the “continuous” instance is the finite one with repeated buyers compressed.

This weakens Theorem 5 as a mirror. Its \(O(1/k)\) guarantee is an iteration bound for a generic ADMM method, not a running-time classification in \(m,\tau,L\). For exact duplicate types, the local updates can be batched by type and the capacity update uses weighted aggregate demand. That appears to be the same algorithm on a symmetric representation of the finite market. Turning it into an input-size bound would require additional work, but the theorem itself supplies no such bound.

Corollary 2 has the same limitation. Its mass-weighted convex program is the finite program after grouping identical buyers and normalizing supply. The tight-capacity test is already posed for that finite market; type masses re-express it without changing the question. The proponent’s versions are well-defined, yet neither anchor presently identifies a new computational obstacle created by the population continuum.

The case against is strongest when exact types are demanding. A type must match in utility, budget, and every constraint. In markets with personalized budgets or preferences, the support can be as large as the buyer count, so the compression disappears. The proposed procurement scenario could have repeated profiles, but that depends on standardized buyer classes and does not establish that the regime is broadly natural for this model.

I would use this to argue against prioritizing the mirror as a flagship ChoCo case: it looks like a high-multiplicity parameterization of a market problem whose optimization structure already aggregates clones. I cannot honestly turn that into the requested universal claim. Repeated buyer classes are plausible, and the paper’s non-homogeneous fixed-point problem could also be studied by type; the exact finite-to-mass correspondence may itself be a useful result under the programme’s stated goals. So the negative case is about limited new computational content, and it remains vulnerable to the programme’s explicit interest in support-size algorithms and formal dictionaries.
