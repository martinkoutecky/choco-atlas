The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no numbered computational result to anchor a continuous mirror on. It states no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise.

The closest result is the unnumbered asymptotic calculation in §2.2: under the assumption that all \(3^N\) yes/no/abstain outcomes are equally likely, a voter’s probability of being decisive satisfies \(P_N \approx \sqrt{3/\pi}/\sqrt{N+1}\) for large \(N\). That is an analytic asymptotic result, not a computational-complexity result. The paper’s population limit could motivate further work, but turning it into a continuous computational problem would introduce a new question without a named result here to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s closest anchor is real, but it is not a computational result: \(P_N\) is a probability calculated from a closed combinatorial sum under the assumption that every voter independently chooses among three options with equal likelihood. The paper gives an asymptotic analysis of that quantity, not a complexity or algorithmic claim. There is no computational problem here for a continuous mirror to classify.

The more fundamental obstacle is what the calculation measures. A voter is pivotal because changing that one person’s discrete vote can flip the outcome. In a society represented only by type masses, that person has mass \(1/(N+1)\), which vanishes in the limit. With the paper’s identical voters, a continuum of random choices instead becomes fixed shares of yes, no, and abstention. The pivotality calculation depends on finite-population fluctuations near the threshold; the mass distribution alone does not retain them. Recovering them requires a stochastic fluctuation model at the scale of one voter, rather than a computational question about a continuous society.

A stronger candidate would allow different voter or constituency types and ask for exact or approximate computation of citizen pivotality, or for optimal vote weights. That could be a sensible research question. But it would need additional structure absent here—heterogeneous probabilities, an aggregation model, and an explicit computational task. If it keeps the one-person pivotal event, it must also retain finite counts or their equivalent to recover the relevant fluctuations. If it drops those, it has changed what pivotality means. Either way, the proposed problem is an extension inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of its result.

So the no-go case is strong for this paper’s actual contribution: its sole substantive result is analytic, and the pivotality it studies does not pass into a mass-only model intact. The honest weakness is the universal claim. A carefully specified high-multiplicity problem about voting power or vote-weight optimization might be worthwhile in its own right; I cannot rule that out. But that possibility does not rescue the anchor the proponent raised.
