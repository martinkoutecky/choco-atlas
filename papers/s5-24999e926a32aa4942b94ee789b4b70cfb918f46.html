The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible setting for a population mirror is a large course system with many students but relatively few complete preference and priority types. A type would include everything relevant to the matching problem: a student’s preferences over course bundles and their priority characteristics. Its mass would be the fraction of students of that type. Course capacities would scale with population size, and a solution could record \(x_{t,B}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(B\). Those bundles remain integral for each student; only the cohort’s allocation is fractional.

That is a natural setting for asking whether a mass-valued renegotiable-acceptance mechanism implements stable mass matchings. It is close to the paper’s subject, but it is not anchored by a named computational-complexity result in this paper. Theorem 1 proves that, with substitutable student preferences and substitutable course priorities, the finite renegotiable-acceptance mechanism implements the stable matchings in Nash equilibrium; Corollary 1 extends that claim to undominated Nash equilibrium. Theorem 2 and Corollary 2 give the corresponding results for immediate acceptance under slot-specific priorities. Propositions 1 and 2 show that, with responsive preferences, the mechanisms preserve their Nash implementation properties when students report individual-course rankings and demand instead of full bundle preferences. These are strategic implementation results, not claims that a problem is in \( \mathrm P \), NP-hard, or in another complexity class.

The paper says the mechanisms are “not computationally demanding” in its conclusion, but it supplies no named result or complexity analysis establishing that claim. Nor does it compute with a continuous model. So I cannot honestly state a continuous computational problem *per eligible anchor*, or assign one a tractability class, as the prompt requires.

The soft spot is fundamental to the positive case: a continuous implementation question would require extending the paper’s priority choice functions and strategic mechanisms to masses, then specifying a meaningful equilibrium concept for infinitesimal agents. That could be a sensible new research question, but it would add the computational question the paper itself does not ask. The paper therefore offers a plausible subject for a continuous extension, but no qualifying computational anchor for a ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, Theorem 2 and Corollary 2, and Propositions 1 and 2 establish strategic implementation and preservation of equilibrium properties. They give no complexity classification or algorithmic bound. The paper’s reference to reducing the “complexity” of the message space concerns the size of the reports students must submit; it does not analyze the running time or complexity of a computational problem. The conclusion’s claim that the mechanisms are “not computationally demanding” is not supported by a complexity result.

The best version of a Theorem 1 mirror would use a large course system, types containing each student’s preferences and priority characteristics, and capacities that scale with population. That is a plausible continuous matching model. But its central claim would concern Nash implementation, and that is where the population limit breaks the correspondence. In an atomless population, one student’s report has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate allocation. The mass outcome therefore cannot capture the individual deviations that drive the paper’s equilibrium proofs. Treating each type as a strategic player with the power to change its whole mass would restore strategic force, but it would turn a type into a coordinated cohort. That is a different game from the one in Theorem 1. Theorem 2 has the same problem: slot-specific priorities do not restore the effect of an individual deviation on the aggregate outcome.

Propositions 1 and 2 fare no better as computational anchors. They show that, under responsive preferences, students can report a course ranking and a demand instead of preferences over all course bundles. That is a reduction in each student’s message space, independent of whether the population is finite or continuous. A population limit does not turn that result into a computation problem; computing aggregate equilibria would again require a new strategic model.

One could instead ask for the computational complexity of finding stable mass allocations, or of running an aggregate acceptance procedure. That could be a sensible new question about course allocation. It would not mirror a computational result in this paper: it changes the target from strategic implementation to computing outcomes, a subject the paper does not analyze. So the negative case is strong on the paper’s anchors, though not on the broader claim that continuous course-allocation research could be worthwhile. Large course markets make a continuous matching model plausible; what is missing here is a computational result from the paper for that model to continuize.
