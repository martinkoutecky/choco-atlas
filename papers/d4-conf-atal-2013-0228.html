I can’t make an anchor-backed computational case from this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest numbered results are Claim 1, which says that when forecasters each observe one attribute, the optimal prediction lies outside the convex hull of their forecasts, and Corollary 1, which extends that conclusion to forecasters observing \(k\) attributes under a stated condition on the observed attributes. They are statistical and geometric claims, not computational-complexity results. Equations 15 and 16 define averaging and voting rules, but do not provide a named complexity result either. So there is no qualifying anchor on which to base one continuous problem per anchor or predict its complexity class.

A plausible population regime does fit the paper’s subject: a large forecasting crowd, with types representing forecasters who share the same relevant forecasting model and signal behavior, and mass representing each type’s fraction of the crowd. That makes a continuous population of forecasters a credible analogue of their setting. But the paper does not computationally analyze aggregation over that population. The positive case is therefore plausible as a modeling direction, but unsupported by the required named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor to defeat. Claim 1 and Corollary 1 are geometric and statistical statements about when an optimal forecast can leave a convex hull; the averaging and voting equations define aggregation rules. None asserts a complexity result for a computational problem. That is the strongest objection here: the paper gives no named algorithmic result for a continuous mirror to explain, extend, or challenge.

The proposed regime is plausible. A large forecasting crowd could be represented by masses of forecasters sharing a model, signal behavior, and other relevant features. For a fixed question, rewriting the paper’s sums as integrals over those types is natural. But that makes the population continuous without producing a substantial computational question. Given the supported types and their forecasts, averaging and voting amount to summing over the support. Choosing weights to minimize Brier loss on known data gives a finite-dimensional convex optimization problem. If the outcome probabilities are known, the Brier-optimal forecast is simply that probability vector. The paper’s main distinction—whether the best aggregate lies inside the forecasts’ convex hull—is about the statistical structure of estimates, not the computational cost of finding an aggregate.

A tempting richer mirror would choose aggregation weights using forecasters’ past accuracy, recency, or other features. That is a reasonable forecasting problem, but it shifts the focus to learning from historical outcomes. If the history is summarized into types, the resulting static optimization remains about fitting an aggregator; if the task depends on how individual forecasters’ signals vary across questions, the input must retain those linked histories. Either way, it is a new learning or data-modeling problem rather than a computational result in this paper. Adding a difficult constraint, such as selecting a bounded-size panel of forecasters, could create a complexity question, but the paper does not pose that task.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: it supports a credible continuous-population description, but not a worthwhile *computational mirror* of a result it proves. The limit of that case is that no one can rule out a future, author-recognizable algorithmic problem built around forecast aggregation. The paper itself gives little reason to expect such a problem to be its contribution.
