The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of piecewise-uniform valuation types \(t\), rational masses μ_t summing to \(1\), and a chore supply scaled with population, can a deterministic mechanism partition the aggregate chores into type allocations \(D_t\) such that \(|D_t ∩ V_t| ≤ μ_t |V_t|\) for every type and no cohort can lower its true aggregate cost by changing its reported valuation set?

The model it lives in

A finite-type society in which each type \(t\) carries mass μ_t and a shared valuation set \(V_t\). The mechanism allocates aggregate chore mass \(D_t\) to each cohort; proportionality bounds its cost by μ_t |V_t|, and strategyproofness tests deviations by an entire cohort.

The objection that survived

A single atomless member's report does not change μ, so Theorem 10 transfers only to a whole cohort's coordinated report; this bounds the mirror to cohort-level truthfulness.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10's strategyproofness and Theorem 2's weighted proportionality for finite-support type cohorts. It leaves aside envy-freeness, Pareto efficiency, and the paper's Robertson–Webb query-complexity discussion.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational-complexity anchor in this paper. The closest named results are Theorem 10, proved here, which says that for every input to split rulership, no player can lower their cost on their true valuation set by reporting another set; and Theorem 2, also proved here, which says every player’s cost is at most their assigned workload \(w_i\) times the measure of their valuation set. Both are mechanism-property theorems. Neither classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The paper also says the split point may not be findable with any bounded number of Robertson–Webb queries, so I cannot honestly relabel these as polynomial-time results.

The best exploratory population mirror would take types \(t\) to be complete chore preferences, represented by valuation sets \(V_t\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). A mechanism would allocate chores across those types and ask whether type-level proportionality and truthful reporting can both be maintained. But that is not an eligible anchored question here: Theorem 10 concerns individual players, while a type-level formulation must decide whether a deviation is by one negligible member or by the entire cohort. The algorithm also depends on player order, so its result does not directly transfer to indistinguishable type masses.

So the positive case is weak: the paper offers named results about proportionality and strategyproofness that could motivate a population-level research question, but it offers no named computational-complexity result for that question to mirror. The paper’s chore space is already divisible; its society remains finite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result to continuize. Theorem 2 proves proportionality, and Theorem 10 proves strategyproofness for a particular mechanism. Neither classifies a computational problem by running time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. The closest passage says the split point has no guaranteed finite Robertson–Webb query bound, but it is not a numbered complexity result, and it concerns an interface to individual valuation oracles. A population version would need a new input and query model.

Theorem 2 does have a natural mass reading. Group agents with identical valuation sets into types \(t\), set each type’s workload to its mass \(w_t=\mu_t\), and ask for a cohort-level proportionality guarantee. For finite support, the paper already permits arbitrary workloads summing to one, so Theorem 2 gives that guarantee by running the mechanism on \(\tau\) representatives. Theorem 10 has a similar strongest reading: treat each cohort as a player that may misreport on behalf of its members. With fixed cohort positions and workloads, its strategyproofness proof applies directly. These are sensible fairness questions, but their answers follow from the paper’s weighted finite-player model; they do not open a new complexity landscape.

The individual-level version fares worse. In an atomless population, one member’s report does not change the mass distribution. A mechanism determined only by that distribution cannot register the deviation; making the member’s own allocation depend on their report requires information beyond the mass vector. If instead a whole cohort deviates, the question is the cohort-level version above. A distribution over a continuum of valuation sets also lacks a finite input description in the paper’s model; restricting to finite binary-encoded support returns to the weighted representative formulation.

The cake’s divisibility is outcome continuity, not population continuity, so it does not settle the question either way. And repeated staff preferences in a large workplace are a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. That makes the claim that *no* continuous chore-division question could be worthwhile hard to defend. The case against is narrower: this paper offers natural continuous fairness interpretations, but no anchored computational result for ChoCo to study.
