What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the transport distance between distributions over rankings, which §4.5.2 identifies with Earth Mover or transportation distance; it leaves the paper’s other structural and axiomatic results alone.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a narrow subproblem: computing the transport distance between two voter populations. Its plausible anchor is Proposition 4.36, proved in this paper. It says that if a consensus \(K\) and distance \(d\) are anonymous, then \(R(K,d)\) is anonymous and can be represented on the quotient of anonymous profiles using the quotient distance \(\tilde d\). Conversely, if a rule is anonymous and distance rationalizable, it has a representation with an anonymous consensus and a totally anonymous distance. This establishes the right population-level object, but it does not assert a complexity result.

The continuous problem would be: given a candidate set \(C\), two finitely supported distributions \(\mu,\nu\) over complete rankings of \(C\), and a ranking distance \(\delta\), find a minimum-cost coupling \(x\), where
\[ \sum_{t'}x_{t,t'}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,t'}=\nu_{t'},\qquad x_{t,t'}\geq 0, \]
minimizing \(\sum_{t,t'}\delta(t,t')x_{t,t'}\). A type is a ranking; mass is the fraction of the population holding it; the coupling describes how much mass moves between types; and the objective is total per-capita change cost. A large electorate with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings is a plausible regime. The paper’s authors would recognize this question: Section 4.5.2 explicitly identifies the anonymous distance with Earth Mover or transportation distance and describes moving voter mass between types.

This lead candidate is Class A: it is a transportation LP, and the paper says the corresponding assignment problem can be solved in polynomial time. But that algorithmic claim is in the prose, not a numbered theorem, proposition, or corollary. Moreover, rational population distributions can be scaled to integer voter counts, so this is a very narrow continuation of a problem the paper already formulates and solves. Further questions about distances to particular consensus sets might be interesting, but Proposition 4.36 does not establish their complexity.

So, under the required anchor rule, the paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Proposition 4.36 is the closest anchor, but it is structural, not a computational result. The transport question is my best honest case for a continuous mirror; its weakness is that it is already essentially present in the paper and cannot supply the requested named complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The difficulty with the proposed anchor is that Proposition 4.36 is a structural result about quotienting anonymous profiles, not a computational result. It says when a rule descends to anonymous voting situations; it gives no complexity bound or algorithm.

The transport question is a closer fit, but Section 4.5.2 already identifies the anonymous distance with Earth Mover distance and points to the polynomial assignment or transportation algorithm. With support size \(\tau\), the mass-transfer formulation is a standard rational min-cost flow problem on a network of size \(O(\tau^2)\). Asking for its running time in \(\tau\) and bit length may be useful bookkeeping, but it adds little social-choice structure. The paper’s own quotient consists of vote distributions, and every rational distribution in the programme’s input model corresponds to a finite election after scaling. So this particular mirror is already present in substance and has a routine computational answer.

A stronger proposal would be to compute winners of a distance-rationalized rule over continuous societies. But the paper does not give a named complexity result to continue, and its consensuses and distances are general enough that this question has no fixed input representation or algorithmic shape until one chooses them. Making those choices could start a worthwhile project, but it would be a new computational study built on the framework, rather than a mirror of one of this paper’s results. The numbered propositions are structural or axiomatic; the transport algorithm appears in prose.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here. Rankings are natural voter types, and the paper itself works with anonymous profiles and vote distributions. That makes the universal negative case weak: it defeats the proposed transport anchor and finds no computational result to anchor a mirror on, but it cannot show that no new, well-chosen computational question could be worthwhile.
