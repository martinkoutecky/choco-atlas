The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no named complexity result of the kind your anchor rule asks for: it does not classify a computational problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar. Its named results concern incentives and Nash equilibria. The strongest honest positive case is therefore a mirror of one such result, with the limitation made explicit.

The natural setting is a massive course where millions of students submit work and review a fixed number of other submissions. Suppose the rubric has finitely many score levels, and students share the same review-cost and measurement-error model. A type \(t\) is then a complete student profile for this model, including the student’s submission quality \(q_t\); \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the class. The support \(\tau\) can be small compared with the class size. Each student still grades an integral number of submissions. At the population level, however, the instructor grades a fraction \(p\) of submissions, and expected losses and workload are per-capita quantities.

The closest named anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. In its flat-supervision scheme, the instructor grades a submission with probability \(p\), review loss is scaled by \(\alpha>0\), and a student incurs cost \(C>0\) for doing an honest review. The theorem states that if \(p>\sqrt{C/(\alpha\sigma^2)}\), then every Nash-equilibrium strategy is \(\sigma\)-truthful: for every true quality \(q\), its expected squared grading error is at most \(\sigma^2\). This is an incentive result, not a complexity classification.

The continuous problem I would put to a prover is Minimum Audit Mass for Equilibrium Truthfulness. An instance gives a finite rational distribution \(\mu\) over score types, the paper’s admissible strategy and random review-assignment model, and parameters \(C,\alpha,\sigma\). Choose the smallest audit fraction \(p\in[0,1]\) for which every Nash equilibrium of the continuum population game is \(\sigma\)-truthful for almost every reviewer. A solution is an audit fraction together with a proof of that equilibrium guarantee, or a proof that no such fraction exists. Because each student has one submission, the instructor’s per-capita workload is \(p\).

Theorem 1 supplies a sufficient target for this mirror: every \(p>\sqrt{C/(\alpha\sigma^2)}\) works. It does not establish that this is the exact minimum; the strict inequality also means the bound itself need not be attained. I would expect the finite-support version to be tractable, given the paper’s quadratic losses and explicit threshold. The central follow-up is to characterize the exact minimum, rather than mistake the theorem’s sufficient bound for an optimality result.

This is a plausible continuation of the authors’ question: it keeps their grade-based peer-review game, effort incentives, and instructor audit decision, while making class size and audit workload naturally proportional quantities. It continuizes the student population; fractional audit mass is the resulting per-capita accounting, not a change to the outcome space. It covers only the flat supervised guarantee in Theorem 1. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s hierarchical or unsupervised schemes.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s actual result is about equilibrium incentives, not computational complexity, and the proposed minimum-audit problem risks being a routine population extension of its threshold analysis rather than a new complexity question. The continuous model is sensible for a massive course with many students in the same score and review-cost categories, but the paper gives no algorithmic hardness or tractability anchor for it. The positive case is therefore for a credible continuous-population research question—not for claiming that this paper already opens a computational-complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an incentive theorem: under assumptions about review costs and losses, it gives a sufficient audit probability for truthful play in every Nash equilibrium. It does not pose an algorithmic problem or analyze running time. “Minimum Audit Mass” adds an optimization objective to that result; it may be a reasonable follow-up, but it is a new computational question, not a continuous version of a computational question the paper studied.

The proposed population model also leaves out information the game uses. A mass distribution over quality, cost, and error types does not specify who reviews which submissions, which reviews share a submission, or which reviewers are connected to an audited item. Those details affect individual losses. Since each student reviews only a few submissions, the interaction graph stays sparse as the class grows: each reviewer’s neighborhood remains consequential, and a reviewer’s grades can affect the losses of nearby reviewers. A type that includes the relevant neighborhood can preserve those payoffs, but the number of such types may grow with class size. Averaging over random assignments instead gives a different, stochastic population game; it does not preserve the realized sparse game automatically.

A stronger proposed mirror would let the instructor choose type-specific audit rates, or select submissions to audit, to minimize workload while guaranteeing truthful equilibria. The first version needs additional assumptions about heterogeneous types and their strategy spaces. The second depends on the review graph and its incidence structure. Those could make worthwhile research questions, but their computational content comes from a new audit-allocation or graph-design problem, not from continuizing a complexity result in this paper. And an exact minimum over the paper’s admissible strategies is not even specified by a finite rational input as stated: those strategies include arbitrary functions over a continuous quality interval.

There is a real high-multiplicity setting here: a large course can have many students in the same rubric, cost, and error categories. So the negative case is not that population mass is inherently meaningless, or that a continuous peer-grading game could never be useful. It is that this paper’s results are about equilibrium incentives in locally coupled games, while the paper gives no computational-complexity anchor for ChoCo to continuize. The case against is strongest as a reason this is a poor target for the programme, and weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous-population question could be built from peer grading.
