The continuous mirror question

For \(p,q\ge 1\) and \(\tau\ge 2\), what is the supremum of the coordinate-wise weighted-median approximation ratio over rational-mass societies \(\mu\) supported on at most \(\tau\) location types in \(\mathbb{Q}^2\)? The paper's planar result predicts the value \(2^{1-1/p}\) when \(q\le p\), and \(2^{1-1/q}\) when \(p<q\).

The model it lives in

Types are resident locations \(x\in\mathbb{Q}^2\), with rational masses \(\mu_x\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). The decision is the facility location \(f\in\mathbb{R}^2\); the objective is \(\mathrm{SC}_{p,q}(\mu,f)=\left(\sum_x\mu_x\|f-x\|_q^p\right)^{1/p}\). The mechanism chooses the lower weighted median in each coordinate, and its ratio is measured against \(\min_{f\in\mathbb{R}^2}\mathrm{SC}_{p,q}(\mu,f)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's planar \(p\)-norm approximation guarantee and its weighted-population analogue; Theorem 4 supplies matching witness examples. It leaves Theorems 1 and 3, higher-dimensional bounds, and general monotone symmetric objectives outside the stated mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here for the paper’s planar approximation-ratio results. The paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-classification theorem, so this is not a case about discrete hardness dissolving in a continuum. Its anchors are computational approximation results: how well a specified facility-location mechanism performs against the optimal facility.

Take a municipality choosing a public facility for a large population. A type is a resident’s modeled location \(x\in\mathbb{Q}^2\); residents at the same location have the same distance cost. Mass \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of residents of that type. A population with millions of residents but, say, a few hundred neighborhood-location types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The facility location \(f\in\mathbb{R}^2\) is the decision variable, and the objective is the population’s \(p\)-norm of travel costs under the \(\ell_q\) distance:

\[ \mathrm{SC}_{p,q}(\mu,f) = \left(\sum_{x\in T}\mu_x\|f-x\|_q^p\right)^{1/p}. \]

Define \(M_\infty(\mu)\) to be the coordinate-wise lower weighted median: in coordinate \(j\), it is \(\inf\{a:\mu(\{x:x_j\le a\})\ge 1/2\}\). This matches the paper’s lower-median tie convention. The mirror compares \(\mathrm{SC}_{p,q}(\mu,M_\infty(\mu))\) with \(\min_f\mathrm{SC}_{p,q}(\mu,f)\). The society itself is the continuous object; the facility’s being in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is already part of the paper’s model.

This is a direct high-multiplicity lift for rational masses. Clear their denominators and replace each type by that many identical residents. The weighted median becomes the ordinary median, and the population objective differs from the discrete sum only by a common normalization factor, which cancels in the approximation ratio.

Lead anchor — Theorem 2, proved here in Section 5.2. It states that in \(\ell_q(\mathbb{R}^2)\), for \(p,q\ge 1\), the coordinate-wise median \(M\) has approximation ratio at most \(2^{1-1/p}\) when \(1\le q\le p\), and at most \(2^{1-1/q}\) when \(1\le p<q\). The continuous question is: given \(p,q\) and a support limit \(\tau\), determine the worst-case ratio over all rational-mass societies on at most \(\tau\) location types. A solution must prove the claimed upper bound for every such society. I expect the Class A answer: for every \(\tau\ge2\), the bound is \(2^{1-1/\max(p,q)}\), independent of support size. Theorem 2 supplies the upper-bound anchor.

Matching witness anchor — Theorem 4 (Lower Bound), also proved here in Section 5.2. It states that in the same planar setting, \(\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_p(M)\) is at least \(2^{1-1/p}\) when \(1\le q\le p\), and at least \(2^{1-1/q}\) when \(1\le p<q\). Its continuous question is constructive: given \(p,q\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), produce a rational-mass society whose coordinate-median ratio is at least \(2^{1-1/\max(p,q)}-\varepsilon\). I also expect a Class A answer; in fact, two types suffice.

For \(q\le p\), put half the mass at \((-1,0)\) and half at \((1,0)\). The lower median is an endpoint, while the optimal facility is the midpoint, giving ratio \(2^{1-1/p}\). For \(p<q\), put half at \((1,0)\) and half at \((0,1)\). The coordinate-wise lower median is \((0,0)\), while the midpoint minimizes the social cost, giving ratio \(2^{1-1/q}\). Together, the upper bound and these witnesses yield the exact continuous support-restricted ratio.

The mirror covers Theorems 2 and 4: the paper’s planar \(p\)-norm approximation guarantee and its matching lower bound. It does not stretch to the higher-dimensional bounds or the arbitrary symmetric-norm theorem. Natural follow-up questions are whether the higher-dimensional bounds in Theorem 3 are tight for small-support populations, and whether the same sparse witnesses persist under other median tie conventions.

The soft spot is strategic interpretation. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass and cannot change the median, so individual strategyproofness at the limit is vacuous. I would present this as a mirror of the paper’s approximation question for its fixed CM mechanism, with finite clone populations retaining the paper’s individual-strategyproof setting—not as a new continuum strategyproofness theorem. The other limitation is that the continuous ratio is inherited by denominator clearing, so this is a faithful mirror but not a new complexity frontier. The answer being the same is a useful identity result; it is simply not evidence that a hardness barrier has disappeared.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a scope objection: the paper has no theorem about the complexity or algorithms for a computational problem. Theorem 2 and Theorem 4 bound the distortion of a fixed mechanism, CM, relative to an optimal facility. That is a real computational social choice result, but it is the kind of approximation-ratio analysis the ChoCo brief distinguishes from its target of complexity landscapes for problems such as bribery, control, and robustness. The paper gives no computational task here whose complexity a continuous population might change.

Theorem 2’s proposed mirror is nevertheless well-defined. For rational masses, clearing denominators turns the society into a finite profile of clones, and the weighted objective differs from the clone objective only by normalization. So the continuous version asks for the same distortion guarantee over another representation of profiles; it does not introduce an algorithmic question. Theorem 4’s witnesses also survive: the \(q\le p\) case uses two equal masses, and the \(p<q\) limiting construction becomes a two-type population with equal masses. Those are valid mirrors of the ratio statements, but neither is a computational-complexity anchor.

A more ambitious version could ask for a weighted facility-location algorithm or a characterization of truthful mechanisms on population measures. The first leaves the paper’s results: CM is easy to compute from finite support, while finding the comparison optimum is a convex facility-location problem. The second has a basic modeling fork. In an atomless population, an individual has no effect on the outcome, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous; giving positive mass strategic power changes the model to agents or blocs who can move whole masses. Neither route turns this paper’s theorems into the kind of computational question ChoCo is designed to classify.

That is the best negative case, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proponent has a plausible population model and faithful mirrors of both named results. I would argue that these mirrors sit outside ChoCo’s computational agenda, rather than claim that the continuous questions themselves are incoherent.
