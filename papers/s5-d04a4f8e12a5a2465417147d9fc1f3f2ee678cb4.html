The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass population with finite support \(\Theta\) over complete worker types, rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\), type beliefs \(P_{\theta,s,j}\), and verified labels \(p_{s,j}\), does the full-feedback update \(w_{s+1}(a)=w_s(a)\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{m_s}\sum_{j=1}^{m_s}(\hat P_{a,s,j}-p_{s,j})^2\right)\) truthfully maximize each tagged worker’s expected cumulative weight and give sublinear regret? The platform aggregates by \(A_{s,j}=\frac{\int w_s(a)\hat P_{a,s,j}\,d\mu(a)}{\int w_s(a)\,d\mu(a)}\), and regret compares its cumulative squared error with the best positive-mass type’s cumulative loss \(\sum_s\frac{1}{m_s}\sum_j(P_{\theta,s,j}-p_{s,j})^2\).

The model it lives in

Use a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete worker profiles, with each agent retaining an individual reputation weight and payoff. On a symmetric type profile, the population integral reduces to the mass-weighted sum over types; an individual deviation is evaluated pointwise. The expected algorithmic route is a weighted-expert regret bound depending on the smallest positive type mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s full-feedback regret guarantee, with Proposition 1’s truthfulness result as support. It leaves Theorem 2’s limited-feedback mechanism, the benchmark results, and the downstream DPO experiments outside the proposed mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the update admit a mass-weighted regret bound of order \(O(\sqrt{T\ln(1/\mu_{\min})})\), where \(\mu_{\min}\) is the smallest positive type mass?
  • Can individual truthfulness be formalized for a tagged agent whose deviation has zero population mass but whose personal reputation and utility still change?

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a positive case for the full-feedback part of the paper, but it is an adjacent fit to ChoCo’s complexity agenda: the paper gives no named P, NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result. Its strongest named algorithmic anchor is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that the online weighted aggregation mechanism in Definition 4, with \(\alpha=\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2\ln N}{T}}\), has regret \(O(\sqrt{T\ln N})\) (written \(O(\sqrt T)\) for fixed \(N\)), so its time-average regret tends to zero. Proposition 1, also proved here, supplies the accompanying truthfulness result: reporting one’s true preference maximizes expected cumulative weight. I would use Theorem 1 as the anchor and Proposition 1 as support, rather than present either as a complexity-class classification.

A credible mirror is Mass-Weighted Truthful Online Preference Aggregation. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(\Theta\) of worker types, with rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to one. A type is a complete worker profile for this problem: its members have the same prompt coverage, private preference probabilities \(P_{\theta,s,j}\), and utility. At each slot \(s\), every worker reports \(\hat P_{a,s,j}\in[0,1]\) for each prompt \(j\); after the reports, the platform observes the binary ground truth \(p_{s,j}\). The action is the report, the mechanism’s state is each worker’s reputation weight \(w_s(a)\), and the platform’s decision is its weighted preference aggregate. On a symmetric type profile, that aggregate is

\[ A_{s,j}= \frac{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta w_{\theta,s}\hat P_{\theta,s,j}} {\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta w_{\theta,s}}. \]

The mechanism’s goal is to elicit truthful reports while keeping cumulative squared-error loss close to the best type’s loss:

\[ \mathcal R_\mu(T)= \sum_{s=1}^{T}\frac{1}{m_s}\sum_{j=1}^{m_s}(A_{s,j}-p_{s,j})^2 -\min_{\theta:\mu_\theta>0} \sum_{s=1}^{T}\frac{1}{m_s}\sum_{j=1}^{m_s}(P_{\theta,s,j}-p_{s,j})^2. \]

A solution is an online reporting and weight-update rule, with a proof that truthful reporting maximizes each worker’s expected cumulative weight and that \(\mathcal R_\mu(T)\) is sublinear in \(T\). The direct mirror of Definition 4 updates each worker’s weight by

\[ w_{s+1}(a)=w_s(a)\left(1-\alpha\frac{1}{m_s} \sum_{j=1}^{m_s}(\hat P_{a,s,j}-p_{s,j})^2\right). \]

When agents of each type follow the same report and have the same initial weight, the population integral reduces exactly to the finite sum above. A weighted-expert version should give a regret bound of order \(O\!\left(\sqrt{T\ln(1/\mu_{\min})}\right)\), where \(\mu_{\min}\) is the smallest positive type mass; the paper itself proves the corresponding finite-worker bound, not this mass-weighted statement. On a finite-support symmetric profile, each slot’s aggregation and updates take \(O(\tau m_s)\) arithmetic operations. I would expect this mirror to be tractable—Class A—though its contribution is an online mechanism and regret guarantee, not a newly resolved worst-case complexity problem.

The high-multiplicity regime is a large crowdsensing deployment with many users but relatively few exact feedback profiles: for example, cohorts sharing the same sensor feed, calibration, prompt coverage, and resulting beliefs. A discrete population with \(n_\theta\) workers of each type gives \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\); the type-weighted aggregate is the discrete aggregate grouped by type. Thus the bridge is exact for such grouped instances, and as the number of workers grows with the support fixed, the number of agents can be far larger than \(\tau\). The paper’s spectrum-sensing setting makes this scenario plausible, though narrow: a broad label such as “same device model” does not suffice if workers have different beliefs, costs, coverage, or utilities. Those differences must split the type, and if almost every worker has a distinct profile, the support advantage disappears.

This is a mirror of the paper’s aggregation problem, not of its downstream DPO objective: preference values are already continuous in the original paper, while the population is what becomes a continuum here. I would cover Theorem 1 and its truthfulness support from Proposition 1 only. I would leave Theorem 2, the limited-feedback result, out of this case: selecting and updating one individual per slot needs extra care in a nonatomic population, since changing one agent’s weight has zero effect on aggregate mass.

My weakest point is the gap between this result and ChoCo’s central complexity question. Theorem 1 studies regret over time; it does not establish that a discrete problem is hard, that continuous optimization dissolves that hardness, or that the authors’ central problem has a nontrivial complexity landscape. The proposed mirror is still recognizable to the authors—the population, reports, reputations, objective, and truthfulness criterion all remain—but it is a modest Class A online-aggregation mirror, not a hardness-transfer or hardness-dissolution result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent’s anchor is that it changes the strategic unit. Theorem 1 studies a finite set of workers, and Proposition 1 says each worker’s own report maximizes that worker’s expected cumulative weight. The proposed mass-weighted version instead gives each type one report and one weight. That works for symmetric, type-wide behavior, but it does not by itself represent a single worker deviating while the other members of the type remain truthful. In an atomless population, that deviation has zero mass, so it does not change the aggregate; yet Proposition 1 concerns the deviator’s personal weight. To model that payoff, the mirror needs individual weight and report histories, beyond the proposed \(\tau\) type-level state. If it keeps only one state per type, its truthfulness claim is about collective type deviations, not the paper’s individual truthfulness.

That is a real modeling gap, but not a decisive one. A pointwise mechanism could maintain each worker’s own weight while aggregating by type on the truthful path. The paper’s multiplicative update is simple enough that this may preserve both the individual incentive and the type-level regret guarantee. So I cannot honestly claim that Theorem 1 has no continuous mirror. Nor should I dismiss the theorem as merely axiomatic: it proves a named regret guarantee for an online mechanism.

The narrower case against is about fit with ChoCo’s computational agenda. The paper’s central result is an online truthfulness and regret guarantee over time; it does not analyze the complexity of a search or optimization problem over a society. The proposed finite-support mirror remains online aggregation with weighted experts. It does not put the problem within reach of the continuous-optimization tools that motivate the programme, or expose a population-driven complexity question. If ChoCo includes online mechanism design as a target, the mirror is credible. If its remit is the complexity landscape described in the programme, this is a neighboring question rather than a strong use of its effort.

Theorem 2 was not an anchor in the case for, so it needs no separate rebuttal. Overall, the negative case is weak on the existence of a sensible mirror: the finite-support, symmetric version is well posed. Its strongest point is that the proposed type-level model does not yet carry Proposition 1’s individual incentive claim, and that even a repaired version appears peripheral to ChoCo’s stated complexity focus.
