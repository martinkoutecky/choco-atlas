The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result that can serve as an anchor under your criteria. Theorem 2.1, proved here, derives LogOp from Bayesian assumptions; Theorem 3.1, cited from Dietrich et al. (2016), characterizes evidence preservation; and Theorem 3.2, proved here, classifies SProdOp as diluting, preserving, or amplifying evidence according to \(w\). None asserts a complexity or algorithmic result for a computational problem. The consensus-time findings are simulation results, not numbered complexity results. So there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem to state.

The most plausible population-continuum setting is a large robot swarm with a small number of shared sensor and reliability profiles. Type mass would be the fraction of robots with each profile; their beliefs would evolve through pooling and Bayesian updates. One could ask how quickly a limiting population reaches a specified consensus threshold, or which \(w\) minimizes that time. That is a sensible continuous-population question, but it is an extension of the paper’s simulation study, not a mirror of one of its named computational results.

That is the weak point in the positive case: the paper studies opinion dynamics and evidence propagation, while its numbered theorems establish properties of pooling operators, not the computational complexity of consensus or a social choice problem. A mean-field version may be natural, but its central question would be analytic dynamics rather than the computational landscape ChoCo targets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the strongest plausible setting: a large robot swarm with shared sensor profiles has a meaningful population distribution. I would not argue that multiplicity is implausible, or that prior mean-field work makes the question already done. The problem is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror.

Theorem 2.1 derives a Bayesian interpretation of LogOp. Theorem 3.1 characterizes operators that preserve evidence, and Theorem 3.2 classifies SProdOp by \(w\). These are mathematical results about a fixed pool of \(k\) agents and its pooling rule; none asks for an algorithm or establishes computational complexity. The consensus-time plots are simulations, not a formal problem with specified inputs and an algorithmic objective.

There are sensible ways to extend those results to a continuum population. For example, track the mass distribution \(\nu_t\) of belief values, model random \(k\)-agent interactions and evidence arrivals, and ask whether enough mass crosses a consensus threshold. The one-agent version of evidence preservation would become vacuous at zero mass, but that does not block the better formulation: intervene on a positive fraction of the population. The resulting question, though, concerns the evolution of a measure-valued state. Keeping only finitely many sensor profiles does not give finitely many complete types, because agents of one profile can hold different beliefs, and pooling and updating generate new belief values.

The proponent’s best candidate—minimize the time to consensus over \(w\)—is well posed and could be worthwhile research on swarm dynamics. But in the infinite-population limit it becomes a mean-field dynamics or control problem. Turning it into a computational-complexity question would require adding choices about how initial belief distributions are represented, what approximation is required, and what counts as an input instance. The paper does not motivate those choices, and the resulting problem is not an algorithmic mirror of any of its theorems.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit: continuous-population dynamics may be a valuable extension of this paper, but the paper does not present a computational social choice problem whose complexity landscape continuization would chart. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous study is possible; the natural swarm setting makes that too strong. The weakness is precisely that a broader definition of the programme might count the proposed mean-field question as a worthwhile mirror.
