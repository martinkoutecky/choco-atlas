The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying anchor: it states no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithms 1–3 describe coalition selection, leader selection, and invitations, and the paper reports simulation outcomes. Those are computational content, but they do not establish a named complexity result that I can mirror as requested.

The nearest positive lead would be a high-multiplicity version of buyer coalition formation. Let types represent buyers with the same reservation price and the same role in a finite-block social network; let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population share. A solution would choose mass \(x_t\leq\mu_t\) to join a coalition of size \(q=\sum_t x_t\), with nonnegative surplus \(\sum_t r_t x_t-qP(q)\), then choose a winning type with maximum degree and recruit reachable mass. A large e-commerce community with many buyers but few price classes and community roles is a plausible regime for that model.

The weak point is central to the paper’s contribution: its leader is an individual chosen by degree in a particular network, and recruitment depends on that individual’s connections. Grouping people into types may erase the identity and neighborhood that drive the scheme. So this is a plausible *unanchored* research question, not a compliant positive case resting on a named result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor, and that is the strongest objection. The paper gives algorithms and simulation results, but no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its pseudocode does not establish correctness or running time, and its simulations compare outcomes rather than classify a problem. So there is no complexity result here for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

The best candidate is a high-multiplicity buyer coalition model: types could encode reservation prices and roles in a finite-block social network, with mass joining the coalition and a leader inviting mass in connected blocks. That is more plausible than treating every buyer as unique. But it would formalize a new optimization problem. The paper does not specify one: its leader is an individual selected by network centrality, invitations depend on that individual’s links, and the relationship between new buyers, winners, and repeated coalition selection is left unclear. A sparse-network limit can also make the invited mass vanish; a dense block model avoids that, but adds a network model the paper does not analyze.

That leaves the negative case strong under the requested anchor rule, but weaker as a universal claim. A dense, typed buyer network could support a worthwhile continuous research question; the paper simply does not supply a named computational result for it to mirror.
