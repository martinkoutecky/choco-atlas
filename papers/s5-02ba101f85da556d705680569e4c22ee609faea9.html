What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. Its numbered results are social-choice characterizations and structural claims, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: when voters have single-peaked preferences over alternatives and responsive extensions over \(L\)-intervals, an interval social choice correspondence is anonymous, strategy-proof, and interval efficient if and only if it is a generalized median-interval rule. Proposition 2, also proved here, says that strategy-proofness and interval efficiency imply the rule is top-\(L\)-only. Neither is a computational result.

There is a plausible population-level extension: for example, residents choosing \(L\) contiguous plots for a public facility. A type would be a complete single-peaked ranking of the ordered plots, and \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s population share. Aggregating the masses of types by their top-\(L\) interval, then taking a weighted median with a limiting measure of fixed “phantom” intervals, gives a concrete continuous analogue of a GMI outcome. But computing that median would be a new, straightforward task—not a computational result anchored in this paper. And in an atomless population, an individual report does not change \(\mu\), so the paper’s strategy-proofness condition becomes vacuous unless it is replaced by a substantive mass or coalition manipulation condition.

So the paper offers a credible setting for a continuous-population follow-on, but it does not supply an eligible named computational anchor. I would not present that extension as a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes which rules satisfy anonymity, strategy-proofness and interval efficiency; Propositions 1 and 2 give structural properties of preferences and rules; Theorem 2 characterizes when responsiveness is needed. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous version of any of these would therefore be an axiomatic or structural result, outside ChoCo’s computational brief.

The proposed setting itself is sensible: a large population of residents choosing contiguous plots can have a distribution over complete preference rankings. Push that distribution forward to the residents’ top-\(L\) intervals, and the natural limit of a GMI rule is a weighted median of that mass together with a limiting measure of phantom intervals. But this gives a continuous formulation of the rule, not a computational question from the paper. Treating the weighted-median calculation as the anchor would add a task the authors did not study; its being easy is not the objection. The objection is that the paper establishes no complexity claim about computing outcomes, manipulation, or intervention.

Proposition 2 does not supply a missing anchor. Its top-\(L\)-only result says the rule ignores the rest of each ranking; in a population model, it lets us summarize the society by its distribution over top-\(L\) intervals. That is useful compression, but it remains a structural fact, not an algorithmic classification. The other candidate results have the same problem.

There is also a mismatch if one tries to turn strategy-proofness into a computational problem. In an atomless population, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot change the outcome, so unilateral strategy-proofness is vacuous. Positive-mass coalition manipulation or minimum-cost bribery could be meaningful, but either introduces a new intervention objective and cost model absent from the paper. That could become a separate project; it would not be a continuous mirror of this characterization.

So I would not count the paper as having a worthwhile continuous mirror for ChoCo. The honest limit of this case is that it rules out a mirror of the paper’s actual results, not every research question one could build around GMI rules. The population regime is plausible; what is missing is a computational result in the paper to carry across.
