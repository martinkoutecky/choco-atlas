What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers only Theorem 1’s co-NP-hardness result for the existence of Max-Egal manipulation by edge deletion; it leaves the paper’s other results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is a mass analogue of Theorem 1 for the paper’s Max-Egal manipulation problem. Theorem 1 is proved in this paper: given a directed graph \(G\) and a manipulator \(m^{-}\) who may remove outgoing edges, deciding whether *any* of LBM, UBM, WIM, or SIM exists under Max-Egal is co-NP-hard. The reduction is from the complement of 3-SAT. I would mirror that result alone; the paper’s other hardness results would require essentially the same change from an individual manipulator to a cohort.

Call the mirror Max-Egal Type-Mass Deletion Manipulation. An instance has a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a fixed \(k\ge2\), a designated type \(r\) with \(\mu_r>0\), and a rational directed interaction-density matrix \(a\). Here \(a_{tu}\) describes the fraction of type \(u\) that a member of type \(t\) regards as friends. An allocation is \(x_{tq}\ge0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to coalition \(q\), with \(\sum_q x_{tq}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tq}>0\) for every coalition. A type-\(t\) member assigned to \(q\) then has normalized utility
\[ v^a_{tq}(x)=\sum_{u\in T}a_{tu}x_{uq}. \]
This is a per-capita version of the paper’s number-of-friends utility; multiplying by population size recovers expected friend counts in a large, block-homogeneous network.

The organizer uses the reported matrix \(\hat a\) to choose a Max-Egal allocation: maximize \(\min_{t,q:x_{tq}>0}v^{\hat a}_{tq}(x)\). Write \(\mathcal O(\hat a)\) for all maximizing allocations. The type \(r\) cohort may hide ties by choosing \(\hat a_{ru}\in[0,a_{ru}]\); all other entries remain truthful. Its true utility in an allocation is computed using \(a\), not \(\hat a\). Define \(L(\hat a)\) and \(U(\hat a)\) as the infimum and supremum, respectively, over \(x\in\mathcal O(\hat a)\) of \(\min_{q:x_{rq}>0}v^a_{rq}(x)\). The decision question is: does there exist a deletion report \(\hat a\) making at least one of LBM, UBM, WIM, or SIM hold? Respectively, these mean \(L(\hat a)>L(a)\), \(U(\hat a)>U(a)\), both inequalities, or \(L(\hat a)>U(a)\). A yes solution is such a report. This preserves the paper’s important treatment of ties: the organizer may choose any reported-optimal partition, and the manipulator’s utility is evaluated on the true network.

A plausible regime is a large employer assigning many employees to \(k\) project teams. Types are recurring job-and-contact cohorts with the same rates of friendship nominations toward each other cohort; there may be hundreds of thousands of employees but only a modest number \(\tau\) of types. A department-sized cohort could report its contacts uniformly, hiding some nominations by rate. For finite populations with type counts \(n_t\), the masses are \(n_t/N\), and allocations can be rounded with small per-capita score error as \(N\) grows. This is population continuity: teams remain discrete coalitions, while the agents and their social interactions are represented by mass and type-level rates.

The mirror retains the organizer’s exact-\(k\) partitioning, the worst-off objective, strategic concealment of directed ties, and the distinction between reported and true utility. The authors could plausibly recognize it as a high-multiplicity, cohort-level version of their problem. The break from their model is real: \(r\) is a positive-mass bloc whose members share a report, whereas their manipulator is one agent. So this is a credible extension only if the authors accept coordinated, homogeneous cohort manipulation. It is not an exact translation of a single individual’s strategy.

For the intended low-support regime, I would provisionally expect the continuous problem to be tractable for fixed \(k\) and \(\tau\): there are finitely many allocation variables, and the reported and true utilities are linear in allocations once the occupied type-coalition cells are specified. That is a Class A direction for bounded support, not a claim of polynomial time when \(\tau\) is part of the input. Theorem 1’s hardness does not automatically transfer: its reduction uses individual graph structure, while the mirror permits type mass to split across coalitions. Hardness may still live in a growing number of types, but that would need its own reduction.

The main weakness is the cohort manipulator. If the authors regard coordinated reports by a positive-mass group as a different strategic problem, this mirror will not pass their recognition test. Its value is that it keeps the paper’s central manipulation question and changes the population representation in a principled way; whether that trade is acceptable is the substantive question for the prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational anchor: it proves co-NP-hardness for deciding whether a single agent can manipulate Max-Egal by deleting outgoing edges. So the negative case cannot rest on there being no result to mirror, and the hardness could dissolve under continuization—that would itself be an interesting result.

The difficulty is keeping that single agent meaningful in a continuous population. If the manipulator is one point in a nonatomic society, changing that point’s report changes the interaction kernel only on a measure-zero row. The organizer’s continuum optimization ignores that change, and Max-Egal’s essential infimum ignores the manipulator too. Give the manipulator positive mass and the report can matter, but the model has become coordinated manipulation by a cohort. That changes the strategic unit and lets the cohort alter an entire row of interaction rates at once. The paper explicitly studies one manipulator and leaves multiple manipulators as future work, so this is a substantial change to the problem Theorem 1 addresses.

There is a related mismatch in the fairness objective. In a finite graph, one exceptional agent can determine the minimum utility. For example, adding a single isolated vertex to a large complete graph makes the Max-Egal value zero, even though that vertex’s fraction of the population tends to zero. The graphon limit of those graphs is the complete graph, whose normalized Max-Egal value is approximately \(1/k\) for \(k\) balanced coalitions. A continuum based on essential infima loses the exceptional agent; a model that retains every such agent as a separate type can require support growing with the population. Restricting to a fixed number of positive-mass types avoids that problem, but again makes the manipulator a cohort.

The proposed rate matrix also assumes a particular social network regime: agents in each type have interchangeable patterns of ties to every other type. That could describe a dense, block-homogeneous organization. In ordinary friendship graphs, however, ties are sparse and point to particular people; replacing those ties with rates changes the utility data that drive manipulation. The resulting cohort problem may be worth studying, but it does not yet provide a clean high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s individual-agent problem or an obvious discrete-to-continuous dictionary.

This is a serious case against this anchor, not a universal no-go. A deterministic dense block network with cohort-wide reports is a plausible scenario, and its Max-Egal manipulation problem could be valuable. The weakness is that its value depends on the authors recognizing cohort manipulation in that network model as their problem, rather than as a different extension.
