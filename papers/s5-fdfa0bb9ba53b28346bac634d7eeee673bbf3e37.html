The continuous mirror question

Given a finite EIP set \(M\), a finite support \(T\) of service-provider types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), type budgets \(b_t>0\), valuations \(v_{tj}\ge 0\), and per-capita capacities \(c_j>0\), compute a Fisher equilibrium: prices \(p_j\ge 0\) and per-member allocations \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) such that each type's allocation maximizes \(\sum_{j\in M} v_{tj}x_{tj}\) subject to its budget, aggregate demand satisfies \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}\le c_j\), positive-price EIPs clear capacity, and type budgets are spent. For the paper's linear utilities, this is represented by maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t b_t\log(\sum_{j\in M}v_{tj}x_{tj})\) subject to those capacity constraints and nonnegative allocations.

The model it lives in

A Fisher market with a finite set \(M\) of EIPs and a distribution over service-provider types \(T\). Each type has a common budget \(b_t\) and valuation vector \((v_{tj})_{j\in M}\); \(\mu_t\) is its population mass, \(x_{tj}\) its per-member allocation, and \(c_j\) the per-capita supply of EIP \(j\).

The objection that survived

The mirror largely reproduces the standard linear Fisher market, for which polynomial-time methods are already known, so it may add little new complexity landscape; this limits novelty rather than well-posedness.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the paper’s first-stage market allocation, anchored on Theorem 1. It says that an optimal solution to the Eisenberg–Gale convex program is a market equilibrium; the dual variables for the EIPs’ capacity constraints are the equilibrium prices; and the resulting allocation is Pareto optimal and envy free, and satisfies sharing-incentive and proportionality properties. The paper derives the equilibrium connection using KKT conditions, with a citation to [39] alongside the welfare and fairness properties. The paper has no named NP-hardness or complexity-class result, so this is an algorithmic anchor rather than a hardness anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large MEC marketplace with many small service-provider tenants or standardized service contracts, but a comparatively small set of EIPs. Providers sharing a type have the same budget and the same valuation of each EIP’s resources. Those valuations can summarize the service’s relevant profile—such as its location, latency tolerance, request rate, and revenue—through the paper’s utility parameters. A type’s mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the buyer population. This could fit, for example, repeated contracts for the same service class and point of aggregation. To model a growing market, express EIP capacities per unit of provider population. The first-stage allocation is already divisible, so type-level per-capita allocations remain faithful to that stage of the paper’s model.

Lead mirror: Continuum CRB Fisher Equilibrium. Given a finite set \(M\) of EIPs and a listed support \(T\) of \(\tau\) provider types, an instance consists of rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), budgets \(b_t>0\), valuations \(v_{tj}\ge 0\), and per-capita EIP capacities \(c_j>0\). A solution is an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and a bundle \(x_t\) for each type. Each \(x_t\) must maximize \(\sum_{j\in M}v_{tj}y_j\) among bundles satisfying \(y\ge 0\) and \(\sum_jp_jy_j\le b_t\); aggregate demand must satisfy \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}\le c_j\); and each EIP with positive price must have its capacity fully allocated. The type allocations spend their budgets. For the paper’s linear utilities, the corresponding Eisenberg–Gale program is to maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t b_t\log(\sum_{j\in M}v_{tj}x_{tj})\), subject to \(x_{tj}\ge0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le c_j\). The question is to compute an equilibrium pair for rational input, with a natural complexity target polynomial in \(|M|,\tau,L\) (and the requested precision for an approximate version).

I expect this mirror to be tractable, Class A: it keeps the paper’s buyer utilities, budgets, market-clearing prices, and resource-allocation question, while representing repeated buyers by their supported types. The resulting program has size governed by \(|M|\) and \(\tau\), not by the number of service-provider accounts. A useful follow-on is to formalize the finite-to-continuum dictionary and quantify how equilibrium prices and allocations behave under finite-population deviations. Theorem 1 establishes the equilibrium relationship, but the paper does not state a polynomial-time bit-complexity bound; that would be part of the mirror’s research question.

This case covers theorem 1’s market-equilibrium result only. I would not extend the mirror to Theorems 2 and 3, which say that DCFA converges from any initial coalition structure and that every final partition it obtains is Nash-stable. Those results concern EIPs as individual strategic coalition members, and a continuum version would need a substantially different account of coalition membership and stability.

The weakest point is the regime: the paper’s motivating SPs include distinct firms, and its experiments use only four SPs. If each provider has a unique budget or valuation profile, then \(\tau\) grows with the population and the aggregation benefit disappears. The positive case depends on a plausible but additional setting—many repeated service contracts or tenants with the same effective profile. The phase-one question survives that assumption because the paper’s equilibrium is already defined by budgets, valuations, and shared resource capacity, rather than by providers’ personal identities.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins at the anchor: Theorem 1 identifies the Eisenberg–Gale optimum with a Fisher market equilibrium; it does not establish a complexity bound. Theorems 2 and 3 concern convergence and Nash stability of DCFA over a finite set of EIPs, not its running time or a population-complexity question. So the proposed mirror is not a continuization of a reported complexity result. It takes the paper’s optimization formulation and asks a new complexity question around it.

That is a real limit on the case for this paper, but it does not defeat the best mirror. A type can include the full valuation vector, budget, and the service parameters that determine it. Many independent tenants with the same profile could then form a type, with mass \(\mu_t\); scaling EIP capacity per capita is an allowed co-modification. This describes a recognizable Fisher market, and the paper’s first-stage model already treats resource allocation as divisible.

The skeptical version is that this mirror collapses to the standard linear Fisher market: each type of mass \(\mu_t\) can be represented by a buyer with budget \(\mu_t b_t\), and the resulting Eisenberg–Gale program has the same form. Exact polynomial-time algorithms for linear Fisher equilibrium are already known ([Devanur et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/market-equilibrium-via-a-primal-dual-algorithm-for-a-convex-program/)). That makes the mirror look routine and gives ChoCo little new complexity landscape to chart. But a known answer is not a valid reason to reject a well-posed mirror, and the weighted-type construction is evidence that the regime makes sense.

So I can make a case against prioritizing this paper: it has no named complexity theorem, and its strongest candidate mirror is a direct Fisher-market formulation. I cannot honestly defend the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Theorem 1’s market-equilibrium question survives; the negative case is weak precisely where the type-based scenario is strongest.
