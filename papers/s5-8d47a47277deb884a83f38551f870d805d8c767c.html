The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case for no worthwhile mirror is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its findings are causal estimates from a market-level experiment: for example, DA reduced justified envy in the implemented markets and had different effects under inexperienced managers. The paper states no complexity, optimization, or hardness result. A continuous algorithmic question inspired by those findings might be worthwhile, but it would be a new question beside the paper’s contribution, not a mirror of a named computational result.

The best candidate is a mass-based matching problem: represent workers and jobs by types with complete preferences and relevant constraints, then compute a stable assignment or the mass of justified envy. A stronger variant would model the mass of preference changes needed to secure first-to-first matches. There are plausible large-organization settings for these models, so I would not reject them merely because the paper’s preferences are individualized. But they separate from the paper’s actual findings. With fixed preferences and standard DA, stability makes justified envy disappear by construction. To recover the paper’s nonzero envy and strategic coordination, the model must also capture manager overrides, qualifications, and communication. The paper describes the traditional manager process as a black box and does not give these features a computational specification. Adding them would require substantial new modeling choices.

The other headline findings are a poor fit for a population-complexity mirror too. Treatment was randomized across 115 markets, and the manager-experience effects depend on market-level managers. Continuizing officers inside a market does not create more independent managers or treatment units. Retention and promotion are individual longitudinal outcomes, while performance evaluations include a cap tied to the officers a supervisor evaluates. A mass model could study average outcomes, but it would need a behavioral or statistical model beyond the paper’s matching mechanism.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about *this paper*: its empirical results do not furnish a computational anchor, and the most promising mass-matching questions require turning its implementation details into a new formal model. It is weaker as the universal claim that no plausible scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous research question. A repeated-cohort labor market could support one; the case against is that such a project would be adjacent to this paper, not its continuous mirror.
