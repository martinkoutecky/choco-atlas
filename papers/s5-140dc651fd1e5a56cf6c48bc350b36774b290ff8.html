What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no strict computational-complexity anchor in this paper: no numbered result classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its named results concern equilibrium existence, uniqueness, and welfare guarantees. So under your anchor rule, the paper has no qualifying anchor.

The closest performance anchor is Theorem 1, proved here. For two alternatives, there is a choice of \(c\) such that the QTM’s pure-strategy Price of Anarchy satisfies
\[ \operatorname{pPoA}(\mathbf v)\geq \max\left\{\frac12,\;1-\left(\frac{2}{T}\right)^{2/5}\right\}, \qquad T=\frac{V_1}{\max_{i,k}v_k^i}, \]
where \(V_1\) is the aggregate value of the better alternative. A useful structural result is Proposition 5, also proved here: with \(m=2\) and \(c\geq \tfrac12\max_{i,k}v_k^i\), the QTM has a unique pure-strategy Nash equilibrium. Neither result is a complexity result, so they cannot serve as anchors in the required sense.

If those equilibrium and performance results are accepted as motivation for a follow-on computational question, the most plausible mirror is Population-Limit QTM Equilibrium Computation. Consider a large civic membership deciding between two public projects, where members’ complete types are their value vectors \(v^t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^2\), and the type support is a modest set of standardized valuation profiles. Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population fraction and \(N\) the population scale, so type \(t\) has mass \(w_t=N\mu_t\).

Given the finite support, rational masses and values, \(c\geq \tfrac12\max_{t,k}v_k^t\), and an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), the problem is to output an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to the equilibrium outcome probability \(p\) and type-level votes \(a^t\). Define \(A_k=\sum_t w_t a_k^t\) and \(p_k=e^{A_k}/\sum_\ell e^{A_\ell}\); the votes must satisfy
\[ a_k^t=\frac{p_k}{2c}\left(v_k^t-\sum_\ell p_\ell v_\ell^t\right). \]
For integer masses, these equations describe type-symmetric equilibria of the finite QTM. Allowing noninteger masses defines the continuous extension. With two alternatives, the equations reduce to one scalar equation: if \(\Delta=\sum_t w_t(v_1^t-v_2^t)\), then
\[ \ln\frac{p_1}{1-p_1}=\frac{\Delta}{c}p_1(1-p_1). \]
This suggests Class A: the equilibrium probability is the unique root on the relevant interval and can be approximated by a one-dimensional numerical search. That tractability claim is my analysis of the proposed problem; the paper does not give an algorithm or a runtime bound for it.

The mirror covers only the paper’s two-alternative QTM analysis in Section 3. It does not cover the prediction stage of SQUAP or the external-welfare extension. Its plausible regime is a large group with many members per valuation type, so \(N\) is much larger than the support size. Theorem 1’s bound applies directly to integer-mass replications; extending it to arbitrary real masses would require checking the proof for the mass model.

The weak point is the equilibrium interpretation. A literal atomless agent has no effect on the outcome, so this model is best understood as a continuous mass extension of finite-replica equilibria, not a standard nonatomic Nash game. More fundamentally, the proposed computation problem is a credible follow-on question, but it is not anchored by a named computational-complexity result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational-complexity anchor in the paper. Theorem 1 bounds the QTM’s Price of Anarchy, and Proposition 5 proves equilibrium uniqueness; neither states an algorithmic problem or a complexity result. That leaves the proposed equilibrium-computation mirror to carry the case.

The natural high-multiplicity scenario is credible: a large membership with many people sharing valuation vectors. But the QTM’s incentives depend on each voter having a nonzero effect on the outcome. In a genuine atomless population, one person’s vote does not change the aggregate \(A\) or the softmax probabilities. With \(c>0\), each person then minimizes their voting cost by casting zero, so the outcome is uniform. That is not the nontrivial equilibrium analyzed in the paper.

The proposed equations avoid this collapse by treating \(w_t=N\mu_t\) as a number of finite, individually influential voters. For integer counts, that is a finite-replica game. Allowing real \(N\) extends its equilibrium equations to fractional counts, but does not make those equations an equilibrium of an atomless society. Treating each type as a single strategic bloc would also change the game: a bloc can coordinate a deviation that the original model gives to each voter separately.

The two-alternative case makes the gap especially clear. In the finite game, equilibrium computation reduces to a scalar equation in the aggregate value difference \(\Delta\), regardless of the support size \(\tau\). The continuum either collapses to zero votes, or retains the finite voters whose behavior already yields that scalar equation. Proposition 5’s uniqueness therefore becomes either trivial in the atomless game or inherited from the finite game; it does not supply a distinct continuous computational result. Theorem 1 likewise gives a welfare bound for finite replications, not a new complexity question.

This is the strongest case against the proposed mirror, though it does not prove that no useful follow-on question could be invented. Repeated valuation types make high multiplicity sensible here; the difficulty is that the QTM’s strategic incentives do not survive the atomless limit that the mirror needs.
