The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive case that meets the anchor requirement. The paper has no named computational result: it proves no hardness or tractability classification, and gives no analysis in terms of computational complexity.

Its numbered theorems are economic characterizations. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes the optimal arbitration rule and shows it is deterministic. Theorem 2, also proved here, shows an optimal mediation rule can be implemented by filtering information and adding noise; the rule itself was proposed by Blume and Board. Theorem 3 shows that finite negotiation reaches the optimal mediated outcome if and only if \(b\le 1/8\); the paper proves the result, building on the equilibrium construction of Krishna and Morgan. These are substantive results, but none is an eligible computational anchor.

The nearest population scenario would be a large institution handling many disputes, with type specifying the parties’ preferences and payoff parameters, and mass representing the share of disputes of each type. One could choose protocols to maximize aggregate decision-maker welfare. But if disputes are independent, this simply aggregates the paper’s per-dispute analysis; adding shared capacity or other constraints would create a new problem. Also, the paper’s continuum is a prior over the state in a single dispute, not a continuum of agents. Treating that prior, or the randomized recommendation, as population continuization would miss ChoCo’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the main obstacle: Theorems 1–3 are not complexity results about a computational problem. Theorem 1 characterizes an optimal arbitration rule, Theorem 2 an optimal mediation rule, and Theorem 3 when finite negotiation matches mediation. The paper formulates no computational task whose complexity those results classify. That is a serious gap for a programme about computational questions; it is not a claim that the economic results are unimportant.

The continuum already in the paper does not fill that gap. The variable \(\theta\) describes uncertainty about the state in one dispute, and \(p(dy,d\theta)\) describes a mechanism’s random recommendations. Neither is a population of agents. Recasting the prior as population mass would continuize the wrong object.

The three theorems do not provide an obvious population mirror either. For Theorem 1, a large caseload could be represented by a distribution over dispute types, such as different biases \(b\). If each case can receive its own mechanism, the cases remain independent; population mass just weights their welfare. A shared protocol, mediator capacity limit, or other coupling could make the aggregate problem more involved, but it would add a constraint the paper does not study. Theorem 2’s randomization over recommendations is within a dispute; representing many disputes by masses does not turn that lottery into a population. Theorem 3’s threshold \(b\le 1/8\) could yield the share of cases for which negotiation matches mediation, but that is a population summary of a per-case characterization, not a complexity result about negotiation.

The strongest plausible mirror is therefore a high-volume institution handling many similar disputes. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and it should not be dismissed merely because the paper studies two parties. But independent cases aggregate the paper’s results; coupled cases require new primitives such as shared capacity or a common protocol. Such an extension might be worthwhile, yet its computational questions would belong to the added institutional model rather than mirror a named result here.

So the negative case is strong for the proposed anchors, but weaker as a universal claim. The paper gives no computational result to anchor, and its existing continuum is over states, not agents. I cannot rule out a useful high-volume dispute-resolution model; the honest objection is that reaching one requires a new coupled population problem, not merely continuizing this paper.
