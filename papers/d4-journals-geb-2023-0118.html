The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest candidate is to compute the minimum price equilibrium in a large unit demand market. I should flag a mismatch with the requested anchor standard: this paper has no named result classifying a problem as polynomial time, NP hard, or otherwise. Its propositions analyze auction outcomes and economic properties. The closest computational process result is Proposition 7, proved here: for any positive increment \(d\) and bidding queue, and for arbitrarily large \(r\), there is a utility profile on which the approximate ascending auction’s price for every object undershoots its MPE price by more than \(r\); and, for each object, there is a profile on which its price overshoots the MPE price by more than \(r\). That is a serious result about an algorithmic procedure, but it is not a complexity result. So the following is a plausible mirror motivated by the paper, not a mirror anchored in the strict sense your instructions require.

My lead candidate is High Multiplicity Minimum Price Equilibrium Computation. An instance has a finite object set \(M\), a finite support \(T\) of complete bidder utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and positive rational per capita supplies \(q_l\) for \(l\in M\). Each type specifies a continuous, strictly decreasing, rational piecewise linear utility curve \(u_{t,l}(p_l)\) for each object, including income effects; the dummy has price zero. The question is to compute the coordinatewise minimum equilibrium price vector \(p^{\min}\) and an aggregate allocation \(y_{t,l}\) supporting it. The allocation must assign all mass, give each type only objects in its demand set at \(p^{\min}\), respect supplies, and fully use every object with positive price. A solution is that price vector and a supporting mass allocation. The computational target is time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), the number of utility breakpoints, and their bit length, independent of the population size beyond the bit lengths of the masses and supplies.

This describes a credible large market: for example, many buyers in a government program seeking copies of a small menu of permits or positions, with a relatively small number of shared willingness-to-pay and financial-constraint profiles. Here mass is the fraction of buyers of each profile; supply scales with the population so the market remains nontrivial. This scaling is a co-modification required by the setting. Fractional \(y_{t,l}\) records the share of a type assigned an object, while every individual still receives an integral bundle. With rational masses and supplies, scaling them to integer counts gives a transportation problem whose integral solutions can be implemented by assigning objects to individual copies of each type.

This is recognizably the paper’s problem: it keeps unit demand, general utility, prices, demand, and the MPE as the target. Proposition 7 motivates asking how to compute that target when the ascending procedure can be arbitrarily far from it. The paper’s discussion of large auctions and financial constraints also makes general utility types relevant. The proposed question covers the MPE computation underlying the paper’s auction comparisons; it does not extend their results on strategy-proofness or manipulation to a nonatomic population, where individual incentives would need separate treatment.

My working prediction is Class A, provisionally: at a fixed price, checking demand and finding a supporting aggregate assignment reduces to a flow problem over types and objects, and type-level representation removes the need to list a huge electorate. That structure suggests an algorithm may be possible. It does not itself prove tractability: finding the minimum prices with general piecewise linear utility curves is the hard step. Further questions include whether the exact problem is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and input bit length, and whether any mass based ascending auction has a finite error bound where Proposition 7 rules one out for individual bidders.

The weakest point is that the paper’s main named results are about the performance of particular auction procedures, not the complexity of computing MPEs. My mirror also requires an explicit utility representation that the paper does not supply, and it scales supplies with population. Those choices make the problem computable and the large market meaningful, but they leave this as a credible research question inspired by the paper, rather than a result its authors have already formulated.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Proposition 7 is not a computational-complexity result. It says that, for a finite-agent approximate ascending auction with increment \(d\), the outcome price can be arbitrarily far from the minimum-price equilibrium. Its counterexamples concern auction behavior at fixed \(d\), under a specified queue and agents’ choices. The paper gives no running-time, hardness, or approximation-complexity result to continuize. The same is true of its other central propositions: they establish failures of equilibrium identification, efficiency, or strategy-proofness.

The proposed minimum-price-equilibrium computation is therefore a new algorithmic problem on an equilibrium concept the paper studies, rather than a complexity mirror of Proposition 7. The paper’s fixed supply of one unit per object also makes the proposed per-capita supply scaling a substantive change in market regime. If supply stays fixed as the population grows, its normalized quantity tends to zero; scaling supply with the population avoids that limit, but changes what is being auctioned. The paper’s AA-auction counterexamples do not establish that either version creates a computational challenge.

That is the best negative case, but it does not support the universal claim. Scaling supplies is an allowed and potentially sensible co-modification: many buyers competing for repeated positions or permits could plausibly fit the authors’ setting. Computing the minimum-price equilibrium for a finite support of fully specified utility types could then be a worthwhile question, even though the paper does not analyze its complexity. Nor does Proposition 7 rule out a meaningful mass-based price-adjustment question; it shows why the finite-agent process needs examination.

So I can reject Proposition 7 as a *computational-complexity anchor*, but I cannot honestly defeat the stronger equilibrium-computation mirror. The case against a continuous mirror is weak precisely there: the paper supplies a natural equilibrium target and a plausible high-multiplicity regime, even if the algorithmic question would be new.
