The continuous mirror question

Given a directed network \(G=(V,E)\), a finite set of buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), source and destination vertices, integral request sizes \(q_t>0\), rational unit values \(p_t\ge 0\), and rational edge supplies \(b_e\ge 0\), choose accepted mass \(x_{tP}\ge 0\) routed on each available path \(P\) for type \(t\). Maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{P}p_tq_tx_{tP}\), subject to \(\sum_Px_{tP}\le\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{P:e\in P}q_tx_{tP}\le b_e\) for each edge.

The model it lives in

A continuum of all-or-nothing buyers grouped into complete types, with accepted mass routed over paths and aggregate requests constrained by per-capita edge supplies; maximize total revenue.

The objection that survived

Mass-AWDP is mathematically equivalent to the continuous-offer AWDP in Proposition 4, so its LP tractability is already established and the mirror adds little new complexity analysis.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the multi-item binary-offer AWDP hardness anchor through its uncapacitated, cost-free case; it leaves the VWDP results, integer-offer variants, special tractable structures, and application-specific models aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mass-LP allocation be rounded to \(N\) all-or-nothing buyers with an additive revenue-loss bound in terms of \(N\), \(\tau\), request sizes, and edge supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the market’s buyer population in a bandwidth auction. Imagine many customers requesting connections over the same network. Each customer’s type records the source and destination, requested volume \(q_t\), and willingness to pay per unit \(p_t\). Customers of a type are identical in every respect the auction uses. A standardized market might have \(N\) customers but only \(\tau \ll N\) such types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of customers of type \(t\), and let \(b_e\) be the available supply of edge commodity \(e\) per customer. Scaling the network’s supplies with the population keeps the market meaningful as \(N\) grows.

My lead mirror is Mass-AWDP. Its instance is a directed network \(G=(V,E)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), integer request sizes \(q_t>0\), rational unit prices \(p_t\ge 0\), and rational per-capita edge supplies \(b_e\ge 0\). Each individual customer still requests an all-or-nothing quantity and must receive it along a path from their source to destination. The decision is how much mass of each type to accept and route. For each type \(t\) and path \(P\) available to it, let \(x_{tP}\) be the accepted mass routed on \(P\). Find a feasible allocation maximizing revenue:
\[ \max \sum_t \sum_{P} p_t q_t x_{tP}, \qquad \sum_{P}x_{tP}\le\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t}\sum_{P:e\in P}q_t x_{tP}\le b_e\quad(e\in E). \]
The output is the accepted mass and its routing. A type’s mass may split across paths because different individuals of that type can take different routes; flow decomposition recovers those path assignments. Thus each individual still gets a whole offer, while the aggregate allocation can be fractional.

The hardness anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that each of the multi-item binary-offer AWDP variants—uncapacitated, capacitated, with flow costs, and with both capacities and flow costs—is NP-complete. The proof reduces Knapsack to the uncapacitated variant, then observes that it is a special case of the other three. Mass-AWDP mirrors that uncapacitated, cost-free variant. Its expected classification is Class A: tractable. The path formulation is equivalent to a multicommodity-flow LP with variables \(x_t\) and \(f_{t,e}\), one flow variable per type and edge; it has polynomial size in the network, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. In the paper’s Knapsack construction, an offer’s weight becomes its request size and its value becomes the total value of accepting it. The continuum lets the market accept a fraction of that type’s mass, which is precisely where the discrete knapsack choice becomes fractional.

This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ problem: it retains the network auction, buyers’ choice among paths, and competition for edge commodities. The many agents are customers, and shared endpoints, volumes, and price classes explain why they repeat types. The mirror does not claim that every market has this regime; when almost every bid is unique, \(\tau\) may be close to \(N\).

A useful corroboration—not a second anchor—is Proposition 4, also proved here: all four multi-item continuous-offer AWDP variants (uncapacitated, capacitated, with flow costs, and with both) are polynomial-time solvable by LP. Aggregating a continuum of identical all-or-nothing customers produces just such a divisible offer. That makes the proposed question especially concrete, but limits its novelty: the paper has already supplied the key algorithmic result in its continuous-offer model. The population mirror is the formal high-multiplicity interpretation and the bridge to the binary-offer hardness result, rather than a claim of a new LP algorithm.

The weakest point is that aggregation makes the mirror nearly coincide mathematically with the paper’s continuous-offer formulation. That is a real limitation on what new complexity result this particular mirror can promise. It does not erase the population interpretation: each person remains an indivisible bidder, and only the mass of identical bidders is continuous. A natural follow-up is to quantify how well the LP allocation can be rounded for a finite population as a function of \(N\), \(\tau\), and bid sizes, then ask which auction side constraints preserve this tractability and which restore hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchors is that Mass-AWDP is the paper’s Proposition 4 under a change of variables. This is a specific correspondence, not merely an objection that the paper already allows fractional offers.

For type \(t\), let \(z_t=q_t\sum_P x_{tP}\) be its accepted volume. In the paper’s continuous-offer AWDP, set the offer cap to \(d_t^{\max}=\mu_t q_t\), its unit price to \(p_t\), and its accepted volume to \(z_t\). The flow on edge \(e\) for that offer is \(q_t\sum_{P\ni e}x_{tP}\), and the edge capacity is the per-capita supply \(b_e\). Revenue and feasibility then match the proposed mass model. Thus the mirror is precisely the capacitated continuous-offer AWDP that Proposition 4 solves by LP; its support size \(\tau\) is the paper’s offer count.

Theorem 1’s hardness anchor has the same limitation. Its Knapsack reduction encodes each item as an all-or-nothing offer with its own weight and value. Treating those offers as population types changes their interpretation, but the continuous version relaxes the same offer variables that Proposition 4 already handles. In a repeated-customer regime, identical customers aggregate into one type offer. In the reduction’s heterogeneous regime, the types retain the separate item data. Neither case creates a new optimization problem beyond the paper’s continuous-offer formulation.

The suggested finite-population rounding question could be useful. It asks how discrete, all-or-nothing allocations relate to the continuous LP; the paper already studies binary and integer offer variants, though a sharper approximation result might add something. That would be a bridge or rounding project, rather than a new continuous complexity classification anchored on Theorem 1.

I would therefore oppose a standalone mirror project built around these results: the paper already provides the matching continuous formulation and its algorithm. The broader claim that no scenario supports a worthwhile population mirror is much harder to defend. Repeated buyers with shared endpoints, quantities, and prices are a sensible population regime, and the proposed capacity scaling avoids a degenerating limit. The negative case is strongest on novelty of the computational question, and weak on the possibility that a population interpretation or a more ambitious finite-to-continuum bridge could still be valuable.
