What the mirror covers

The proposed scoring translation covers Theorem 2; the paper’s other tops-only, maxmin, scoring, Condorcet, and small-domain results are also incentive characterizations, not computational anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is that the paper’s voting rules have a natural population version. Let types be rankings \(t\in T\), with mass \(\mu_t\); scoring totals become \(\sum_t \mu_t s(t,c)\), and pairwise majority comparisons become weighted comparisons. Large elections with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings are a plausible regime for this model.

To preserve the paper’s regret question, however, a reporter needs positive influence. One possible extension gives a focal cohort mass \(\alpha>0\), a true ranking \(P\), and the action of reporting \(P'\); the rest of society has distribution \(\nu\), and the outcome is determined from \(\alpha\delta_{P'}+(1-\alpha)\nu\). Regret can then be defined by the paper’s comparison across other-society distributions consistent with the observed truthful outcome. But this makes the strategic agent a cohort. A single agent in an atomless population has zero mass, so their report cannot change the outcome and regret-free truth-telling becomes trivial. The cohort version is a plausible extension, but it changes the unit of strategic action.

The paper’s nearest relevant anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for \(n>2\), no anonymous or neutral scoring rule with \(s_{m-1}<s_m\) is regret-free truth-telling. That is a substantive impossibility result, but it is not a computational result: it asserts no NP-hardness, tractability, or other complexity classification. The other named results likewise characterize or rule out incentive properties rather than classify computational problems.

So the paper contains no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor for this assignment. A cohort-based continuous question could ask whether regret-free truth-telling holds for weighted scoring rules at a given \(\alpha\), and finite profiles embed when \(\alpha=1/n\). That offers a route to study whether the paper’s impossibility persists. It does not supply a ChoCo complexity question per anchor, and the finite embedding alone says nothing about the high-multiplicity limit where \(\alpha\) tends to zero.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the closest thing to an anchor, but it is an impossibility result about an incentive axiom, not a computational result. It classifies when scoring rules are regret-free truth-telling; it gives no complexity question about an algorithm, an intervention, or an optimization problem. The other named results are likewise incentive characterizations or impossibilities. So the paper supplies no computational result for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

The strongest natural version of Theorem 2 replaces voter counts with masses and scoring totals with weighted sums. But its strategic unit is still one agent. In an atomless society, that agent has mass zero: changing their report leaves every score and the selected outcome unchanged, including at ties. The regret-free condition then holds automatically. That is a real degeneration of the paper’s central question: the individual’s report no longer has the influence whose consequences the paper studies.

The proposed focal cohort of mass \(\alpha>0\) avoids that degeneration by giving the cohort power to change the outcome. But its deviation is a coordinated group report, so the question becomes coalition-level regret, not whether an individual regrets their own report. Setting \(\alpha=1/n\) recovers the finite model, but as \(n\) grows the cohort’s influence vanishes; keeping \(\alpha\) positive keeps a strategically special bloc in the model. Treating the whole type mass as the deviator has the same problem: it changes the unit of action from one agent to a group.

One could still formulate worthwhile population-based computational questions about the minimum mass needed to change a scoring winner, or about finding regretful deviations for a positive-mass bloc. Those would be new questions about control or robustness, not computational mirrors of this paper’s results. The negative case is strongest for the paper-level mirror; it is weaker if ChoCo broadens its remit to invent adjacent computational problems around incentive axioms.
