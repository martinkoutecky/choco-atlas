The anchor — algorithmic

Proposition C.3

Every anchor argued

  • Proposition C.3 — survived: The continuous QRMed objective preserves the paper’s scale-calibration computation over a distribution of contributor types.

The continuous mirror question

Given a population of \(N\) contributors with rational type distribution \(\mu\) over finite support \(T\), where each type \(t\) specifies a QRMed input \(x_t\), uncertainty \(\Delta_t\), and weight \(\omega_t\), approximate within \(\varepsilon\) the minimizer \(q^*\) of \(\frac{q^2}{2\ell}+N\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\omega_t H_{1/2}(q\mid x_t,\Delta_t)\).

The model it lives in

Contributors are agents; each type records the complete calibration input to one QRMed call, including its estimate, uncertainty, and weight. Type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\); the scalar decision is \(q\), chosen to minimize the paper’s regularized Huber objective over that mass distribution.

The objection that survived

The paper’s deployed calibration records depend on pair-specific overlaps, so their support may be close to the contributor count; the paper provides no evidence that the proposed standardized regime achieves substantial compression.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the QRMed computation used in scale calibration, including its resulting scale adjustment; it does not continuize the full pipeline’s trust, preference-learning, scaling, or aggregation modules.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bit-complexity bound for approximating \(q^*\) follows from binary rational type data, in terms of \(\tau\), \(N\), \(\ell\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a focused one: Continuous QRMed scale calibration, mirroring the robust scale-calibration step in Solidago’s collaborative scaling module.

The anchor is Proposition C.3, stated in the paper’s Appendix C and presented there as the paper’s result. It says that QRMed can be computed by dichotomic search: initialization takes \(O(\log(1+|\mathrm{QRMed}|))\) iterations, the search takes \(O(\log(1/\varepsilon))\) iterations, and each iteration costs \(O(U)\) to evaluate the derivative for \(U\) contributors. This is an algorithmic result, not an NP-hardness or P classification; the paper gives no comparable hardness result to anchor a mirror on.

Here is the corresponding continuous problem. Consider a calibration cohort of \(N\) contributors, with types \(t\) drawn from a finite support \(T\). A type records everything relevant to this QRMed calculation: its relative scale adjustment \(x_t\), left and right uncertainty \(\Delta_{t,L},\Delta_{t,R}\), and per-contributor calibration weight \(\omega_t\). The society is the distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort of type \(t\). Given a regularization parameter \(L>0\) and precision \(\varepsilon>0\), find a rational \(\hat q\) within \(\varepsilon\) of the unique minimizer \(q^*\) of
\[ q^*=\arg\min_{q\in\mathbb R}\left\{\frac{q^2}{2L} +N\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\omega_t H_{1/2}(q\mid x_t,\Delta_t)\right\}, \]
where \(H_{1/2}\) is the paper’s uncertainty-aware Huber loss at quantile \(1/2\). For example, \(H_{1/2}(q\mid x,\Delta)=\sqrt{\Delta_L^2+(x-q)^2}-\Delta_L\) when \(q\le x\), and \(\sqrt{\Delta_R^2+(q-x)^2}-\Delta_R\) when \(q\ge x\). The calibrated scale factor is \(1+q^*\), as in the paper’s scaling construction.

This is a population mirror of a real operation in the paper: contributors’ relative scale estimates and uncertainties are aggregated to calibrate user scores. If \(N\mu_t\) is integral, the objective is exactly the finite QRMed objective after grouping identical contributors; fractional type masses extend that model to the continuum. The quadratic term makes the objective strictly convex, so the answer is unique. The cohort could be a large review community using shared calibration items and standardized rating and trust bands, where many contributors produce the same calibration record and \(N\) is much larger than \(|T|=\tau\). The paper’s report of more than 10,000 active users makes that population setting credible, though it does not establish that the deployed data actually has small \(\tau\).

The expected direction is tractable. Proposition C.3’s per-iteration cost becomes \(O(\tau)\) after grouping by type, with the same logarithmic search dependence on the target precision. A bit-complexity analysis for rational inputs would make the guarantee fully explicit. Further questions include whether the same population formulation and running-time bound extend to the asymmetric quantile QRQtl used for global scores, and how the type representation interacts with the full scaling pipeline.

My weakest point is that this is a scalar robust-statistics subproblem inside a collaborative scoring pipeline, rather than a conventional manipulation or control problem. Also, exact repeated calibration records may be uncommon unless the community uses standardized inputs. The case therefore supports a real, narrow mirror of the paper’s scaling computation; it does not support continuizing Solidago’s whole pipeline.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition C.3 is a real computational result, so I can’t dismiss the anchor on the ground that Solidago offers nothing to mirror. The difficulty is that QRMed’s input is already just a weighted collection of scalar calibration records. Its objective depends on contributors only through the weighted empirical distribution of \((x,\Delta)\). Grouping identical records into types therefore rewrites the existing computation: evaluate the same one dimensional objective by summing over \(\tau\) records instead of \(U\). The paper’s QRMed already accepts arbitrary real voting rights; there is no integrality constraint on the contributors or their influence for a continuous population to relax. A support based formulation is valid, but its computational content follows directly from the stated result.

The deployment gives little reason to expect that compression to matter for Solidago’s actual scale calibration. Each relative scale estimate is formed from a particular pair of contributors’ overlapping comparisons, and each contributor’s scale is calibrated against those specific peers. The resulting estimates and uncertainties depend on individualized comparison data. A complete type for that operation must preserve the relevant calibration record; the paper gives no reason to expect many contributors to share one exactly. The proposed scenario with common calibration items and standardized bands could manufacture repeated records, but those bands change the data the calibration module receives. It is a plausible redesign, not evidence that the deployed problem has a useful high multiplicity regime.

The wider mirror over types \((x,\Delta,\omega)\) remains mathematically sensible. Its objective is a direct weighted sum over those types, and derivative evaluation takes \(O(\tau)\). But it introduces no separation problem, combinatorial feasible set, or new optimization structure: the proposed algorithm is simply the existing scalar search with repeated terms combined. There is also a modeling choice hidden in the factor \(N\): with fixed regularization, increasing \(N\) makes that term negligible relative to the data loss; normalizing the loss instead changes the finite QRMed objective unless the regularization is rescaled. Neither choice makes the question invalid, but the mirror needs a specific population regime to say what Solidago computation it represents.

That is the strongest case against the anchor: it is a valid, narrow reformulation, yet the apparent population gain is either absent in the individualized calibration data or amounts to compressing repeated inputs to a scalar routine already given in the paper. I cannot honestly claim that no redesigned calibration system could make it worthwhile; the common-item, finite-alphabet scenario is conceivable. The negative case is therefore strongest against this being a compelling ChoCo complexity question for Solidago as studied, rather than against its mathematical well-posedness.
