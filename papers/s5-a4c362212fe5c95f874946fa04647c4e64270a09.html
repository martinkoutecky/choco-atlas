The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(A\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of strict-ranking types, decide whether there is a linear order \(\prec\) of \(A\) such that, for every \(t\in T\) and \(a\in A\), the upper contour set \(U_t(a)=\{b\in A:b\succeq_t a\}\) is contiguous in \(\prec\); return such an order or a forbidden subprofile.

The model it lives in

A society assigns mass \(\mu_t\) to each complete ranking type \(t\). The decision variable is a candidate order \(\prec\), equivalently an arrangement of candidates on a line, and every type with positive mass must have contiguous upper contour sets. Feasibility depends on \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\), not on the mass values.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers convex-representation recognition through Proposition 1; it leaves the paper's Euclidean-representation construction and its algorithmic steps aside. The forbidden-pattern answer is already supplied by the cited characterization.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one, anchored on Proposition 1. It is attributed to Ballester and Haeringer (2007), not proved in this paper, and the paper uses it in Step 2 of its Euclidean recognition algorithm. The proposition says that a profile has a convex representation on the line exactly when it contains neither of two forbidden patterns: a \(3,3\)-twisted subprofile, where three voters have three distinct alternatives as their respective least-preferred alternative among those three; or a \(2,4\)-twisted subprofile, where two voters rank four distinct alternatives in the specified pattern—one has \(a_r \succ_b a_q \succ_b a_p\), the other \(a_p \succ_c a_q \succ_c a_r\), and both rank \(a_s\) above \(a_q\). Here a convex representation means placing the alternatives on a line so that each voter’s upper contour sets are intervals among those alternatives.

My lead mirror is Continuous Convex-Representation Recognition. An instance is a finite candidate set \(A\) and a distribution \(\mu\) over a support \(T\) of strict rankings, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type is a complete ranking; its mass is the share of the population with that ranking. Decide whether there are candidate locations \(x_a\in\mathbb R\) such that, for every supported type \(t\) and every alternative \(a\), the candidates ranked at least as high as \(a\) occupy an interval in the induced candidate order. A yes answer can include the candidate arrangement; a no answer can include one of the forbidden subprofiles.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime when a large electorate or community of prospective residents has many people but a modest catalogue of distinct rankings: for example, millions of residents choosing among amenities, grouped by their complete rankings. The continuous input records the fractions in those groups. Proposition 1 gives the expected direction: tractable by checking the forbidden patterns across the support. That is Class A by structural recognition.

The weakness is substantial: \(\mu\)’s values do not affect the answer; only its support does. This is a continuous-population formulation of the convex-representation problem, but the masses add no computational content or new tractability. It also covers only the paper’s convex-representation subproblem, not its main Euclidean-representation algorithm. That main polynomial-time result is described through the algorithm’s steps and conclusion, but has no numbered theorem or proposition here. Proposition 1 is therefore the best named anchor I can defend; it is a cited structural characterization with an immediate polynomial-recognition consequence, rather than a numbered result explicitly stating a complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is a weak anchor for a computational mirror: it is a forbidden-subprofile characterization attributed to Ballester and Haeringer, not a numbered complexity result of this paper. The paper’s own polynomial-time result is the Euclidean-recognition algorithm, presented in prose rather than as a numbered theorem. Even granting the convex-recognition question, though, the deeper problem is that its population coordinate does no computational work.

For either convex or Euclidean representation, each supported ranking imposes constraints based on that ranking alone. Repeating a ranking adds no constraint, and its mass does not change the ones it imposes. So the proposed continuous convex-recognition problem is a support-pattern test: the distribution can be discarded after recording which rankings have positive mass. The homeowners scenario does not help; whether all homeowners’ rankings can be realized on a line does not depend on how many homeowners hold each ranking. This is a poor fit for a programme about computational questions that arise when the society itself becomes a distribution.

The stronger mirror would target the paper’s actual Euclidean-recognition algorithm: give each ranking type positive rational mass, then ask for a Euclidean representation for every type. It runs into the same obstruction. The paper assumes distinct preferences, and Step 1 observes that any representable profile has at most \(\binom{m}{2}+1\) distinct voters. Thus increasing the population while holding the candidate set fixed cannot make a representable instance grow through multiplicity; the algorithm’s relevant input is the distinct rankings. This is a case of no meaningful high-multiplicity regime for the recognition question, not a claim that its continuous answer is known or uninteresting.

A mass-sensitive variant could allow some population share to remain unrepresented, or ask how much mass must be removed to make the rest representable. That would make the proportions matter, but it changes recognition of a profile into a coverage or control problem and adds an objective absent from the paper. It might be worth studying on its own; it does not rescue a mirror of the results here. The negative case is strongest for the exact recognition questions. It cannot rule out every newly invented mass-tolerant problem, but the paper offers little reason to spend the programme’s time on those extensions.
