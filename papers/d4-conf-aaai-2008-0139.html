The proposed mirror changes that to parallel campaigns with deterministic mass flows while retaining a single indivisible prize, altering both the action and reward structure. That is a substantial extension rather than a continuous version of the anchored result.

What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I believe is a narrow mirror of Theorem 3, proved in this paper. Under Assumptions 1–4—values never decrease with deliberation, agents’ processes are independent, deliberation starts when the mechanism begins, and one action occurs per time step—the paper proves that the optimal policy is to activate the pruned, locally optimal Markov chain with the highest Gittins index at each step. The proof uses the paper’s reduction to bandits and the Gittins result cited as Theorem 1; the latter is prior work, not a result proved here. The paper gives no named hardness result, so there is no hardness anchor to prefer.

Lead mirror: Cohort-Mass Metadeliberation. Imagine a large industry competing for one exclusive technology licence. Firms fall into a small catalogue of types: each type completely specifies a firm’s initial state, possible research outcomes, value in each state, and research cost. There are many firms per type, with type \(t\) holding mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The number of firms is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of types. A research coordinator can run a campaign for a cohort of firms with the same type and current state; each firm in that cohort takes one deliberation step, with independent outcomes.

Precisely, an instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\), finite state spaces \(S_t\), initial states \(s_t^0\), transition probabilities \(P_t(s,s')\), nondecreasing values \(v_t(s)\), per-step costs \(c_t\), a common discount factor \(0\leq\gamma<1\), and a minimum viable campaign size \(\eta>0\). Let \(x_{t,s}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) firms in state \(s\). In a period the planner can stop and give the indivisible licence to one firm in an occupied state, or choose an occupied cell \((t,s)\) and a campaign mass \(q\in[\eta,x_{t,s}]\). The campaign costs \(q c_t\); in the continuum limit its agents’ independent transitions produce deterministic mass flows, with \(qP_t(s,s')\) moving to each successor state. The licence goes to a firm in the occupied cell with the highest \(v_t(s)\). The planner maximizes discounted licence value minus discounted campaign costs. The computational question is: given the instance and rational \(\epsilon>0\), approximate the supremum welfare to additive error \(\epsilon\), or provide a policy with that guarantee.

This preserves the paper’s central decision: whom to research, when to stop, and whom to award the resource to. It also gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many similar firms, but relatively few research-process types. Prices and costs varying by named individual would simply require more types, as in any high-multiplicity model. The authors might recognize this as their planning problem for a large cohort with coordinated research campaigns, though they may regard the campaign action as a substantive extension.

For this stated mirror, I would initially expect Class C, continuum-specific hardness. The mass-flow state and the terminal reward for the highest-valued *occupied* cell create structure absent from the individual-arm Gittins policy. The theorem is still a useful anchor: it suggests pruning and index-based bounds, and motivates asking whether those tools extend to campaign decisions. It does not establish that they do. A prover could investigate whether an LP or index formulation handles the mass flows, or whether the support-based terminal reward makes the problem hard.

This covers only the paper’s optimal deliberation-allocation policy in Theorem 3. It does not mirror the incentive-compatibility results, the dynamic-VCG mechanism, or the strategic-deliberation extension.

The weakest point is the change in action granularity. A single action on one person has zero mass in an atomless population, so a workable population model needs cohort actions; the law of large numbers also turns individual random outcomes into deterministic mass flows. The minimum campaign size is a realistic way to make cohort research operational, but neither change is part of Theorem 3. If the authors require the literal single-agent, one-action-per-period model, this mirror fails. I think it remains worth asking because it keeps the paper’s research-and-allocation objective intact while making the population itself the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine computational anchor, and a large population of firms sharing a small number of complete research-process types is plausible. The difficulty is that the theorem’s decision-relevant unit is one firm at a time, while its payoff comes from giving one indivisible resource to one firm. In a population of \(N\) firms, a deliberation changes only \(1/N\) of the mass and costs \(c_i\), yet it can change the eventual winner’s value by an amount of order one. The mass distribution therefore loses information that matters to the theorem. Normalize welfare by \(N\), and the value of the single winner vanishes; keep welfare unnormalized, and it depends on an extreme—the best individual—not an average captured by the distribution.

The proposed cohort campaign tries to repair this by deliberating on mass \(q\) at once. But that changes the bandit problem’s action: it replaces one deliberation with roughly \(qN\) independent deliberations in parallel. It also changes the cost scale. If each firm pays \(c_i\), a campaign on \(qN\) firms costs \(qNc_i\); a cost of \(qc_i\) instead makes each firm’s cost shrink with \(N\). Keeping the winner’s value at order one while using that shrinking per-firm cost is a new economic model, not a limit of the paper’s one-agent-at-a-time process.

Parallel campaigns also erase the uncertainty that makes the Gittins-index result meaningful. If a successor state has positive transition probability, then a positive campaign mass sends positive mass to that state deterministically. The winner can be selected from its occupied cell, so every positive-probability high-value outcome is available in the continuum. Without a minimum campaign size, the planner can take \(q\) arbitrarily close to zero, pay arbitrarily little, and still obtain those outcomes. A fixed minimum \(\eta\) avoids that collapse, but makes the answer depend on a new campaign-scale parameter and no longer approximates single-agent deliberation.

The best repair would preserve a finite number of individual research actions, retaining their random outcomes, while treating the unresearched firms as a continuous reservoir. That can be a sensible model, but its state must still track the researched individuals as atoms: their states determine both the remaining options and the eventual winner. The continuous population then supplies repeated copies of arms; it does not replace the individual-level state that drives Theorem 3. The paper’s mechanism results face the same split: individual payments and cross-agent deliberation require individual-level consequences, while cohort-level incentives would introduce a different game among participants.

This is a strong objection to a faithful continuous mirror, but not an airtight argument against every worthwhile extension. A real research consortium might deliberate on cohorts, and its mass-control problem could merit study. The negative case is that the cohort version changes the action scale and removes the stochastic search structure, while the version that preserves that structure still needs individual atoms. Whether the authors would recognize the former as their problem is the unresolved weak point.
