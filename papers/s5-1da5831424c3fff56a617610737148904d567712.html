The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates \(C\), a target \(p\), a finite support \(S\) of complete rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(\beta\), does there exist a transfer \(x_{t,u}\geq 0\) for distinct \(t,u\in S\), with \(\sum_{u\ne t}x_{t,u}\leq\mu_t\) for every \(t\) and \(\sum_{t\ne u}x_{t,u}\leq\beta\), such that \(p\) is a 3-Veto winner under the resulting masses \(\nu_u=\mu_u-\sum_{v\ne u}x_{u,v}+\sum_{t\ne u}x_{t,u}\)?

The model it lives in

Treat each ranking \(t\in S\) as a type carrying mass \(\mu_t\). Choose transfers \(x_{t,u}\) only between types in \(S\), minimize the total moved mass \(\sum_{t\ne u}x_{t,u}\), and ask whether the minimum is at most \(\beta\), subject to the linear 3-Veto winner inequalities for \(p\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 8's continuous transfer problem is already covered by the existing Minimum Move\(_\infty\) result, so this mirror adds no new continuous algorithmic result; that limits novelty, not the mirror's validity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the computational question behind Theorem 8, and the same mass-allocation approach also applies to Theorem 1. It leaves the exact-control trichotomy and the first-last equivalences untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the fractional optimum yield an additive approximation for finite \(n\)-voter conformant bribery when the support size \(\tau\) is moderate, with the guarantee accounting for the winner margin?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is the paper’s Theorem 8, with Theorem 1 as a second, distinct anchor. Both are proved in this paper by reductions from 3-Dimensional Matching.

The regime I have in mind is a large membership election or party primary: perhaps \(N\) is in the hundreds of thousands, while ballots cluster into \(\tau\) recurring complete rankings, with \(\tau\) much smaller than \(N\). In the bribery setting, a campaign can persuade a share of voters to use ballot patterns already present. In the manipulation setting, a coordinated bloc shares a preferred candidate and chooses among those same patterns. Each person still casts a complete ranking; the continuum lets mass of one ranking type be split across choices. These are scenario claims, not claims that every election has few ranking types.

Lead anchor: Theorem 8. As stated and proved here, *3-Veto Conformant Bribery is NP-complete*. In the paper’s problem, the input is an election, a bribe limit \(k\), and a preferred candidate \(p\). The question is whether the briber can change the votes of at most \(k\) voters so that \(p\) wins, with every changed vote required to be a vote that appeared in the original election.

Here is the continuous version I would put to a prover. Call it Continuous 3-Veto Conformant Bribery. An instance consists of \(m\) candidates, a target \(p\), a finite support \(S\) of complete rankings, positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) for \(t\in S\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(\beta\). Choose nonnegative transfer masses \(x_{t,u}\) for distinct \(t,u\in S\), where \(x_{t,u}\) is mass initially ranking \(t\) that the briber persuades to rank \(u\). Require \(\sum_{u\ne t}x_{t,u}\leq\mu_t\) for each source type \(t\), and \(\sum_{t\ne u}x_{t,u}\leq\beta\). The resulting mass on ranking \(u\) is \(\nu_u=\mu_u-\sum_{v\ne u}x_{u,v}+\sum_{t\ne u}x_{t,u}\). The question is whether some such transfer makes \(p\) a 3-Veto winner; a solution is the transfer matrix \(x\).

This is a linear-program feasibility question: every candidate’s 3-Veto score is a linear function of \(\nu\), and winner status is the set of inequalities \(\mathrm{sc}_\nu(p)\geq\mathrm{sc}_\nu(c)\). There are \(O(\tau^2)\) transfer variables and polynomially many constraints, so the continuous problem is in P, polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. This is a direct continuation of the paper’s action: the source voter’s ballot determines where mass leaves, and the set of initially observed ballots remains the briber’s menu. The 3-Dimensional Matching reduction makes a particularly apt hardness anchor: its discrete construction relies on selecting whole voters and enforcing an integral cover. The continuous question tests whether that integrality is what makes the problem hard.

Second anchor: Theorem 1. The paper proves that *4-Approval Conformant Manipulation is NP-complete*. Its underlying decision problem takes an election \((C,V)\), a collection \(W\) of manipulators, and a preferred candidate \(p\); it asks whether the manipulators can cast votes drawn only from votes occurring in \(V\) so that \(p\) wins in the resulting election \((C,V\cup W)\).

Call the continuous version Continuous 4-Approval Conformant Manipulation. Its input is a support \(S\) of complete rankings with binary-rational background masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a target \(p\), and a rational coalition mass \(\kappa\), measured relative to the background population. Choose \(x_t\geq0\) for \(t\in S\), with \(\sum_{t\in S}x_t=\kappa\), where \(x_t\) is the coalition mass casting ranking \(t\). The final society is the distribution \(\nu_t=(\mu_t+x_t)/(1+\kappa)\); the question is whether \(p\) is a 4-Approval winner there. Again, a solution is a mass allocation, and the winner inequalities are linear, so this is an LP with \(O(\tau)\) variables and polynomial running time.

The authors should recognize both questions as mirrors of their problems: the paper defines conformity through the menu of votes already present, and both continuous versions preserve that menu and the same winner condition. The modifications are to the granularity of action—mass can be divided among rankings—and to the input’s representation as a distribution. For bribery, destination ballots have no capacity bound: the paper lets any number of bribed voters adopt a ballot that appeared at least once, so the continuous mirror should not cap incoming mass by the ballot’s original mass.

I am covering only these two conformant-action hardness results. I am not claiming that the paper’s full exact-control trichotomy, first-last results, or every result about control has an equally convincing continuous mirror. For the two questions above, I expect tractability: the integral selection choices in the discrete problems become linear mass allocation.

They also generate useful follow-up questions. If type-to-type persuasion costs \(\gamma(t,u)\) are supplied, does minimum-cost conformant mass bribery remain an LP for an explicitly listed support? How well can an optimum with fractional transfers predict a finite election whose voters must move in whole units, especially near ties? And if the allowed ballot menu is described implicitly rather than listed as the support, does the problem acquire a genuine pricing or separation challenge?

The soft spot is that the relaxation removes exactly the indivisibility that the theorems study: a briber may transfer a fraction of a type, and a coalition may split its mass among votes. Some authors might regard that as a useful high-multiplicity version; others might regard indivisible voters as essential to their question. The continuum also makes these two particular problems rather simple LPs rather than examples requiring advanced continuous-optimization machinery. My positive case is therefore deliberately narrow: these are credible population mirrors whose central computational question is whether the paper’s discrete hardness dissolves when voters of each type are treated as mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is asymmetric. Theorem 8 is the weaker anchor: its proposed continuous bribery problem is already covered by ChoCo’s continuous Minimum Move\(_\infty\) result. For support \(S\), assign cost \(1\) to changing a type into a different type in \(S\), cost \(0\) to leaving it unchanged, and \(+\infty\) to any output outside \(S\). Then the minimum move cost is exactly the mass bribed, so the paper’s budget question is whether that optimum is at most \(\beta\). Since 3-Veto has linear winner constraints, this is an instance of the existing scoring-rule formulation. Theorem 8 could illustrate that result, but it does not supply a new continuous question.

That does not dispose of Theorem 1. Its best mirror is coherent: take recurring ballot types with masses \(\mu_t\), and a coordinated bloc of positive mass \(\kappa\) whose members share the target \(p\). Allocating that bloc across the explicitly listed support \(S\) gives linear winner constraints. This really does continuize the population, and the problem tracks ballot types rather than named voters. A positive-mass coalition is a plausible regime; the paper’s assumption that manipulators share \(p\) gives a complete enough description for this problem.

The strongest objection is that conformity depends on the *support*, not just on the masses: a ballot type with arbitrarily small positive mass is available as a destination, potentially without limit. So the action menu can change discontinuously when a type’s mass reaches zero. That complicates interpreting the model as a stable limit of finite elections. But the input explicitly records the support, and a regime with recurring ballot types avoids the vanishing-type case. This is a modelling caveat, not a fundamental failure.

Theorem 1’s continuous problem is also a small LP, and its discrete matching hardness disappears under divisible mass. Neither point defeats it: the programme treats tractability after continuization as a valuable result, and this is exactly the question the mirror is meant to ask. The honest case against is therefore narrow: Theorem 8 is already subsumed by existing ChoCo work, and Theorem 1 may be a modest contribution with limited new optimization machinery. I cannot make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists; Theorem 1 survives it.
