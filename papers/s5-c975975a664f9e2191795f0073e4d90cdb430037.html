What the mirror covers

The proposed type-mass tally covers only the weighted-vote pass predicate; it is not anchored by a named computational result and does not mirror Theorem 4.3's LDP guarantee or the empirical utility comparisons.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability or hardness. Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.2 give randomized-response matrices that satisfy differential-privacy guarantees; they are cited from Wang, Wu, and Hu (2016). Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper, says that DPWeVote satisfies \(\epsilon\)-local differential privacy. None classifies the computational complexity of a problem. The paper’s experiments report utility measurements, but they are not numbered complexity results. So there is no eligible anchor for the requested continuous-complexity mirror.

The strongest plausible *candidate* mirror is the underlying weighted-vote tally. Imagine a large consortium in which many participating organizations share one of three voting weights and, for a given proposal, one of two opinions. The six complete types are \((w,\phi)\in\{1,2,3\}\times\{0,1\}\); \(\mu_{w,\phi}\) is each type’s fraction of the consortium. Given these rational masses, ask whether the proposal passes, and return the exact answer. With the paper’s majority quota, this is whether
\[ \sum_{w,\phi} \mu_{w,\phi}w\phi \;\ge\; \frac12\sum_{w,\phi}\mu_{w,\phi}w. \]
It is a sensible continuous-population version of weighted voting and is directly computable from the type masses.

But that candidate does not mirror the paper’s central contribution: protecting each partner’s private weight and opinion with local differential privacy. In a continuum of mass, an individual partner has zero mass, so changing that partner’s data does not change the aggregate. The privacy question therefore loses its individual-level meaning unless the model adds a finite-agent or atomic structure—which would need its own justification. And the tally candidate is not anchored by a numbered computational result in this paper. I would not present it as satisfying the requested standard.

That is the case’s weakest point, and it is decisive for this task: the paper may motivate a continuous model of weighted voting, but it supplies no named computational result to anchor a continuous-complexity question. Theorem 4.3 is a privacy result, not a tractability result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.3 proves an \(\epsilon\)-LDP guarantee; Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 state randomized-response matrices. The experiments measure utility. None classifies the complexity of a computational social choice problem. That leaves no result-level anchor for the requested mirror.

The proposed tally is a sensible continuous weighted-vote model: a large consortium could have masses \(\mu_{w,\phi}\) over the six \((w,\phi)\) types, and the proposal passes when
\[ \sum_{w,\phi}\mu_{w,\phi}w\phi \ge \frac12\sum_{w,\phi}\mu_{w,\phi}w. \]
I would not object that such a population is implausible. But this is the tally that DPWeVote uses, not a computational result the paper claims. It strips away the paper’s contribution: collecting individual weights and opinions under local privacy.

The stronger attempt to mirror Theorem 4.3 runs into a more fundamental mismatch. Local differential privacy protects each partner’s report. In a nonatomic society, one partner has zero mass, so changing that partner’s data cannot affect the aggregate; privacy becomes vacuous. If instead each infinitesimal mass independently applies randomized response, the exact continuum aggregate is deterministic and equals the expected report distribution. That removes the finite-sample noise behind the paper’s privacy–utility analysis. Restoring that analysis requires a finite number \(n\) of partners, where each report again belongs to an individual. Defining privacy between distributions that differ by some mass \(\delta\) is possible, but it changes the protected unit and the privacy question.

One could study an optimized private voting protocol for a distribution over types, but that would be a newly posed privacy and statistical-design problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The negative case is strongest against using DPWeVote as a ChoCo complexity anchor; it cannot rule out every worthwhile research question adjacent to continuous populations.
