The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(q>1\), a rationally encoded piecewise-polynomial density \(\mu\) on \([0,1]\) promised to have strictly increasing \(G_\mu^q\), and precision \(\varepsilon>0\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-uniform approximation to \(g=(G_\mu^q)^{-1}\) and an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to \(F_g(\mu)=\sup\{y:\mu([y,1])\ge g(y)\}\). Require that for every electorate size \(n\), the empirical rule \(\phi_g(r_1,\ldots,r_n)=\sup\{y:\#\{i:r_i\ge y\}/n\ge g(y)\}\) is anonymous, voter-sovereign, strategy-proof, and minimizes the paper’s expected \(L_q\) loss for \(n\) i.i.d. ideal points from \(\mu\).

The model it lives in

Types are residents’ ideal points \(x\in[0,1]\), with mass distribution \(\mu\). The decision variable is an increasing grading curve \(g\) in the limit family of finite anonymous, strategy-proof rules; the finite-i.i.d. welfare objective selects the curve, and the population outcome is \(F_g(\mu)\). The atomless limit does not itself impose individual strategy-proofness.

The objection that survived

For a fixed society \(\mu\), limiting welfare depends on \(g\) only through \(F_g(\mu)\), so it does not uniquely select a curve; curve selection comes from the finite-i.i.d. objective, and strategy-proofness itself is vacuous for an atomless individual.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the anonymous ex-ante welfare optimization theorem in §6.2 and its uniform-prior consequences, including the linear median at \(q=2\); it leaves the axiomatic characterizations, consistency and participation results, minimax objective, and weighted extensions outside this formulation.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the bit and approximation complexities of computing \(g\) and \(F_g(\mu)\) from the stated finite encoding?
  • Can strict increase of \(G_\mu^q\) be checked efficiently from that encoding?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a high multiplicity version of the paper’s ex ante welfare optimization under strategyproofness, rather than for its axiomatic characterizations generally. The society would consist of many residents with ideal points on a line. A type is an ideal point \(x\in[0,1]\), and \(\mu\) gives the fraction of residents of each type. A single increasing grading curve \(g\) specifies a family of anonymous voting rules. On a finite electorate with empirical type distribution \(\nu_n\), the rule selects
\[ \phi_g(\nu_n)=\sup\{y:\nu_n([y,1])\ge g(y)\}. \]
For a continuum society, the corresponding outcome is \(F_g(\mu)=\sup\{y:\mu([y,1])\ge g(y)\}\). The decision is the curve \(g\); the objective is to choose it to minimize aggregate \(L_q\) distance from residents’ ideal points, using the paper’s finite-electorate ex ante objectives to select the curve. The continuum rule is the limit of those anonymous, strategy-proof rules as the electorate grows.

My lead anchor is the theorem in §6.2 beginning “Let \(p\) be a probability distribution…” No numerical theorem number is printed in the supplied text, so I can’t quote one without inventing it; if a numbered result is mandatory, that limits the case. The theorem says that when \(G_p^q\) is strictly increasing, its inverse \(g_p^q\) generates the unique anonymous, voter-sovereign, strategy-proof voting function minimizing \(L_q\) welfare loss integrated over iid voter inputs drawn from \(p\). This result is proved in the paper’s Appendix G.2, with an extension there to weighted voters. It is a computational anchor in the paper’s stated sense: it gives the optimizing mechanism, and the paper supplies formulas for computing outcomes from its grading curve. The special uniform-prior result in §6.2.2 is a consequence; for \(q=2\) on \([0,1]\), it gives the linear median \(g(y)=y\).

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Limit Welfare-Grading Curve. Its input is \(q\) and a computable distribution \(\mu\) of ideal points on \([0,1]\), with \(G_\mu^q\) strictly increasing. Its output is an increasing curve \(g\) such that, for every electorate size \(n\), the rule
\[ \phi_g(r_1,\ldots,r_n) =\sup\left\{y:\frac{\#\{i:r_i\ge y\}}{n}\ge g(y)\right\} \]
is the anonymous, voter-sovereign, strategy-proof welfare optimizer under iid draws from \(\mu\); the prover must also give the induced mass-society outcome \(F_g(\mu)\). The paper’s candidate solution is \(g=(G_\mu^q)^{-1}\). A concrete input regime would be a large municipality choosing a public facility location, with many residents but a describable distribution of ideal locations. The continuous output is a threshold rule on population fractions, rather than a count of individual ballots.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, or Class A: the paper reduces the optimization to explicit one-dimensional integrals and inversion of a monotone function. For piecewise rational input distributions, the downstream questions are the bit complexity of evaluating those integrals and curve, how to check the strict-monotonicity condition, and approximation guarantees for the finite-electorate rule. This mirror covers the welfare optimization in §6.2, supported by the grading-curve and variable-electorate results; it does not claim to continuize all the paper’s axiomatic characterizations.

The weak point is that the paper’s \(p\) is a prior over finite iid profiles, whereas \(\mu\) here is the society itself. Also, individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous for an infinitesimal agent acting alone. The mirror therefore relies on treating admissible continuum rules as limits of the paper’s finite anonymous, strategy-proof rules, and on reading the prior as the high-multiplicity type distribution. That is a genuine modeling bridge the authors would need to accept; it is not a direct restatement of their theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The single anchor is the welfare-optimization theorem in §6.2 beginning “Let \(p\) be a probability distribution…”; the supplied text gives it no theorem number. It characterizes an optimal grading curve, but it is not a complexity result for a population intervention. More fundamentally, its \(p\) is a prior that independently generates each voter’s report. The theorem optimizes a rule over the resulting distribution of finite profiles, not over one society described by its type masses.

Set \(p=\mu\) and take the proposed limit. For regular grading curves, the empirical profile converges to \(\mu\), and the per-capita objective converges to
\[ \int |x-F_g(\mu)|^q\,d\mu(x),\qquad F_g(\mu)=\sup\{y:\mu([y,1])\ge g(y)\}. \]
That objective depends on \(g\) only through the single outcome \(F_g(\mu)\). It selects a welfare-optimal location—such as the mean when \(q=2\)—but generally does not select a unique grading curve. The paper’s finite-electorate uniqueness uses welfare across random profile fluctuations; those fluctuations disappear in the deterministic population limit.

There are two ways to restore what the limit loses. Keep the paper’s iid ex ante objective, and \(\mu\) is again a prior over profiles rather than the realized society. The paper already supplies \(g_\mu^q=(G_\mu^q)^{-1}\) for that problem. Or optimize welfare at the fixed mass distribution, and the curve is underdetermined: many rules can yield the same optimal outcome there while behaving differently for other societies. Asking for the curve’s exact representation and bit complexity could be a separate numerical-computation project, but the proposed “Limit Welfare-Grading Curve” does not yet specify a finite encoding for a general computable distribution or an output representation for the whole curve.

Individual strategyproofness also vanishes in an atomless society: one resident’s report changes no mass and cannot change the outcome. Restricting attention to limits of the finite strategyproof rules recovers a meaningful family of grading curves, but the incentive condition then comes from those finite rules, not from the continuum model. Treating a positive-mass cohort as a strategic actor instead changes the deviation being studied.

The paper’s anonymous, consistent results in §5.4 do support the high-multiplicity setting: size-independent grading curves already make voter fractions central to the model. I would not count that prior work against a mirror. It does, however, make this anchor’s negative case hard to press: a large population of residents with ideal locations is plausible, and the finite theorem provides a natural limit candidate. The strongest objection is narrower: the anchor either reverts to the paper’s prior-based problem or loses the unique rule-level optimization that makes it distinctive. That is a substantial modeling gap, but not an airtight case that the authors would reject every such mirror.
