The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives and a finite-support society \((T,\mu)\) of rational unit-sum utility types \(u_t\in\Delta_m\), compute the expected lottery \(q\) produced by the paper’s randomized hyperspherical mechanism: for each \(t\in T\) and \(R\in\Gamma_m\), compute \(p_{t,R}\in\arg\max_{p\in S^2_m(R)}u_t^\top p\), then return \(q(a)=\frac{1}{2m}+\frac{1}{2|\Gamma_m|}\sum_{R\in\Gamma_m}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_{t,R}(a)\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete cardinal utility vectors \(u_t\), with masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. The decision variables \(p_{t,R}\) are lotteries in \(S^2_m(R)\); each maximizes its type’s expected utility, and the resulting lotteries are averaged by type mass and radius to form \(q\).

The objection that survived

The population enters only through a mass-weighted average of independent projections, so the mirror adds a batch evaluation layer without a new population-coupled optimization; this limits its reach but does not eliminate the stated computational task.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: this paper has no named NP-hardness result or complexity-class trichotomy to continuize. Its useful algorithmic anchor is Theorem 11, proved here. Given \(m\), a utility vector \(x\in\Delta_m\), and a radius \(R\), Algorithm 1 returns a lottery \(p\in S^2_m(R)\) maximizing \(p^\top x\), where
\[ \Delta_m=\{p\in\mathbb R^m_{\ge 0}:\|p\|_1=1\},\qquad S^2_m(R)=\{p\in\Delta_m:\|p-\tfrac1m\mathbf 1\|_2\le R\}. \]
The paper presents this projection algorithm as polynomial time. Theorem 11 is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result.

A direct population mirror is Mass-Hyperspherical Evaluation. An instance has \(m\) alternatives and a finite support \(T\) of cardinal utility types. Each type \(t\) has a rational unit-sum utility vector \(u_t\in\Delta_m\) with distinct coordinates, and a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Thus the society has \(n\) agents but only \(\tau=|T|\) distinct utility types, potentially with \(n\gg\tau\). For each radius \(R\) used by the paper’s mechanism, find
\[ p_{t,R}\in\arg\max_{p\in S^2_m(R)}u_t^\top p. \]
Return the mechanism’s lottery
\[ q(a)=\frac{1}{2m}+ \frac{1}{2|\Gamma_m|} \sum_{R\in\Gamma_m}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,p_{t,R}(a), \qquad \Gamma_m=\left\{\frac{k}{\sqrt{m(m-1)}}:k=1,\ldots,m-1\right\}. \]
A solution consists of the type-and-radius lotteries \(p_{t,R}\), each optimal for its type, and their resulting aggregate lottery \(q\). The objective for each \(p_{t,R}\) is that type’s expected utility; equivalently, the separable sum \(\sum_{t,R}\mu_t u_t^\top p_{t,R}\) is maximized subject to the stated constraints. This is the paper’s hyperspherical mechanism with its uniform-voter draw expressed as a draw from the society’s mass distribution.

The regime is plausible for the automated recommender agents that motivate the paper: a platform may have millions of agents assigned one of a comparatively small number of shared, normalized utility templates. The type is the complete utility vector, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction assigned to it. This is a specific cohort regime, not a claim that arbitrary users have identical utilities. It leaves the paper’s alternatives and lotteries as they are; the continuous object is the population.

The computational prediction is Class A. Run Algorithm 1 for each of the \(\tau\) types and each of the \(m-1\) radii, then take the mass-weighted average. This is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) in the algorithm’s arithmetic model; an exact output can be represented by the recursive algebraic expressions, or specified to a chosen rational precision. It also has a clean discrete bridge: grouping an explicit electorate by identical utility vector and setting \(\mu_t\) to each group’s fraction gives exactly the same lottery as sampling a voter uniformly. The continuous input therefore makes the mechanism’s dependence on the society’s support explicit.

This case covers only Theorem 11’s projection computation and its use in evaluating the hyperspherical mechanism. It does not mirror the paper’s lower bounds for truthful mechanisms or its iterative-voting results. The soft spot is that type identity here requires exact agreement on cardinal utilities, and the proposed task mostly compresses an existing mechanism’s input rather than changing its strategic problem. The mirror is still credible because uniform voter sampling already makes type mass the natural quantity for this mechanism.

Further questions are whether welfare guarantees for the mechanism extend unchanged to arbitrary rational masses, whether evaluation remains tractable when the utility population is given by a succinct continuous distribution rather than finite support, and what a non-vacuous truthfulness notion would be for a nonatomic population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is not that the population model is impossible. The recommender-platform cohort is plausible, and Theorem 11 is a real algorithmic result, so “the paper has no named computation” would be too strong.

The proposed Mass-Hyperspherical Evaluation is well-defined: sample a type according to \(\mu\), run the paper’s projection for that type, and average the resulting lotteries. But the population does not enter the optimization. Each \(p_{t,R}\) is computed independently by the same single-utility-vector algorithm as before; the masses only weight the final sum. This is a batch application of Theorem 11, not a population-level computational problem the paper studies. There is no choice to make about the society, no transfer or intervention, and no interaction between types that could create a new pricing or optimization problem. The finite-support version may be a useful implementation exercise, but it adds no population-dependent computational question for ChoCo to classify.

A stronger candidate would lift the distortion results to arbitrary mass distributions over utility vectors. That is a coherent welfare question, but distortion is a performance guarantee, not a complexity result. The same is true of extending the approximately truthful mechanism’s welfare guarantee to \(\mu\): these are questions about a mechanism’s properties over societies, rather than algorithms for changing or computing with a society. The distinction is about the kind of contribution the programme targets, not whether the continuous and discrete guarantees happen to agree.

The incentive results expose a more fundamental problem with trying to make the society genuinely nonatomic. In a population with an atomless distribution, one individual has zero mass. A unilateral report cannot change the aggregate input, so truthfulness becomes vacuous for mechanisms based on that input. The paper’s random-dictator mechanism can still sample a type and produce a lottery, but no named individual has a positive probability of being that sampled type. To preserve strategic force, one would have to make a positive-mass cohort the deviating player. That changes the unit of strategy from one voter to a bloc, and hence changes the problem the paper analyzes.

The iterative-voting results have the same issue. At an atomless society, a single voter cannot change the reported welfare totals or the winning alternative; starting from truthful reports, the welfare-maximizing mechanism already selects the true welfare winner. Letting a positive mass move together might yield interesting dynamics, but those would not be the paper’s individual best-response process.

So the strongest negative case is that the paper’s only named algorithmic anchor admits a population-weighted wrapper, while its richer strategic results lose their substance in the nonatomic limit. The weakness in that case is real: the wrapper is a valid high-multiplicity formulation, and the paper does not rule out follow-on work on distributional welfare. What is missing is a compelling *computational* mirror of this paper’s central results, rather than a population encoding of its projection routine.
