The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for a continuous-population version of MenuNet’s *mechanism-design objective*. The paper itself, however, has no qualifying named computational anchor under your rule. Its only numbered theorem, Theorem 1 (proved here), says MenuNet is strategy-proof: each student’s leave-one-out menu stays fixed when that student changes their report, and the sequential choice rule makes truthful ranking optimal. The proof compares adjacent schools; ordering \(c\) before \(c'\) changes expected utility by \(\alpha p_{s,c}p_{s,c'}(v_{s,c}-v_{s,c'})\). That is a formal incentive result, not a complexity classification or a formulation the paper computes with. The scalability claims are experimental, not numbered complexity results. So there are no eligible anchors to restate or classify as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

A plausible unanchored candidate question is this: in a large school-choice district, let a type \(t\) specify a student’s complete utility vector \(v_{t,c}\) and priority vector \(u_{t,c}\) across schools. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of students of type \(t\), with a modest support of \(\tau\) types compared with the population size \(n\). Given per-capita school capacities \(q_c\), global slack \(\kappa\), and objective weights, choose a menu probability \(p_{t,c}\in[0,1]\) for each type and school, with the outside option always available. If type \(t\)’s schools are ranked \(c_1,\ldots,c_m\), the menu induces assignment probabilities
\[ P_{t,c_j}=p_{t,c_j}\prod_{i<j}(1-p_{t,c_i}). \]
The solution is a menu table minimizing the paper’s per-capita version of its welfare, capacity-penalty, envy, and waste objective, with each student’s envy and waste calculated against the type masses \(\mu_t\). This asks about the underlying menu and assignment problem, not a neural-network encoding.

The regime is credible for large cohorts where preference lists and policy priority categories recur: \(n\) can be large while \(\tau\) is much smaller. A single student has zero mass, so changing their report does not change the aggregate distribution used to select menus. Conditional on a fixed menu, Theorem 1’s truthful-choice argument carries over. Here the fractional type-level assignments and per-capita capacities are co-modifications needed to express the population model; the continuous object is still the society. This is not merely outcome-space continuity: the paper already uses probabilistic assignments, but its agents remain individually indexed.

The weakest point is that the paper’s own experiments use perturbed individual preferences and priorities, which may yield nearly as many distinct types as students. The proposed low-support regime needs shared preference and priority profiles, a plausible school-choice setting but not the paper’s demonstrated one. Also, the paper optimizes expected capacity overflow; its Appendix D cautions that this need not yield ex-post feasible decompositions. I would keep the mirror at that ex-ante level. It is a sensible research question, but this paper supplies no named result from which to predict its complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent offered no qualifying computational anchor. The paper’s only numbered theorem, Theorem 1, proves strategy-proofness; it gives no complexity result or algorithmic classification. Its scalability claims are empirical. That is strong grounds against treating this paper as a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The proposed menu optimization also does not inherit Theorem 1 as written. The theorem fixes each student’s menu when that student changes their report. But if a menu is indexed by the student’s reported type \(t\), a deviation from \(t\) to \(t'\) can change the menu from \(p_t\) to \(p_{t'}\). The adjacent-swap proof assumes the menu stays fixed, so it does not establish truthfulness for that proposal. An atomless agent’s report leaves the population distribution unchanged, but it can still change which type-indexed menu that agent receives.

There is a coherent repair: choose menus from the aggregate distribution and nonreportable public attributes, such as priority class. Agents with the same public attributes then share a menu; capacities and slack scale per capita, and envy and waste become type-mass averages. That is a plausible continuous-population problem. It also removes preference-dependent menu personalization, the paper’s main device for distributing instability. Menus that vary by identity lose the small-support high-multiplicity regime; menus that vary by reported preference risk breaking strategy-proofness.

The repaired problem would be a new nonlinear optimization over roughly \(m\tau\) menu variables. It may be interesting, but the paper supplies no computational theorem or canonical complexity problem for it: MenuNet trains a neural mapping across sampled finite markets, and its reported results are experiments. Formalizing and classifying the repaired optimizer would take additional choices about the objective and approximation guarantees.

So I would argue against prioritizing this paper as a direct ChoCo mirror: it has no computational result to classify, and the natural type-indexed proposal needs an incentive repair. The stronger claim that *no* scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is weak. A common-menu or public-priority-class version is plausible, and may be worth asking as a new problem.
