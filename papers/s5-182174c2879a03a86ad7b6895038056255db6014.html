What the mirror covers

The proposed model concerns the exact-potential and local-equilibrium dynamics in Theorem 6.15, specialized to pairwise interactions. It leaves the paper’s general transferable-utility results, convexity and grand-coalition claims, and numerical findings without mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most credible high-multiplicity setting is a labor market or multi-agent system with many workers and a fixed, much smaller set of ongoing teams. A type records everything the model uses about a worker: their singleton value and their interaction with every other worker type. The number of workers can be enormous while the number of types stays modest. Aumann–Drèze payoffs, exit-and-join moves, and even singletons remain meaningful there. This is a plausible setting for the paper’s model, though it does restrict attention to markets where teams already exist.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.15, proved in this paper. It says that when exit-and-join payoff changes equal changes in a potential \(\Phi\), the induced game is an exact-potential game; the potential represents unilateral incentives exactly; equilibrium is equivalent to a Nash profile in the induced game; and every admissible improving move strictly raises the potential. Since the finite society has only finitely many partitions, the dynamics terminate at a partition locally maximal against accepted exit-and-join moves. The result does not require convexity, superadditivity, or efficiency. This is an algorithmic convergence result, not a polynomial-time or hardness classification.

Here is a continuous problem that puts that result to work. Let there be \(\tau\) worker types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and \(K\) existing teams. A type’s interaction with another type is given by a symmetric rational matrix \(W\); \(a_t\) is its singleton value. A state is a matrix \(X=(x_{t,k})\), where \(k=0\) denotes workers who are singletons and \(k=1,\ldots,K\) denotes teams, with \(x_{t,k}\geq 0\) and \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\). In the pairwise-value model, a type-\(t\) worker in team \(k\) receives the limiting Aumann–Drèze payoff
\[ \pi_t(k;X)=a_t+\frac12\sum_s w_{ts}x_{s,k}, \]
and a singleton receives \(\pi_t(0;X)=a_t\). Take zero switching costs and the paper’s automatic-consent benchmark. The action is a transfer of type mass from one team or the singleton pool to another.

Call this the Type-Mass Exit-and-Join Equilibrium problem: given \((\mu,a,W,K)\), find a feasible state \(X\) in which no positive mass of an occupied type has a strictly payoff-improving infinitesimal move to another destination. The potential to search along is
\[ \Phi(X)=\sum_{t,k}a_t x_{t,k} +\frac14\sum_{k=1}^{K}x_k^\top W x_k. \]
Its directional derivative for moving type \(t\) from destination \(k\) to destination \(\ell\) is \(\pi_t(\ell;X)-\pi_t(k;X)\). Thus the potential expresses exactly the local incentive comparison in the continuous limit. The requested output is a type-mass equilibrium, not a centrally chosen globally efficient team structure.

I would expect the unrestricted version, with variable \(\tau\), \(K\), and signed interactions, to be hard for continuum-specific reasons: the potential can be nonconcave, and finding a stable configuration involves selecting which types occupy which teams. When the potential is concave, the corresponding search has a plausible convex-optimization route. That is a prediction for this proposed problem, not a complexity result in the paper.

The main weakness is substantial: Theorem 6.15 relies on the finite partition space to guarantee finite-time termination. The type-mass state space is continuous, so that argument alone gives no termination or complexity guarantee. The mirror is therefore a well-motivated new computational question, rather than a continuous result already implied by the theorem. It covers the paper’s exit-and-join equilibrium and potential-dynamics strand; it does not mirror the paper’s results for arbitrary transferable-utility games or its convexity and grand-coalition claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The single anchor is Theorem 6.15, but it does not assert the complexity of a computational problem. It assumes an exact potential already exists, then derives the standard consequences: unilateral improvements raise that potential, and the finite partition space prevents an infinite improvement path. The theorem gives no algorithm for finding an equilibrium, no complexity bound, and no input representation or size measure. The paper’s algorithm template and simulations add no such result. So the proposed continuous equilibrium search is not a mirror of the theorem’s computational claim; it adds a new computational problem.

The worker model is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. In particular, the paper’s pairwise-game experiments give some footing for types with shared interaction profiles. I would not reject that model on the ground that workers cannot be grouped or that mass is meaningless.

The stronger objection is that the proposed model changes what is being asked. It replaces individual moves on a finite partition space with nonatomic flows among a fixed set of teams, then asks for a type-mass equilibrium. The potential derivative is a clean continuous analogue of the paper’s incentive identity, but it does not carry over the finite-termination result: continuous state space removes the finiteness argument. To ask a computational question, one must separately choose a representation for payoffs and moves and define an equilibrium-finding task. The paper does not supply those choices.

One could broaden the mirror to arbitrary type-based transferable-utility games, but the paper’s characteristic function is defined on individual subsets. Its values on finite coalitions do not specify how to evaluate coalitions with fractional type masses. A continuous extension must be chosen. The pairwise quadratic extension is defensible, and the paper’s experiments support it, but its equilibrium-computation problem depends on that added structure, not on Theorem 6.15. It could be worthwhile adjacent research; the paper gives no basis for presenting it as the continuous counterpart of a computational result.

That is the strongest negative case I can make. It is not airtight against the broader claim that a continuous coalition-formation problem is worth studying: the proposed labor-market regime is credible, and the paper’s pairwise model makes a natural specialization available. The case against is strongest when the question is whether this paper has a computational result whose continuous mirror belongs in the programme.
