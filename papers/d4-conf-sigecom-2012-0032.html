The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Corollary 3.2, proved in this paper by extending the preceding argument. It says that in a sequential first-price auction where each round sells a set of items simultaneously and bidders are unit-demand, the mixed Bayes–Nash price of anarchy is at most \(2e/(e-1)\approx 3.16\). This is a welfare guarantee, not a runtime or hardness result; the paper has no named theorem about computing equilibria or classifying a problem as polynomial-time or NP-hard.

A continuous mirror I would put to the authors is Continuous Sequential Matching PoA. An instance has an ordered list of \(m\) item classes and a finite support \(T\) of valuation types. Type \(t\) has a complete unit-demand value vector \(v_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\), and occupies mass \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Round \(j\) sells supply \(q_j\) of item class \(j\), where supply is measured as a fraction of the population. Remaining agents submit first-price bids; the highest bids receive the available mass, ties at the cutoff are split proportionally, and winners leave subsequent rounds. Each individual still receives at most one item; a type’s fractional allocation records what share of its mass receives each item. Strategies may depend on type and observed round history, and equilibrium means no infinitesimal agent can improve expected utility by changing their strategy.

The benchmark welfare is
\[ \operatorname{OPT}(\mu,q)= \max_{\alpha\ge 0}\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{tj}\alpha_{tj} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_j\alpha_{tj}\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t\alpha_{tj}\le q_j. \]
The question is whether every mixed Bayes–Nash equilibrium of this continuous auction has expected welfare at least \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,q)/(2e/(e-1))\), and, if not, what the worst-equilibrium ratio is. A solution is a proof of the bound for all such instances or a counterexample equilibrium; finding the exact supremum would sharpen the result.

The regime is a large matching market: many agents seek one service or slot, there are many copies of each slot class, and agents with the same full valuation vector are interchangeable. With \(n\) agents, the discrete approximants have about \(n\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(nq_j\) copies of item class \(j\). Thus \(n\) grows while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct value vectors can remain small. Scaling supply with population is the necessary co-modification that keeps welfare per capita meaningful. This mirrors the paper’s matching-market welfare question rather than making the outcome space itself the continuous object.

I expect the positive result to survive, perhaps with a stronger bound: in an atomless population, one agent’s deviation does not change later aggregate histories, which may make the paper’s deviation argument easier to adapt. In the programme’s trichotomy, this is closest to Class A, though that label is imperfect here: the anchor concerns equilibrium welfare, not computational complexity. The mirror covers the matching-market guarantee only; it does not claim to continuize the matroid results or the paper’s equilibrium calculation in Theorem 2.1.

The weak point is substantial: atomlessness removes an individual’s ability to affect later information and play. That is part of what makes the sequential Bayesian game in the paper difficult, so the authors might regard this as a meaningful large-market limit but still a different auction model. The follow-up questions are whether every continuous equilibrium can be approximated by equilibria of the finite auctions, whether the bound remains valid with correlated types, and what the tight continuous price of anarchy is.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 3.2 is not a computational result in ChoCo’s sense. It bounds equilibrium welfare; it does not give an algorithm, classify a problem’s complexity, or prove hardness. The other named results are of the same kind: welfare bounds, an inefficiency example, and an explicit equilibrium strategy. So the paper offers no computational problem whose continuous version would fill the programme’s stated gap. A continuous analogue of the price-of-anarchy bound may be a mathematical question, but it is a welfare-analysis question of the kind mean-field and nonatomic game theory already asks.

The proposed mirror does pass an important test: a large service market with repeated valuation types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Scaling supply with population is also a reasonable co-modification. I would not reject the idea on the grounds that types compress agents or that supply must scale.

The stronger objection is what that co-modification does to the game. With positive per-capita supply, each round sells a positive mass of copies. One agent’s deviation then has no effect on aggregate prices, allocations, or later histories. In the paper, by contrast, the sequential structure matters because an individual’s early bid and outcome can reveal information about that individual, change later competition, and make bluffing useful. The continuous model replaces this with agents choosing against aggregate bid cutoffs and rationing rules. That can be a coherent nonatomic auction model, but its equilibrium and welfare questions concern a different strategic mechanism.

Keeping one item per round preserves the original mechanism more closely, but with a fixed number of rounds the served share vanishes as the population grows. Increasing supply to avoid that limit returns to the atomless model, where individual actions cannot move the aggregate state. Neither choice makes the proposed welfare bound into a computational result.

One could repair the proposal by asking for an algorithm to compute the worst equilibrium welfare of the nonatomic sequential auction. But that adds a new computational agenda: the paper gives no finite representation of the equilibrium strategies or output, and the resulting problem would need those choices specified. It might be worthwhile research, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The negative case is strongest within ChoCo’s stated scope, and weaker as a claim that the large-market question has no value at all. The proposed model is plausible and could support a useful welfare analysis. Its weakness as a ChoCo mirror is that it changes the game’s strategic structure and, even in its best formulation, continuizes an analytic welfare question rather than a computational one.
