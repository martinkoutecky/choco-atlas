The continuous mirror question

Given finite item classes \(\mathcal G=[m]\), finite complete valuation types \(\Theta\), rational type masses \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and value-oracle access to monotone submodular valuations \(v_\theta:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\), find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{\theta,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal G}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S\ni j}x_{\theta,S}\le b_j\), with \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)\ge \mathrm{OPT}_\infty/380\), where \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)=\exp(\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}\log v_\theta(S))\), in time polynomial in the compressed input and oracle complexity.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity course-allocation market with \(\tau=|\Theta|\) complete valuation types: \(x_{\theta,S}\) is the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving integral bundle \(S\), \(b_j\) is the per-capita supply of item class \(j\), and the objective is the \(\mu\)-weighted geometric mean of realized utilities.

The objection that survived

The paper proves polynomial time in the number of named agents, not in the compressed parameters \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\) and \(b\); the mirror therefore needs a new aggregation argument before inheriting the claimed approximation algorithm.

What the mirror covers

It directly mirrors Theorem 1's \(380\)-approximation for monotone submodular \( \mathrm{NSW} \); Theorem 11 supports the relaxation-level analogy, while the internal lemmas, asymmetric \( \mathrm{NSW} \), fairness guarantees, and commented-out XOS hardness are not independently covered.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(380\)-approximation be implemented in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the bit length of \((\mu,b)\), without expanding the population?
  • Can the continuous-greedy, rounding, and matching-recombination analysis be aggregated with arbitrary rational type masses and per-capita supplies?
  • What is the pricing or oracle complexity of the bundle formulation, and does it create a continuum-specific barrier?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a Class A mirror of the paper’s main result.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1 (Main Result), proved in this paper. It states that, for \(m\) indivisible items and \(n\) agents with monotone submodular valuation functions \(v_i:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\) accessible by value queries, there is a polynomial-time constant-factor approximation algorithm for Nash Social Welfare. The paper makes the constant explicit: its algorithm returns an allocation with Nash Social Welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/380\). This is the only anchor I would use. The XOS lower bound in the commented-out Corollary 6 is outside the paper’s main valuation class and is not a reliable anchor for this case.

The continuous mirror is:

NSW\(_\infty\)-Submodular Allocation. An instance consists of a finite set of item classes \(\mathcal G=[m]\), a finite set of valuation types \(\Theta\), a population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), per-capita item supplies \(b_j\ge0\), and for each \(\theta\in\Theta\) a monotone submodular valuation \(v_\theta:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\), accessible by value queries.

A feasible solution is a mass allocation \(x=(x_{\theta,S})\), where \(x_{\theta,S}\) is the mass of type-\(\theta\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq\mathcal G\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal G}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta \qquad\text{for every }\theta, \]

and

\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{\theta,S}\le b_j \qquad\text{for every }j\in\mathcal G. \]

The objective is the geometric mean of realized utilities across the population,

\[ \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x) = \exp\!\left( \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal G} x_{\theta,S}\log v_\theta(S) \right), \]

with the usual convention that a zero-valued bundle makes the objective zero. The requested solution is a feasible finite-support mass allocation satisfying

\[ \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)\ge \frac{1}{380}\mathrm{OPT}_\infty. \]

This is not a model in which an individual receives a fractional bundle, nor one that replaces \(v_\theta\) by \(v_\theta(\mathbb E[S])\). Every unit of mass receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate allocation is continuous.

The natural regime is a large course-allocation or public-service market. There may be millions of students, residents, or households, but only a small number \(\tau=|\Theta|\) of complete valuation types: for example, students with the same curriculum priorities and the same diminishing-returns valuation over courses. Each course has many seats, so \(b_j\) is its number of seats per capita. One might have \(n\) in the millions, \(\tau\) in the tens or hundreds, and \(m\) in the tens. A type is the entire valuation function, so agents with different prices, priorities, eligibility constraints, or marginal values are different types.

The per-capita supplies are forced by the limit. With a fixed number of unique items and a continuum of agents, almost everyone receives nothing and the Nash objective degenerates. Repeated indivisible seats or service slots are therefore the appropriate high-multiplicity realization of the paper’s allocation problem.

The bridge to the discrete problem is exact. If \(N\mu_\theta\), \(Nb_j\), and \(Nx_{\theta,S}\) are integral, take \(N\mu_\theta\) agents of type \(\theta\), \(Nb_j\) copies of item class \(j\), and allocate \(Nx_{\theta,S}\) agents bundle \(S\). Then

\[ \left( \prod_{\theta,S} v_\theta(S)^{Nx_{\theta,S}} \right)^{1/N} = \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x). \]

Thus this is genuinely the high-multiplicity relaxation of NSW, rather than merely an outcome-space fractionalization.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The discrete object remains exactly the one they study: allocate indivisible bundles to agents with monotone submodular valuations while maximizing the geometric mean of utilities. The only changes are that repeated agents are represented by their type masses and repeated item copies by per-capita capacities. Their own proof architecture supports the analogy. The initial and final matchings become capacity-constrained transportation problems; sums over agents become \(\mu\)-weighted sums over types; the continuous-greedy objective becomes

\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\log V_\theta(y_\theta); \]

and the rounding step can be realized by partitioning mass among integral bundles. The finite theorem’s use of named agents is not conceptually essential.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable in the same approximate sense as Theorem 1: a universal constant-factor algorithm, plausibly a weighted version of the \(380\)-approximation. The exact mass-allocation LP has exponentially many bundle variables, and its pricing problem may be difficult with only value-oracle access. But the paper’s result is already an approximation theorem designed to avoid exact optimization over all bundles. Its multilinear relaxation, continuous-greedy phase, submodular rounding, and matching recombination all have natural mass-weighted counterparts.

The supporting Theorem 11 strengthens this expectation. It proves, for the paper’s LogMultilinear relaxation, a feasible solution satisfying

\[ \frac1n\sum_i \frac{V_i(y_i^*)}{V_i(y_i)} \le e. \]

In the mirror, this becomes the weighted condition

\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta \frac{V_\theta(y_\theta^*)}{V_\theta(y_\theta)} \le e. \]

I would not count Theorem 11 as a separate anchor because it concerns a relaxation used inside the NSW algorithm, not the underlying integral allocation problem. It nevertheless shows that the paper already contains the right type of continuous optimization machinery.

The main weakness is substantial: Theorem 1 is polynomial in the number \(n\) of named agents, not automatically in the compressed description \((m,\tau,L)\) of a high-multiplicity instance. Simply running the paper’s algorithm on \(N\) replicated agents gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(N\), not a high-multiplicity algorithm polynomial in \(\log N\). One must aggregate the matching, rounding, and continuous-greedy arguments and remove hidden dependence on the replication factor. The randomized-rounding proof also needs to be reformulated carefully for a geometric mean over mass, rather than merely inherited informally from a finite sample.

That weakness does not destroy the mirror; it identifies the actual research question. The paper supplies a strong algorithmic anchor, and the continuous version asks whether its agent-level machinery survives aggregation over \(\tau\) valuation types. Further questions include whether the factor \(380\) can be preserved or improved, whether exact optimization has a continuum-specific pricing barrier, and how the result changes when the type distribution itself has large or continuous support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case begins with a faithful population limit. Keep the paper’s \(m\) items fixed while the number \(N\) of agents grows. Then only \(m\) agents can receive nonempty bundles. If \(v_i(\varnothing)=0\), a positive mass of agents receives value zero and the Nash objective becomes zero; if all empty bundles have positive value, allocation becomes asymptotically irrelevant. Thus the literal continuum of agents is degenerate.

The proposed escape—turning items into repeatable classes with per-capita supplies—is not determined by the paper. A valuation \(v_i:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\) gives no canonical valuation over many copies: copies might be interchangeable, jointly usable, or distinguishable. Interchangeable course seats produce a new multi-unit allocation model; distinguishable copies make the valuation description grow with \(N\); count-based valuations require a new valuation domain. Consequently, the claimed bridge to Theorem 1 is not automatic, and the paper’s \(380\)-approximation does not itself yield an algorithm polynomial in the compressed high-multiplicity description.

That is the best serious objection, but it does not defeat the better mirror. The programme expressly permits per-capita supplies when population continuization forces them, and course allocation with repeated valuation types is a sensible realization. In that model every agent still receives an integral bundle, while

\[ \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x) = \exp\!\left(\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}\log v_\theta(S)\right) \]

is exactly the finite Nash objective under rational replication. The resulting configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables and a genuine pricing problem, so it is precisely the kind of computational question the programme targets. Agent identity carries no essential information when agents have identical complete valuation types.

Theorem 11 does not independently rescue the case—it concerns an internal relaxation—but its weighted form is compatible with this model. More importantly, the failure of the paper’s algorithm to aggregate automatically is an unresolved algorithmic obstacle, not evidence that the continuous question is meaningless.

So the honest negative verdict is weak. One can challenge the proponent’s unproved claim that a \(380\)-approximation survives compression, but one cannot defeat the existence of a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 1.
