The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a future extension, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results. I can’t identify a named result here that asserts a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity.

The closest positive hook is Proposition 2, proved in this paper: if there are at least three agents, the social choice function is responsive (distinct states select distinct outcomes), satisfies Maskin monotonicity, and satisfies the no-worst-alternative condition (at every state, each agent has some lottery they like less than the prescribed outcome), then it is implementable in rationalizable strategies. The closest complementary hook is Proposition 1, also proved here: rationalizable implementability requires strict Maskin monotonicity—whenever the prescribed outcome at state \(\theta\) is strictly preferred to a lottery \(y\) by every agent, and those comparisons remain weakly true at \(\theta'\), the prescribed outcomes at \(\theta\) and \(\theta'\) must be equal. Equivalently, if the outcomes differ, some agent and lottery \(y\) must make the prescribed outcome strictly better at \(\theta\), but \(y\) strictly better at \(\theta'\).

Both are implementation-existence results, not computational classifications. The paper gives no running time, finite input encoding, or algorithm for finding a mechanism. Its mechanisms may have countably infinite message spaces, and the results quantify over mechanisms. So neither proposition qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard.

A possible follow-on would be Continuous Rationalizable Implementation. An instance would give finite states \(\Theta\), outcomes \(Z\), a finite set \(T\) of complete state-contingent utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a target function \(f:\Theta\to\Delta(Z)\). A society could be a large set of local authorities or jurisdictions grouped by identical utility type, with \(N\gg\tau=|T|\). A mechanism would receive reported message masses \(x_{t,m}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_m x_{t,m}=\mu_t\), and map the aggregate reports to a lottery over outcomes. The decision question would be whether there exists an admissible mechanism for which the rationalizable mass profiles are nonempty at each state and every such profile produces \(f(\theta)\). A solution would be the mechanism and a proof of that property. This is a feasibility question, not an optimization problem.

That proposal is most plausible if each type group can coordinate its report as a bloc. If instead the population consists of individually acting nonatomic agents, a single agent’s report has zero effect on an aggregate public outcome; the rationalizability and incentive structure can change sharply. Choosing between those models is substantive, and the paper does not make that choice for us. Nor does it establish whether the proposed problem is tractable, hard, or even suitably finitely encoded.

So the positive case is limited: Propositions 1 and 2 offer a recognizable implementation problem to extend to type masses. They do not supply a computational anchor, and the cited “continuous implementation” work concerns mechanisms for nearby types, not a continuous population. Under the programme’s anchor rule, this paper supports a possible research direction, but not an anchor-valid case for a continuous computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive analyst identifies the central problem with their own anchors: Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 are implementation existence results, not computational classifications. Proposition 1 gives a necessary condition for some mechanism to exist; Proposition 2 gives a sufficient condition using a mechanism with countably infinite message spaces. Neither states an algorithm, complexity bound, or finite encoding of the mechanism-search problem. They are important results, but they do not supply the computational result a continuous mirror would normally extend.

The proposed population model also does not slot into the paper’s problem unchanged. Its agents are strategic players with state-dependent utility functions, and its target \(f\) maps states to lotteries. A distribution over complete utility types is not an input to that target. If masses merely count how many copies of each utility type there are, they repeat the paper’s per-agent conditions; they do not give the mechanism a population distribution to act on. Making the target or outcome depend on those masses would be a new environment.

The proposed aggregate-report version runs into a deeper mismatch. If an anonymous mechanism responds only to report masses in an atomless society, a single agent’s report changes no mass. Under a direct extension of individual best-response reasoning, that agent cannot use a unilateral deviation to affect the outcome. Rationalizability then loses the individual incentive comparisons that drive both propositions. If instead each type mass can coordinate and change its report as a bloc, the strategic players are the blocs: that is a different deviation model, not a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s individual-player model.

Keeping finitely many atomic players avoids that collapse, but then the proposed mass description need not preserve the relevant strategic information. The original mechanism may respond to individual message profiles, and rationalizability is defined through beliefs about opponents’ messages and their best responses. Replacing those profiles with masses requires restrictions such as anonymity and a proof that rationalizability survives aggregation and the limit. The paper supplies neither. Restricting mechanisms to a finite, encoded class could make a computational problem well-posed, but it would be a new study of restricted mechanism design, not a computational mirror of these results.

So the strongest negative case is that neither named proposition survives as an anchor, and the proposed “Continuous Rationalizable Implementation” must add both a population-sensitive objective and a new strategic model before it becomes a computational problem. I would not claim this proves that no worthwhile extension can be imagined. A complexity study of a carefully specified finite mechanism class, or a study of rationalizability for weighted blocs, could be worthwhile. But those are new problems; this paper provides little reason to expect a continuous-population mirror of its results.
