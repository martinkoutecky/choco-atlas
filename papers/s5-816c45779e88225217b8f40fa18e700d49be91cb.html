The case FOR (proponent)

There is no honest anchor for a positive case under the stated criteria. The paper has numbered results—among them Proposition 1 on the unique policy bargaining equilibrium for \(\delta<1\), Proposition 3 on the existence and shape of connected coalition-proof adjudication equilibria, and Corollary 1 on their number and uniqueness. But these characterize equilibria; none establishes that a computational problem is tractable, hard, or complete, and the paper gives no algorithmic formulation it computes with. So there are no qualifying named computational results to mirror, and I can’t supply an anchor-backed continuous problem or predict its complexity without inventing one.

The most promising intuition for a future project would be a large regulatory commission whose members are described by a distribution over complete preference types, such as ideal rule cutpoints and utility parameters. Mass would represent the share of commissioners of each type; their votes, bargaining participation, and endorsements would determine the disposition and the adopted rule. One could ask how to compute equilibrium outcomes from that distribution. But that would be a new continuum equilibrium-computation project, not a mirror grounded in a named computational result of this paper. Its main weakness is substantial: the paper’s strategic logic depends on pivotal individual judges, especially the median, and those incentives may change when the population becomes atomless.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes the policy equilibrium of a finite bargaining game; Proposition 3 describes refined adjudication equilibria; Corollary 1 bounds their number and gives a uniqueness condition. None formulates a computational problem or establishes its complexity. Their claims can motivate questions, but treating them as algorithmic anchors would erase the distinction ChoCo is meant to study.

The strongest version of the proposed commission project would give a large body a distribution over complete judge types—ideal cutpoints, utility parameters, and any institutional roles—and ask for its equilibrium outcomes. That is a coherent population-continuum question. It still requires a new game specification. In the atomless model, an individual judge’s vote has zero effect, while the paper’s strategic-voting argument turns on judges changing coalition membership and sometimes being pivotal. Coalition-proofness would need a new definition for measurable groups. The proposer rule also needs redesign: under uniform recognition, each judge’s chance of proposing is zero. Proportional quotas, mass coalitions, and faction bargaining may preserve interesting structure, but deriving that structure would be new continuum game theory, not a computational result carried over from this paper.

That issue reaches all three suggested anchors. Proposition 1’s decisive judges and proposer-dependent offers refer to members of a finite coalition. Proposition 3’s pivotal median judge and connected majority refer to a finite voting order. Corollary 1’s bound of two refined equilibria depends on those finite-game results. A distribution over types could support new analogues of each, but the paper gives no algorithmic target whose continuous complexity ChoCo could classify.

The negative case is strongest as a claim about this paper’s eligibility as a mirror, not as proof that no future continuum model of appellate bargaining could be worthwhile. A large commission with type-level masses may be a valuable separate project; the mirror case is weak exactly there. The paper’s policy and case spaces are already continuous, while its population remains finite. Those continuous outcome variables do not supply the missing population-based computational result.
