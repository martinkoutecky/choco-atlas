  • Theorem 6 — survived: The opponent disputes its computational status, but the formula determines the projection explicitly, and its mass-distribution version preserves the same question.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\ge3\) candidates and \(\tau\) strict-ranking types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), define \(d_\mu(i,j)=\sum_{t:\,i\succ_t j}\mu_t-\frac12\). Compute the orthogonal projection \(y\) of \(d_\mu\) onto the strongly transitive vectors, where \(y_{ij}=p_i-p_j\); return \(y\) and the resulting Borda ranking, including ties.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types. The input determines pairwise differences \(d_\mu(i,j)\); choose a strongly transitive vector \(y_{ij}=p_i-p_j\) minimizing \(\sum_{i<j}(y_{ij}-d_\mu(i,j))^2\). The output is the projection and its Borda ranking.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s Borda and projection correspondence over ranking profiles; it leaves the Arrow discussion, Dodgson and Kemeny comparisons, and TSP results outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 6, the paper’s Borda projection result. The paper gives no named theorem that explicitly classifies a problem as NP-hard, in P, or in a parameterized class. Theorem 6 is the closest computational anchor: its formula gives an exact algorithm, so the continuous problem below is in P. If the anchor rule requires the printed theorem itself to name a complexity class, then this paper has no qualifying anchor.

Theorem 6, attributed in the paper to Saari [tandd], says that for any \(n\ge 3\), the orthogonal projection of the pairwise-difference vector onto the strongly transitive subspace is equivalent to the Borda Count. In the paper’s notation, \(\mathcal B(A_j)=(n-1)N/2+S_A(A_j)\), and \(\mathcal B(A_j)-\mathcal B(A_k)=S_A(A_j)-S_A(A_k)=n d_{j,k}\), where \(d_{j,k}\) is the projected strongly transitive pairwise difference. The paper explains and uses this formula, but cites it as an existing result rather than presenting it as proved here.

Here is the continuous problem I would mirror. Call it Continuous Borda Projection. An instance consists of \(m\ge3\) candidates and a list of \(\tau\) strict rankings \(t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type is a complete ranking; mass is the fraction of society with that ranking. For each pair, set
\[ d_\mu(i,j)=\sum_{t:\,i\succ_t j}\mu_t-\frac12, \qquad S_\mu(i)=\sum_{j\ne i}d_\mu(i,j). \]
Find the unique vector \(y\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{i<j}\bigl(y_{ij}-d_\mu(i,j)\bigr)^2 \]
over all strongly transitive vectors, meaning \(y_{ij}=p_i-p_j\) for some real scores \(p_i\). A solution is the projected vector \(y\), together with its Borda scores and hence the Borda ranking, including ties.

Theorem 6 gives the answer:
\[ y_{ij}=\frac{S_\mu(i)-S_\mu(j)}{m}, \qquad \mathcal B_\mu(i)=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(m-\operatorname{rank}_t(i)\bigr) =\frac{m-1}{2}+S_\mu(i). \]
Thus \(\mathcal B_\mu(i)-\mathcal B_\mu(j)=m y_{ij}\). The formula yields an exact polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length; the ranking types provide the society input, rather than an arbitrary real edge-weight vector.

A plausible regime is a large electorate using a ballot with a moderate number of candidates, where many voters share rankings—for example, because ballots cluster around a relatively small menu of ranking patterns. Then the number of voters can be far larger than \(\tau\), and \(\mu_t\) expresses the kind of population share the programme is intended to model. This is a direct normalization of the paper’s own ranking-profile input. It preserves the Borda projection question rather than replacing it with a problem about fractional outcomes.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. A natural next question is how much mass must be shifted between ranking types, given specified transfer costs, to make a chosen candidate a Borda winner.

The scope is just Theorem 6’s projection and Borda correspondence. I would not stretch this case to cover the paper’s Arrow discussion or its TSP results: the latter operate on edge-weight graphs, and making those weights arise from a voter population would need a separate argument.

The weak point is substantial: the paper uses Theorem 6 as a geometric identity, not as a complexity question, and extending it from voter counts to rational masses adds little computational difficulty. The formula is already explicit, and finite profiles normalize directly to mass distributions. Still, the mirror is faithful—the full preference types remain the input, and the computed object is the paper’s own projection. It is a modest positive case for a continuous-population mirror, rather than evidence that the paper contains a broader complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 6 is not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. It is a geometric identity: the projection onto the strongly transitive subspace is given by a Borda-derived formula. The paper does not pose a search or optimization problem for that projection, analyze its complexity, or offer an algorithmic classification. The other named results are also geometric or axiomatic; the TSP section gives path-length identities and examples, not a complexity result to continuize.

The proposed population model is sensible: a large electorate can have mass \(\mu_t\) on each of a small number of ranking types. But the continuous computation follows immediately from the same identity:
\[ d_\mu(i,j)=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[i\succ_t j]-\frac12, \qquad y_{ij}=\frac{S_\mu(i)-S_\mu(j)}{m}. \]
That makes a valid continuous extension of the formula. It does not supply the missing mirror of a discrete computational result: the paper’s theorem gives no complexity question whose high-multiplicity version this would be.

A stronger proposal—such as finding the least mass transfer that makes a chosen candidate win under Borda—could be a worthwhile ChoCo problem. But it adds an intervention objective and transfer costs that this paper never studies. It would be a new problem motivated by Borda, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 6.

I would not base the negative case on multiplicity: repeated ranking types in a large electorate are entirely plausible. The limitation is narrower. This paper provides a natural population input for a Borda calculation, but no computational result for that calculation to mirror. That is a strong objection under the programme’s stated target; it does not establish that no useful adjacent continuous Borda problem could be posed.
