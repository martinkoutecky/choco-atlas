The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is narrow and rests on Proposition 8, proved in this paper. It says that, under the linear probability domain, equal-gap seeding is the unique maximizer of the paper’s weighted objective over seedings. That objective combines probabilities that the strongest \(2^{j-1}\) participants all win \(n-j+1\) matches, with the weights specified in Proposition 8. The paper also singles out the objective of advancing the strongest half at each round, and relates it to the strongest participant winning, the strongest two reaching the final, and so on. This is a named discrete-optimization result, not a complexity classification. The introduction’s NP-hardness statement about maximizing one player’s winning probability cites earlier work; it is not a numbered result proved here, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A plausible mirror is Bulk Seeding of Parallel Divisions. Imagine a large circuit of parallel knockout tournaments, each with the same fixed \(d\)-round format. The entrants across the circuit are numerous, but share a relatively small set \(T\) of complete strength types: members of a type have the same strength and the same probabilities of beating every other type. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s fraction of all entrant slots. Pairwise win probabilities follow the paper’s linear domain, \(p(t,u)=F(g_t-g_u)\) for stronger \(t\), with \(F\) nondecreasing and concave.

A deterministic bracket profile \(\sigma\) assigns a type to each of its \(2^d\) slots. Let \(N_t(\sigma)\) count type \(t\)’s slots, and let \(\Phi_d(\sigma)\) be the Proposition 8 advancement score for that bracket, computed from the independent match outcomes. The continuous instance is \((d,T,\mu,p,\alpha)\). Its decision variable is a distribution \(\pi\) over deterministic bracket profiles, and its objective is

\[ \max_{\pi\ge 0}\ \sum_{\sigma}\pi_\sigma\Phi_d(\sigma) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{\sigma}\pi_\sigma=1,\qquad \sum_{\sigma}\pi_\sigma\frac{N_t(\sigma)}{2^d}=\mu_t \quad(t\in T). \]

A solution is an optimal \(\pi\) and its value. Each bracket in the circuit still has a fixed, predetermined seeding; \(\pi_\sigma\) says what fraction of the circuit’s tournaments use profile \(\sigma\). This is a genuine mass model: a finite circuit gives its empirical distribution over profiles, and any rational \(\pi\) can be realized by a sufficiently large finite circuit. A plausible setting is a large school or amateur sports circuit with many parallel divisions, a common bracket format, and entrants concentrated in a few rating bands.

This mirrors Proposition 8’s bracket objective while asking a new computational question about allocating a high-multiplicity population across brackets. The local matches, probabilistic fairness criterion, and predetermined bracket structure remain. The co-modification is that the organizer assigns entrant mass across many divisions, rather than seeding one fixed roster. That is the part the authors would need to accept for this to count as their problem.

For fixed \(d\), this is a linear program with one variable per type-labelled bracket profile; enumerating profiles takes \(|T|^{2^d}\) time, polynomial in \(|T|\) when the format is fixed. For variable \(d\), I would expect a continuum-specific hardness possibility: the type-mass LP has exponentially many profiles, and its pricing problem still has to choose a bracket with the best advancement score after type-specific prices are applied. Proposition 8’s equal-gap proof is suggestive, but does not solve that priced problem. A useful next question is whether its uncrossing argument survives those prices; another is how much rounding from an optimal \(\pi\) preserves the circuit’s objective.

The scope is just Proposition 8’s advancement objective. I would not stretch this mirror to cover the paper’s axioms or Proposition 9’s competitive-intensity objective.

The soft spot is that the paper studies one fixed roster, while this mirror studies a circuit of parallel tournaments and adds an allocation problem across them. Although large circuits with repeated strength types make that population regime plausible, an author could reasonably say that cross-division allocation is a new problem rather than the continuous form of theirs. There is no numbered complexity theorem here to strengthen the case; the positive argument stands or falls on whether that aggregate seeding question is recognizable as a worthwhile extension of Proposition 8.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 8 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but it proves an optimizer exists in a structured probability model; it does not classify the complexity of computing one. The paper’s NP-hardness statement is prior work cited in the introduction, not a result established here. So a continuous mirror would add a computational question to the paper rather than continue a complexity analysis it already undertakes.

The proposed bulk LP does preserve Proposition 8’s score, but it changes what is being optimized. Its variables assign entrant types across *many separate brackets*, and its constraints fix only their aggregate proportions. The paper’s problem takes one roster as given and chooses its bracket. Calling the pooled entrants a society makes the aggregate assignment a legitimate extension, but it does not make any one tournament continuous. With a fixed bracket format, multiplicity comes from repeating the same finite tournament problem across divisions.

The more faithful alternative is one growing tournament with many participants per strength type. That gives a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but Proposition 8’s objective becomes a poor population-scale criterion. Write \(k=2^{j-1}\) and \(r=n-j+1\). Its event for index \(j\) requires all \(k\) strongest participants to win \(r\) matches. With fixed finite support and non-deterministic match probabilities, there is some \(\varepsilon>0\) such that every match has probability at most \(1-\varepsilon\) of a specified participant winning. Under the paper’s independent-match model, the event has probability at most \((1-\varepsilon)^k\) from the first round alone, and at most \((1-\varepsilon)^r\) from one participant’s required run. Thus it is bounded by \((1-\varepsilon)^{\max(k,r)}\). Across \(j=2,\ldots,n\), \(\max(k,r)\) grows at least linearly with \(n\), so the unweighted advancement score vanishes as the tournament grows. The paper’s equal-weight corollary is therefore optimizing simultaneous successes that become vanishingly rare in the population limit.

One can repair that criterion by measuring expected fractions of each strength class advancing, rather than the probability that every member of a designated top group advances. That is a natural mass-level objective, but it changes Proposition 8’s joint-success criterion. Alternatively, retaining its exact objective points back to the circuit LP, where multiplicity lies across separate fixed-size tournaments. I think that leaves a credible adjacent research problem, but a weak case that Proposition 8 itself has a continuous-population mirror. The negative case is not airtight: an author might embrace either repair as a worthwhile extension.
