The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), complete-ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a polynomial-time computable weakly separable scoring family \(\alpha_k^m\), find every \(k\)-element committee maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{c\in W}\alpha_k^m(\operatorname{pos}_t(c))\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types; the decision variable is an integral committee \(W\) of size \(k\); the objective is average committee score, with ties retained.

The objection that survived

The mirror adds little beyond compactly encoding a finite election and introduces no new optimization or complexity phenomenon.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2 and weakly separable rules such as SNTV, \(k\)-Borda, and Bloc. It leaves the paper's non-weakly-separable rules, axiomatic results, and cited hardness results untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the high-multiplicity formulation of \(\ell_1\)-CC preserve the meaningful computational distinctions between candidate selection and population multiplicity?
  • What is the exact continuous formulation and complexity of Monroe's rule when each selected candidate must receive mass \(1/k\)?
  • Can winner-robustness questions be formulated as minimum mass transfers that change the winning committee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow one centred on Proposition 2. The paper contains no numbered NP-hardness theorem: its hardness claims for \(\ell_1\)-CC, Monroe, and STV appear in unnumbered prose and cite earlier work. I therefore would not present those as formal anchors.

My lead anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper: if a committee scoring rule is weakly separable, meaning that for every \(m\) and \(k\) its scoring function has the form
\[ f(i_1,\ldots,i_k)=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\alpha^m_k(i_r), \]
where the functions \(\alpha^m_k:[m]\to\mathbb{N}\) are nonincreasing and polynomial-time computable, then the rule is polynomial-time computable. This includes \(k\)-Borda and SNTV as separable rules, and Bloc as a weakly separable rule.

The continuous mirror I would propose is High-Multiplicity Weakly-Separable Committee Winner\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(C\), a committee size \(k\), a list \(T=\{t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\}\) of distinct complete rankings of \(C\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with ranking \(t\). The question is to choose exactly \(k\) candidates maximizing the population-average committee score
\[ \operatorname{sc}_{\mu}(W) = \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \sum_{c\in W}\alpha^m_k\bigl(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr). \]
A solution is any \(k\)-element committee \(W\) attaining this maximum; ties retain the paper’s parallel-universes interpretation.

This is genuinely a population continuization. The committee remains an integral \(k\)-subset, and rankings remain complete ordinal types. Only the electorate becomes a distribution. For a discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives exactly the same winners, because normalization merely multiplies every committee score by \(1/n\). Conversely, any rational \(\mu\) can be converted into a finite discrete profile by clearing denominators, possibly producing an exponentially large electorate. Thus this is a genuine high-multiplicity representation, not a fractional committee or an outcome-space relaxation.

The algorithm is immediate but meaningful in the programme’s terms. Define each candidate’s population score by
\[ q_{\mu}(c)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\alpha^m_k\bigl(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr). \]
Then
\[ \operatorname{sc}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_{c\in W}q_{\mu}(c), \]
so one selects the \(k\) candidates with largest \(q_{\mu}(c)\). With explicitly listed rankings, this takes polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the bit-length of the rational masses, assuming the \(\alpha^m_k\) family is polynomial-time computable. The continuous problem is therefore Class A: the high-multiplicity representation preserves the paper’s tractability and permits an electorate whose numerical size is exponentially larger than its type description.

A credible regime is a large parliamentary or organizational election with millions of voters but a relatively small number of standardized preference profiles. For example, voters may belong to recurring regional, party, union, or membership cohorts whose members submit the same complete ranking over \(m\) candidates. One could have \(n\) in the millions, \(m\) around a few dozen, and only \(\tau\) in the hundreds or thousands. This is much more plausible than a seven-person hiring committee, and it is close to the paper’s own parliamentary-election motivation. The relevant assumption is not that all voters everywhere are interchangeable; it is that this instance regime contains many repeated types.

The authors should recognize this as their problem rather than a tractability-destroying simplification. Their rules already aggregate voter contributions by summing scores over rankings. Replacing repeated counts by rational masses preserves the rule, the committee-size constraint, the scoring objective, and the treatment of ties. It simply exposes the high-multiplicity structure that an explicit voter sequence hides. The resulting question is not “can we fractionally elect candidates?” but “what committee maximizes average representation for a society described by type proportions?”

The scope is deliberately limited. This mirror covers Proposition 2 and the weakly separable rules to which it applies. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s \(\ell_1\)-CC, Monroe, Greedy, or STV results. In particular, I would not pretend that Proposition 2 demonstrates discrete hardness dissolving in the continuum: the anchored discrete problem is already polynomial-time solvable. Its value is as a clean baseline showing how the paper’s score-based computation behaves under an exponentially compressed electorate.

It also generates more consequential follow-up questions. The natural next one is a continuous \(\ell_1\)-CC problem in which the objective becomes
\[ \max_{|W|=k} \sum_{t}\mu_t\max_{c\in W} \alpha^m\bigl(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr), \]
with the Monroe analogue imposing mass \(1/k\) for each selected representative. Those would test whether the paper’s cited discrete hardness is driven by population multiplicity or by the combinatorics of choosing representatives. Another direction is continuous winner robustness: the minimum mass that must change type before a given \(k\)-committee ceases to win.

The weakest point is that this anchor is almost a baseline rather than a dramatic continuization result. Since Proposition 2 is already an elementary top-\(k\) algorithm in the finite model, its continuous analogue does not reveal a new complexity separation. Moreover, the high-multiplicity story requires genuine repeated ranking types; it is less convincing for electorates where nearly every individual has an idiosyncratic ballot. Still, the mirror is exact, computationally well-defined, and plainly recognizable as the high-multiplicity form of the paper’s weakly separable committee-selection problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Proposition 2 is too thin to justify a continuous mirror as a research priority. Its proof already aggregates voters by candidate scores. With masses \(\mu_t\), one computes
\[ q_\mu(c)=\sum_t \mu_t\alpha^m_k(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)) \]
and selects the \(k\) largest values. This is exactly the finite algorithm with voter counts replaced by normalized counts. The only genuine gain is representational: binary-encoded multiplicities can describe an exponentially large electorate compactly. There is no new optimization structure, pricing problem, approximation phenomenon, or population-driven complexity transition. It is a weighted top-\(k\) computation.

The proposed parliamentary-cohort story does not strengthen the case much. It makes the high-multiplicity representation plausible, but it also confirms that the mirror is merely a compressed encoding of the same election. If “worthwhile” requires substantive computational content beyond normalization and input compression, Proposition 2 should be treated as a baseline sanity check, not as the paper’s central continuization result.

That is, however, not enough to sustain the universal negative requested here. The objection is about scientific payoff, not validity, and the programme explicitly treats a well-posed high-multiplicity question as worthwhile even when its answer is straightforward. Proposition 2 is a named computational result, its voters are anonymous ordinal types, and its continuous version is exactly a distribution over those types. None of the permitted objections—identity dependence, lack of multiplicity, or degeneration—applies.

Nor does a stronger mirror disappear. The paper’s \(\ell_1\)-CC rule naturally becomes
\[ \max_{|W|=k} \sum_t \mu_t\max_{c\in W} \alpha^m_k(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)), \]
which is a genuine population-level representative-selection problem for movie audiences or political constituencies. Monroe’s rule similarly becomes a mass-assignment problem in which each selected candidate receives mass \(1/k\). A rational mass instance can be expanded into a finite profile by clearing denominators, while the continuous formulation removes irrelevant rounding effects. These models preserve an integral committee and integral representation for each unit of population; only the type-level description is fractional, exactly as the programme permits.

Those stronger mirrors may remain hard because the combinatorics lie in selecting candidates, but that is not an objection under the programme’s rules. They are still computational questions about a continuous society, and the paper’s own applications supply credible high-multiplicity regimes.

So the honest negative conclusion is narrow: Proposition 2 is a weak anchor whose continuous counterpart adds little beyond weighted aggregation. It cannot support the claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper should receive, at most, a modest positive assessment based on its anonymous population structure and its natural \(\ell_1\)-CC/Monroe continuizations; a universal negative case is not credible.
