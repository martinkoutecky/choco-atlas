The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is a compressed, high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Leontief Nash aggregation. But there is an important limit: the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorems 1 and 2 prove axiomatic impossibilities; Theorem 3 characterizes a mechanism. None classifies a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar. So Theorem 3 is the nearest useful result, not a qualifying computational anchor.

Its content, proved in this paper, is: with Leontief utilities, NASH is the only continuous mechanism satisfying group-strategyproofness and core fair share. Here “continuous” means continuity in the reported peaks of a finite electorate; it does not mean the population is a continuum.

A credible population regime would be a city-wide budget consultation with many residents choosing among a relatively small menu of standard budget proposals. Let a type be a peak \(p_t\in\Delta_m\), with the paper’s Leontief utility \(u_t(q)=\min_{j:p_{t,j}>0}q_j/p_{t,j}\). The type description must include the utility model, so agents sharing a type are identical in every respect the aggregation uses. The instance is a list of \(\tau\) such types with positive rational population shares \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); the intended regime has many residents and comparatively few distinct types.

One precise computational extension is Continuous Leontief Nash Budget Aggregation: given \(m\), the rational type peaks \(p_t\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\), find a distribution \(q\in\Delta_m\) maximizing
\[ \prod_{t=1}^{\tau}u_t(q)^{\mu_t}, \]
or, for an approximation version, return a rational \(q\) whose weighted log-welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(q)\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of optimum. This asks for the collective budget itself, not for a fractional outcome in place of a continuous population.

This is a sensible extension of the object characterized by Theorem 3, and it has a clean high-multiplicity dictionary: for rational \(\mu\), choose a common denominator \(D\) and make \(D\mu_t\) copies of each type. The finite Nash product then has the same maximizers as the weighted product above. I would expect the approximation version to be tractable through convex optimization: introduce utility variables \(v_t\), impose \(v_t\le q_j/p_{t,j}\) for each positive peak coordinate, and maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t\log v_t\). That expected algorithmic result would be a new analysis of a compressed input, not a complexity result established by this paper. Exact-output complexity and the extension of the paper’s strategic and fairness properties to mass-based deviations would need separate treatment.

The mirror’s main weakness is that the anchor does not meet your required criterion, and the rational-mass instance has an exact finite-replication counterpart. The positive case is therefore for a plausible computational extension of the paper’s Nash rule, rather than a continuous mirror anchored in one of its computational results. It covers Theorem 3’s Leontief aggregation only; I would not stretch it to Theorems 1 and 2, which concern axiomatic impossibilities rather than complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is Theorem 3, but it is not a computational result. It characterizes the unique finite-population mechanism satisfying group-strategyproofness, core fair share, and continuity. Here, “continuous” means continuity of the mechanism as reported peaks vary; the agents remain a finite set. Theorem 3 gives no algorithmic problem, encoding, or complexity claim to continuize. The other prominent results in the paper are also axiomatic characterizations or impossibilities. Under the programme’s stated criterion, that is decisive: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror.

The proponent’s city-budget scenario is credible. Residents can share one of \(\tau\) standard peak types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\). The weighted Nash objective is well-defined, and rational masses have an exact finite-replication interpretation: for a common denominator \(D\), \(D\mu_t\) copies of type \(t\) give the same maximizers. That is a genuine high-multiplicity bridge, so I would not object that the population regime is implausible or that replication makes it a collision.

The limitation is that this bridge creates a new computational question around the Nash rule, rather than a computational version of Theorem 3. The weighted objective can be written directly as maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\log v_t\) over \(q\in\Delta_m\), with \(v_t\le q_j/p_{t,j}\) for each positive coordinate of each type. That is an explicit finite-dimensional convex formulation. It may be useful to study its precise input and output guarantees, but it does not uncover a pricing problem or complexity question supplied by the paper. The rule’s fairness and strategyproofness characterization remains axiomatic; extending it to mass-based deviations would be a separate axiomatic project.

So the strongest case against inclusion is narrow but solid: the paper does not anchor the computational continuization programme. The proposed population model is sensible, and a computational companion could still be useful, but that companion would introduce its own question rather than continue one of this paper’s computational results. The negative case is weaker if “worthwhile” includes any new algorithmic study motivated by an axiomatic paper; on the programme’s stated standard, I would vote against this paper as a mirror candidate.
