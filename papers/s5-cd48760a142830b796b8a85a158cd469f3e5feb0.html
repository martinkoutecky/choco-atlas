The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. It does give Algorithm 1 (CATD), states an unnumbered \(O(|C|)\) running time, and derives the weight optimization in Eqs. (6)–(7). Those provide a basis for a plausible mirror, but they do not meet your anchor rule. I can’t honestly present an anchor-based positive case.

The strongest candidate is a high-multiplicity version of CATD for categorical crowd answers. Imagine a large quiz or polling service where many participants answer questions from a recurring question bank, often answering only a few. A type is a participant’s complete response profile: exactly which questions they answered and what they answered. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these profiles; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants with profile \(t\). This is plausible when the number of participants is very large but repeated question formats and partial response patterns keep the number of distinct profiles \(\tau\) comparatively small.

Given the question set, the supported profiles and their masses, a significance level \(\alpha\), and CATD’s initial truth estimate for each question, CATD over Source Populations asks for a nonnegative reliability weight \(w_t\) for each type and the resulting truth estimate for every question. For each type, compute \(u_t^2\), CATD’s upper confidence bound on its error variance, from its answered questions and their deviations from the initial truths. Choose weights to minimize

\[ \sum_t \mu_t u_t^2 w_t^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t \mu_t w_t=1,\qquad w_t\ge 0. \]

Then aggregate each question’s answers using these weights over the types that answered it; for categorical answers, choose the highest-weighted category. Restricting to instances where \(u_t^2>0\) keeps the paper’s weight formula defined.

This is my lead mirror, but it is unanchored under your rules. It covers CATD’s categorical truth discovery and reliability-weight computation, not the paper’s full range of numerical applications. Its expected outcome is tractability: the paper gives a closed-form weight rule proportional to \(1/u_t^2\), followed by weighted aggregation. The continuous input makes repeated participant profiles carry mass; it does not change the computation into a new hard problem.

The weakest point is whether exact response profiles really repeat enough for \(\tau\ll N\). In personalized surveys, they may not, and then the proposed population description offers little compression. A further question is how the finite CATD output relates to the population version as participant counts grow. But even a convincing version of that mirror cannot supply the missing named hardness or algorithmic result required for an anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor. The paper has Definitions, Algorithm 1, equations (6)–(7), experiments, and an unnumbered \(O(|C|)\) running-time statement. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary asserting a computational result. Under the assignment’s stated rule, that is decisive: the candidate mirror is explicitly unanchored.

The source-population model itself is coherent. If a type is a respondent’s complete answer profile, identical respondents can be grouped by mass, and the proposed weighted aggregation follows CATD’s equations. But exact profiles preserve the paper’s key small-source quantity: duplicating a profile increases its mass without increasing the number of claims per source, so its confidence bound \(u_t^2\) stays the same. With a fixed small question bank, this is chiefly a compressed representation of the finite-source calculation. As the bank grows, the number of possible profiles grows exponentially and repeated profiles become less useful for compression.

A stronger mirror could group sources by coverage pattern and latent reliability, then pool their independent answers to estimate type-level reliability. That has a plausible crowd-sourcing story, but pooling changes CATD’s per-source variance estimation into a hierarchical model. It may be worthwhile research; this paper supplies no named computational result for it to mirror.

The honest limit of this negative case is that I cannot establish the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. Large populations answering a recurring, small set of questions make high multiplicity plausible, and CATD’s weighted categorical aggregation gives a natural starting point. My case against rests on the missing qualifying anchor and on the exact-profile mirror’s limited computational payoff. The broader claim that no continuous mirror could be worth the programme’s time remains weak.
