The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is at the level of the paper’s problem definition, not its named results. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, so it has no qualifying anchor. Section 2.3 defines the simultaneous clustering and ranking problem, and Equations (5)–(7) give an objective the authors optimize in their experiments; neither is a numbered named result. The experiments do not establish a complexity result. I can’t honestly present an anchor-driven case from this paper.

A plausible mirror of the underlying problem would be SCARPA over a continuous evaluator population. Consider a large platform where reviewers compare ideas or designs for preference and similarity. A type \(t\) is a reviewer response profile: for each queried pair \((i,j)\), it specifies probabilities \(q^P_{t,ij}\) of preferring \(i\) and \(q^S_{t,ij}\) of judging the pair similar. These probabilities capture the type’s full response behavior, so reviewers of the same type are interchangeable. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over a small support of such types, with \(N\) reviewers large relative to the number \(\tau\) of types.

The population’s pairwise labels are
\[ \bar p_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_t q^P_{t,ij}, \qquad \bar s_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_t q^S_{t,ij}. \]
Given the objects, queried pairs, embedding dimension \(d\), and objective parameters, ask for embeddings \(x_i\in\mathbb R^d\) and a criterion vector \(w\in\mathbb R^d\) maximizing the paper’s expected joint objective: the Bradley–Terry log-likelihood using \(\sigma(w^\top(x_i-x_j))\), plus the similarity log-likelihood using \(\phi_{ij}=\exp(-\|x_i-x_j\|^2)\), with the paper’s regularization. A computational version could request a rational \(\varepsilon\)-optimal solution, from which the ranking and pairwise similarity estimates are derived.

This is a recognizable continuous-population interpretation of the paper’s crowdsourced evaluation setting: mass is evaluator share, and the decision variables and objective remain the authors’ joint embedding and preference estimation. The paper’s own studies have only tens of objects and tens or hundreds of workers; the stronger regime would be a large review panel with a small number of repeated rubric or market-segment response profiles.

I would not assign this candidate a defensible A/B/C classification from the paper. The mirror leaves the object embeddings and the nonconvex objective in place, so a large evaluator population does not by itself suggest tractability. If hardness comes from the object-side geometry, it may persist; if it comes specifically from finite panel size, the population limit could change it. The paper establishes neither possibility. Further questions include how the finite-worker and population objectives relate, and the complexity of finding a global \(\varepsilon\)-optimum as a function of \(n,\tau,d\), and input bit length.

The weakest point is decisive: the continuous evaluator formulation is a plausible extension of the problem setup, but the paper already represents pairwise votes as fractions in \([0,1]\), and it proves no computational claim for the continuous version. So this is a credible mirror of the modeling problem, but not a theorem-backed continuization case of the kind the programme is meant to classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no qualifying computational result to mirror. Its contributions are a problem definition and an objective in Section 2.3 and Equations (5)–(7), followed by experiments. It proves no complexity theorem, algorithmic guarantee, or approximation result. Even the statement that SCARPA has the same order of running time as its baselines is not a complexity analysis. That leaves no result here to place in ChoCo’s tractable, inherited-hardness, or continuum-specific-hardness trichotomy.

The proposed reviewer-population model is a plausible way to describe where the labels come from. But if type \(t\) has pairwise response probabilities \(q^P_{t,ij}\) and \(q^S_{t,ij}\), the model feeds SCARPA only the means
\[ \bar p_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_tq^P_{t,ij},\qquad \bar s_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_tq^S_{t,ij}. \]
Those are precisely the kinds of fractional pairwise labels the paper already accepts. Its objective cannot distinguish two reviewer populations with the same means, regardless of their support sizes or internal composition. Supplying \(\mu\) and the type profiles therefore reduces to computing the means, then solving the same embedding problem. A global-optimization question could still be asked, but its computational difficulty would come from the object-side objective, with no population structure left in the problem.

A more population-sensitive proposal would let someone reweight or persuade reviewer types until a target design becomes top-ranked, or until the inferred clusters change. That could be a worthwhile new robustness or control problem. It requires choices the paper does not make: which reviewer changes are allowed, their costs, and how the output is selected when the joint objective has multiple optima. It also adds an intervention problem to the estimator; it does not continue a computational result established by SCARPA.

The negative case has a real limit: a large panel drawn from a few stable evaluator types is plausible, and the paper’s setting makes that story easy to tell. I would reject this as a paper-anchored continuization because the paper has no computational theorem and its estimator sees only aggregate pairwise rates. I would not claim that continuous crowd-evaluation or robustness research is inherently uninteresting.
