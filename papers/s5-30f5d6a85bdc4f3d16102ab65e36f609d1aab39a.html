The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results—Lemmas 1–2 and Propositions 1–4, all proved here—concern consistency of types, existence and efficiency of assignments, symmetry, and approximate incentive compatibility. None classifies a computational problem as tractable, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. Proposition 2 defines a welfare-maximizing assignment, and Proposition 3 proves an asymptotic incentive property, but neither analyzes the computation required. The cited continuum results are related work, not computational results established by this paper.

The closest positive lead is a large allocation market with many applicants sharing a finite set of complete preference-and-knowledge types. One could represent each type by its population mass and ask how a symmetric welfare-maximizing assignment rule behaves as the population grows. But that would mirror the paper’s mechanism-design and asymptotic incentive results, not a named computational result. Under the anchor requirement, I can’t honestly state it as a continuous mirror of this paper’s complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent did not actually name a computational result as an anchor. That is the strongest objection to a continuous *mirror* here: Propositions 1–4 establish welfare, symmetry, and incentive properties, but none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Equation (5) asks the mechanism to find a welfare-maximizing assignment; the paper does not analyze the cost of doing so. A complexity study of that step could be useful, but it would supply a computational result the paper lacks rather than mirror one it proves.

The most plausible rescue is a large assignment market with repeated complete types: many applicants with the same cardinal values or ordinal information, and object supplies scaled with population. That regime is credible, and the paper’s own \(N\)-growth results support it. But the most tempting named anchor, Proposition 3’s \(\varepsilon\)-Bayesian incentive compatibility, loses its content in an atomless population model. A single agent’s report does not change the distribution of reports, so a mechanism based on that distribution gives the agent no gain from deviating. The result becomes automatic. Keeping an agent’s deviation consequential requires retaining positive-sized agents, which leaves the continuum model.

Propositions 2 and 4 offer a better modeling route: represent each type by a mass and assign type mass across objects, allowing fractional type-level averages. That gives a sensible welfare-allocation problem. But it does not preserve the Simple Mechanism’s specific selection rule: uniform randomization over the finite set \(\sigma\) of optimal assignments to named players. A continuum flow does not itself specify a uniform lottery over those assignments. One could define a limit along finite populations, perhaps yielding an entropy-based selection, but that would be an additional limit result whose outcome depends on the sequence and scaling chosen. It would be a distinct project in continuum random assignment, not a computational result mirrored from this paper.

I would therefore reject the proponent’s lead as an anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme. I would not claim the broader universal conclusion is airtight: a computational study of type-mass assignment, or of limits of the mechanism’s randomization, could be worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is strongest—and the evidence supports it—when “mirror” means a continuous counterpart to a named computational result of this paper.
