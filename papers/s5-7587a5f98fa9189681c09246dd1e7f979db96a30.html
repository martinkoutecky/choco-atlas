The continuous mirror question

Given parties \(C\), coalition \(A\), rational threshold \(t\), target \(\phi\), per-capita budget \(B\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) ranking types with linear coalition-shift costs, decide whether there are nonnegative transfers \(x_{r,r'}\) with \(\sum_{r'}x_{r,r'}=\mu_r\), each from \(r\) to a ranking \(r'\) reachable under the paper’s coalition-shift rule, such that \(\sum_{r,r'}\gamma(r,r')x_{r,r'}\le B\) and \(A\)’s seat share is at least \(\phi\). Compute plurality scores from the resulting distribution \(\mu'\), activate each party whose score is at least \(t\), and allocate seats among active parties in proportion to their scores.

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types; \(x_{r,r'}\) assigns mass from each original ranking to an allowed post-bribe ranking. Minimize the average shift cost \(\sum_{r,r'}\gamma(r,r')x_{r,r'}\), or decide whether it is at most \(B\), subject to the paper’s thresholded plurality seat-share goal for coalition \(A\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 7’s Plurality\(_t\)-CB hardness result for coalition-shift bribery with multiplicative shift costs, using its linear-cost exact-cover construction; leaves the paper’s other bribery types, rules, and CBP results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this decision problem in the number of parties \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\); does Theorem 7’s reduction establish NP-hardness for rational mass transfers?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the continuous-population version of Theorem 7. The theorem, proved in this paper by reduction from 3-4-Exact-Cover, says that Plurality\(_t\)-CB with coalition-shift bribery is NP-hard even when the shift-price functions are multiplicative; the appendix’s reduction uses linear shift costs. The underlying problem is the paper’s budgeted coalition-bribery decision problem: can a briber spend at most \(B\) to make the coalition’s seat share at least \(\phi\)?

I would call the mirror Continuous Plurality-Threshold Coalition-Shift Bribery. An instance gives parties \(C\), a coalition \(A\subseteq C\), a threshold \(t\), a target \(\phi\), a budget \(B\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete voter types. Here a type is a ranking together with its shift-cost schedule; in the theorem’s restricted case, every type has unit linear shift cost. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r=1\).

A solution is a nonnegative transfer \(x_{r,r'}\) of mass from each original ranking \(r\) to a resulting ranking \(r'\). Transfers must obey the paper’s coalition-shift rule: coalition parties may move upward, but not downward past noncoalition parties. Each unit of transferred mass pays the corresponding shift cost, so the total cost is \(\sum_{r,r'}\gamma(r,r')x_{r,r'}\), and each source type’s outgoing mass sums to \(\mu_r\). The resulting distribution is \(\mu'\). Compute plurality scores from its masses, activate parties using the paper’s threshold rule, and allocate seats by the paper’s renormalized seat-share formula. The decision question is whether some such transfer costs at most \(B\) and gives \(A\) seat share at least \(\phi\). This keeps the paper’s coalition objective and its thresholded seat rule intact; it continuizes the population, not the outcome.

A plausible regime is a large parliamentary electorate in which voters fall into recurring ranking-and-cost cohorts: many people share a ballot type and are treated alike by a campaign. For any finite construction, each type can represent a very large cohort, while the number of represented agents grows far beyond the support size. This is consistent with the paper’s parliamentary setting. Its seats are already fractional, even for a finite electorate, so that feature is not the continuization here.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: the hardness survives. That expectation is an inference from the proof, not a result established by the paper for continuous societies. In its 3-4-Exact-Cover construction, each element contributes a voter unit of mass \(1/(2n)\), there is a protected noncoalition cohort of mass \(1/2\), and each set is a coalition party associated with four elements. The threshold is \(t=2/n\), so a set party needs four element-units to activate. The target is \(\phi=1/2\), and the fixed noncoalition support makes the movable mass tightly constrained: the coalition needs \(n\) element-units in active set parties, while each such party can receive at most four. Thus exactly \(n/4\) set parties must be filled to capacity, which is the exact-cover condition.

Fractional transfers do not obviously undo that constraint. The element-to-set assignments form a bipartite packing problem with integer capacities and demands; if there is no exact cover, the minimum additional protected mass needed to fill the set parties is at least one element-unit. In the reduction, the protected rankings require more than \(3n\) shifts to reach the coalition, while the normalized per-capita budget is \(3/2\). That budget can move less than one element-unit of protected mass. So the construction appears to retain its exact-cover obstruction under mass splitting. If correct, the continuous problem remains NP-hard for this family.

This is a mirror the authors could recognize: it asks whether a coalition can reach the same thresholded plurality seat share using the same coalition-shift actions, with cohorts replacing named voters. I would cover Theorem 7 alone rather than stretch the claim across the paper’s Borda and preferred-party variants.

My weakest point is the regime the reduction actually certifies. Its threshold is \(t=2/n\), shrinking with the exact-cover instance, and the number of parties and types grows with that instance. It does not establish hardness for a fixed statutory threshold, bounded party set, or every high-multiplicity election. Those are natural next questions, along with whether the threshold-free continuous counterpart has a flow or LP algorithm, and whether the Borda and CBP reductions also survive fractional mass transfers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly defeat the Theorem 7 anchor for the continuous model as stated. Its exact-cover reduction appears to survive fractional transfers. In the construction, each element type has one element-unit of mass, and a coalition party becomes active at four units. Reaching the target requires at least \(n\) units of active coalition support. The budget permits moving less than one element-unit from protected rankings. With \(n/4\) active set parties, a collection that is not an exact cover leaves at least one element-unit unavailable to those parties; splitting mass cannot fill that deficit. The tight threshold and budget block the obvious fractional escape.

The strongest negative case is about the regime the reduction represents. Its threshold is \(t=2/n\), so it shrinks as the construction grows, and its \(n\) element-specific ballot types make the support size grow with the reduction. That is a poor fit for the most familiar high-multiplicity picture: a large electorate with a stable, relatively small set of ballot types and a fixed statutory threshold. The paper’s result does not establish hardness in that regime; a mirror focused on it would need new work.

But this narrows the result rather than defeating it. The stated model permits variable thresholds and growing support, and each type can represent a large cohort. Those choices are legitimate under the programme’s scope. Nor does an inherited hardness result make the question unworthy: locating a boundary where continuization fails is one of the programme’s aims. So my case against is weak. The proponent’s anchor survives unless the intended mirror is restricted to fixed-threshold, bounded-support regimes.
