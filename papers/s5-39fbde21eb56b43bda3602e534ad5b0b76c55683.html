What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors cover the paper's oligarchy, oligarchic-democracy, and random-sampling implementation guarantees; the paper's remaining results likewise concern implementation conditions, not computational complexity.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its closest named results concern mechanism existence:

  • Theorem 4.4 (proved here): in an economic environment, if \(3\le |S|<n\) and \(F\) is weak \(S\)-monotonic, then \(F\) is implementable by \(S\)-oligarchic democracy.
  • Corollary 4.5 (derived here using Maskin’s cited monotonicity result and Theorem 4.4): every Nash-implementable \(F\) in an economic environment is implementable by any oligarchic democracy with at least three oligarchs.
  • Corollary 5.7 (derived here using Maskin’s cited result and Theorem 5.4): in an economic environment, every Nash-implementable \(F\) is implementable by random sampling of at least four individuals.

These classify which social choice rules admit mechanisms in the paper’s finite-agent game. They do not classify the computational complexity of a problem as polynomial-time, hard, or parameterized.

There is a plausible *new* population model: represent citizens by a distribution \(\mu\) over preference types and ask whether a rule \(F(\mu)\) can be implemented using a small oligarchy or random sample. The main weakness is that in an atomless population an individual has zero probability of being sampled, so the unilateral deviations that drive these results disappear. Making types or blocs the strategic players would change the problem. So this is not an anchor-backed continuous mirror under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of these results states a computational problem. Theorem 4.4 and Corollaries 4.5 and 5.7 classify which social choice rules admit exact Nash implementation under particular political processes. The paper gives no encoded input, decision problem, running time, or algorithmic resource bound to carry over to a continuous society. Its sets of states and alternatives can be infinite, and the constructed mechanisms use messages containing \(\Theta\), \(A\), and unbounded integers. To turn the results into complexity questions, one would first have to choose representations for the rules, environments, and mechanisms, and restrict what mechanisms count. That would create a new computational problem rather than continuize a computational result from this paper.

Theorem 4.4 is the best candidate for a substantive mirror: represent citizens by a distribution over preference types, then ask whether a target rule can be implemented with oligarchic democracy. But its proof depends on individual agents’ unilateral deviations. In an atomless population, a single person’s report changes no aggregate mass, so the theorem’s incentive argument does not transfer. Giving each preference type a representative who can change the type’s entire mass restores pivotal deviations, but makes a type a strategic bloc; it no longer models many indistinguishable citizens. Adding a few named oligarchs to an atomless population is more faithful to the original mechanism, but then the continuous population is only background to a finite-agent implementation problem. Neither version supplies a computational question about the distribution.

Corollary 4.5 does not improve that prospect. It says that, in the stated finite-agent economic environments, three named oligarchs suffice for every Nash-implementable rule. Treating those three people as a negligible share of a continuous society makes the message-collection fraction vanish; taking the regulation probability toward zero also makes its per-capita cost vanish, while exact implementation in the theorem is maintained only at positive probabilities. One could study costs under a chosen finite-population approximation or impose a positive minimum regulation probability, but the resulting optimization would depend on those added choices. The corollary itself gives a constant-size existence guarantee, not an algorithmic landscape over population distributions.

Corollary 5.7 has the same mismatch. In a population of \(n\) named agents, four sampled reports are pivotal with positive probability. In an atomless society, each individual has zero probability of being selected, so the usual ex-ante Nash incentives disappear. A sample of four *types* drawn from \(\mu\) could preserve randomness, but it changes the political process: the paper samples agents, not types, and its exact implementation requirement concerns the outcomes across finite-agent senates. A limiting sequence of finite populations retains the finite game at every \(n\); its limit need not retain the same equilibria or implementation result.

So the proponent is right that the finite-sample incentive structure degenerates in a literal atomless limit, but that is only part of the case. The deeper issue is that every proposed anchor is an existence theorem whose operative objects are named players, their deviations, and an unrestricted mechanism. A type-level or coalition-based redesign might lead to worthwhile research, but it would need a new equilibrium concept and a computationally represented input. The negative case is strongest for these anchors and this paper; it cannot prove that no related continuous computational question could ever be worthwhile.
