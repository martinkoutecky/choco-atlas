The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are about mechanism properties, not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. For example, Theorem 1 characterizes incentive compatibility through weak monotonicity and three payment conditions; Theorem 6 proves that VCG payments are revenue-optimal for the efficient allocation; and Theorem 7 proves that the Exhausted-Reference Pricing mechanism is IC, IR, and weakly budget balanced. These are proved here, but none gives a complexity result. Algorithm 1 optimizes over item bundles, yet the paper does not analyze its running time.

There is a plausible *future direction*: large online auction markets where many buyers share complete valuation and referral roles. One could represent each role by a type, let \(\mu_t\) be its population share, and study allocation and payment policies over that population, with item supply scaled to the market. That would make the society continuous and connect naturally to the paper’s revenue and incentive questions. But it would require specifying how referral links and invitation decisions work in the continuum.

That is also the weakest point in the positive case. An individual buyer’s invitation can change who becomes valid and participates. In a standard atomless population, one individual has no effect on aggregate participation; preserving a non-negligible diffusion effect may require a new referral model that the authors might not regard as their problem. So this is a possible research direction, not a prover-ready continuous problem anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Under the stated anchor rule, there is no honest A/B/C prediction to give.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes incentive compatibility; Theorem 6 establishes revenue optimality of VCG payments; Theorem 7 proves properties of the ERP mechanism. None classifies a computational problem or analyzes an algorithm’s running time. Algorithm 1 gives a procedure, including a search over item bundles, without a complexity claim. These are real results, but the continuous questions suggested by them would begin a new computational project rather than mirror a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 1 is the clearest mismatch. A continuous version could characterize IC and IR for mass-based mechanisms, or ask whether a specified allocation rule is implementable. Those remain mechanism-design questions, not complexity questions. A more computational formulation might test IC from a succinct description of a rule. Yet a distribution over valuation types does not capture the theorem’s central strategic feature: a buyer’s invitations can change which other buyers become valid. If an individual’s report has no effect on aggregate validity, that part of the incentive constraint loses force. If a buyer can change validity for a positive population, the model needs to retain that buyer’s network position and influence; a small list of type masses alone will not describe it. A continuum network could retain those relationships, but the resulting problem would need its own computational formulation.

Theorem 6 suggests the strongest replacement: compute efficient allocations and VCG payments for a high-multiplicity market, with supply scaled to population. Grouping buyers by valuation could yield a natural mass allocation problem, but it discards the referral graph that determines who participates. Two markets with identical valuation shares can have different valid buyers and different VCG outcomes because their referral structures differ. Keeping enough network information to preserve those outcomes may require a large number of distinct roles or a new assumption such as repeated network modules. That could support a worthwhile algorithmic study, but it would add a computational question the paper never asks. In a genuinely atomless model, removing one buyer also removes zero mass, so VCG’s individual externality can collapse. Replacing that payment with a marginal cohort price may be sensible, but it changes the object whose optimality Theorem 6 proves.

Theorem 7 has a similar obstacle. ERP selects a particular minimum-reach buyer, excludes that buyer’s reachable set, and processes the remaining named buyers in BFS order. A mass version would need new rules for selecting and ordering cohorts, and a new incentive argument for what an individual deviation does to those cohorts. Repeated network modules could give many buyers the same role and make an ERP-like mechanism worth studying. But batching those modules changes the mechanism’s defining steps; Theorem 7 gives no computational claim for that new mechanism to mirror.

So I would reject the proposed mirror as stated. The case is strongest on the formal gap: none of the three cited theorems is a computational result. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous research can grow from this paper. Large markets with repeated network roles could motivate useful work on allocation and incentives. That possibility establishes a direction to formulate; it does not supply a computational mirror of these results.
