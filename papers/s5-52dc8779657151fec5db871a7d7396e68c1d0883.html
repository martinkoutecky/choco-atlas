What the mirror covers

No computational mirror covers Theorem 1 or Theorem 2; both are axiomatic impossibility results, and Theorem 2 is the paper’s contribution.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I can’t make a strong continuous-mirror case under the programme’s criteria.

Theorem 1, attributed to Grether and Plott, says that with unrestricted linear preferences and agendas containing every set of at least \(k\) alternatives, no social choice correspondence can satisfy Arrow’s Choice Axiom, Independence of Infeasible Alternatives, Weak Pareto, and Nondictatorship. Theorem 2, attributed to Dutta, Jackson, and Le Breton and proved here, says that no voting function satisfies Independence of Nonvoters’ Preferences, Independence of Infeasible Alternatives, Unanimity, Candidate Stability, and Strong Nondictatorship. These are axiomatic impossibility results, not results about computational complexity or algorithms. The paper’s contribution is a proof of Theorem 2 using Theorem 1.

The closest population-continuous formulation would take voter types to be linear rankings \(\ell\in L\), let \(\mu\) be a distribution over those rankings, and ask whether a rule mapping each candidate agenda and \(\mu\) to a winner can satisfy continuous versions of the paper’s axioms. But that asks an axiomatic existence question. It also needs a new account of Strong Nondictatorship when society is represented by masses rather than named voters. Under this programme’s scope, that is not an acceptable mirror.

So there is no lead problem, expected complexity class, or further computational question to state from a qualifying anchor here. The paper contains named theorems, but no named computational result of the required kind.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no computational result to continue. Theorem 1 is a prior axiomatic impossibility result used in the proof; Theorem 2 is also an axiomatic impossibility result. Neither defines an encoded input, an algorithmic task, or a complexity question. The paper’s contribution is a simpler proof of Theorem 2.

The proposed distribution over rankings, \(\mu\), gives the axioms a continuous-population domain, but the resulting question is still whether a voting function can satisfy those axioms. That is an axiomatic existence question, outside ChoCo’s scope. Giving \(\mu\) rational masses and tracking its support size does not create an algorithmic problem: the paper specifies no finite representation of a voting function to test or construct.

The strongest computational follow-up would be to choose a particular voting rule and ask, given \(\mu\), whether the full candidate slate is stable or which entry equilibria exist. That uses a continuous voter population and is related to the paper’s subject. But it adds a rule and an algorithmic objective the paper does not specify; its complexity would depend on that choice. It is a possible new project, not a computational mirror of either theorem. The same is true of adding mass-transfer costs to ask how much campaigning would change stability.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: its named results offer no computational anchor, and the closest continuous formulations remain axiomatic. I cannot establish that no related continuous candidate-entry problem could ever be worthwhile; that would be a broader claim than the evidence supports.
