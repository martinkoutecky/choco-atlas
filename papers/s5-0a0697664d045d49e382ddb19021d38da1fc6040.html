The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is a population version of the paper’s rule-choice experiment. A type could record a participant’s position, egalitarian values, information condition, and experience of the first-stage outcomes. A society would be a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with mass representing each type’s share of participants. Each type chooses between plurality and approval; under the paper’s random-dictator procedure, the mass choosing approval would be the probability that approval is used.

That is a coherent continuous-population scenario, but it only recasts the paper’s empirical question as a prediction about population choice shares. It does not supply a computational problem or a named computational result to mirror. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only foothold—a population version of the rule-choice experiment—is plausible. A large citizen assembly could be represented by masses of complete types recording values, political positions, information, and prior experience. Aggregating their choices would give the probability that approval voting is selected under the random-dictator procedure.

But that does not give this paper a ChoCo anchor. It states no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result: its hypotheses are tested through an experiment and regressions. In the population version, the main quantities are choice shares and payoff distributions, obtained by aggregation. That could motivate a larger empirical study, but it does not turn a result of this paper into a computational question.

The stronger alternatives also change the paper’s contribution. Optimizing over many electoral rules would add a collective selection mechanism and a welfare objective the experiment does not specify. Modeling mass voting under plurality or approval would require a behavioral or strategic model beyond the paper’s, and tallying specified ballots is not its research question. Either could become worthwhile research, but neither is a continuous mirror of a named result here.

So the negative case is strong for the paper-anchored question: there is no eligible result to mirror, and a sensible high-multiplicity setting does not supply one. The broader claim that no scenario could ever yield a worthwhile continuous mirror is weaker; the citizen-assembly setting makes that too sweeping. The defensible conclusion is that such a mirror would require inventing a new computational problem rather than continuizing this paper’s results.
