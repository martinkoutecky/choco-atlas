The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a compliant named computational anchor in this paper. Its own contribution is empirical: the simulations estimate about a 1.4% chance of effective tanking in the original format, and show that 14 added draw restrictions reduce that estimate below 0.013%. Those are important computational findings, but they are not numbered Theorems, Lemmas, Corollaries, or Propositions asserting a complexity or algorithmic result.

The paper cites Proposition 3 of Dagaev and Sonin (2018) for the claim that this kind of qualification format permits manipulation. As the paper describes the case, a lower-ranked team can benefit by losing to a higher-ranked team, helping it take a group’s top-two place and opening a secondary qualification slot. That proposition is from elsewhere, and it establishes manipulability, not a computational classification. It cannot serve as the requested anchor.

If I relax the anchor requirement to offer the strongest *prospective* mirror of the paper’s central study, it would be Continuous Incentive-Risk Design. Consider a scaled qualification system with \(G\) groups of fixed size \(q\), where \(G\) grows while the support size \(\tau\) stays fixed. A team type \(t\) specifies its secondary-priority tier, draw-pot and eligibility constraints, strength, and match-outcome and tanking behavior; \(\mu_t\) is its share of the teams. Draws sample teams from these type masses into groups, retaining a finite group in which one team can still affect its own standing. The decision is a set of prohibited type-pair clashes \(z_{tt'}\), subject to a restriction budget and any stated limits on changes to qualification chances. For a realized tournament, mark a team as vulnerable if it does not qualify under full effort but can qualify by unilaterally tanking against a higher-priority opponent, using the paper’s counterfactual test. The goal is to choose \(z\) to minimize
\[ R(z)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,b_t\right], \]
where \(b_t\) is the fraction of type-\(t\) teams vulnerable in that tournament. A solution is an optimal feasible set of type-level restrictions.

A plausible regime is a large system of regional or youth qualifiers: many teams share the same priority tier, strength class, pot, and constraints, with \(N=Gq\) much larger than \(\tau\). The authors could recognize the question as a scaled version of their central concern—measuring and reducing profitable tanking through draw restrictions. I would expect fixed-policy risk evaluation to be tractable when \(q\) and \(\tau\) are small and match outcomes have finite rational descriptions; choosing the restrictions is a separate open optimization question, potentially hard for continuum-specific reasons.

The weak point is substantial: this is not a mirror of a numbered computational result in the paper. It also extends the setting from one 55-team tournament to many groups with repeated priority tiers, and changes the measured quantity from the probability that *any* team can tank to the expected mass of vulnerable teams. Keeping groups finite preserves local pivotality, but does not erase those changes. So this is a coherent research direction, not an anchor-based positive case under the stated rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no eligible computational anchor. It has no theorem, lemma, or corollary classifying the complexity of a named problem. Its main findings are simulation estimates and a recommendation about draw restrictions. The cited Proposition 3 is an external result about manipulability, not a computational result. So there is no named result here whose complexity landscape a continuous mirror could chart.

The proposed Continuous Incentive-Risk Design is the strongest prospective candidate, but it has to build a new model around the paper. In the actual tournament, teams have distinct Nations League ranks, draw pots, and Elo ratings; those attributes determine both which clashes can be prohibited and who can fill a scarce playoff place. Retaining those distinctions makes the type support grow with the number of teams. Collapsing teams into a fixed set of types requires treating ranks as tiers and specifying how ties within a tier affect the allocation of playoff places.

There is also a scaling problem. With a fixed number of Nations League playoff places and more groups, only a bounded number of teams can occupy the pivotal positions in the ranking. The mass of teams exposed to this particular incentive can then vanish as the tournament grows. Scaling the number of secondary places with the number of groups avoids that degeneration, but changes a central feature of the paper’s mechanism and requires new choices about eligibility and priority. The paper does not select among these continuations.

Those objections defeat the proposed model as a *mirror of a result in this paper*: it is a plausible new stochastic design problem, but the paper provides neither the computational problem nor the asymptotic family. They do not establish that the new problem would be unworthy. With repeated team types, finite groups, and a sensible rule for secondary places, the risk-minimization question could be coherent, and the authors might recognize it as an extension of their concern.

So the negative case is strong on the narrow anchor requirement and weak on the user’s universal claim. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario; the best candidate survives as a prospective research direction, though not as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.
