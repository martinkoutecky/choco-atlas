The continuous mirror question

Each period \(t\), draw an i.i.d. shock \(\omega_t\) from a finite rational distribution of support size \(\tau\). It specifies arriving worker masses \(a_H^{\omega_t},a_L^{\omega_t}\) and job masses \(a_h^{\omega_t},a_l^{\omega_t}\), with each side’s total mass equal to \(1\). Given rational joint match surpluses \(u_{xy}\), \(x\in\{H,L\}\), \(y\in\{h,l\}\), and per-period waiting cost \(c>0\), the planner chooses feasible match masses \(m_{xy,t}\geq 0\) from the available masses and carries unmatched mass into the next period’s queues \(q_{z,t+1}\). Given these inputs and \(\epsilon>0\), compute the optimal value of \(\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{x,y}m_{xy,t}u_{xy}-c\sum_{z\in\{H,L,h,l\}}q_{z,t+1}\right)\) within additive error \(\epsilon\), together with a stationary policy attaining that guarantee.

The model it lives in

A two-sided, four-type fluid matching market with queue masses as the state, feasible match masses as decisions, and long-run expected match surplus minus per-period waiting costs as the objective. Finite-support common shocks generate the arrival masses each period; agents within each type share preferences, match values, and waiting costs.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a mass version of the paper’s centralized optimal dynamic matching problem. The anchor is Proposition 2 (Optimal thresholds), proved in this paper. It says that an optimal mechanism uses
\[ \bar{k}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)U}{2c}}\right\rfloor: \]
it matches every available \((H,h)\) and \((L,l)\) pair immediately, and matches any excess \((H,l)\) or \((L,h)\) pairs immediately once their queue exceeds that threshold. The optimal mechanism is generically unique. This is a closed-form optimization result, not a theorem about a complexity class; the paper contains no NP-hardness or other formal complexity classification to anchor on.

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study. Call it \(2\times2\) Fluid Optimal Dynamic Matching. Each discrete period, a large cohort arrives on each side of a matching market: workers of types \(H,L\), and jobs of types \(h,l\). Type is a complete description for the model—preferences, match values, and waiting costs are identical within each type. Normalize each side’s arriving mass to one. A market-wide shock \(\omega\) determines the type-mass vector
\[ a^\omega=(a_H^\omega,a_L^\omega,a_h^\omega,a_l^\omega), \]
where each side’s two masses sum to one. Shocks are drawn i.i.d. from a finite rational distribution with support size \(\tau\). The instance gives that distribution, rational joint match surpluses \(u_{xy}\), and a rational per-period waiting cost \(c>0\).

After arrivals, the planner chooses matching masses \(m_{xy}\geq 0\), subject to the available mass of each type. Unmatched mass joins the corresponding type’s queue for the next period. The objective is to maximize long-run expected welfare,
\[ \liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \sum_{x\in\{H,L\},\,y\in\{h,l\}}m_{xy,t}u_{xy} -c\sum_{z\in\{H,L,h,l\}}q_{z,t+1} \right), \]
where \(q_{z,t+1}\) is the mass of type \(z\) left waiting after period \(t\). The computational question is: given these rational inputs and an error tolerance \(\epsilon>0\), compute the optimal long-run value to within \(\epsilon\), together with a stationary policy that chooses feasible matching masses and attains that guarantee. The algorithmic target for this fixed four-type model would be polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\).

The natural regime is a large hiring clearinghouse serving repeated cohorts in one city or industry: perhaps thousands of workers and openings per season, but only four relevant worker/job types and relatively few recurring cohort-composition shocks. Mass is the fraction of a cohort, so the model’s fractional type-level matching decisions represent integral matches among many individuals. Time remains discrete; the continuity is in the population and its allocation.

This is a plausible extension of the authors’ problem because it keeps the same two-sided assortative matching trade-off, per-person waiting costs, and planner’s long-run welfare objective. Proposition 2 suggests a tractable direction: in the symmetric two-type case, the dynamics may reduce to a signed queue and a threshold, after which one can optimize the threshold or solve a low-dimensional average-reward problem. I would expect Class A for this restricted model, while treating a polynomial-time guarantee as the research question rather than something established by the paper. It also raises a useful bridge question: can a policy for the mass model yield welfare guarantees for finite markets with many agents per cohort?

The mirror covers Proposition 2’s centralized-planner result only. I am not proposing a mirror of the discretionary equilibrium results or of the paper as a whole.

The weak point is material: the paper has one square and one round arrive per period with independently drawn types. In a large market with only independent individual-level type draws, cohort proportions concentrate, and the imbalance that makes waiting valuable can disappear. My mirror therefore uses common market-level composition shocks, as might arise from industry-wide changes in hiring demand. That gives a sensible high-multiplicity matching problem, but it changes the arrival regime; the authors might regard it as an extension rather than the direct continuous version of their model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the only anchor, and it is a structural welfare result: the optimal threshold is \(\left\lfloor\sqrt{p(1-p)U/(2c)}\right\rfloor\). The paper gives no numbered result on the complexity of computing an optimal mechanism or on an algorithmic class. So the proposed fluid MDP would be a new computational project, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The direct high multiplicity version also loses the result’s central trade-off. Suppose \(n\) independently drawn square-round pairs arrive each period, as in the paper. Since both sides have the same type probability \(p\), their cohort proportions converge to the same vector. The number of residual incongruent agents after immediate assortative matching is only \(O(\sqrt n)\), or \(O(n^{-1/2})\) per capita. The queue needed to absorb those fluctuations likewise vanishes as a share of the cohort. In the population limit, arrivals are balanced by type and the planner can match them assortatively as they arrive. The paper’s nontrivial queue is driven by finite-agent fluctuations; those fluctuations disappear at fluid scale.

The proposed common market shocks preserve macroscopic imbalance, so they evade that degeneration. They also change the paper’s independent type draws into correlated cohort composition shocks. That makes the algorithmic question one about long-run control under external regime shocks, rather than the high multiplicity version of Proposition 2’s arrival process. It is a plausible related project, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as an extension of their dynamic matching problem.

That is the limit of the negative case: it defeats the direct mirror and puts the proposed one outside the paper’s stated stochastic model, but it cannot rule out the common-shock scenario as a worthwhile mirror under ChoCo’s broader scope. The claim that no scenario works is therefore weak.
