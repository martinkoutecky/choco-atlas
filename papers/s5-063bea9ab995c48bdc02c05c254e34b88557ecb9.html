The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is that this paper does not pass the programme’s computational-anchor gate. It contains a natural continuous-population mirror, but no named result about polynomial-time solvability, NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, approximation, or an optimization formulation.

The closest substantive anchor is the main result printed as Theorem QuantitativeTransitive. It is proved in the paper. It states that if \(f:[q]^V\to[q]\) is a fair and monotone aggregation function, and \(m=m(f)\) is the size of its smallest social type, then

\[ \mu_\delta(f)\ge 1-C_q\exp\!\left( -C_q\frac{\delta\log m}{\log(1/\delta)} \right), \]

where \(\mu_\delta(f)\) is the worst-case probability that alternative \(0\) wins when its initial probability exceeds every other alternative’s probability by at least \(\delta\). Thus information is aggregated efficiently as every social type becomes large.

This is a strong substantive invitation to continuization. The authors already identify social types as the relevant units of interchangeability, and their conclusion is explicitly controlled by the smallest type size. The natural mirror is therefore:

Continuous Type-Mass Aggregation. An instance consists of:

  • a finite set of complete social types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\);
  • for each type, its common network role, degree or neighbour-type profile, update rule, and tie-breaking convention;
  • \(q\) alternatives and a finite number \(H\) of interaction rounds;
  • an initial distribution \(p\) over alternatives, with \(p_0\ge p_a+\delta\) for every \(a\neq0\);
  • a fair monotone final election rule.

The state is \(z_{t,a}(r)\), the mass of type \(t\) holding alternative \(a\) after round \(r\). The update rule maps the type-level opinion densities at round \(r\) to those at round \(r+1\), preserving total type mass \(\mu_t\). The final winner is

\[ Y_\infty=\arg\max_{a\in[q]} \sum_{t\in T}z_{t,a}(H). \]

The decision problem is: does \(Y_\infty=0\)? A quantitative version asks for the minimum initial bias \(\delta\) making \(0\) win, or for an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to that threshold.

The regime is plausible: a very large anonymous peer network, with \(N\gg\tau=|T|\), in which many users have the same degree, neighbourhood role, update rule, and signal distribution. A vertex-transitive expander is the cleanest case: \(\tau=1\), while \(N\) may be enormous. This is genuinely population continuization, not outcome-space fractionalization. Mass means the fraction of users occupying each social role; alternatives remain discrete, and interaction rounds remain discrete.

The expected classification is Class A for a fixed finite type system, bounded local degrees, and finite \(H\): the continuum state is finite-dimensional, and each update can be evaluated by dynamic programming over local opinion counts. More ambitious versions—where \(H\), degrees, or the type interaction structure are succinctly encoded—could have a nontrivial complexity landscape.

The paper’s negative boundary is also informative. Theorem 2.1, proved here, says that for every \(\delta>0\) there is a sequence of graphs \(G_n\) with \(|G_n|\to\infty\) such that

\[ \sup_n\sup_{H\ge1}\mu_\delta(G_n,H)<1. \]

Thus a constant error probability persists for every population size and every number of rounds. In the proposed mirror, this warns that ordinary mass vectors are insufficient to capture vanishing but structurally pivotal types: the bad type in the construction has constant cardinality and asymptotically zero population mass. A faithful extension would need a two-scale model retaining a finite “core” of rare types alongside the continuous bulk.

That is also the weakest point. The paper studies correlations generated by a concrete finite graph, whereas the type-mass recurrence above is a mean-field closure. Exact unanimity and the failure construction depend on local graph geometry and finite-population correlations that a plain distribution \(\mu\) forgets. One would need to include a finite type-level interaction schema or a graph-lift family, and proving that the resulting limit preserves the paper’s dynamics would itself be new work.

So the positive case is real but conditional: the authors’ social-type framework makes a credible high-multiplicity mirror, and the resulting continuous aggregation-threshold problem is worth studying. But under the programme’s strict requirement that the mirror be anchored in a named computational result, this paper has no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper fails the computational-anchor gate before the modelling question begins. Its named results are probability and dynamical statements, not complexity results: Theorem QuantitativeTransitive gives a sharp-threshold bound, Theorem 2.1 gives an existential graph family, and the expander results give convergence conditions. None asks for an algorithm, a complexity classification, an optimization problem, or an approximation guarantee. A continuous reformulation might be mathematically interesting, but it would not be a mirror of a computational result from this paper.

The proposed mirror of Theorem QuantitativeTransitive also does not preserve the theorem’s object. The parameter \(m(f)\) is the number of independent input coordinates in the smallest orbit of the aggregation function. It is not a population mass. A type with mass \(0.1\) could contain ten agents or \(10^{10}\) agents, but the theorem distinguishes those cases through the \(\log m(f)\) factor. Passing to an atomless population erases precisely the finite multiplicity that drives the bound.

Indeed, with finitely many types and independent initial signals, a continuum mass model makes the type-level initial proportions equal to their expectations almost surely. The finite-\(n\) error probability that the theorem studies disappears. One obtains a deterministic mass evolution, if such an evolution is defined at all, rather than a continuous analogue of the sharp-threshold probability estimate. Retaining the multiplicities \(N_t\) merely gives a finite-population asymptotic analysis, not a population continuum.

More seriously, the proposed state \(z_{t,a}(r)\) is not sufficient to describe the paper’s dynamics. Social types are orbits under symmetries of the entire aggregation function; they do not form a Markov quotient for local majority dynamics. Two vertex-transitive graphs can have the same single type, the same type mass, and even the same degree and neighbour-type profile, while their finite-round majority dynamics differ because their higher-order neighbourhood geometry differs. A cycle and a high-girth regular expander illustrate the issue. Type masses record marginal opinions but not the spatial correlations and overlapping neighbourhoods created by the updates.

The best repair would be to enrich the model with rooted neighbourhoods, overlap data, or a continuum graphing carrying a random opinion field. But then the continuous object is the network geometry and the stochastic field, not merely the population. For sparse graphs, an ordinary graphon is not even the right limit object; one needs a graphing or local weak limit retaining microscopic structure. Its computational input representation, precision model, and decision problem would all have to be invented independently. That could support a new analytic or computational programme, but it is not a faithful population continuization of this paper.

Theorem 2.1 is no better. Its obstruction is a constant-size set \(A\) whose initial unanimous error event has probability bounded away from zero, despite \(|A|/|V|\to0\). The set has vanishing population mass but order-one causal influence through the network. An ordinary continuous society necessarily loses it: mass-zero types disappear. The proposed “bulk plus finite core” repair retains exactly the exceptional named agents that continuization was supposed to replace, and is therefore a mixed discrete–continuum model rather than a continuous population.

Assigning the core positive mass changes the asymptotic regime and the graph’s influence ratios. Duplicating the core so that it has high multiplicity makes the unanimous-error event exponentially unlikely and removes the theorem’s phenomenon. Letting its mass tend to zero while its influence remains macroscopic requires a singular scaling or an infinitesimal atom, not a distribution \(\mu\) over ordinary population types.

Thus the two anchors fail for complementary reasons. The first has no computational question and loses its finite multiplicity under the continuum limit; the second depends essentially on a vanishing-mass exceptional core and cannot be represented by mass alone. A graph-aware mixed model might be worthwhile as a different project, but it would not be a continuous mirror of this paper in the ChoCo sense. The negative case is strongest at the programme gate; the broader claim that no related continuum-network model could ever be worthwhile is necessarily less than airtight.
