The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many complete valuation types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), goods \(G\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and nonnegative integer valuation vectors \(v_{t,g}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{\ell}\), does there exist a finitely supported distribution \(x_{t,b}\ge 0\) over type-and-integral-bundle states \( (t,b) \), where \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\), such that \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g\) for every \(g\)? Require that for every ordered pair of occupied states \((t,b),(t',b')\) and every dimension \(k\), some deletion vector \(r\in\{0,1\}^{G}\) satisfies \(r_g\le b'_g\), \(\sum_g r_g\le 1\), and \(\sum_g b_gv_{t,g,k}\ge\sum_g(b'_g-r_g)v_{t,g,k}\). Theorem 12’s reduction uses one valuation type, binary valuations, and \(q_g=1/2\) for every good kind.

The model it lives in

A finite-type population with mass \(\mu_t\) of each valuation type, per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and a distribution over integral bundles for individuals. The decision variable is the mass \(x_{t,b}\) assigned to each type-and-bundle state; feasibility means supplies are met and every pair of occupied states satisfies weak sEF1 in every dimension.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 12’s weak sEF1 existence hardness. It leaves the existence bounds and algorithms, the strong sEF1 hardness in Theorem 13, and the verification hardness in Theorem 15 unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuous problem become tractable under fixed numbers of dimensions or good kinds?
  • What is the complexity landscape for continuous weak sEF\(c\) as \(c\), the type support, and the number of dimensions vary?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s hardness result for weak sEF1. The anchor is Theorem 12, proved in this paper: deciding whether a weak sEF1 allocation exists is strongly NP-complete even with two agents who have identical binary valuations. The proof reduces from monotone NAE-3-SAT, using items for variables and valuation dimensions for clauses. In each clause dimension, exactly three items have value \(1\); a weak sEF1 allocation must split those items between the agents, which is exactly the not-all-equal condition.

Here is a continuous population version of that decision problem. Call it Continuous Weak-sEF1 Existence (CW-sEF1). Its instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(G\) of item kinds, rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and valuation vectors \(v_{t,g}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\ell}\). An individual of type \(t\) receives an integral multiset bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\); its value in dimension \(k\) is \(\sum_{g\in G} b_gv_{t,g,k}\). The decision variable is a finite-support distribution \(x_{t,b}\) of population mass across type-and-bundle states, with
\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,b} b_gx_{t,b}=q_g \quad (g\in G). \]
The question is whether such a distribution exists where, for every ordered pair of occupied states \((t,b),(t',b')\) and every dimension \(k\), type \(t\) values its own bundle at least as highly as \(b'\) after removing at most one good copy from \(b'\). Thus each person still receives an integral bundle; only the population masses and type-level averages are continuous.

A plausible regime is a large cohort of users with a small number of complete valuation profiles evaluating the same kinds of resource packages across several criteria. For \(N\) users, \(q_g=1/2\) means \(N/2\) copies of kind \(g\). The anchor’s reduction uses the especially high-multiplicity case \(\tau=1\): everyone has the same binary valuation vector.

The reduction transfers directly to CW-sEF1. For each variable-item kind \(g_j\), set \(q_{g_j}=1/2\), and give it value \(1\) in exactly the clause dimensions containing \(x_j\). In any clause dimension, the population-average value is \(3/2\). Weak sEF1 forces the values of any two occupied bundles in that dimension to differ by at most \(1\): removing one binary-valued good can lower the envied bundle’s value by at most \(1\). Since these values are integers with average \(3/2\), every occupied bundle has value \(1\) or \(2\) in that clause. Mark \(x_j\) true if a bundle contains at least one copy of \(g_j\). Each clause then has one or two distinct true variables, so this assignment satisfies the NAE instance. Conversely, given a satisfying assignment, put half the population in the bundle of true variables and half in the bundle of false variables. Every kind has supply \(1/2\), and each clause has values \(1\) and \(2\) across the two bundles, so one removal suffices. Hence CW-sEF1 is strongly NP-hard even with one valuation type and binary values.

This is my lead mirror. Its expected outcome is Class B: discrete hardness transfers. The proof’s combinatorics remain in the item kinds and valuation dimensions; distributing agents across a continuum does not erase them. The paper’s authors should recognize the question: it preserves the allocation of indivisible goods, additive vector valuations, and the weak sEF1 test. It makes the population high-multiplicity and scales item supplies per capita, so the mass assigned to each bundle may be fractional while each individual bundle stays integral.

The mirror covers Theorem 12, not the paper’s existence bounds, algorithms, strong sEF1 hardness, or verification result. It raises further questions about CW-sEF\(c\) for larger \(c\), about the strong version with one shared removal set across dimensions, and about whether fixed \(\ell\) admits tractable algorithms.

The weakest point is the supply interpretation: a fixed finite inventory does not itself produce an atomless population, so the mirror uses repeated item kinds whose supplies scale with population. Also, the anchor’s hardness grows with the number of dimensions and item kinds; it says nothing about fixed-dimensional regimes. Those limits do not undo this mirror, but they define where the claim applies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat the Theorem 12 anchor. Its continuous mirror is coherent: with one valuation type, half a unit of per-capita supply for each item kind, and integral bundles for individuals, every clause dimension has average value \(3/2\). Weak sEF1 forces any two occupied bundles’ values in that dimension to differ by at most \(1\). Since bundle values are integers, each occupied bundle has value \(1\) or \(2\) in every clause. Any one such bundle therefore gives a not-all-equal assignment. Conversely, a satisfying assignment and its complement, each assigned to half the population, meet the supply and fairness constraints. This is a strong, direct mirror of a named computational result.

The best objection I can make is that the continuum is not canonical. The agents’ valuation type can stay fixed at a single point, while the continuous variable is the distribution of integral bundles across the population. Fairness then depends on every bundle state of positive mass, however small. A type-average or almost-everywhere interpretation could define a different problem. The proposed model is therefore a hybrid of a continuum of agents and indivisible individual allocations, and its fairness quantifier deserves explicit justification.

That concern does not defeat this mirror under the programme’s stated scope. Per-capita supplies may scale with population, and individuals may keep integral bundles. The paper’s group-fairness discussion also makes it plausible that its authors would recognize fairness across a population of bundle states as their problem. I find no solid basis for saying that no scenario makes the mirror worthwhile; the negative case is weak, and this anchor survives.
