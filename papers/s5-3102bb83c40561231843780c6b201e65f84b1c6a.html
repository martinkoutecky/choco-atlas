The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), innate opinions \(s_t\), baseline resistances \(\alpha_t\), a rational column-stochastic influence kernel \(P\), and a quota \(\kappa\), choose real masses \(z_{t,0},z_{t,1}\geq 0\) with \(z_{t,0}+z_{t,1}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t(z_{t,0}+z_{t,1})\leq\kappa\). For each allocation, let \(r_t=\mu_t-z_{t,0}-z_{t,1}\), \(a_{t,*}=\alpha_t\), \(a_{t,0}=0\), \(a_{t,1}=1\), and let the unique equilibrium satisfy \(x_{t,h}=a_{t,h}s_t+(1-a_{t,h})\sum_uP_{ut}y_u\) and \(y_t=(r_tx_{t,*}+z_{t,0}x_{t,0}+z_{t,1}x_{t,1})/\mu_t\). Determine the global minimum and maximum of \(F_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\sum_t[r_t(x_{t,*}-\bar{s})^2+z_{t,0}(x_{t,0}-\bar{s})^2+z_{t,1}(x_{t,1}-\bar{s})^2]\), where \(\bar{s}=\sum_t\mu_ts_t\); equivalently, for rational \(q\), decide whether a feasible allocation achieves \(F_{\mathrm{MSE}}\geq q\) or \(F_{\mathrm{MSE}}\leq q\).

The model it lives in

A block-structured FJ society with type masses μ, shared innate opinions and baseline resistances, and a type-to-type influence kernel \(P\). Decision variables allocate up to quota κ of the population across endpoint resistance values \(0\) and \(1\); the objective is the mass-weighted MSE from the original mean innate opinion at equilibrium.

What the mirror covers

This selected mirror covers Theorem 2’s MSE maximization and minimization under resistance interventions. Theorem 1’s polarization result is a second viable mirror; the non-submodularity results, greedy heuristic, and empirical findings are not directly covered.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the vertex-cover hardness survive when a type’s treated mass can be split fractionally?
  • If it does not, is the mass-allocation problem tractable or hard for a continuum-specific reason?
  • What approximation guarantees or rounding bounds relate type-mass solutions to finite block-homogeneous populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large opinion network in which many people share a small number of behavioral and interaction roles. Picture millions of users, but only dozens or hundreds of recurring types: each type has the same innate opinion, baseline resistance to persuasion, and distribution of whom it listens to. A type’s mass is its fraction of the population. An operator can turn at most a fraction \(\kappa\) of that population into “stooges,” changing their resistance to one of the two endpoint values used in the paper, \(0\) or \(1\). The question is how to distribute that intervention across types to raise or lower the population’s error, or its polarization, after opinions settle.

This keeps the paper’s central ingredients: the Friedkin–Johnsen equilibrium, resistance interventions, and the MSE and polarization objectives. Only the population and the intervention budget become continuous. Time remains discrete, opinions remain in \([0,1]\), and the question is still computational optimization over interventions—not analysis of an opinion-dynamics PDE.

Here is a type-level version precise enough to study. Let \(T\) be a finite set of types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), innate opinions \(s_t\), baseline resistances \(\alpha_t\), and an influence kernel \(P_{ut}\). The entry \(P_{ut}\) is the probability that an agent of type \(t\) listens to an agent from type \(u\), with \(\sum_u P_{ut}=1\). Choose masses \(z_{t,0}\) and \(z_{t,1}\) from type \(t\) to receive resistance \(0\) and \(1\), respectively, subject to \(z_{t,0}+z_{t,1}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t(z_{t,0}+z_{t,1})\leq\kappa\). The remaining mass \(r_t=\mu_t-z_{t,0}-z_{t,1}\) keeps resistance \(\alpha_t\).

Write \(x_{t,*},x_{t,0},x_{t,1}\) for the equilibrium opinions of the untreated and treated parts of type \(t\), and \(y_t\) for the mean equilibrium opinion within that type. For \(h\in\{*,0,1\}\), let \(a_{t,*}=\alpha_t\), \(a_{t,0}=0\), and \(a_{t,1}=1\). The equilibrium is specified by \(x_{t,h}=a_{t,h}s_t+(1-a_{t,h})\sum_uP_{ut}y_u\), with \(y_t=(r_tx_{t,*}+z_{t,0}x_{t,0}+z_{t,1}x_{t,1})/\mu_t\). As in the paper, restrict attention to instances where the relevant intervention choices have a unique equilibrium.

Set \(\bar s=\sum_t\mu_ts_t\) and \(\bar x=\sum_t\mu_ty_t\). The continuous MSE is \(F_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\sum_t[r_t(x_{t,*}-\bar s)^2+z_{t,0}(x_{t,0}-\bar s)^2+z_{t,1}(x_{t,1}-\bar s)^2]\). The continuous polarization is \(F_{\mathrm{pol}}=\sum_t[r_t(x_{t,*}-\bar x)^2+z_{t,0}(x_{t,0}-\bar x)^2+z_{t,1}(x_{t,1}-\bar x)^2]\). These are the paper’s objectives averaged over population mass.

My lead is Mass-MSE Stooge Optimization, mirroring Theorem 2. The theorem states that maximizing and minimizing the MSE in Problem 1 are NP-hard. This paper proves that result, adapting its vertex-cover construction; it is not merely a cited theorem. The continuous problem has the same two directions: given rational type data and a mass budget \(\kappa\), find a feasible allocation of stooge mass that globally maximizes or minimizes \(F_{\mathrm{MSE}}\). Its decision versions take a rational threshold \(q\) and ask whether some feasible allocation attains \(F_{\mathrm{MSE}}\geq q\) for maximization, or \(F_{\mathrm{MSE}}\leq q\) for minimization. A solution is the allocation and its resulting equilibrium; an algorithmic version could instead seek an additive-\(\varepsilon\) approximation to the optimum. This is the strongest anchor because MSE is the paper’s direct measure of the wisdom of crowds, and its hardness construction targets that objective.

A second, independent mirror is Mass-Polarization Stooge Optimization, anchored by Theorem 1. It states that maximizing and minimizing polarization in Problem 2 are NP-hard. The theorem is proved in this paper: its maximization proof extends Gionis et al. [31], and its minimization proof adapts the construction. In the continuous version, the input and feasible mass allocations are as above, but the objective is \(F_{\mathrm{pol}}\), centered at the post-intervention population mean \(\bar x\). The threshold decision questions are whether a feasible allocation can make \(F_{\mathrm{pol}}\geq q\) or \(F_{\mathrm{pol}}\leq q\). A prover would need to determine the complexity of those optimization questions over mass allocations and type-level equilibria. The endogenous center \(\bar x\) makes this a distinct question from MSE, even though the paper finds their optima closely related in experiments.

I would expect hardness to transfer (Class B) for both mirrors. The paper’s reductions get their combinatorics from vertex cover in the influence network, rather than from the number of copies of each behavioral role. That makes network structure—and thus the number and arrangement of types—look like the likely source of hardness. For a block-homogeneous finite society with \(N_t\) agents of type \(t\), \(\mu_t=N_t/n\) and \(\kappa=k/n\) translate the discrete setting into this model. Discrete interventions sit on a grid of mass allocations in the continuous feasible region. If the network’s type-level influence pattern retains the reduction’s structure, there is a plausible route to a hardness-transfer theorem.

That route is a research question, not a consequence of Theorems 1 or 2. The continuous feasible region also allows a type’s mass to be split fractionally between treated and untreated agents. The paper’s reductions choose whole vertices; their proofs do not show that a fractional allocation cannot evade the vertex-cover threshold. The first question is therefore whether the hardness survives that divisibility, perhaps through a gap-preserving reduction. If it does not, can the equilibrium feedback still yield continuum-specific hardness (Class C), or does fractional treatment make the problem tractable (Class A)? Further questions include additive approximation, rounding a continuous solution back to a finite population, and how complexity depends on the type-support size \(\tau\) and the structure of \(P\). For polarization, one can additionally ask whether its moving center changes the answer relative to MSE.

The scenario is plausible for a large platform or a recurring population of users grouped by opinion, susceptibility, and interaction role. It is also a proper high-multiplicity regime when the population is much larger than the support \(T\). Each type must be complete for the model: members share the attributes that determine their innate opinion, baseline resistance, and type-level influence behavior. A network with repeated communities or stable user cohorts supplies a credible setting; the paper’s own social-media application points toward it. The continuous question does not rely on fractional outcomes or on making time continuous. Fractional mass is the natural description of the population and of an intervention quota.

My weakest point is the same one the prover must confront: this is not simply the paper’s finite graph with \(n\) replaced by infinity. It assumes agents within a type have the same influence profile and permits an intervention to select a fractional part of that type. Those are natural assumptions in a block-structured high-multiplicity scenario, but they may smooth away the very integral choice that makes the paper’s hardness proofs work. The mirror still seems worth asking because it preserves the paper’s model and objectives in a realistic population regime; whether its hardness survives is precisely the computational question the continuous version would put on the table.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against both anchors is that the paper’s combinatorics live in the network at the level of individual vertices. Its stooges are chosen partly for how their positions in the graph shape the equilibrium. For an exact high-multiplicity type, it is not enough to share an innate opinion and resistance: members must also be indistinguishable in the influence structure the problem uses. In the paper’s sparse, heterogeneous networks, that can leave nearly every vertex as its own type. Then \(\tau\) grows with the population, and describing the graph by type masses gives little of the high-multiplicity simplification ChoCo seeks.

That challenges the proposed mirrors of Theorems 1 and 2: their vertex-cover reductions use specific graph structure, while a small type space with a kernel \(P\) replaces that structure with type-to-type mixing. This is a substantive modelling choice, not just a continuum limit of the paper’s input. MSE and polarization both inherit the same issue, so they are not independent escapes from it.

But that objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A block-structured population with exchangeable members and a fixed positive intervention fraction is a plausible regime of the FJ problem. Its mass-weighted MSE and polarization preserve the paper’s objectives, and deciding the best intervention over type masses is a genuine computational question. The fact that fractional treatment may change the hardness is not a reason to dismiss it under ChoCo’s criteria. Nor does the network mismatch rule out a scenario the authors would recognize.

So the negative case is weak: it identifies a serious limit on how much of the paper’s arbitrary-graph setting a small type space represents, but it does not rule out either mass-optimization anchor in a plausible block-structured regime. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
