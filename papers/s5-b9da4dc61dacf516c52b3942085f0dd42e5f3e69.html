The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t \ge 0\) summing to \(1\), a standard-compatibility relation \(S \subseteq T \times T\), and a rational per-capita reserve budget \(\beta\), let \(\mathcal{C}_3\) be the feasible directed cycle patterns of lengths \(1\) through \(3\), with repeated types allowed and counted by their occurrences. For each pattern \(C\), choose a rational mass \(q_C \ge 0\). Find an optimal allocation maximizing \(\sum_{C \in \mathcal{C}_3} |C|q_C\), subject to \(\sum_C a_t(C)q_C \le \mu_t\) for each \(t \in T\) and \(\sum_C r(C)q_C \le \beta\), where \(a_t(C)\) counts occurrences of type \(t\) and \(r(C)\) counts arcs outside \(S\); return the optimum and an optimal mass allocation.

The model it lives in

An atomless population of recipient-donor pairs with complete compatibility types \(t \in T\) and mass \(\mu_t\). Decision variables \(q_C\) assign population mass to feasible cycles of at most three pairs; each person in a cycle still donates and receives one whole kidney. The objective maximizes transplanted mass, and the reserve-arc budget is \(\beta\) per unit population.

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers \(K=3\) KEP-RA optimization over type masses and the associated one-reserve-arc structure. It leaves out KEP-HCA generation, chains, the experiments, and the managerial results.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous, type based version of KEP-RA with cycle limit \(K=3\). It targets the paper’s hardness result: Proposition 1, proved in this paper, establishes the polynomial reduction chain \(\mathrm{KEP}\le_p\mathrm{KEP\text{-}RA}\le_p\mathrm{KEP\text{-}HCA}\le_p\mathrm{KEP\text{-}C}\). The reductions encode KEP in KEP-RA by setting the reserve budget to zero; KEP-RA in KEP-HCA by treating reserve arcs as half-compatible and allowing no incompatible arcs; and KEP-HCA in KEP-C by assigning costs \(0\), \(1\), and \(B+1\) to standard, half-compatible, and incompatible arcs, respectively. Table 1 consequently reports KEP-RA as NP-hard for \(K\ge3\). The underlying KEP hardness is cited from Abraham et al. (2007); this paper proves the reductions that transfer it.

Here is the continuous question. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete recipient–donor types, binary rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and a standard compatibility relation \(S\subseteq T\times T\). A type includes all details relevant to this problem, so members of a type have the same compatibility pattern to each type. As in KEP-RA, every arc outside \(S\), including a self-loop if applicable, is a reserve arc. The instance also gives a rational per-capita reserve budget \(\beta\). A cycle pattern \(C=(t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\), with \(1\le\ell\le3\) and \(t_{\ell+1}=t_1\), specifies the types participating in one exchange cycle. Repeated types are allowed: their occurrences use distinct members of that type. Let \(a_t(C)\) count the occurrences of \(t\), and let \(r(C)\) count the arcs of \(C\) outside \(S\).

Call this problem Continuous 3-Cycle KEP-RA. For each cycle pattern \(C\), choose a nonnegative rational mass \(q_C\) of the population to execute that cycle. The question is to find feasible \(q_C\) maximizing the fraction transplanted, \(\sum_C |C|q_C\), subject to \(\sum_C a_t(C)q_C\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_C r(C)q_C\le\beta\). A solution is the optimal value and a rational mass for each chosen cycle pattern. Each participating person still receives and donates a whole kidney; \(q_C\) is the mass of complete cohorts executing that cycle, not a fractional kidney or outcome.

The regime I have in mind is a large regional or national exchange pool, perhaps across repeated matching runs, with many pairs sharing the same medically relevant compatibility profile. Those profiles determine each pair’s type, while \(N\), the number of pairs, is much larger than the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct types. The problem is recognizable to the authors: it keeps their cycle limit, compatibility arcs, reserve budget, and transplant-count objective, while replacing counts of indistinguishable pairs by their population masses. The budget’s per-capita scaling, \(\beta=B/N\) in a finite instance of size \(N\), is the natural co-modification as the population grows.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. For fixed \(K=3\), there are at most \(\tau+\tau^2+\tau^3\) cycle patterns, so the stated problem is an explicit rational linear program, solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The discrete NP-hardness need not transfer: the discrete problem chooses whole cycles subject to indivisible vertex conflicts, whereas the continuous problem shares type mass among cycle patterns. That is the population relaxation being studied, not a change to fractional individual transplants.

There is also a concrete bridge back to high multiplicity. If a finite instance has \(n_t\) pairs of type \(t\), total \(N\), and budget \(B\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(\beta=B/N\). Any discrete solution gives a feasible continuous solution by dividing its cycle counts by \(N\), so the continuous optimum is an upper bound. Conversely, flooring \(Nq_C\) for each cycle in a continuous solution preserves type capacities and budget and loses fewer than \(\sum_C|C|\le3(\tau+\tau^2+\tau^3)\) transplants. Thus for fixed \(\tau\), the relaxation gives a bounded additive loss as the population grows.

The same cycle-splitting idea also appears to carry over from Theorem 2, proved here: it says KEP-RA has an optimal solution in which every cycle contains at most one reserve arc. In the mass model, split each positive-mass cycle at its reserve arcs and close the resulting path components. This preserves the transplanted mass and uses no more reserve mass, so the paper’s structural insight remains relevant to the continuous problem.

This case covers the bounded-cycle KEP-RA hardness result and its one-reserve-arc structural property. It does not claim to mirror the HCA generation algorithm, chains, or the computational experiments. Further questions include what happens when \(K\) is variable, when only a subset of incompatible arcs are usable as half-compatible arcs, and how the finite-instance approximation behaves in realistic pools.

The soft spot is the regime: exact compatibility can depend on detailed HLA and other clinical information, leaving some registries with nearly one type per pair. The mirror is strongest where many pairs genuinely share complete decision-relevant profiles; it should not be sold as representative of every exchange pool. That limitation narrows the application, but it does not erase the problem: in such a regime, the authors’ own cycle objective and constraints admit a precise population version, and the resulting complexity question is different because the society itself is continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the gap between a finite *type* set and a finite set of coarse clinical categories. To preserve the paper’s compatibility graph exactly, two pairs can share a type only if their compatibility behavior is identical across the other types. With donor and recipient immunology driving compatibility, that can leave nearly one type per pair. Then the proposed cycle LP still has at most \(O(\tau^3)\) patterns for \(K=3\), but the high-multiplicity interpretation has little force, and the flooring bound of \(O(\tau^3)\) transplants need not be small compared with a pool of \(N\) pairs. Coarsening to blood-group or other broad categories would create type pairs whose members are not uniformly compatible, so it would no longer represent the paper’s graph exactly. The negative case is strongest here: the mirror’s value depends on a compact support regime that may not fit the medically detailed instances this paper targets.

The budget adds a related concern. The paper’s \(B\) is a cap on the *number* of reserve arcs. If \(B\) stays fixed while \(N\) grows, the per-capita budget \(\beta=B/N\) tends to zero, and the normalized limit loses the reserve-arc feature. Keeping \(\beta>0\) requires scaling the allowed number of immunosuppressed transplants with pool size. That is a reasonable co-modification, but it is a policy assumption rather than a consequence of the paper’s model. Neither point rules out a useful regime; both make the proposed large-pool scenario carry more of the argument than the paper itself does.

I cannot honestly defeat Proposition 1’s anchor on mathematical grounds. Its reductions establish the discrete hardness transfer into KEP-RA for \(K\ge3\), and a population model with type masses asks a distinct question even if the answer is an explicit LP. The programme treats that change in answer as a potential result, so “the continuous version is easy” is no objection. Nor does Theorem 2 fail under the proposed model: splitting mass among the resulting cycles is compatible with an atomless population. A stronger KEP-HCA mirror could retain the paper’s three-way arc classification and budget, so the objection that reserve arcs are stylized also does not dispose of the broader anchor.

The surrounding type-based literature makes a blanket claim of implausibility harder, not easier: Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver study type-compatible exchanges, and Ünver studies dynamic exchange with pair types and arrival rates. Those are not continuous computational treatments of this paper’s reserve-arc problem, so they are not a novelty collision; they are evidence that type-based kidney exchange is a sensible object ([Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.97.3.828), [Ünver](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/cs286r/courses/fall09/papers/unver_dynamic.pdf)).

So the honest negative case is a case for caution about *where* this mirror would matter: exact clinical types may not stay few, and a nonvanishing reserve budget needs justification. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The KEP-RA LP and the one-reserve-arc result survive the objections allowed here; I would expect the proponent’s case to carry more weight.
