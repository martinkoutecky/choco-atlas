The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the paper’s Algorithm 2, but it does not meet your required anchor standard: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithm 2 is followed by the claim that it finds a maximum set of stable groups in \(O(mn^2)\) operations. That is the closest named result, not a theorem I can honestly cite as one.

If Algorithm 2 is accepted as a provisional anchor, its claimed result is: given students’ group-size preferences, research groups, and a preferred group size \(X\), the algorithm checks group stability using individual-rationality and regret-free conditions, then returns stable groups; the paper claims polynomial running time \(O(mn^2)\). The paper proves no hardness result to anchor instead.

A plausible lead mirror is Fractional Anonymous Stable Beneficial Group Formation. An instance has activities \(E\), a finite set of complete anonymous student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and, for each type \(t\), a qualification score \(b_{t,e}\) for each activity and a preference order over group masses in \([0,1]\) and nonparticipation \(0\). For each activity \(e\), choose \(x_{t,e}\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to it, and let \(q_e=\sum_t x_{t,e}\). A solution must maximize \(\sum_e q_e\), subject to stability: assigned mass must be qualified (\(b_{t,e}>T\)) and prefer participation at \(q_e\) to \(0\); any qualified mass left out must weakly prefer \(0\) to participation at \(q_e\). The output is the maximizing mass allocation \(x\).

The regime could be a large university network or online programme running independent subject workshops. Many students share the same course background, qualification vector, and anonymous preferences over workshop size, so \(n\) can be large relative to the number \(\tau\) of supported types. For this anonymous variation, type-level modeling preserves the preferences the paper actually uses; it does not erase colleague identities because ABGA does not model them. With finite, explicitly represented preference breakpoints, I would expect this mirror to be tractable: each activity’s stability depends on a single mass \(q_e\) and the types’ comparisons at that mass.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s preferences are over integer headcounts, and its regret-free test compares an outsider’s preference at \(|G|+1\). In an atomless population, an individual deviation leaves \(q_e\) unchanged, so the continuous stability test compares at \(q_e\); preferences over population shares also change the interpretation of group size. The paper’s authors might regard that as a different stability model. The claimed \(O(mn^2)\) result is also not backed by a named theorem, and the printed algorithm is not a reliable complexity proof. So this is a plausible continuous question for the paper’s anonymous core, but it cannot be presented as a fully anchored case under your stated rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not meet the stated bar for a named computational result. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary asserting a complexity bound. Its nearest candidate is Algorithm 2’s claim of \(O(mn^2)\) operations, but the pseudocode does not clearly define the problem whose complexity it measures: the instance definition omits the qualification data \(B,T\) later used by the algorithm; stability is defined for a group, while the algorithm refers to multiple activities; and the algorithm takes a preferred size \(X\) without clearly specifying how it finds the maximum stable assignment. The paper supplies no proof that resolves these mismatches. There is therefore no precise complexity statement for the proposed continuous model to mirror.

The strongest repair would be a fractional anonymous formation problem with finitely many complete student types, rational type masses, qualification data, and preferences over group mass. An atomless Nash interpretation is coherent: one student’s deviation does not change the group’s mass. A large online workshop programme could also provide a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Those points make the question sensible; they do not supply the missing result in this paper. To connect it to Algorithm 2, one must choose how its integer target \(X\), group capacities, activity assignments, and maximization objective scale with the population. Those choices define a new computational problem rather than recover a complexity claim the paper has established.

The identity-dependent GBGA variant is a weaker candidate: arbitrary wish lists, reject lists, and mutual-contribution data can make agents’ complete types grow with the population. But that does not defeat the anonymous mirror, which deliberately avoids identity preferences.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed *anchor*: it is not a complexity theorem that can be mirrored under the task’s rule. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weak. The fractional anonymous question is plausible on its own terms; what fails here is its status as a mirror of a definite computational result in this paper.
