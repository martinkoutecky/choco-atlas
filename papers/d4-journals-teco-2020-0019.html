The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(K\), deviation bound \(L\), tolerance \(\epsilon>0\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over approval-set or ranking types, compute a finite-support lottery \(\Delta\) over committees \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=K\) such that, for every deviation \(S'\subseteq C\) with \(1\le |S'|\le L\), \(\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\Delta}[\sum_{t:S'\succ_t S}\mu_t]\le (1+\epsilon)|S'|/K\).

The model it lives in

The types are complete approval sets or rankings, with mass \(\mu_t\); the decision variable is a lottery \(\Delta\) over integral \(K\)-committees, and the objective is to minimize \(\max_{1\le |S'|\le L}(\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\Delta}[\sum_{t:S'\succ_t S}\mu_t]-|S'|/K)\).

The objection that survived

The paper already depends only on normalized aggregate comparison frequencies and has running time independent of \(n\), so the mirror adds little population-sensitive algorithmic leverage and may be mainly a weighted-profile reformulation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's stable-lottery existence and approximate computation, including the Ranking singleton reduction, and Theorem 2's deterministic \(K=3\) result. It leaves aside the probabilistic lemmas as standalone results, the PAV lower bound, deterministic stability for larger \(K\), and extensions to additive or submodular utilities.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact stable lotteries be represented or computed efficiently from an explicitly supported distribution \(\mu\)?
  • Can the Approval Set case remove the \(m^L\) dependence for unrestricted \(L\)?
  • Do deterministic stable committees exist for general \(K\) in the continuous Approval Set model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s stability problem. I would call it Continuous Stable-Lottery Committee Selection.

Let \(C\) be the candidate set and let \(T\) be either the approval-set types \(2^C\), the ranking types \(\operatorname{Perm}(C)\), or their disjoint union. A type is a complete approval set or ranking, so agents of one type are indistinguishable for the problem. The continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), where \(\mu_t\) is the population fraction of type \(t\).

For a committee \(S\) of size \(K\) and a nonempty deviation \(S'\) of size at most \(L\), define the captured mass by
\[ V_\mu(S,S')=\sum_{t:S'\succ_t S}\mu_t. \]
The decision variable is a lottery \(\Delta\) over integral committees \(S\in\binom{C}{K}\). Its objective is to minimize the worst proportional blocking violation
\[ \max_{1\le |S'|\le L} \left( \mathbb{E}_{S\sim\Delta}[V_\mu(S,S')] -\frac{|S'|}{K} \right). \]
An \(\epsilon\)-approximately \(L\)-stable solution is a lottery satisfying
\[ \mathbb{E}_{S\sim\Delta}[V_\mu(S,S')] \le (1+\epsilon)\frac{|S'|}{K} \]
for every \(S'\) with \(1\le |S'|\le L\). For \(L=K\), this is the continuous analogue of the paper’s stable-lottery problem.

This is genuinely population continuization. The committees remain integral, and \(\Delta\) is the paper’s existing randomization device; the new continuous object is only the society \(\mu\). The taxation interpretation transfers exactly: a deviation of size \(|S'|\) costs \(|S'|/K\) units of population mass, while \(V_\mu(S,S')\) is the mass able to finance it.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1 (Main), proved in this paper. Its full content is that, in both the Approval Set and Ranking settings with \(m\) candidates and for every committee size \(K\), a stable lottery over committees of size \(K\) exists; moreover, for every \(\epsilon>0\), an \(\epsilon\)-approximately \(L\)-stable lottery can be computed in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m^L,1/\epsilon)\). Combined with Observation 1, the Ranking case has running time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,1/\epsilon)\), because singleton deviations suffice there.

The continuous question handed to a prover is therefore:

Given \(C\), \(K\), \(L\), \(\epsilon\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over approval-set or ranking types, output a finite-support lottery \(\Delta\) over \(K\)-committees satisfying the preceding inequalities for every deviation \(S'\) of size at most \(L\).

I would expect this to be Class A, tractable. The paper’s zero-sum formulation survives after replacing voter counts by mass. Probability matching still scales the attacker’s candidate marginals, dependent rounding still produces an integral committee, and all payoff calculations are linear in \(\mu\). In the Ranking setting, the singleton reduction gives polynomial time even when \(K\) is large. In the Approval Set setting, the natural guarantee is \(\operatorname{poly}(m^L,1/\epsilon)\); removing the \(m^L\) dependence for unrestricted \(L\) remains an interesting question.

The natural regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballots: for example, millions of residents choosing a \(K\)-member council from a fixed candidate slate, with only \(\tau\) distinct approval sets or rankings induced by demographic, organizational, or ideological profiles, where \(n\gg\tau\). The same story works for the paper’s caching application: many applications may share one of a modest number of access-preference types. This is not merely a convenient encoding. A rational distribution \(\mu\) is exactly a finite election after multiplying by a common denominator, and every finite election induces such a \(\mu\). The continuous and discrete instances therefore have a precise two-way dictionary.

A secondary, narrower anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It states that in the Approval Set setting with committee size \(K=3\), a stable committee always exists and can be computed in time \(O(m^3n)\). Its continuous counterpart is:

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval-set types, output a deterministic committee \(S\) of size \(3\) such that \(V_\mu(S,S')<|S'|/3\) for every \(S'\) with \(1\le |S'|\le3\).

I would again expect Class A. The theorem’s case analysis uses only quantities such as \(n_i(S)/n\) and \(V(S,S')/n\), which become type-mass sums. With an explicit type list, the natural overhead is polynomial in \(\tau\); with exact mass queries, the dependence on the number of named voters disappears. This secondary mirror is useful because it keeps the action deterministic: the population, and only the population, is continuous.

The authors should recognize these as their problems rather than as weakened substitutes. The candidate committees, preference relations, blocking coalitions, proportional cost threshold, and lottery semantics are unchanged. Only the redundant identities of same-type voters are collapsed. The paper itself already estimates capture counts by sampling voters and asking them to compare committees, so sampling from \(\mu\) is a direct high-multiplicity implementation of its computational model. A mixed population of approval-set and ranking types is also explicitly compatible with the paper’s common comparison-based algorithm.

There is no named NP-hardness theorem in the supplied paper that I can honestly use as an anchor. Its NP-hardness remarks about committee-scoring rules cite results from elsewhere. Thus this is an algorithmic Class A case, not a claim that a discrete hardness result disappears. Further questions include whether exact stable lotteries have compact descriptions under explicit \(\mu\), whether Approval Set stability can be computed efficiently for unrestricted \(L\), whether deterministic stable committees exist for \(K>3\), and whether the same continuum framework extends to additive or general submodular utilities.

The weakest point is that the main result already has running time independent of \(n\), so a skeptic may say the continuous mirror merely renames normalized voter counts. That criticism is fair. The case nevertheless survives because the paper’s stability notion depends only on the empirical type distribution, the high-multiplicity interpretation is natural in large elections and shared systems, and the continuous formulation exposes a genuine computational object: optimization over a population measure while every realized committee remains discrete.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both proposed anchors are already population-anonymous in the paper’s own model. For every committee pair \(S,S'\),

\[ \frac{V(S,S')}{n} = \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[S'\succ_t S]. \]

Thus replacing voter counts by type masses does not relax, fractionalize, or otherwise alter the computational object. It merely divides the payoff matrix of the paper’s zero-sum game by \(n\). The paper’s probability matching, dependent rounding, and multiplicative-weights algorithm never uses voter identities or integer multiplicities; it already operates through aggregate comparison frequencies. Its stated running time is independent of \(n\). The proposed Continuous Stable-Lottery problem would therefore be the paper’s existing algorithm with \(V/n\) renamed \(V_\mu\).

The best possible high-multiplicity scenario—millions of voters with repeated rankings or approval sets—does establish that the distribution \(\mu\) is sensible. It does not establish that continuization creates a worthwhile computational question here. With fixed \(m\), the purported continuous type space is actually finite: \(2^m\) approval types or \(m!\) ranking types. An explicitly given \(\mu\) is simply a weighted profile, and a rational \(\mu\) can be converted back to the paper’s finite election by clearing denominators. If \(\mu\) is supplied through a sampler, the proposed algorithm is just the paper’s own voter-sampling implementation. If it is supplied succinctly, any new difficulty comes from evaluating that encoding, not from continuizing the population.

A richer latent population does not rescue the anchor. If voters have continuous ideological locations but only rankings or approval sets matter, all locations inducing the same type can be merged, returning to the same finite \(\mu\). Retaining the latent coordinate would require utilities, distances, prices, or other information absent from the paper. That could motivate a new continuous-social-choice paper, but not a mirror of Theorem 1. Likewise, making committees fractional would continuize the outcome rather than the population, while making deviations or assignments identity-sensitive would abandon the paper’s stability notion.

Theorem 2 is an even weaker anchor. Every quantity in its \(K=3\) case analysis—\(n_i(S)/n\), \(n_{\ge i}(S)/n\), and \(V(S,S')/n\)—already has the direct weighted replacement obtained by summing over types. The proof and enumeration remain unchanged, with \(O(m^3\tau)\) work for an explicit support of \(\tau\) types. This is useful duplicate compression, but there is no population granularity to eliminate: blocking is already defined solely by the aggregate mass of voters who prefer the deviation. Allowing that mass to be divisible adds no new decision variable.

The negative case therefore defeats both anchors as worthwhile ChoCo continuizations: neither exposes a population-driven complexity phenomenon, a new continuous optimization structure, or a meaningful discrete-to-continuous boundary. The paper’s substantive contribution is randomization over committees and a probabilistic existence argument, not computation over an individuated society.

That said, the universal claim is not airtight. The proposed mirror is mathematically well-posed, and repeated-ballot electorates are a credible high-multiplicity regime. I cannot honestly claim that no one could publish a useful weighted-profile study of it. The narrower and stronger verdict is that this paper offers no population-sensitive computational contribution for such a study: Theorem 1 survives by immediate linearity, and Theorem 2 survives by direct normalization.
