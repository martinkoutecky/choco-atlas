What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: this paper supplies a natural continuous-population equilibrium problem that could be studied computationally. But under your anchor rule, it has no numbered result asserting a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The paper’s named theorems characterize equilibrium existence and positions; its search algorithm appears in Section 7, outside a numbered theorem, and the authors say it is not polynomial.

The closest lead is Theorem 6.1, proved here. It says that for four candidates, a nonconvergent Nash equilibrium exists exactly when \(c(s,4)>1/2\) and \(s_1>s_2=s_3\). When it exists, it is unique and symmetric, with profile \(x=((x^1,2),(x^2,2))\), where
\[ x^1=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{s_1-s_4}{s_1-s_2}\right), \qquad x^2=1-x^1. \]
This is an exact equilibrium characterization, not a stated complexity result. If a closed-form characterization counts as an algorithmic anchor, it suggests the following problem.

Call it Uniform Four-Candidate NCNE Search. The input is a nonincreasing rational scoring vector \(s\) for four candidates. Voters have ideal points \(z\in[0,1]\) distributed with uniform mass; they rank candidates by distance from \(z\), breaking ties between co-located candidates by the paper’s fair lottery. Candidate \(i\)’s payoff at positions \(x\) is
\[ v_i(x)=\int_0^1 \mathbb{E}\!\left[s_{\operatorname{rank}_z(i;x)}\right]\,dz. \]
The task is to decide whether there is a profile with at least two distinct candidate positions at which no candidate can improve by moving anywhere in \([0,1]\); if one exists, return its exact positions. Theorem 6.1 solves this restricted problem. With fixed \(m=4\) and rational scores, its condition and formula give a direct exact computation, so the expected direction for this slice is tractable.

The regime is a large electorate spread across an ideological line, with many voters represented by the same ideal-position or preference types and only four candidates. Mass is the fraction of voters at each type; candidates choose policy positions and maximize their integrated scoring-rule payoff. This is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, and the authors would recognize the equilibrium question.

The main limitation is also decisive: the manuscript already assumes a continuum of voters uniformly distributed over \([0,1]\). This is therefore not a new continuous mirror of its population model; it is a computational question about a model the paper already studies analytically. Theorem 6.1 covers only the four-candidate, uniform case. Variable \(m\), nonuniform populations, and the relationship between finite voter cohorts and the continuum limit remain open extensions, not results anchored by this paper. That collision is the weakest point, and it keeps this from being a strong ChoCo mirror despite the natural computational follow-up.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not meet the programme’s gate. Theorem 6.1 is an equilibrium characterization, not a computational complexity result. For four candidates it completely settles the suggested search: check \(c(s,4)>1/2\) and \(s_1>s_2=s_3\), then use the stated formula for the positions. That is a constant-candidate calculation already done in the paper, not a new computational question exposed by continuization.

The stronger version would let \(m\) vary and ask for the complexity of finding or deciding the existence of an NCNE. That is a coherent computational problem, but the population in it is still the paper’s uniform continuum on \([0,1]\). Section 7 already gives an algorithm for this same equilibrium model, enumerating candidate clusterings and formulating an LP for each. Studying its worst-case complexity could be worthwhile; it would be computational analysis of a model whose population is already continuous, not a continuous mirror of a discrete population problem.

One could instead use a rational distribution over voter ideal points—perhaps finitely many locations with mass fractions, or a piecewise-constant density—and ask how equilibrium existence changes with that distribution. The authors would likely recognize this as a spatial-election question. But it changes the population law in a model that already treats voters as a measure; it does not continuize a population appearing in one of the paper’s results. With finitely many locations, rankings also depend on the candidates’ chosen positions, so this is not simply a fixed distribution over ranking types of the kind in the programme’s central model.

So the paper offers no named computational result for a continuous-population mirror, and its clearest proposed anchor is already answered in the paper’s continuous model. That is the strongest case against a mirror here. It is not a case against doing further computation on this spatial game: the variable-\(m\) complexity question and nonuniform-population variants may be worthwhile research. The negative case is weaker if “mirror” is broadened to include those follow-ups, but then the population continuization has already happened in the source paper.
