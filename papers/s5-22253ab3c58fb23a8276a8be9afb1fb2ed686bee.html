What the mirror covers

The proposed search concerns medium stability for type-composition worth functions; it does not mirror a named computational result, and leaves the paper's other stability relations and core-compatibility claims without computational mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which classes of worth functions \(W\) does a medium-stable finite mass partition exist?
  • Can such a partition be decided and found efficiently from a succinct description of \(W\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper gives a natural population-level stability question, but no named computational result to anchor a computational mirror. The closest numbered result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: strong stability is tighter than medium stability, which is tighter than weak stability; each notion is compatible with its corresponding core; and medium and weak stability are universal, meaning every finite coalitional game has at least one solution under each. That is an existence theorem, not a complexity classification.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large consortium of member firms whose payoff-relevant characteristics fall into a modest number of types—for example, production capability and outside-option tier. The population has type masses \(\mu_t\), and a coalition’s worth depends on its composition \(q\), through a function \(W(q)\). A coalition structure is then a partition of those masses, and allocations divide each coalition’s worth among its members. This preserves the paper’s central objects—partitions, coalition worths, and allocations—while making the population itself continuous. The paper’s discussion of “real players” and its citation of work on games with a continuum of players make this a plausible setting for the authors, though it is not the scenario they analyze.

A concrete follow-on problem would be Continuous Medium-Stability Search. Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), reservation payoffs \(r_t\), and a rational piecewise-linear worth function \(W(q)\) with \(W(q)\geq\sum_t r_tq_t\), decide whether a medium-stable mass partition exists and, if so, return one. A proposed solution is a finite list of nonzero coalition compositions \(q^1,\ldots,q^p\) summing to \(\mu\), together with per-type allocation rates \(a_{jt}\geq r_t\) satisfying \(\sum_tq^j_ta_{jt}=W(q^j)\). It is medium-stable if every finite decomposition \(q^j=\sum_\ell q^{j\ell}\) satisfies \(W(q^j)\geq\sum_\ell W(q^{j\ell})\), and every merger of a subset \(J\) of current coalitions satisfies \(W(\sum_{j\in J}q^j)\leq\sum_{j\in J}W(q^j)\). Atomless type masses let these composition vectors be realized by actual measurable groups. There is no separate welfare objective in the paper’s stability concept; the solution is a partition-allocation pair satisfying these resistance conditions.

I would expect the unrestricted continuous version to expose continuum-specific obstacles. For example, let \(W(q)=\sqrt{\sum_tq_t}\) and \(r_t=0\). A coalition of mass \(h\) can split into \(k\) equal pieces, raising its total worth from \(\sqrt h\) to \(\sqrt{kh}\). No finite partition can then resist every fission. This shows that Theorem 1’s universality does not carry over automatically. It does not prove computational hardness; it identifies a continuous boundary worth studying. Follow-up questions include which restrictions on \(W\) restore existence, how to price the most destabilizing split or merger, and whether the strong and weak variants admit different existence conditions.

The main weakness is decisive for the requested anchoring standard: Continuous Medium-Stability Search is a new problem suggested by the paper, not a computational result the paper establishes. Section 7 describes a steepest-ascent method and finite convergence, but does not give it a numbered complexity result or a running-time bound; it even leaves the large neighborhood computations to possible IP or DP formulations. So, under the stated anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. The case for a continuous mirror is plausible as a research direction, but cannot honestly be presented as a mirror of a named complexity finding.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with the anchor rule: Theorem 1 is an existence and axiomatic compatibility result, not a computational result. Propositions 1–6 establish relationships between stability notions. Section 7 gives the steepest-ascent method finite convergence because the finite partition graph has finitely many vertices and each move strictly increases worth. The paper gives no running-time bound; it says the neighborhood computations may need an IP or DP formulation. There is therefore no named complexity result here to continuize.

The proposed Continuous Medium-Stability Search is a coherent follow-on, but it changes the problem. Theorem 1 ranges over arbitrary finite characteristic functions \(v\). A type-composition worth function \(W(q)\) restricts the game to cases where coalition worth depends only on the masses of member types. That can describe a large consortium with repeated firm types, but it does not preserve the theorem’s generality. Encoding arbitrary coalition-specific worths through types would make type support track individual identities, removing the high-multiplicity regime. In the restricted model, searching over mass decompositions and testing split and merger conditions is a new computational question inspired by this paper; the paper does not establish its complexity.

The continuous version also loses the finite termination argument. With \(W(q)=\sqrt{\sum_t q_t}\), a coalition of mass \(h>0\) can split into \(k\) equal pieces, whose total worth is \(\sqrt{kh}>\sqrt h\). No finite partition can resist every such split. That shows the paper’s universality theorem does not carry over automatically. It does not make the continuous question unworthy: finding the conditions for existence or efficient search could be useful. It does mean that the theorem itself supplies neither a guarantee nor an algorithmic result to mirror.

A type-homogeneous consortium is a plausible continuous-population scenario, and the paper’s discussion of coalition evolution makes such a model recognizable. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile project in that setting. The negative case is narrower: the only named result offered as an anchor is outside the programme’s computational target, and the stronger search problem is a new extension rather than a computational finding of this paper. The evidence supports “no qualifying computational anchor here”; it does not support the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous mirror.
