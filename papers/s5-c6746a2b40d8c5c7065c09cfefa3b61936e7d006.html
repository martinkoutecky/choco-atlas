What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 1’s offline robust-benchmark computation; it leaves the benchmark comparisons and the online results under their temporal feedback models aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s offline robust benchmark, interpreted as a decision problem for a large cohort rather than a sequence of days. A plausible setting is a large pool of insurance claims or triage cases, each with a binary outcome and forecasts from several calibrated models. If reports come from a finite grid, many cases share the same joint forecast profile: the cohort may have \(N\) cases but only \(\tau\) distinct profiles. Cases sharing a profile also share the relevant payoff structure. Each case still receives an ordinary action; mass records the fraction of cases of each profile.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, with \(r=\sum_{i\in[n]}|E^i|\), the robust-maxmin benchmark can be computed by a linear program with \(md+m+2r\) variables and \(md+3m+2r\) constraints, and hence in time polynomial in \(m,d,r\). Here \(m=|E|\) is the explicitly represented profile-space size, which the paper notes may be exponential in the number of experts. This is an algorithmic anchor; the paper provides no named hardness theorem to anchor instead.

I would pose the corresponding continuous problem as Robust Profile-Mass Decision. An instance consists of a finite support \(S\) of joint forecast profiles \(e=(e^1,\ldots,e^n)\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_e\) summing to one, a finite action set \(\mathcal A\), rational payoffs \(u(a,\omega)\) for \(\omega\in\{0,1\}\), and calibration tolerance \(\varepsilon\). Write \(\mu_i(z)=\sum_{e:e^i=z}\mu_e\). Nature may choose any posterior assignment \(\rho\in[0,1]^S\) satisfying
\[ \left|\sum_{e:e^i=z}\mu_e(\rho_e-z)\right|\leq \varepsilon\mu_i(z) \quad\text{for every expert }i\text{ and report }z. \]
The decision variable is a rule \(x_e\in\Delta(\mathcal A)\), one action lottery for each profile. The task is to return an optimal rule and its guaranteed per-case payoff:
\[ \max_{x\in(\Delta(\mathcal A))^S}\ \min_{\rho\in\mathcal C_\varepsilon(\mu)} \sum_{e\in S}\mu_e\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}x_e(a) \bigl(\rho_eu(a,1)+(1-\rho_e)u(a,0)\bigr). \]
Assume the calibration set is nonempty. For a cohort of \(N\) cases, multiply the value by \(N\) to get the guaranteed total payoff. A threshold version asks whether this value is at least a given rational \(K\).

This is a close match to the paper’s question: types are full joint forecast profiles, mass is the share of cases at each profile, and the adversary’s posterior assignments obey exactly the experts’ marginal calibration restrictions. The action rule and payoff objective are unchanged. Theorem 1’s LP applies to the profile support after zero-mass profiles are removed; thus the mirror is tractable in \(\tau\), \(d=|\mathcal A|\), \(r\), and the input bit length. It is not a claim of polynomial time in \(n\) alone: the support can itself be exponential in the number of experts. The expected classification is therefore tractable (Class A), with the support size as the key parameter.

My weakest point is also the main qualification: the paper already groups days by joint profile and computes the benchmark from their empirical masses. Recasting those days as cases gives a credible high-multiplicity population regime, but much of the mathematical object and its LP are already present in the paper. And although this is a sound continuous decision problem, it is less clearly a central computational social choice problem than a robust decision problem. The positive case is that the population interpretation is exact and plausible, not that it reveals an entirely new algorithmic landscape. Further questions include whether the problem stays tractable when the profile support is given implicitly, and how its guarantees change when calibration constraints or the distribution over profiles vary across the cohort.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor, but its proposed mirror is already the mathematical object the paper computes. In Section 3.1, the paper groups days by joint forecast profile, assigns each profile its empirical mass \(\edp_T(e)\), and gives it a fractional state frequency \(\rho(e)\in[0,1]\). Its calibration constraints are the mass-weighted constraints in the proposed Robust Profile-Mass Decision problem, and its objective is the same worst-case payoff, multiplied by \(T\). Setting \(\mu_e=\edp_T(e)\) makes the two formulations coincide.

This is more than a resemblance between discrete and continuous models. Every rational mass vector in the proposed mirror can be realized as the empirical distribution of a finite multiset of days: take a common denominator and repeat each profile accordingly. Conversely, every finite sequence yields such a rational mass vector. The paper already relaxes the profile-level state frequencies to real values and proves an LP result for that formulation. So the exact step that might have made this a worthwhile continuization has already happened inside the paper.

The support-size refinement does not rescue the mirror. Theorem 1 states its LP size using the full profile space \(E\), but profiles with zero mass have zero coefficients in both the objective and the calibration constraints; their variables can be deleted. The LP therefore restricts directly to the positive-mass support \(S\), with the same value. The continuous proposal’s parameters \(\tau,d,r\) describe this existing computation after removing zero-mass profiles, rather than a new algorithmic question.

The case-cohort interpretation is plausible, and it confirms that a high-multiplicity regime makes sense. But relabeling days as cases changes no optimization problem: a profile determines the same action lottery, the same payoff vector, and the same calibration restrictions on the cohort’s profile masses. Adding case-specific costs, capacity constraints, or other features might create new questions, but those would be extensions of the model, not continuous mirrors of Theorem 1. The online theorems are not a stronger alternative anchor: their regret claims depend on temporal order, feedback, and learning across rounds, which a static population distribution does not encode.

My negative case is therefore strong for the anchor actually raised, though not a proof that no related research could be useful. The cohort scenario is sound; the obstacle is that the paper itself already formulates and solves its mass-based computational version. This is a direct collision with the proposed mirror, not an objection to high multiplicity or to the result’s answer.
