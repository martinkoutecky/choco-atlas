The continuous mirror question

Given \(m+1\) candidates with preferred candidate \(p\), a positional scoring vector \(\vec{\alpha}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) on nonmanipulator ranking types summing to \(1-\rho\), and a homogeneous manipulator bloc of mass \(\rho>0\), compute the minimum \(T_\infty^*\) and a mass allocation attaining it. Each unit of bloc mass reports a complete ranking \(r\) with \(p\) first; for \(x_r\ge 0\) with \(\sum_r x_r=\rho\), minimize \(\max_{c\ne p}\left(\sum_t\mu_t s(t,c)+\sum_{r:\,p\text{ first in }r}x_r s(r,c)\right)\). Determine whether \(p\) can win by testing \(T_\infty^*\le B_p+\rho\alpha_m\), where \(B_p=\sum_t\mu_t s(t,p)\).

The model it lives in

Represent the society by rational ranking-type masses \(\mu_t\) for nonmanipulators and bloc mass \(\rho\). Let \(B_c=\sum_t\mu_t s(t,c)\), and let \(y_{c,j}\) be the bloc mass assigning opponent \(c\) score \(\alpha_j\). Minimize \(T\) subject to \(B_c+\sum_j\alpha_j y_{c,j}\le T\), \(\sum_j y_{c,j}=\rho\) for each opponent, \(\sum_{c\ne p}y_{c,j}=\rho\) for each score position, and \(y_{c,j}\ge0\). The scaled matrix \(y/\rho\) is doubly stochastic and decomposes into mass on complete ballots with \(p\) first, so this is a polynomial-size LP.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the unweighted positional-rule min-max objective in Theorem 1, including Borda-UCM in Corollary 3. It does not cover the weighted variant or transfer the discrete approximation guarantee itself.

Open questions for a prover

  • How large can the gap be between \(T_\infty^*\) and the optimum with finitely many manipulators, as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and population size?
  • Which type-dependent costs or reporting constraints preserve an efficient optimization formulation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The formal anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For a positional scoring rule \(R_{\vec\alpha}\), let \(T^*\) be the minimum, over valid manipulation strategies for the \(k\) unweighted manipulators, of the highest final score of any candidate other than \(p\). With \(\beta=d\sqrt{m\log m}\) for a constant \(d\), and \(g(\vec\alpha)=\max_{i=0,\ldots,m-\beta}(\alpha_{i+\beta}-\alpha_i)\), the theorem gives a randomized Monte Carlo algorithm producing a \(kg(\vec\alpha)\)-additive approximation to \(T^*\), with exponentially small failure probability. Corollary 3, also proved here as the Borda specialization, gives an \(O(k\sqrt m\log m)\)-additive approximation for Borda-UCM. The paper reports that Borda-UCM is NP-hard, citing earlier work [7, 4], but that claim is not a numbered result proved here, so I use Theorem 1 as the anchor.

My proposed mirror is Continuous Positional Min-Max Manipulation. An instance has \(m+1\) candidates, a preferred candidate \(p\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) on \(\tau\) nonmanipulator ranking types, summing to \(1-\rho\), where \(\rho>0\) is the mass of a homogeneous, unweighted bloc whose members all prefer \(p\). The bloc may coordinate its reported ballots, but each unit of mass casts one complete ranking with \(p\) first. For every such ranking \(r\), choose mass \(x_r\ge 0\), with \(\sum_r x_r=\rho\). If \(s(t,c)\) is the scoring rule’s score for candidate \(c\) under ranking \(t\), define
\[ B_c=\sum_t\mu_t s(t,c),\qquad T_\infty^*=\min_x\max_{c\ne p}\left(B_c+\sum_{r:\,p\text{ first in }r}x_r s(r,c)\right). \]
The requested solution is the optimum \(T_\infty^*\) and a mass allocation \(x\) attaining it. The bloc’s score for \(p\) is fixed at \(B_p+\rho\alpha_m\), so \(p\) can be made a winner exactly when \(T_\infty^*\le B_p+\rho\alpha_m\). This is the paper’s min-max objective, with \(k\) replaced by a population fraction; it does not make the outcome fractional.

This problem is tractable. For each opponent \(c\) and score position \(j\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\), let \(y_{c,j}\) be the mass of bloc ballots assigning \(c\) score \(\alpha_j\). Require each opponent to receive one score per unit of bloc mass, \(\sum_j y_{c,j}=\rho\), and each score position to be used once per unit of mass, \(\sum_{c\ne p}y_{c,j}=\rho\). Minimize \(T\) subject to \(B_c+\sum_j\alpha_jy_{c,j}\le T\) and \(y_{c,j}\ge0\). This is a polynomial-size linear program. Its feasible matrices, scaled by \(\rho\), lie in the Birkhoff polytope, so they decompose into mass on complete rankings with \(p\) first. The input masses contribute through the \(B_c\)’s, giving polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and their bit length.

The regime I have in mind is a large party, union, or slate election: many ordinary voters cast ballots drawn from a small menu of \(\tau\) rankings, while a large organized bloc with a common first choice coordinates its reports. Both populations grow, the bloc’s fraction approaches \(\rho\), and \(\tau\) remains small relative to the population. The bloc is one type for this problem: its members have the same weight, preferred candidate, and available reports. A mass split across rankings means subgroups of members cast different whole ballots; it does not mean anyone casts a fractional ballot.

The authors would, I think, recognize this as their problem’s continuous form. The preferred candidate, admissible reports, scoring rule, and min-max target all remain in place. The paper itself writes down a natural fractional LP for the same objective, and Lemma 6 shows that it can have an \(\Omega(m)\) integrality gap. That is useful evidence that the fractional question is meaningful, while also marking the main modeling change: the continuous version allows the bloc’s aggregate ballots to split freely across rankings. The paper’s stronger configuration LP and rounding algorithm address the discrete problem; I am not claiming their LP is identical to this continuous feasible region.

My weakest point is the source hardness. The paper notes Borda-UCM is NP-hard even with only three nonmanipulator voters and two manipulators, citing [7, 4]. That hardness is not obviously caused by population multiplicity, and the programme’s heuristic would normally make hardness driven by the number of candidates a warning that it might transfer. So I would expect this stated continuous problem to be tractable, but would not claim that the paper’s discrete hardness dissolves for every high-multiplicity regime. The proposed mirror is strongest as an aggregate optimization question whose exact solution is an LP, not as a prediction that the discrete problem becomes easy.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s unweighted positional-rule min-max objective, including Corollary 3’s Borda case. It does not attempt to continuize the weighted variant or other manipulation problems. Natural follow-up questions are how large the discrete-to-continuous optimum gap can be as a function of \(m,\tau\), and population size, and which type-dependent prices or costs preserve an efficient pricing problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed mirror is already written down in the paper’s Section 4 as its “natural” LP relaxation. For each opponent \(c_i\) and score position \(j\), that LP has variables \(x_{i,j}\ge 0\), with
\[ \sum_i x_{i,j}=k,\qquad \sum_j x_{i,j}=k,\qquad \sigma_i+\sum_j\alpha_jx_{i,j}\le T. \]
These are exactly the constraints for distributing the manipulators’ score positions across the opponents while minimizing the highest final score.

To see that this is the continuous-population model, let the manipulator bloc have mass \(\rho\), let \(B_i\) be the nonmanipulator society’s score for \(c_i\), and let \(y_{i,j}\) be the bloc mass giving \(c_i\) score \(\alpha_j\). The corresponding constraints are
\[ \sum_i y_{i,j}=\rho,\qquad \sum_j y_{i,j}=\rho,\qquad B_i+\sum_j\alpha_jy_{i,j}\le t. \]
With total population scale \(N\), the substitutions \(y_{i,j}=x_{i,j}/N\), \(\rho=k/N\), \(B_i=\sigma_i/N\), and \(t=T/N\) turn the paper’s LP into this one. And the mass allocation really does represent complete ballots: \(y/\rho\) is doubly stochastic, so it decomposes into a distribution over permutations of the score positions, each corresponding to a ballot with \(p\) first.

The proposed bloc scenario is therefore coherent, but its continuous optimization is not a new computational question. The paper already gives the fractional formulation, and a standard polynomial-time LP solver computes its optimum. Lemma 6’s integrality gap is relevant because it shows why that LP does not solve the *discrete* problem; for the continuous mirror, the fractional solution is the intended solution. That is a direct overlap with the anchor, not an objection based on what the continuous answer happens to be.

A richer distribution of nonmanipulator ranking types does not avoid the overlap: for this scoring objective, it contributes only the baseline scores \(B_i\). Giving the manipulators different underlying rankings also changes nothing unless the problem adds type-dependent costs, weights, or constraints; those additions would pose a different question from Theorem 1. The strongest negative verdict is thus that this mirror has little new computational content because the paper already formulated its continuous core.

That case is strong on novelty, but it does not show that the mirror is ill-posed or that a formal high-multiplicity dictionary could never be useful. If the programme counts reinterpreting and situating an existing LP as worthwhile, the negative case weakens there.
