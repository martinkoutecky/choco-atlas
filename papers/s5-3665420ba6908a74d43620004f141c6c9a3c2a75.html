What the mirror covers

The adjacent aggregation task covers computing AWPR outcomes from distributions over complete labelings; it leaves the paper’s experimental preference estimates and effects of framing, vote ratio, and harm untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a qualifying anchor-based case: the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Its numbered material includes sections and Example 1, while its actual findings are experimental and reported through percentages and an ANOVA table. The formal rules and their postulates are defined or discussed, but the paper does not state numbered computational results about them.

The strongest adjacent mirror I can see is a continuous version of the paper’s labeling aggregation problem. Consider a large online community evaluating one shared argumentation framework \(\AF\). A type \(L\) is a complete labeling of \(\AF\), and \(\mu_L\) is the fraction of the community with that labeling; the regime would have many participants but relatively few distinct labeling types. Given \(\AF\) and rational masses \(\mu\), the task is to return the AWPR label for each argument by choosing the label with greatest mass, reporting ties as undefined. This is a sensible high-multiplicity form of a problem the paper defines, and it is tractable by summing masses.

But it is only an adjacent direction, not a valid mirror of a named result in this paper. It computes aggregation outcomes; the paper’s contribution is experimental evidence about which outcomes people favor. That is the central weakness: a continuous population could restate the preference proportions, but doing so would not create the computational result this programme requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The paper’s findings concern people’s preferences among outcomes under different experimental conditions. It gives percentages and statistical tests, but states no computational problem with a complexity result or algorithm. That leaves no anchor for the programme’s central question: how does the computational landscape change when the population becomes continuous?

The best adjacent model is still sensible. A large online community could evaluate one argument graph, with a type for each complete labeling and a rational mass for each type. But computing the paper’s aggregation outcomes from those masses would not reproduce its central finding about which outcomes people favor. It would answer a different question: what AWPR or SSCO outcome follows from this distribution of labelings? The fact that the paper defines aggregation does not make that outcome a computational result of the paper.

A stronger proposal might ask how much mass must change to alter the collective outcome. That would require adding a new intervention model, including which agents can change their labels and at what cost. Those ingredients do not appear in this study. The question might be worthwhile, but it would be a new computational project attached to the paper, not a continuous mirror of its experimental result.

I would not base the case on there being no plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large web community with repeated labeling types is credible. Nor can I prove that no adjacent computational question could be useful. The negative case is strongest and narrower: none of the proposed or improved candidates mirrors a computational result in this paper, because the paper’s contribution is experimental evidence about human judgment.
