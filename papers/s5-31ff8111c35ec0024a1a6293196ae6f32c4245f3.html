The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive foothold is the natural high-multiplicity regime: in a large ranked-choice election, a type could be a complete top-truncated ballot over a fixed candidate set \(C\), and its mass the fraction of voters casting that ballot. Many voters might share relatively few ballot types. But this paper gives no named computational result to mirror, so that plausible regime does not yield an anchored continuous question.

Theorem 3, proved here, shows that each ranked-choice ballot is a join semilattice. Proposition 4, also proved here, shows that a top-truncated order is modular; Corollary 6 gives a submodular representation; and Theorem 7 supplies a condition used in the utility analysis. Theorem 8 states equivalences between that condition and the existence of concave or quasiconcave utility representations, citing Kalandrakis (2010). These are structural and representation results, not claims that a computational problem is tractable or hard.

Although the model permits a possibly infinite voter set, the results concern an individual ballot and its utility representation. They do not take a distribution over ballot types as the computational input, specify an action or objective over that population, or analyze complexity. Defining a continuous population problem from these results would therefore be inventing a new question rather than mirroring a named result. I can’t give a defensible lead question, anchor-specific complexity prediction, or further questions under the stated criteria. The weakness is decisive: the paper may be relevant background for a future computational study, but it contains no named computational result on which this positive case could rest.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case identifies a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many voters could share a top-truncated ballot. I grant that. But a plausible population regime alone does not give this paper a continuous computational mirror.

Theorem 3, Proposition 4, Corollary 6, Theorem 7, and Theorem 8 concern the structure of an individual ballot or the utility representation of an individual preference relation. None specifies a population-level decision problem, an objective to optimize, or an algorithm whose complexity is analyzed. Putting a distribution \(\mu\) over ballot types leaves those results unchanged: they apply ballot by ballot, regardless of each type’s mass. A mass-weighted count of which types satisfy a property would be a new statistic, not a mirror of the paper’s theorems.

A stronger proposal might ask for an aggregate utility representation. But the paper gives each voter an ordinal relation and an individual representation; it supplies neither a common cardinal scale nor a social-welfare rule for combining utilities. Choosing those would add the central modeling question rather than continuize a result. A population model of strategic voting would likewise be a new project; the paper explicitly leaves strategic voting for future work.

So the proponent is right that there is no defensible lead question or anchor-specific complexity prediction here. The limit of this negative case is that no one can rule out every useful future project inspired by ranked-choice ballots. But such a project would be motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of its named results.
