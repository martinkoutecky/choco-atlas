Why no mirror

Theorem 2 is a named algorithmic result, but the proposed problem has only \(\tau\) indivisible constituency players and still orders finitely many coalitions. In aggregate coordinates, \(\mu\) disappears from payoffs except insofar as it is folded into an otherwise unrestricted \(V_\mu\), so this remains a finite TU-game instance with a mass label. Allowing coalitions to take partial type mass would create a continuum of excesses and require a new nucleolus ordering that the paper does not define.

What the mirror covers

The proposed verification mirror would cover Theorem 2’s iteration bound, but it does not yield a continuous-population analogue of that result; it leaves the constructive algorithm and the paper’s other computational results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is a narrow mirror for nucleolus verification in a society organized into bargaining constituencies.

Let \(T\) be a set of \(\tau\) constituencies, with rational population shares \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Within each constituency, members share the same game-relevant characteristics and bargain collectively; coalitions form from whole constituencies. The regime could be a large worker cooperative or regional consortium: millions of members, but perhaps only a dozen bargaining units. A type must capture everything relevant to the game, including its contribution, outside option, and bargaining mandate.

Specify a rational characteristic-function table \(V_\mu(S)\) for each \(S\subseteq T\), with \(V_\mu(\varnothing)=0\), and assume the imputation set is nonempty. Let \(p_t\) be the per-member payoff rate to constituency \(t\), so its aggregate payoff is \(\mu_t p_t\). The feasible rates are

\[ \mathcal I_\mu= \left\{q:\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t=V_\mu(T),\quad \mu_tq_t\ge V_\mu(\{t\})\ \forall t\in T\right\}. \]

For a coalition \(S\), its excess at rates \(q\) is

\[ e_\mu(S,q)=V_\mu(S)-\sum_{t\in S}\mu_tq_t. \]

The lead problem is Type-Constituency Nucleolus Verification: given \((T,\mu,V_\mu)\) and a candidate \(p\in\mathcal I_\mu\), decide whether \(p\) lexicographically minimizes the non-increasingly sorted vector \(\bigl(e_\mu(S,q):S\subseteq T\bigr)\) over \(q\in\mathcal I_\mu\). The answer is yes exactly when it is the nucleolus; otherwise it is no. In aggregate-payoff coordinates \(x_t=\mu_tp_t\), this is precisely the paper’s nucleolus problem for the constituency game.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that Improved Kohlberg Algorithm 2 correctly decides whether a given imputation is the nucleolus, and its while-loop terminates after at most \(n-1\) iterations. The paper proves the bound by increasing the rank of the tight-coalition collection at each iteration. In this mirror, the corresponding bound is \(\tau-1\). This is an algorithmic structural result, not a claim of polynomial time in \(\tau\): finding the maximum-excess coalition can still require examining exponentially many coalitions.

For the stated explicit-table version, I would expect the problem to be tractable in time polynomial in the table size and the rational bit length, roughly \(\mathrm{poly}(2^\tau,L)\), rather than \(\mathrm{poly}(\tau,L)\). Each maximum-excess search can scan the table, and balancedness checks can be posed as linear programs of size polynomial in that table. The direction is therefore Class A in the explicit representation, with the important qualification that this gives no compact-input tractability result. For a succinct description of \(V_\mu\), the central further question is whether maximum-excess coalition search and balancedness can be handled by efficient pricing oracles. Theorem 2 does not settle that; hardness may persist in such representations. The introduction’s weighted-voting hardness citation [8] is relevant warning, but it is not a numbered hardness theorem proved in this paper, so I would not use it as an anchor.

The discrete dictionary is exact for this constituency regime: if constituency \(t\) has \(M_t\) members out of \(M\), then \(\mu_t=M_t/M\), and the same coalition game has aggregate payoffs \(x_t=M_tp_t/M\) after normalization. This does not claim a mirror for a game in which arbitrary subgroups of a constituency’s individual members may defect.

That restriction is the weakest point. If the authors or referee require the continuous population itself to be nonatomic *coalition participants*, rather than members of collective players, this mirror will not suffice. The paper lexicographically orders a finite list of coalition excesses; with partial-mass coalitions there are continuously many excesses, and the text supplies no canonical continuum version of that ordering. The narrower constituency scenario survives because whole-unit coalition formation is part of the real institution being modeled, and the resulting question remains exactly nucleolus verification for a TU game. It covers Theorem 2’s verification result, not the paper’s constructive algorithm or all of its computational claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The constituency formulation is a valid nucleolus problem for \(\tau\) bloc players, but it leaves the paper’s population finite in the relevant sense. Coalitions remain the \(2^\tau\) whole-type subsets. A member cannot join or leave a coalition independently of the rest of their constituency.

In aggregate-payoff coordinates \(x_t=\mu_t p_t\), the constraints and excesses become

\[ \sum_{t\in T}x_t=V_\mu(T),\qquad x_t\ge V_\mu(\{t\}),\qquad e_\mu(S,x)=V_\mu(S)-\sum_{t\in S}x_t. \]

The masses disappear from these expressions except where they have already been folded into the input game \(V_\mu\). Theorem 2 therefore applies as stated to a finite game with \(\tau\) players; its \(\tau-1\) iteration bound is the original rank argument with a renamed dimension. Its claim is a bound on iterations, while the paper explicitly leaves coalition search as a computational bottleneck. A full characteristic-function table may permit polynomial time in the table’s size, but that is the explicit-input finite-game problem. The paper supplies no cross-population rule for \(V_\mu\) that would make varying \(\mu\) a substantive continuous limit.

A stronger mirror would let a coalition contain any mass vector \(z\in[0,\mu]\), with a value function \(W(z)\). That would make population multiplicity meaningful: two coalitions could contain different fractions of each type. But the paper’s nucleolus lexicographically orders the finite list of coalition excesses. A continuum of mass vectors gives an uncountable family of excesses and no canonical sorted list. One could minimize the worst excess first, or introduce a measure on coalition mass vectors and lexicographically compare excess distributions. The first is a least-core question; the second requires a choice of measure that the finite game does not supply. Neither choice is the paper’s nucleolus-verification problem without further modeling decisions. The rank bound might inspire a new algorithm once such a model and a pricing procedure are fixed, but Theorem 2 does not establish one.

So the proposed anchor fails in its stated constituency setting, and its stronger population-coalition version requires defining a new continuum solution concept before the verification question is well-posed. The negative case is weakest at exactly that boundary: a principled continuum nucleolus for type-mass coalitions could be worthwhile in its own right. I cannot rule out a scenario where the authors would recognize and want that question; the material here does not make it a mirror of their theorem.
