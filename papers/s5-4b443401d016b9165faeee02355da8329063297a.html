The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest position is that this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under the programme’s rule. Its named results are structural and axiomatic, not complexity or algorithmic results.

The closest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For the characteristic functions \(u,v_1,\ldots,v_4\), it states that all are nonnegative and monotone, while \(v_2,v_3,v_4\) are also superadditive. Its table further states that, for \(u\), the Shapley ranking satisfies unanimity, monotonicity, and the Condorcet property, but not the majority rule or the strong Condorcet property. The theorem is not a result that the underlying problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Lemmas 1–3 and Theorem 1 have the same status: they prove voting properties and comparative-statics facts, not computational complexity. The paper’s remarks about “bulky computations” likewise are not named complexity results.

Subject to that caveat, the strongest mirror is:

Continuous \(u\)-Shapley Tournament Winner. Let \(A\) be a set of \(m\) candidates and let \(T=S_A\) be the set of complete rankings of \(A\). An instance is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of voters with ranking \(\pi\). Define the normalized pairwise support

\[ h_\mu(i,j)=\sum_{\pi:\,i\succ_\pi j}\mu_\pi . \]

For every candidate coalition \(K\subseteq A\), define

\[ u_\mu(K)= \max_{p\in\Delta(K)} \min_{q\in\Delta(A\setminus K)} \sum_{i\in K}\sum_{j\in A\setminus K} h_\mu(i,j)p_iq_j . \]

Thus \(u_\mu(K)\) is the value of the zero-sum game in which \(K\) and \(A\setminus K\) each randomize over their nominated candidates. Define the Shapley score

\[ \varphi^\mu_x= \sum_{K\subseteq A\setminus\{x\}} \frac{|K|!(m-|K|-1)!}{m!} \bigl(u_\mu(K\cup\{x\})-u_\mu(K)\bigr). \]

Given a designated candidate \(c^\star\), the decision problem asks whether

\[ \varphi^\mu_{c^\star} = \max_{x\in A}\varphi^\mu_x. \]

A solution may be the complete Shapley ranking, or the set of maximizers.

This is a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. The types are complete ballot rankings; mass is the fraction of the electorate of each type; and two voters of one type are interchangeable for every quantity used by the paper. The regime is a large repeated electorate—say millions of voters choosing among a small fixed candidate set—with \(n\gg m\) and only a comparatively small number of distinct ranking types. The paper itself repeatedly motivates its procedure by the fact that the number of ballot papers greatly exceeds the number of candidates, so the authors should recognize this as their ranking problem expressed by population proportions.

The internal decision variables are the coalition mixed strategies \(p\) and \(q\); the external decision is the Shapley winner. The objective is to compute the candidate with maximum aggregate coalition marginal contribution. This is not continuization of the outcome space: candidates remain discrete, coalitions remain subsets of candidates, and the Shapley value remains the paper’s solution concept. Only the electorate becomes a distribution over types.

If this exploratory mirror were admitted, I would expect a Class A result under an explicit-type encoding. First compute \(h_\mu\); then compute \(u_\mu(K)\) by a polynomial-size linear program for every coalition \(K\); finally evaluate the Shapley sums. The algorithm is exponential in \(m\), but when the full ranking-type universe of size \(\tau=m!\) is explicit, \(2^m\) is polynomial in the type-universe size. A compact version in which only the pairwise matrix or a sparse support is given could have a different complexity classification. Further questions include exact comparison of rational Shapley scores, winner robustness under mass perturbations, and minimum-cost mass transport that makes a designated candidate the Shapley winner.

The main weakness is decisive for the present grading exercise: this is not anchored in a named computational theorem. Moreover, the paper’s rule depends only on the aggregate tournament matrix \(h_\mu\), so the continuous model may collapse to a normalized finite matrix rather than expose a genuinely new population-level computational phenomenon. Thus the paper supplies a plausible mirror, but not a compliant ChoCo case based on a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor fails at the programme’s first gate: this paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 2, Theorem 1, Lemmas 1–3, and the appendix establish axiomatic and comparative-static properties of Shapley rankings. “Bulky computations” and the instruction to evaluate \(2^m\) coalitions are not complexity classifications. Thus “Continuous \(u\)-Shapley Tournament Winner” is a newly invented computational problem, not a continuization of a computational theorem in the paper.

The high-multiplicity regime itself is perfectly sensible here; the paper explicitly assumes that the number of ballots greatly exceeds the number of candidates. That is not the objection. The problem is that the paper’s rule does not see the population distribution. Every quantity used by \(u\), \(v\), and \(v_1,\ldots,v_4\) factors through the pairwise matrix:

\[ \mu \longmapsto h_\mu \longmapsto u_\mu \longmapsto \varphi^\mu . \]

If two distributions over complete rankings have the same \(h_\mu(i,j)\) for every pair, they produce exactly the same characteristic functions and Shapley values. The full ranking type space \(S_A\) is therefore redundant. One may replace it by the weighted tournament matrix, whose dimension is only \(O(m^2)\). The proposed “continuous society” is consequently just a normalized weighted tournament, not a population-level computational object.

The exact finite-to-continuous correspondence does not rescue this. Every rational \(\mu\) can be multiplied by a common denominator to obtain a finite profile, and every finite profile yields such a \(\mu\). That is a valid identity, but it shows that the proposed problem is merely a change of representation. The paper’s axiomatic proofs already apply to the resulting pairwise numbers; no new continuous optimization question arises.

Nor does the claimed algorithm provide a convincing Class A result. Computing an LP for every coalition still requires \(2^m\) coalition instances. Calling this polynomial in \(\tau=m!\) only pads the input with an explicitly listed universe of rankings; it does not give an algorithm polynomial in the natural parameters \(m\), support size, and encoding length. With sparse support, the stated bound is not polynomial in the actual input size. Any genuine complexity question here concerns Shapley computation over candidate coalitions, not population multiplicity.

A stronger proposal—mass perturbations, robustness, or minimum-cost changes to make a candidate the Shapley winner—would be more computationally interesting, but it would add an intervention objective, costs, and constraints absent from the paper. It would be a new ChoCo problem based on the paper’s rule, not a mirror of any result the paper establishes. Likewise, continuizing the candidates rather than the voters would destroy the finite-player Shapley game that defines the paper’s method.

The honest negative verdict is therefore narrower than the literal universal claim: distributions of ballot types can certainly be studied, and a high-multiplicity reformulation is mathematically natural. But this paper supplies no computational anchor, and its ranking rule compresses the entire population to a finite pairwise statistic before the relevant computation begins. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, there is no worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
