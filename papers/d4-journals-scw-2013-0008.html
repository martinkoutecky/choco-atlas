What the mirror covers

The proposed query covers Theorem 1 and Corollary 1’s stability and uniqueness results; it leaves the implementation results in Theorems 2 and 3 aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for a narrow mirror of Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that for any distribution of party weights and any party bliss points, every weighted rule produces a profile of agreements with at least one stable coalition. Corollary 1, also proved here, sharpens this: the stable coalition is unique, its two members rank it first, and the remaining party ranks it last. These are stability results, not standard complexity classifications: the paper has no named theorem putting a problem in P, proving it NP-hard, or giving a parameterized classification.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large parliament with three disciplined party caucuses. Each representative’s complete type is their party affiliation \(i\), that party’s bliss point \(x_i\), and its per-representative office benefit \(k_i\). Representatives within a caucus share those features, and the caucus acts as one bloc, as parties do in the paper. The society is the distribution over the three types, with rational seat shares \(\mu_i>0\), \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and \(\mu_i<1/2\). Thus the number of representatives \(n\) can be large while the support size is \(\tau=3\). Use the proportional weighted rule \(f(z)=z\), so the agreement for coalition \(S=\{i,j\}\) is

\[ a_S=\frac{\mu_i x_i+\mu_j x_j}{\mu_i+\mu_j}. \]

A bloc’s utility from coalition \(S\) is \(U_i(S)=k_i-\lVert a_S-x_i\rVert\) if \(i\in S\), and \(U_i(S)=-\lVert a_S-x_i\rVert\) otherwise. The paper’s assumption that office benefits are sufficiently large ensures each party’s preferred agreement includes it.

The continuous problem is: given these masses, bliss points, and office benefits, return a coalition \(S\) for which there is no other pair \(S'\) such that both members of \(S'\) strictly prefer \(S'\) to \(S\). The solution is the stable pair; there is no scalar objective in the paper, so stability is the selection criterion. Theorem 1 supplies existence, and Corollary 1 supplies uniqueness. For rational input and a fixed efficiently evaluable weighting rule, this problem is tractable: compute the three agreements and compare the utilities for the three possible coalitions. Exact comparisons of the resulting rational distances can be made with polynomially many arithmetic and sign tests. The result is proved in the paper; the polynomial-time algorithm is the direct way to compute the mirror’s output.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: they already treat votes or seats as party weights, define agreements from those weights and bliss points, and ask which coalition is stable. Interpreting the weights as shares of a large, low-support parliamentary population makes that population explicit while preserving the coalition and stability questions. The mirror covers the weighted-agreement stability result in Section 3; it does not try to extend the paper’s implementation results.

The soft spot is that this continuization adds little computationally. The paper already uses party weights directly, and with exactly three parties the algorithm checks only three coalitions. So this is a plausible high-multiplicity interpretation and a tractable mirror, but a weak example of the programme’s hoped-for complexity landscape: it neither dissolves a discrete hardness result nor brings a difficult problem within reach of continuous-optimization tools.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 are the only anchors the proponent raises, but neither is a computational result. They establish stability and uniqueness for agreements selected by weighted rules. The paper states no complexity problem, algorithmic classification, or optimization task. One can derive a new task—compute the stable coalition from the inputs—but that is not a computational result this paper proves.

The proposed population also sits awkwardly with the model. The agents who bargain and send messages are the three parties. Their vote or seat totals enter as weights; individual voters or representatives have no preferences, actions, or coalition decisions in the paper. A large parliament with three disciplined caucuses is plausible, but it still has three strategic actors. Treating MPs as a high-multiplicity population either leaves their masses as parameters in the same three-party calculation, or requires a new model of how individual members form coalitions or act for their caucuses. Treating voters as types requires adding an electoral stage: elections have already happened in the paper, and voter types do not determine the agreements or stability test.

The strongest version of the mirror is therefore: given rational seat shares, bliss points, and office benefits, compute the stable pair. The paper’s unique-stability result makes that a natural question, but it involves only three candidate coalitions and turns the seat totals into normalized bargaining weights. It does not expose a computational problem whose size grows with a continuous population. That is the strongest negative case: this is a stability theorem in a three-actor bargaining model, not a computational result about a population.

The case is not airtight. If ChoCo regards three party caucuses as a continuous population of representatives, the stable-pair query is a recognizable, if very small, mirror. The objection is that the population interpretation adds little to the model’s existing weight parameters and creates no population-level computation—not that the question is impossible to pose.
