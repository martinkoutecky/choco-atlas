What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers equal-\(\sigma\) group formation and its ordered dynamic-programming approach; it leaves individual tariff segmentation and unrestricted heterogeneous-\(\sigma\) grouping aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the ordered equal-\(\sigma\) problem be solved in time polynomial in \(M\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length, independently of \(H\)?
  • Does allowing a type's mass to split across tariffs change the optimum compared with assigning each type as a whole?
  • Does the group-risk trade-off remain meaningful under a normalized large-population limit, where its per-capita contribution scales as \(H^{-1/2}\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying numbered complexity anchor. Corollary 1 and Lemmas 1–3 establish tariff-choice and group-structure properties, but none says a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper does present Algorithm 1 and claims polynomial running time for its dynamic program, but an algorithm is not one of the numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition anchors your instructions require. So I can’t honestly provide a compliant anchor.

The best positive lead, if the paper’s algorithmic claim can be considered despite that restriction, is the group-buying problem in the equal-\(\sigma\) case. The supporting result is Lemma 3, proved here: when all customers have the same prediction-error standard deviation, if \(\sigma_i/\mu_i < \sigma_j/\mu_j\) and an optimal structure assigns \(j\) to tariff \(\tau_k\) and \(i\) to tariff \(\tau_l\), then \(\tau_k\) must be less flat than \(\tau_l\). In other words, customers with higher coefficients of variation cannot be assigned to more predictive tariffs than customers with lower ones. The paper then gives Algorithm 1, a dynamic program over ordered customers; it claims quadratic worst-case time, reduced to linear time with two tariffs. Lemma 3 supplies an optimality guarantee for the equal-\(\sigma\) regime. These are promising computational results, but they do not meet the stated anchor rule.

Here is the continuous question I would nominate if that rule were relaxed. Take \(M\) prediction-of-use tariffs and a population of total mass \(H\). A type is a household’s prediction-error distribution \(N(\mu_i,\sigma)\), with the same \(\sigma\) for every type; the society is a rational distribution \(\rho\) over finitely many distinct means \(\mu_i\). Mass \(H\rho_i\) represents households of type \(i\). A solution assigns that mass to tariff groups, allowing fractional assignment, with at most one group per tariff and groups ordered by increasing coefficient of variation and tariff flatness as in Lemma 3. It minimizes the sum of groups’ expected payments.

More explicitly, if \(x_{ik}\) is the mass of type \(i\) assigned to tariff \(k\), then \(\sum_k x_{ik}=H\rho_i\). For tariff \(k\), let \(b_k\) be its baseline rate, \(u_k,o_k\) its under- and over-consumption rates, and \(r_k^*=u_k/(u_k+o_k)\). The assigned group’s aggregate mean and standard deviation are \(a_k=\sum_i x_{ik}\mu_i\) and \(v_k=\sigma\sqrt{\sum_i x_{ik}}\), assuming independent household errors. Its expected payment is
\[ b_k a_k-(u_k+o_k)v_k\int_0^{r_k^*}\Phi^{-1}(y)\,dy. \]
The problem is to find the feasible ordered assignment of minimum total payment, and return the assignment and that minimum.

The regime is a large collective-switching pool with many households but a small number \(\tau\) of consumption-profile types: households share a standardized forecast-error spread, while differing in mean usage. That retains the paper’s central decision—forming groups and choosing tariffs to minimize total expected cost. The authors would plausibly recognize it: equal standard deviations are precisely the restricted grouping case in which they prove the ordering property and apply their dynamic program.

I would expect this restricted mirror to be tractable. Sorting the \(\tau\) types by coefficient of variation reduces the choice to ordered group boundaries; a support-based dynamic program is a natural route to a running time polynomial in \(M\) and \(\tau\), rather than total population mass \(H\). The paper does not prove that high-multiplicity bound: its stated runtime is in the number of explicit customers, so a support-compressed algorithm and its treatment of fractional mass would still need proof. Useful follow-up questions are whether the result extends to heterogeneous \(\sigma_i\), where the paper gives a counterexample to the ordering property, and whether relaxing mass assignments can ever improve the optimum over assigning each identical type as a whole.

My weakest point is therefore substantial: this is a convincing continuous-population model of one restricted regime, but the paper’s named results do not provide the required complexity anchor, and its stated algorithm does not itself establish tractability in the support-size parameters.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Under the stated anchor rule, the case for a mirror starts with a problem: none of the paper’s numbered results classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Corollary 1 characterizes individual tariff choice by a coefficient-of-variation threshold; Lemmas 1–3 give structural properties of tariff choice and group assignments. Algorithm 1 has a polynomial-time claim, but the paper does not state it as a numbered complexity result. The individual-choice characterization could be applied to a population distribution, but computing the market shares would amount to measuring how much mass falls into each threshold interval.

The strongest proposed mirror is Lemma 3’s equal-\(\sigma\) grouping result. Its population scenario is plausible, and the authors would recognize the tariff-grouping objective. The difficulty is that the model’s distinctive risk-pooling term does not survive a standard large-population limit. If type \(i\) has mass fraction \(\rho_i\), and \(x_{ik}\) of that mass joins tariff \(k\) in a population of \(H\) households, the group’s expected cost has the form
\[ H b_k\sum_i x_{ik}\mu_i +\sqrt{H}\,\kappa_k\sigma\sqrt{\sum_i x_{ik}}, \]
where \(b_k\) is the baseline rate and \(\kappa_k\) is the tariff’s penalty coefficient. Dividing by \(H\), the risk-pooling term is proportional to \(H^{-1/2}\). With a unique cheapest baseline rate, all positive mass goes to that tariff in the limit; with tied baseline rates, the limiting per-capita objective does not distinguish among the tied group structures. The sorting and segmentation that motivate the proposed mirror are finite-population effects.

A stronger version of the mirror can keep \(H\) as an input and retain those effects. That is a coherent high-multiplicity optimization problem, but it is then a finite-scale relaxation whose tariff assignments depend on population size. The proposed support-based algorithm is still a conjectured adaptation: Algorithm 1 runs in the number of explicit customers, and its guarantee applies to the restricted ordered case. Keeping a nonvanishing risk term at unit total mass instead requires a different aggregate-noise interpretation, such as a stochastic field or correlated shocks. That may be a sensible energy model, but it changes the independent household-error aggregation behind Lemma 3.

So the strongest negative case is narrow: the named results are not complexity classifications, and the equal-\(\sigma\) mirror loses its central group-risk trade-off under the usual normalized continuum limit. That does not establish the universal claim. A fixed-\(H\), support-parameterized mirror remains plausible for large consumer pools with repeated forecast types; the paper’s permutation-invariant group cost makes such types sensible. I would reject the proposed anchor as insufficient under the strict rule, while judging the broader “no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario” case weak.
