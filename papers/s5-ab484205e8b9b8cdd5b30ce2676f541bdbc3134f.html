The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its “New Jury Theorem” is a probabilistic theorem: under New Independence and New Competence, majority correctness weakly increases with group size and converges to \(\Pr(p^\pi>\tfrac12)+\tfrac12\Pr(p^\pi=\tfrac12)\). The appendix’s “Theorem” derives a conditional-independence claim from causal-network assumptions. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

A continuum of voters might be a natural model for a large electorate, but attaching one here would require inventing a computational question rather than mirroring a named result of this paper. Under the requested standard, there is no valid anchor, so I can’t give a theorem-anchored continuous problem for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest apparent anchor is the New Jury Theorem, but it does not supply a computational problem. It proves how majority correctness changes with group size and gives its limit. In the paper’s own model, that limit is already \(\Pr(p^\pi>\tfrac12)+\tfrac12\Pr(p^\pi=\tfrac12)\). A continuum of voters makes the population interpretation natural, but does not turn this probabilistic result into a complexity question.

The best version of that mirror would take a society with masses of voters and ask for its limiting probability of a correct majority. For a finite, explicitly listed distribution over problems, that amounts to summing the masses above and at the competence threshold. For a general continuous distribution, the calculation is an integral whose tractability depends on how the distribution is encoded. Neither version exposes a computational social choice problem in the paper: the first is a direct scan, and the second adds an input-representation question the authors did not study. The paper already derives the large-group limit analytically.

One could add a computational question about choosing deliberation or education to improve collective accuracy. But the paper specifies no intervention set, costs, or mapping from interventions to competence distributions. Supplying those ingredients would create a new optimization model, not mirror one of its results. The interchangeable-voter result and the causal-network appendix do not fill that gap: they establish probabilistic claims, not computational tasks.

The case is strongest for the claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror under ChoCo’s computational brief. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile adjacent project could ever be built: policy optimization could be interesting, but it would need substantial new modeling choices and should be presented as a follow-up, not as a mirror of this paper.
