The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make the requested anchored computational case for this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest results are analytic. Theorem 1.1 bounds the measure of product distributions \(\mu\) for which a symmetric monotone function has a non-negligible chance of returning a specified value. Theorem 1.3 says that, under a product distribution with enough bias toward candidate \(i\), every fair monotone voting function elects \(i\) with high probability. Corollary 3.2 gives a corresponding threshold statement for monotone graph properties, and Theorem 5.2 is an influence inequality. None analyzes the complexity of computing or optimizing anything.

There is a natural large-electorate setting nearby: voters have complete rankings drawn from a small set of \(\tau\) types, with type proportions \(\mu_t\) and \(n\gg\tau\). But the paper’s results concern the probability of outcomes in a *random finite profile*. Replacing that profile with a deterministic society of type masses would change the question; retaining the sampling randomness would make it a noise-over-discrete-profiles question. Neither supplies the required computational anchor.

So there is no continuous problem per eligible anchor to hand to a prover, and I would not present these theorems as evidence that the paper has already made a continuous-population computational contribution. The plausible connection is an analytic precursor, not the computational mirror this programme asks for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continuize. Its numbered results bound threshold widths, election probabilities, graph-property probabilities, and influence sums. None asks for an algorithm or establishes a complexity classification. That is a decisive mismatch for this programme: a computational problem cannot be recovered just by changing the population model.

The apparent bridge is the distribution \(\mu\) over votes. But in Theorems 1.1 and 1.3, \(\mu\) is the sampling law for \(n\) discrete voters. The outcome is still \(f(x)\) for a random finite profile \(x\in A^n\); \(\mu\) is not the society on which a mass-transfer or winner-robustness problem is defined. Replacing the random profile with a deterministic distribution of vote types would require a new rule on mass distributions. The threshold theorem does not specify that rule or pose a computational question about it.

The other plausible anchors do not repair the gap. Corollary 3.2 applies the same probabilistic threshold idea to finite, structurally organized graphs; making their edges or vertices into masses would require a different graph model, and still would not supply a computational task from the corollary. Theorem 5.2 is an influence inequality, not a social-choice algorithmic problem.

One could invent adjacent questions—for example, finding the smallest change in vote-type proportions that changes an expected winner. But those would need a chosen representation of the rule, an input model, and a computational objective that the paper never provides. The paper’s results might later help analyze such questions; that would make them useful background, not continuous mirrors of its results. I cannot rule out every worthwhile project inspired by the paper, but I see no anchored computational mirror here.
