The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper’s matching problem has a plausible high-multiplicity regime. For example, a large residency or professional hiring market could have many workers and employers but relatively few worker types (rankings of employer types) and employer types (hiring rules, quotas, and preferences over worker bundles). The mass of a type would be its share of the market; an allocation could be a distribution over integral worker bundles for firms, with each worker unit still assigned to at most one firm. That makes a continuous stable-assignment problem natural enough that the authors would recognize the underlying market.

But this paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror to. Theorem 1, proved here, establishes weak Pareto optimality for workers under substitutable, quota \(q\)-separable firm preferences. Theorem 2, proved here using the Blocking Lemma, says the workers-optimal stable mechanism is group strategy-proof for workers. Theorem 3, proved in the appendix, states that an individually rational matching improving some workers over the workers-optimal stable matching has a blocking pair with the specified properties. These are substantive results, but none classifies a problem as tractable or hard. The paper describes deferred acceptance, but does not give a numbered runtime or complexity result. Example 2 is a counterexample, not a hardness result.

One could propose a new computational question: given rational masses of finitely many worker and employer types, compute a stable mass allocation, represented by a distribution over firms’ integral bundles, with no positive mass of blocking worker–firm pairs. That is a plausible extension of the paper’s market. It is not, however, a mirror of a named computational result in this paper; making it the workers-optimal mechanism’s output would also require defining worker optimality for type-level distributions.

So the positive case is limited to model plausibility: high multiplicity fits some large matching markets, and the paper supplies natural stable-matching structure to build on. It does not supply the required computational anchor. Its main results concern strategy-proofness and other economic properties, rather than the complexity questions this programme is meant to chart.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is not that this market lacks a high-multiplicity regime. Large hiring or residency markets with repeated worker and employer types are plausible, and the paper’s setting makes that case especially natural. But none of the three theorems the proponent names is a computational result. The paper gives no complexity classification, runtime bound, approximation result, or parameterized algorithm. It describes deferred acceptance, but does not analyze its computational complexity. That leaves no computational claim here for ChoCo to mirror.

Theorem 1 says that, under the stated preference conditions, no individually rational matching makes every worker strictly better off than the workers-optimal stable matching. Its continuous counterpart could make the same claim for a mass allocation, with suitable definitions of worker welfare and improvement. That would still be an axiomatic result. Asking an algorithm to find a Pareto improvement, if one exists, would be a new search problem; Theorem 1 rules out such a witness under its assumptions but says nothing about computational complexity.

Theorem 2 is a group strategy-proofness theorem. Its best continuous restatement would define how a positive mass of workers can misreport and whether that improves their assignments. A single worker’s misreport has no effect on a distribution-based mechanism, so a nontrivial version must specify mass deviations. That may be a coherent question, but it remains a question about incentives. Turning it into a complexity question—say, deciding whether a coalition can manipulate the mechanism—would add a new computational problem absent from the paper.

Theorem 3 establishes the existence of a blocking pair under specified conditions. One could ask for an algorithm that finds a blocker in a mass market, or define blocking by a positive mass of agents. Either might be worth studying. But the theorem itself is a structural existence result, not a bound on the difficulty of finding a blocker.

The strongest alternative is to ask for an algorithm to compute a stable, worker-optimal mass allocation, perhaps represented by distributions over firms’ integral bundles. That is a plausible computational research question, and the paper’s deferred-acceptance discussion offers a starting point. It is nevertheless a new problem: the paper neither claims a computational result about its finite mechanism nor establishes how that result changes under a continuum. The proposed mirror therefore has credible model plausibility but no computational anchor in the paper.

On the programme’s stated criteria, that is a strong case against treating this paper as a continuization target. It does not prove that no continuous matching project could be worthwhile; the large-market regime argues against that broader claim. What it supports is the narrower verdict that any such project would need a new computational question, rather than a mirror of the results these authors prove.
