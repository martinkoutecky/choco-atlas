The anchor — other

Theorem \cref{thm:k-exact}

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem \cref{thm:k-exact} — survived: Replacing the finite ballot counts by rational masses gives a well-posed high-multiplicity evaluation problem for the same distortion quantity.
  • Proposition \cref{prop:cyclic} — fell: The equal-mass cyclic society is already the profile analyzed in the proposition, so this mirror merely restates that result.

The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a top-\(k\) ballot support \(T\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a binary-rational lottery \(q\) over \(C\), compute the exact worst-case metric distortion over metric realizations consistent with those ballots. Let \(r_t(i)\) be candidate \(i\)'s reported position in type \(t\), with unlisted candidates at position \(k\). Equivalently, evaluate \(1+2\max_{i^\star\in C}\max_{\ell\ge 0,\,\ell_{i^\star}=0}\frac{\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{i\in C}q_i\min\!\left(\ell_i,\min_{j:r_t(i)<r_t(j)}\ell_j\right)}{\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max\!\left(0,\max_{i,j:r_t(i)<r_t(j)}(\ell_i-\ell_j)\right)}\), the mass-weighted version of the paper's exact characterization.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over supported ordered top-\(k\) reports; voters of one report type may occupy different metric locations as long as their reports are respected. For a fixed lottery \(q\), the sufficient optimization variables are a proposed minimum-cost candidate \(i^\star\) and the distances \(\ell_i\) from it; the objective is the mass-weighted ratio in the exact characterization.

The objection that survived

The paper already uses vote shares for first-choice inputs, and its top-\(k\) expression depends on ballot multiplicities, so the mass formulation may add only a succinct input encoding; the paper does not analyze that encoding's complexity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact worst-case distortion evaluation for arbitrary top-\(k\) profiles and fixed lotteries, including the formula for \(k\ge 3\). It leaves aside the paper's optimality results for first-choice mechanisms and the separate cyclic-family result.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact bit complexity of evaluating the maximum as a function of the number of candidates, support size, mass encoding length, and \(k\)?
  • Does the optimization admit a polynomial-time algorithm for \(k\le 2\), or when \(k\) is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s exact top-\(k\) distortion characterization, identified in the supplied text as Theorem \(\cref{thm:k-exact}\). The text prints no theorem numbers, so I can give its reference label but not a number without inventing one. This theorem is proved in the paper. It says that for every mechanism \(q\), its worst-case distortion is exactly the displayed maximum over the choice of optimal candidate and nonnegative candidate-to-optimum distances \(\ell_i\). For each voter, the numerator uses the smallest relevant \(\ell_i\) among candidates ranked below \(i\); the denominator uses the largest positive difference \(\ell_i-\ell_j\) over pairs ranked in that order. The paper proves the expression tight by constructing a metric that attains it. This is an optimization characterization the paper computes with, though it is not a conventional theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Let \(C\) be \(n\) candidates, \(T\) a set of supported ordered top-\(k\) ballots, and \(\mu_t\) a binary-rational mass for each \(t\in T\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(q\) be a lottery over candidates. For a metric realization, each ballot type has mass \(\mu_t\) at a voter location inducing that report; social cost is the integral of distance to a candidate over this population. Compute the worst-case metric distortion of \(q\) over all such realizations: the supremum of expected social cost under \(q\), divided by the minimum social cost of any candidate. A solution is the exact value, with a matching metric witness or a certificate of the upper bound. Equivalently, one can ask the threshold question of whether this supremum is at least a given rational \(\rho\).

To state the theorem’s formula in this mass model, set \(\ell_{i^\star}=0\) for the proposed optimal candidate \(i^\star\). Let \(r_t(i)\) be candidate \(i\)’s reported position in type \(t\), with unlisted candidates at position \(k\). Define
\[ G_t(i;\ell)=\min\!\left(\ell_i,\min_{j:r_t(i)<r_t(j)}\ell_j\right),\qquad H_t(\ell)=\max\!\left(0,\max_{i,j:r_t(i)<r_t(j)}(\ell_i-\ell_j)\right). \]
Replacing the paper’s sums over voters by mass-weighted sums gives
\[ 1+2\max_{i^\star\in C}\ \max_{\ell\ge0} \frac{\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{i\in C}q_iG_t(i;\ell)} {\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tH_t(\ell)}. \]
This is my lead: the population is the distribution \(\mu\) over reported types, while the lottery over the finite candidate set remains discrete.

A useful second anchor is Proposition \(\cref{prop:cyclic}\), also proved here and likewise unnumbered in the supplied text. For the cyclic profile \(C(n,k)\), with \(n\) equally numerous ballot types \(t\mapsto(t,t+1,\ldots,t+k-1)\bmod n\), the optimal randomized distortion is
\[ D^\star(n,k)=3-2\cdot\frac{k(2n-k-1)}{2n(n-1)}, \]
attained by the uniform mechanism \(q_i=1/n\). The corresponding continuous problem is: given the cyclic society with mass \(1/n\) on each of its \(n\) types, find a lottery minimizing worst-case metric distortion, and return the optimum and an optimal lottery. The proposition answers it exactly; for \(n\ge2\), the answer is the formula above and the uniform lottery. Its result is tractable by symmetry, in the sense of Class A.

The regime is plausible: a large public consultation or city-wide vote can have many residents but relatively few distinct ordered top-\(k\) reports. For binary-rational masses, \(\mu\) is exactly the empirical profile of a finite election after replication by a common denominator. In the metric witnesses used by the theorem, agents with the same ballot can share the same distances to candidates; the paper’s construction assigns those distances from the ballot order and the common \(\ell\)’s. So the continuous model does not need to distinguish named individuals. Its type support is the set of supported ballots, of size \(\tau\), rather than the number of voters or all possible ballots.

For the lead problem, my provisional expectation is hardness transfers (Class B) if exact evaluation proves hard: the \(\ell\)-optimization still carries combinatorics in the alternatives and their reported orders, while finite rational populations embed directly by replication. The paper itself proves no such hardness, so that is a forecast rather than a result. It also supplies no complexity classification for evaluating the general formula. Further questions include whether exact evaluation is polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\), whether the \(k\le2\) subset expression admits an efficient optimization algorithm, and how the answer changes for \(k\ge3\), where the paper says the \(\{0,1\}\)-edge reduction fails.

The authors would almost certainly recognize these as their distortion questions: the paper already defines vote shares as fractions and derives results from them. That is also the main weakness of this case. The proposed society distribution is very close to a model already present in the paper, especially for the cyclic proposition, whose “equally numerous voter types” are already an empirical type distribution. This makes the mirror credible as a computational-complexity question about their exact characterization, but weak as a claim that the paper has not already formulated a continuous population. The paper’s exact formulas and closed-form examples are analytic results, not complexity results; the potential contribution is to classify the computation they expose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirrors is that the paper already works with the relevant population statistics. In first-choice voting, its input is the vote-share vector \(\nu\). In the top-\(k\) theorem, every voter contributes through a sum of terms determined by that voter’s ballot. Grouping equal ballots and replacing those sums by mass-weighted sums gives the proposed formula directly.

For rational masses \(\mu_t=a_t/D\), replicating each type \(t\) exactly \(a_t\) times produces the same profile: both social costs scale by \(D\), so distortion is unchanged. Thus the continuous version of the exact-distortion formula is already implicit in the paper. That is the best collision argument against the lead anchor.

It does not defeat the stronger computational question, though. A binary-encoded mass vector can describe a population exponentially larger than an explicit voter list, and the theorem still leaves an optimization over candidate distances \(\ell\). The complexity of evaluating that maximum, or optimizing \(q\) from \((\mu,q)\) in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\), is not answered by the formula. The mass model is well-posed, and the remaining optimization could make it worth studying. The population weights enter linearly, but that alone does not settle the computational question.

The cyclic anchor is weaker. With masses \(1/n\), the proposition already gives the exact answer and the uniform mechanism. Replicating each ballot type equally leaves the distortion unchanged. Allowing unequal masses would create a broader question, but it would no longer be the cyclic result; it would return to the general optimization above.

So I cannot honestly defeat every anchor. The cyclic proposition is already answered for its stated family, and the paper’s formulas already extend to ballot masses. But the compact-input complexity question based on the exact top-\(k\) characterization survives: it is sensible, within scope, and unresolved here. My negative case against a worthwhile continuous mirror is weak.
