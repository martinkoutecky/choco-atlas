The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) projects \(A\), rational costs \(q_a\), budget \(1\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete utility types \(f_t:2^A\to[0,1]\), each normalized by \(f_t(A)=1\), monotone, submodular, and satisfying \(f_t(a\mid S)\ge(1-c)f_t(\{a\})\), define \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_t\mu_t f_t(S)\). For \(m\) a power of two, a mechanism samples \(\alpha\) uniformly from \(\Lambda=\{2^{j-1}/m:1\le j\le\log_2m\}\) and observes only the mass distribution of approval reports \(R_\alpha(f_t)=\{a:f_t(\{a\})\ge\alpha\}\). Construct a randomized map from the sampled threshold and approval-mass profile to a lottery over sets \(S\subseteq A\) with \(\sum_{a\in S}q_a\le1\), such that its expected per-capita welfare is at least \(\frac{(1-\epsilon)(1-c)}{4\log m}\max_{S:\sum_{a\in S}q_a\le1}W_\mu(S)\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity participatory-budgeting society has rational mass μ_t on each complete utility type \(f_t\); the mechanism sees threshold-approval masses and returns a lottery over discrete budget-feasible project sets, evaluated by \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_t\mu_t f_t(S)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's threshold-approval guarantee, including its approximate knapsack branch and uniform-singleton branch; it leaves Theorems 1 and 2 on ranking elicitation outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the optimal worst-case distortion among randomized rules using only the sampled-threshold approval-mass profile, and establish matching lower bounds.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is narrow: the paper’s Theorem 3, proved here, gives a named algorithmic result for threshold approval votes. It says that, when every voter’s utility is normalized, monotone and submodular, and satisfies the paper’s curvature condition \(\Delta_f(a\mid S)\ge (1-c)f(\{a\})\), the authors’ randomized aggregation rule achieves expected welfare at least \((1-\epsilon)(1-c)\operatorname{sw}(S^*)/(4\log m)\). Thus its distortion is \(O(\log m/(1-c))\). The rule randomly selects a threshold, uses the resulting approval counts as weights in an approximately solved knapsack problem, and mixes that choice with selecting one alternative uniformly at random. Theorem 3 is proved in the paper; the knapsack FPTAS it uses is cited. This is an approximation guarantee, not a named classification such as NP-hardness or membership in P.

The continuous mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous Threshold-Approval PB Distortion. There are \(m\) projects \(A\), rational costs \(q_a\le 1\), and budget \(1\). A type is a complete utility function \(f:2^A\to[0,1]\) satisfying the theorem’s normalization, monotonicity, submodularity and curvature promise. A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\delta_{f_t}\), with rational masses summing to \(1\). Its per-capita welfare for a funded set \(S\) is \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_t\mu_t f_t(S)\), and its benchmark is \(\max_{S:\sum_{a\in S}q_a\le1}W_\mu(S)\).

For \(m\) a power of two, let the threshold grid be \(\Lambda=\{2^{j-1}/m:1\le j\le\log_2m\}\). The rule samples \(\alpha\in\Lambda\); a type \(f_t\) reports \(R_\alpha(f_t)=\{a:f_t(\{a\})\ge\alpha\}\). The rule sees only the resulting distribution of approval sets, not the underlying utility functions, and returns a lottery over budget-feasible project sets. The task is to construct such a rule and prove that, for every permitted \(\mu\), its expected per-capita welfare is at least \((1-\epsilon)(1-c)/(4\log m)\) times the benchmark. A solution is an explicit mapping from the threshold and approval-mass profile to a lottery, together with the guarantee. Theorem 3 supplies the model for that solution: replace approval counts by approval masses, solve the weighted knapsack approximately, and retain the uniform-singleton branch.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for city-scale participatory budgeting: many residents fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of recurring utility types over the project set, so \(n\) can be large while \(\tau\ll n\). Their masses express population shares. Types must include the full utility function, so anyone with different relevant valuations belongs to a different type. That is the high-multiplicity assumption itself, and it is a reasonable regime to study rather than a distortion of this model. The project set stays discrete; the continuous object is the population. The same inequalities in the theorem’s proof carry over by replacing sums over voters with mass-weighted sums. The rule needs only singleton approval information; with explicit type access or singleton-value queries, computing the masses and running the FPTAS is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length and \(1/\epsilon\).

I would classify this constructive guarantee as tractable: the mirror asks for a rule meeting a welfare bound, and the paper’s knapsack-based method provides one. A natural next question is whether the best possible continuous distortion is smaller: define \(D^*=\inf_\pi\sup_\mu \max_{S\text{ feasible}}W_\mu(S)/\mathbb E[W_\mu(\pi)]\), and seek its value or matching lower bounds. This case covers Theorem 3’s threshold-approval result, not the two ranking theorems or the paper as a whole.

The weak point is that the paper studies distortion from limited preference reports, rather than complexity of bribery, control, campaigning or robustness. Its theorem does not identify a discrete hardness result that might dissolve in the continuum; much of the proof survives by replacing counts with masses. So this is a clean continuous-population analogue of the authors’ problem, but a modest contribution to the programme’s complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3 is a distortion guarantee, not a complexity result. Its contribution is a bound on how much welfare a rule can lose under threshold approval reports. The FPTAS solves the project-side knapsack subproblem; the theorem does not classify the complexity of a new PB problem or identify a computational boundary. That makes it a poor fit for ChoCo’s complexity landscape.

The proponent’s continuous model is nevertheless well formed. A society can be a finite-support distribution over complete submodular utility functions, and welfare is the mass-weighted sum of their utilities. A city with many residents sharing a modest number of utility types is a plausible regime. Replacing voter counts with approval masses makes the knapsack rule implementable, and the proof’s sums over voters become mass-weighted sums. Neither individual identity nor a vanishing continuum defeats this mirror.

That leaves a narrower case against its priority: the population continuum adds no computational question to the theorem. The new algorithmic work still concerns a knapsack over projects; the population enters as approval weights, and the stated guarantee follows by translating the proof to masses. A further question about optimal continuous distortion could be interesting, but it is a new minimax problem, not a result of this paper being mirrored. This is a reason to rank the mirror below questions that expose a new complexity landscape, not a reason it is invalid.

So I cannot honestly sustain the requested universal negative. Theorem 3’s mirror survives: the case against is weak on whether a worthwhile continuous question exists, and stronger only on whether it belongs near the centre of ChoCo’s programme.
