The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered Propositions 1–4 are proved equilibrium and welfare claims, not results that place a computational problem in a complexity class or give an algorithm. The experimental findings are reported in tables, not as named computational results.

The closest lead is Proposition 2 (proved here): in the most informative equilibrium under transparency, members reveal their signals truthfully, cannot credibly communicate their abilities, and vote according to their signals. The group’s error probability is \((1-q)^2(1-\sigma)^2\) in state \(B\) and \(1-(q+(1-q)\sigma)^2\) in state \(R\); a member receives payoff \(q/(q+(1-q)\sigma)\) when their vote matches the state, and zero otherwise. That is a formal result about the underlying game, but it is not a computational result under your anchor rule.

The strongest positive route, if treated as an unanchored extension, is a mass version of that committee game. Let \(T=\{h_b,h_r,l_b,l_r\}\) and give each type a rational mass conditional on the state. For example, in state \(B\), set \(\mu_{h_b}=q\), \(\mu_{l_b}=(1-q)\sigma\), and \(\mu_{l_r}=(1-q)(1-\sigma)\), with the remaining mass zero; define the state-\(R\) masses symmetrically. Members of each type use strategies over the paper’s messages, ability claims, and votes. The question would be: given \(q,\sigma\), the state-conditioned masses, the transparency regime, and the principal’s state weights, compute a Bayes-consistent, sequentially rational mass-strategy profile and its state-conditional error rates; then identify the regime maximizing the principal’s expected payoff.

There is a plausible regime of large, interchangeable expert panels, and the paper itself says in a footnote that member behavior would not change in an \(n\)-member committee. But that does not establish that the continuous game preserves the paper’s result: under unanimity, an atomless member is never pivotal, which may change the equilibrium incentives. So I would not present this as an anchor-specific mirror or assign it a reliable A/B/C classification. The missing computational anchor is the decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–4 characterize equilibria and compare welfare across transparency regimes; the experiments measure behavior. Proposition 2 is the closest anchor, but its formulas describe an equilibrium of a fixed, two-member Bayesian game. The paper gives no algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or scalable family of computational instances. Under ChoCo’s stated criterion, that is a decisive mismatch.

A high-multiplicity regime is plausible: many committee members could share the same ability and signal, and the paper itself notes that finite committees could have more members. But repeating the experiment across many identical committees just averages the outcomes of the same small game. In the proposed mass model, \(q\) and \(\sigma\) determine the four type masses; the model has no growing support or combinatorial input. Computing its mass strategies and error rates would be a new equilibrium exercise, not a complexity landscape exposed by the paper. Proposition 4’s transparency choice likewise reduces to a threshold in the scalar \(x\).

The proposed large-panel route has a further limit. The paper’s footnote concerns finite \(n\); it does not establish an atomless limit. Under continuum unanimity, an individual has zero mass, so cannot change whether the entire population votes unanimously. A quota rule could preserve a meaningful aggregate decision, and reputational incentives could still affect individual communication. But that version remains a fixed, four-type game unless one adds growing signal spaces, action spaces, or interaction structure. Those additions might create an algorithmic research project, but they would supply the computational content that this paper lacks.

I would not claim that no continuum model of expert committees could be worthwhile. A continuum version could be a sensible analytic study, and the paper’s equilibrium results give it a starting point. The negative case is that it is not a worthwhile *continuous-computational mirror* of these results: the paper has no computational anchor, and its natural population reformulations either replicate a fixed game or require new structure to create an algorithmic problem.
