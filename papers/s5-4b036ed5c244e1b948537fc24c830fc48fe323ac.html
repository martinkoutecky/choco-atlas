The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(s_g\), and additive valuation vectors \(v_t\) sharing a common good order, find a complete mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=s_g\). Define \(\operatorname{MMS}_{3/2,t}(s)\) as the supremum, over distributions \(\pi\) on integral bundles with \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[b_g]=s_g/(3/2)\), of \(\inf_{b\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi)}v_t(b)\); require \(v_t(b)\ge\operatorname{MMS}_{3/2,t}(s)\) whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\), and require \(v_t(b)\ge v_t(c-e_g)\) whenever \(x_{t,b}x_{u,c}>0\) and \(c_g>0\), where \(e_g\) is one copy of good \(g\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity fair-allocation model with finitely many valuation types \(t\), continuum masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita copy supplies \(s_g\), integral bundles \(b\), and configuration masses \(x_{t,b}\); the objective is to find an allocation meeting each type's continuum MMS threshold and support-wise EFX, with \(\rho=3/2\) for Theorem 1 and \(\rho=4/3\) for Theorem 3.

The objection that survived

The paper proves no discrete-to-continuous limit theorem, and a vanishing-mass type can consume nonvanishing supply; the mirror therefore needs explicit positive-mass or tightness assumptions.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the constructive guarantees of Theorems 1 and 3 in the ordered setting. It leaves Theorem 2's top-\(n\) variant, partial allocations, and auxiliary normalization and envy-cycle lemmas untreated as separate mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(\operatorname{MMS}_{\rho,t}(s)\) and a feasible bundle support be represented or separated efficiently without expanding the population?
  • Does support-wise EFX or EF1 admit a finite configuration formulation with a tractable separation oracle?
  • Which positive-mass, tightness, and rounding assumptions give a rigorous bridge between finite high-multiplicity instances and the continuum model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified one. This paper has no named theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm P \), FPT, or any other complexity classification. Its numbered results are constructive existence theorems. Thus it is not a fully compliant ChoCo complexity anchor under the strictest reading. Still, its algorithms give a credible high-multiplicity mirror, and Theorem 1 is a worthwhile lead if constructive allocation results are admitted.

The natural regime is a large institution allocating many copies of a finite catalogue of indivisible goods to a large cohort: for example, course seats, benefit packages, or aid items. There are \(N\) agents but only \(\tau\) valuation types, with \(\tau\ll N\). A type \(t\) records the agent’s entire additive valuation vector and common ranking of goods. Goods also scale with \(N\): if \(s_g\) is the per-capita supply of good \(g\), then an \(N\)-agent instance has approximately \(Ns_g\) indivisible copies. This is essential; keeping a fixed finite inventory while sending the population to a continuum would make fairness vacuous.

The continuum society is a distribution \(\mu\) over valuation types. An allocation is not a fractional-good allocation. Let \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{G}\) be an integral bundle, where \(b_g\) counts copies of good \(g\). The variable \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving exactly bundle \(b\). A complete allocation satisfies \( \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t \) and \( \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=s_g \) for every good \(g\). Thus population mass is divisible, but every individual receives an integral bundle.

To take the paper’s \(d\)-MMS guarantee to the limit, write \(d_N=\lceil \rho N\rceil\). For \(\rho>1\), define the continuum MMS threshold of type \(t\) by \( \operatorname{MMS}_{\rho,t}(s)=\sup_{\pi}\inf_{b\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi)}v_t(b) \), where \(\pi\) ranges over distributions on integral bundles satisfying \( \mathbb E_\pi[b_g]=s_g/\rho \) for every \(g\). This is exactly the high-multiplicity limit of partitioning \(Ns_g\) goods into \(d_N\) bundles.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper:

For every ordered instance \( (M,N,(v_i)_{i\in N}) \), there exists a complete allocation that is simultaneously \(1\)-out-of-\(\lceil 3n/2\rceil\) MMS and EFX.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Ordered \(3/2\)-MMS–EFX Allocation. Given rational type masses \(\mu\), rational per-capita supplies \(s\), additive valuations \(v_t\) with a common good order, find a complete bundle-mass allocation \(x\) such that every bundle \(b\) receiving positive mass from type \(t\) satisfies \(v_t(b)\ge \operatorname{MMS}_{3/2,t}(s)\), and such that, for every types \(t,u\), every \(b\) in the support of \(x_t\), every \(c\) in the support of \(x_u\), and every \(g\) with \(c_g>0\), \(v_t(b)\ge v_t(c-e_g)\).

The support-wise condition is important. It says that almost every individual agent satisfies EFX against almost every positively represented bundle; it does not weaken EFX to an average-utility condition. A solution is the allocation measure \(x\), preferably with finite rational support.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem: orderedness, additive valuations, integral bundles, MMS thresholds, and EFX are all preserved. Only the named population is replaced by repeated valuation types and aggregate assignment mass. The paper’s bag-filling algorithm suggests a mass version in which equal-type agents are processed in batches, while the envy-cycle completion becomes a bundle-mass reassignment procedure.

I would provisionally expect this problem to be Class A in the intended fixed-catalogue regime. The common order gives the same monotonicity that drives Algorithm 1, and aggregate assignment can plausibly be expressed through a configuration LP or a monotone flow. However, this is not proved by Theorem 1. The main open issue is whether the EFX support constraints and the integral-bundle configuration space admit a polynomial-size certificate and a pricing oracle independent of the population denominator. If exact MMS computation introduces packing hardness in the number of good types, that would be hardness inherited from indivisible goods, not continuum-specific hardness.

A second useful anchor is Theorem 3, also proved in this paper, although its proof uses the cited Theorem 5 of Akrami, Garg, Sharma, and Taki:

Given an ordered instance \(I\), there exists a complete allocation that is simultaneously \(1\)-out-of-\(4\lceil n/3\rceil\) MMS and EF1.

This gives Continuous Ordered \(4/3\)-MMS–EF1 Allocation. It has exactly the same input and allocation model, but uses \(\rho=4/3\) and replaces EFX by support-wise EF1: for every \(t,u\), every own bundle \(b\) in the support of \(x_t\), and every target bundle \(c\) in the support of \(x_u\), either \(v_t(b)\ge v_t(c)\), or there is a good \(g\) with \(c_g>0\) such that \(v_t(b)\ge v_t(c-e_g)\).

This second problem is also plausibly Class A, and perhaps more approachable because EF1 is weaker than EFX. It mirrors the paper’s stronger ordinal share guarantee while retaining indivisible bundles and population-level multiplicity. Further questions include whether the \(4/3\) threshold and EF1 can coexist with EFX in the continuum, whether the top-\(n\) setting of Theorem 2 has a meaningful “top-one-unit-per-capita” analogue, and whether finite-population allocations can be rounded from a continuum solution with controlled error.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper does not establish a complexity result for either mirror. Its algorithms are written for explicitly named agents and may have runtime or normalization costs depending on \(N\), while the ChoCo question is whether the problem can be solved from \((\mu,s,v)\) without expanding the population. Moreover, rational masses can be denominator-cleared into a large finite instance, so without a compressed algorithm this may look like a relabelling rather than a computational advance.

So my positive verdict is: Theorem 1 supports a genuine and technically nontrivial continuous-population allocation problem, with Theorem 3 as a strong secondary anchor. But under the programme’s strict named-complexity-anchor rule, the paper should be marked as lacking a qualifying computational anchor; the proposed mirror is a promising research question, not a result already supported by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject this paper as a ChoCo mirror under the programme’s strict gate. The decisive point is that none of its numbered results is computational in the required sense. Theorems 1–3 assert existence of allocations; they do not classify a decision or optimization problem, establish membership in \( \mathrm P \), prove hardness, or give a complexity-bounded algorithm.

The displayed algorithms do not repair this. Algorithm 1 relies on the normalization \( \mu_i^{d}=1 \), but obtaining the relevant MMS value or a witnessing MMS partition is not shown to be computationally efficient. The same issue is more explicit in Theorem 3: the proof uses a normalized instance only analytically and gives no running-time theorem for constructing the required normalization or allocation. Thus the paper’s computational content is, at most, an unanalysed witness procedure. A continuous problem based on it would be a new high-multiplicity fair-division project, not the continuization of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed Theorem 1 mirror is nevertheless a legitimate-looking mathematical model, so the negative case should not pretend otherwise. The difficulty is that it is not obtained merely by replacing agents with masses. It simultaneously scales the inventory with the population, replaces finite partitions by distributions over integral bundle vectors, defines a new limiting MMS quantity, and chooses a support-wise interpretation of EFX. Those choices may all be defensible, but the paper supplies no limiting theorem connecting its finite guarantee to this object.

There is also a genuine stability problem. Finite EFX allocations need not have limits represented by a distribution over ordinary bundles. For example, with \(N\) copies of one good, let one agent value every copy at \(1\) and all other agents value them at \(0\). Giving all copies to the exceptional agent satisfies the paper’s EFX and ordinal-MMS requirements. As \(N\) grows, that agent’s type has mass \(1/N\), yet it consumes the entire per-capita supply. The population distribution loses the type while the resource allocation does not disappear. A continuum model must therefore impose tightness or bounded-bundle assumptions, retain vanishing types separately, or add some “mass at infinity” representation. None is part of the paper’s theorem, and each changes the proposed mirror.

Keeping exact support-wise EFX does not make this merely technical. For every bundle in the support of one type to be safe against every bundle in the support of another is a nonconvex bundle-compatibility condition. If one weakens it to an almost-everywhere or expected-utility condition, the resulting notion is no longer the paper’s EFX. If one keeps it, the population masses are only an outer layer around a difficult support-selection problem; the paper gives no reason to expect its bag-filling argument to become a finite, encoded continuous optimization problem.

Theorem 3 cannot independently rescue the case. Its \(4\lceil n/3\rceil\) guarantee has the same normalization and missing-runtime issues, while its EF1 witness is a particular good in a particular target bundle. In the continuum, one must again choose between support-wise EF1, which inherits the nonconvexity and limiting problems, and an averaged relaxation, which is a different fairness notion. Theorem 2 is no stronger: “top-\(n\)” is tied to the finite population size, and a top-per-capita interpretation would be an additional modelling decision. It also gives partial EFX and complete EF1 rather than a computational classification.

I am not objecting that the continuous answer might be hard, easy, or identical to the discrete answer; any of those could be valuable. The objection is prior to the answer: the paper does not state a computational problem whose continuous population version it would be meaningful to classify, and its constructive proofs do not supply the missing bridge.

The universal claim is stronger than the evidence permits. If ChoCo admits constructive existence papers as anchors and accepts newly designed high-multiplicity fair-division models, the proponent’s scenario is sensible and I would not honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Under the programme as stated, however, the paper should receive a negative verdict: the apparent mirrors are promising new questions, not continuizations anchored by this paper’s results.
