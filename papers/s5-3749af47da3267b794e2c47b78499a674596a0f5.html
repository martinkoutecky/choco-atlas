A search procedure added by a reviewer would not make the paper's result computational; moreover, retaining a voter of mass \(1/N\) preserves an atomic player, while an atomless limit removes that voter's effect.

What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 are the proposed anchors; neither supplies a computational result for a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest model fit is a large approval election: many voters share a small number of complete types, where a type includes preferences over candidates and winner sets. Represent the electorate by rational type masses. For an exact high-multiplicity version with \(N\) voters, a focal voter’s ballot \(B\subseteq X\) changes each approval score by \(1/N\); the objective is to maximize that voter’s preference over the resulting winner set. This is a sensible compressed representation of the paper’s individual best-response problem.

The central named result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: for any voter with arbitrary preferences over candidates, any other voters’ ballots, and preferences over sets satisfying (R), deletion, and replacement, there is a strictly sincere best-response ballot. If the voter’s preferences over candidates are nontrivial, there is such a best response that is also nontrivial. Corollary 1, also established in the paper, says that with dichotomous candidate preferences and set preferences satisfying (R), everyone casting their unique strictly sincere ballot is in a Nash equilibrium in non-weakly-dominated strategies.

These are meaningful existence and equilibrium results, but neither is a computational result in the programme’s sense: the paper gives no complexity classification or running-time bound for finding a best response. In an atomless population, an individual’s ballot has zero effect on the winners, making the best-response question vacuous. Giving a positive mass of a type control over its ballots preserves strategic impact, but turns the question into a coordinated bloc deviation rather than the individual best response studied here.

So the paper offers a natural high-multiplicity setting and a nearby best-response question, but it has no named computational anchor of the required kind. I can’t honestly present a continuous computational problem as mirroring one of its computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that neither cited result is computational. Theorem 1 establishes existence of a strictly sincere best response; Corollary 1 establishes an equilibrium. Neither gives a complexity bound or specifies how preferences over winner sets are represented. A search problem can be built around either theorem, but its complexity would depend on that added representation choice.

The atomless version of Theorem 1 loses its strategic content: one voter’s ballot cannot change the approval scores, so every ballot yields the same outcome. The best attempt to preserve the question is to represent the other voters by type masses while retaining a distinguished voter of mass \(1/N\). That is a coherent high-multiplicity encoding, but the pivotal decision still belongs to one indivisible voter. Under the paper’s assumptions, Theorem 1 guarantees an optimum among the voter’s strictly sincere ballots, which are just cutoffs between preference tiers. Searching those ballots requires at most \(m+1\) outcome comparisons once the other voters’ approval totals are aggregated. With explicit preferences over winner sets, that is a direct finite search; with succinct preferences, the resulting complexity depends on the chosen encoding. The continuous masses compress the opponents’ profile, but do not supply the computational question or its difficulty.

Corollary 1 has an even thinner mirror. For a distribution over dichotomous preference types, assign each type’s mass to its unique strictly sincere ballot. That computes the stated equilibrium directly from the types. In an atomless game, unilateral ballots are payoff-equivalent, so the equilibrium claim becomes vacuous; if a whole type mass can coordinate its ballot, the model asks about group deviations, which the corollary does not establish.

So the paper offers no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize, and its natural search variants either make individual response vacuous or retain a finite pivotal voter. The negative case is weakest at the high-multiplicity boundary: the rational-mass representation with one tagged voter is sensible and could be useful for compressing instances. But the paper supplies no complexity result for that problem, and any complexity claim would require choices about input representation beyond its results.
