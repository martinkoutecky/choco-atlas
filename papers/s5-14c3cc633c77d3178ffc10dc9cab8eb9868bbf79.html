The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying numbered computational anchor in the supplied text. Sections 3.2–3.3 describe the authors’ AUPCR algorithms and give a running time, but do not state that result as a numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition. The unnumbered theorem in §4.6.1 says that every AUPCR-maximizing matching is Pareto optimal; it is neither numbered nor a complexity result. I can’t honestly supply the required anchor number.

The closest substantive result is the paper’s own claim that AMM finds a matching maximizing the AUPCR weighted-rank objective, and MC-AMM chooses a maximum-cardinality matching among those maximizers. The paper reduces these problems to maximum-weight perfect matching and reports running time \(O(n^2\sqrt{n}\log n)\). That gives a plausible lead for a mirror, but it does not satisfy the requested numbered-anchor rule.

A concrete mirror would model a large admissions or hiring system with many applicants and recurring preference templates. An applicant type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking of \(q\) interchangeable post categories, including which categories are acceptable; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of applicants. Each category \(p\) has per-capita supply \(\nu_p\), scaled with the population. Let \(x_{tp}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to category \(p\), and let \(\rho_t(p)\) be its rank. The feasible assignments satisfy
\[ x_{tp}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_p x_{tp}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tp}\le \nu_p, \]
with \(x_{tp}=0\) for unacceptable pairs. The Continuous AUPCR-Max Matching problem asks for a feasible \(x\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t,p} x_{tp}\bigl(q-\rho_t(p)+1\bigr). \]
This is the paper’s weighted-rank objective over the category rank universe; dividing by a fixed normalization does not change its maximizers. For the MC-AMM analogue, among score-maximizing assignments, maximize \(\sum_{t,p}x_{tp}\). A solution is the resulting type-to-category mass flow: individual applicants still receive one category, while the type-level totals can be fractional.

In a regime with \(N\) applicants, a fixed or slowly growing number \(\tau\) of preference templates, and a moderate number \(q\) of categories whose capacities grow with \(N\), this is a sensible high-multiplicity object. Its expected class is tractable: the formulation has \(O(\tau q)\) assignment variables and linear constraints, so linear programming (or min-cost flow) solves it in polynomial time in the support size and input bit length. With integer-scaled supplies and masses, the underlying transportation formulation also has integral optima. Further questions include when that integrality gives an exact correspondence with finite instances, and how the answer changes when applicants rank individual posts rather than interchangeable categories.

This mirror covers the AUPCR objective and its maximum-cardinality tie-break, not the paper’s experiments or its comparisons with popularity, fairness, or rank-maximality. Its weakest point is also the main modeling concession: the paper ranks individual posts strictly, whereas the mirror ranks interchangeable post categories. That is a plausible large-scale admissions model, but authors might regard it as an extension rather than the exact continuous form of their problem. The lack of a numbered computational anchor is a separate limitation: under the requested rules, this remains a candidate mirror, not a fully anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor requirement: neither proposed anchor is a numbered computational result. Sections 3.2–3.3 describe the AMM and MC-AMM algorithms and report their running time, but their correctness claims are unnumbered; the unnumbered theorem in §4.6.1 establishes Pareto optimality, not computational complexity. So the continuous flow problems are plausible questions, but they do not supply the requested mirror of a named result.

The proposed flow model also changes what the paper optimizes. Its applicants rank individual, unit-capacity posts; the mirror ranks post categories whose capacities grow with the population. Its AUPCR weights therefore apply to category ranks, not ranks among the individual posts in the paper. Keeping the individual posts leaves finite unit capacities that cannot absorb positive applicant mass in a continuum. Scaling the posts as well requires a growing ranking universe and a new definition of how rank weights behave as that universe grows. The MC-AMM tie-break has the same modeling shift: maximizing assigned mass among score maximizers is sensible, but it is a lexicographic objective in the category-flow model.

The best version of the mirror is still credible: a large admissions or hiring market with recurring applicant preference types and homogeneous post categories is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Its AUPCR objective gives a capacitated flow problem, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as an extension of their work. That is where the negative case is weak.
