What the mirror covers

No computational result is mirrored; the paper's structural results about Condorcet and Pareto paradoxes remain statements about finite sequential games.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: this paper offers a possible subject for a future continuous-population study, but it has no named computational result to anchor the mirror required here.

The closest named results are about whether paradoxical outcomes occur, not the complexity of deciding or computing them. For example, Claim 3.1, proved in the paper, says that with three alternatives, each of the four voting systems has examples of both a Condorcet-winner paradox and a Condorcet-loser paradox. Proposition 4.1, also proved here, rules out a Pareto-dominated weak paradox with three alternatives for approval voting under either tie-break and for plurality voting with uniform tie-breaking. Claims 4.2 and 4.3 give examples of Pareto-dominated paradoxes with at least four alternatives under the specified systems. These are substantive structural results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or has another complexity classification.

A continuous study of these phenomena is imaginable: represent the electorate by masses of preference types and ask whether sequential strategic voting can produce a paradoxical outcome. But the paper’s voters act one at a time, and their order shapes the subgame-perfect equilibrium. A mass distribution over rankings does not capture that order. Treating same-ranking voters as a sequential bloc, or giving a continuum of voters turns in sequence, would require changing the game; the paper supplies no named computational result whose continuous analogue could be stated as the requested anchor.

So I would not nominate an anchor or claim a valid mirror question per the stated criteria. The paper’s explicit focus on small committees makes a large-population scenario especially hard to defend as a continuation of its problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Claim 3.1, Proposition 4.1, and Claims 4.2–4.3 establish when paradoxical outcomes can or cannot occur. They do not classify the complexity of deciding or computing anything. One could invent a problem such as “given a profile, does its unique SPE produce a paradox?”, but the paper neither poses nor studies it. That would be a new computational project prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The strongest rescue would ask whether the paradoxes in Claim 3.1 persist, or the impossibility in Proposition 4.1 holds, for a society represented by masses over rankings. But the mass distribution omits the order of play. The examples and proofs depend on particular voters acting at particular points in the sequence, including the last voter’s response. Adding position to the type preserves that information, but then voters at different positions are strategically distinct; the type support may grow with the electorate, removing the high-multiplicity compression.

Taking position to a continuum creates a more basic problem. Each voter has measure zero, so changing one ballot does not change the aggregate tally. The pivotal threats that drive SPE behavior disappear, and the paper’s unique-outcome equilibrium structure need not survive. Keeping each voter’s \(1/n\) influence preserves finite pivotal effects, especially at ties, but those effects depend on order and finite-scale details that the limiting mass distribution forgets. Treating a positive mass as a single actor instead gives a bloc game, with different strategic units and incentives.

That problem defeats the proposed anchors individually as well as collectively: the witnesses in Claim 3.1 and Claims 4.2–4.3 rely on sequential threats by particular voters; Proposition 4.1 is also a statement about the finite sequential game, not a computational classification. A plausible large legislature or public roll call could supply many voters, but it does not remove the dependence on position. Grouping voters into blocs changes the game; retaining their turns retains the atomistic order.

So I would reject all of these as anchors for ChoCo. The negative case is strongest at the programme’s stated gate: there is no computational result here to continuize, and the natural mass-based versions risk erasing the strategic feature the paper studies. I would not claim to have proved that no future re-modelling could be worthwhile. A continuum model of ordered blocs or large sequential electorates might inspire a separate project, but this paper does not provide a convincing continuous-population complexity mirror for it.
