The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Equation (1) gives the transaction-price CDF, and Section 3.4 uses it to calculate expected prices, but neither is a qualifying anchor under your rules. So there is no honest anchor-based positive case to make.

The closest candidate is a new problem one could ask about the paper’s model: ZI Expected-Price Approximation. An instance is a finite set of beliefs \(b_j\in(0,1)\), each with rational mass \(\mu_j\), where \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\), and an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\). Each unit of population has one of those beliefs; when called, an agent chooses to bid or ask with equal probability and draws an order price uniformly from \([0,b_j]\) or \([b_j,1]\), respectively. The question is to return an additive-\(\varepsilon\) approximation to the expected transaction price under the paper’s market mechanism. Its input has \(N\gg\tau\) agents sharing \(\tau\) belief types. For explicit finite support, I would expect this to be tractable: the order CDFs are piecewise linear, and the expectation is a one-dimensional integral. That is a plausible research question, but it is not a result the paper proves.

The problem is that the paper already represents agents by a distribution of beliefs and integrates over that distribution to derive prices. The proposed problem would study the computational cost of evaluating its existing model, rather than supply a continuous mirror of a discrete hardness result. Its population is already continuous in the relevant sense, and the paper reports no discrete problem whose high-multiplicity version could serve as an anchor. The paper’s continuous-belief model may be a useful precedent for ChoCo, but it is also the central obstacle to presenting it as a new mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the point that matters: this paper offers no computational complexity result to mirror. Its main formal result, Equation (1), is a formula for the transaction-price distribution. Section 3.4 uses that formula to compute expected prices by numerical integration. The paper does not analyze the running time of an algorithm for a discrete problem, or establish a hardness result for one. Its conclusion—that a simple agent model can reproduce observed market prices—is a claim about price formation, not computational complexity.

The proponent’s best candidate is to take finitely many belief types with rational masses and approximate the expected transaction price. But the paper already works at the level of a belief distribution \(f\): the aggregate order distributions and the expected price are computed from that law. In the proposed finite-support version, the number of agents disappears once their beliefs are represented by masses. The order CDFs are piecewise linear, so evaluating the expectation reduces to a one-dimensional integral of a piecewise rational function. That may invite a careful analysis of precision and bit complexity, but it offers no evident population-driven combinatorial problem, and it is not a complexity result from this paper.

A finite-population variant could ask for exact expected prices using finite-\(N\) order statistics, or bound the error between those prices and the paper’s distribution-level calculation. Those are plausible probability questions. They are not continuous mirrors of a computational result in the paper; taking the population limit returns to the distribution-based calculation the paper already uses. Likewise, one could invent a market intervention problem that moves mass between belief types to achieve a target price. That might become a worthwhile continuous optimization problem, but its decision problem and objective would be additions, not mirrors of this paper’s findings.

So the case against a continuous mirror of this paper is strong: there is no computational anchor, and the closest proposed problem largely asks how to evaluate an already distribution-level model. The honest limit is that this cannot establish that no useful high-multiplicity market problem could be invented from the paper’s setting. It establishes that such a problem would be a new research direction, not a continuous mirror of the results reported here.
