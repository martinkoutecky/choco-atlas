The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: exact computation of Shapley–Shubik power in a continuous electorate whose party shares are super-increasing. The paper has no named NP-hardness, P, or parameterized-complexity theorem of its own. Its best computational anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in the paper, which gives a closed-form way to compute the power values.

In that theorem, the parties’ weights satisfy \(w_1>\cdots>w_n>0\) and \(w_i>\sum_{j>i}w_j\) for every \(i\). For quota \(q\), let \(A(q)=\{a_0,\ldots,a_r\}\), with \(a_0<\cdots<a_r\), be the subset whose interval \((w(A(q)^-),w(A(q))]\) contains \(q\); \(A(q)^-\) is the immediately preceding subset in the paper’s ordering of subsets by their sums. The theorem gives the exact Shapley value: if \(i\notin A(q)\), then \(\phi_i(q)=\sum_{t:\,a_t>i}1/[a_t\binom{a_t-1}{t}]\); if \(i=a_s\in A(q)\), then \(\phi_i(q)=1/[a_s\binom{a_s-1}{s}]-\sum_{t:\,a_t>i}1/[a_t\binom{a_t-1}{t-1}]\). The paper also gives a greedy linear-time procedure for finding \(A(q)\), so this is an exact polynomial-time computation for the super-increasing case.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous-Electorate SI Shapley Computation. An instance consists of \(n\) parties and a continuous society with voter types \(T=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), where type \(i\) means “votes for party \(i\).” Each type has positive rational mass \(\mu_i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and the masses, ordered decreasingly, satisfy \(\mu_i>\sum_{j>i}\mu_j\). The instance also gives a rational quota \(q\in(0,1]\). A coalition \(S\) of parties passes a bill when \(\sum_{i\in S}\mu_i\ge q\). The task is to output the exact Shapley–Shubik power \(\phi_i\) of every party, meaning each party’s probability of being pivotal in a uniformly random ordering of the parties. There is no optimization action here: the solution is the vector of power values.

A plausible regime is a large electorate whose voters fall into a small set of ballot types, with \(N\gg n=\tau\). For example, the party-support shares might decline geometrically, \(\mu_{i+1}/\mu_i=\rho<1/2\). Each voter’s type is their party choice, and the mass of a type supplies that party’s normalized voting weight; the quota is expressed as a share of total weight. This is a direct population continuation of the paper’s plurality Balls and Bins setting, and the SI condition is exactly the kind of structure its exponential case studies. The authors could reasonably recognize it as their quota-and-power question with sampled counts replaced by exact population shares.

For this stated problem, I expect tractable: Theorem 6.1 supplies the formula, and \(A(q)\) can be found from the rational masses in polynomial time. The complexity is polynomial in the number of parties and the bit length of the masses and quota. This conclusion is limited to SI shares; it says nothing about arbitrary rational societies. It raises two natural follow-ups: what is the exact complexity for unrestricted mass vectors, and how do finite-electorate sampling fluctuations change the power values near subset-sum quota boundaries?

The mirror covers the SI characterization in Theorem 6.1 and the deterministic population version of the paper’s exponential case. It does not cover the uniform Balls and Bins fluctuation results or the paper as a whole. Its weakest point is that the paper already studies real-valued SI party weights, so the continuous electorate interpretation maps directly onto a model the authors have analyzed. It also leaves the parties finite and keeps the Shapley permutation over those parties. This is a plausible population mirror, but a modest one: it gives an exact continuous-population formulation of an existing tractable case, rather than showing that continuization removes hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

For the proposed anchor, the strongest objection is an exact collision: Theorem 6.1 already computes the Shapley values for continuous population shares. Given masses \(\mu_i\) and quota \(q\), set \(w_i=\mu_i\). For every party coalition \(S\), \(\sum_{i\in S}\mu_i\ge q\) defines exactly the same winning game as the weighted game in the paper. Scaling all weights and the quota by the same factor changes nothing, and the super-increasing condition is preserved. The paper allows real SI weights, gives the power formula, and supplies a greedy procedure for finding \(A(q)\). Calling those weights normalized voter masses introduces no new input, question, or computation.

A stronger variant might ask how the paper’s sampling results behave when voter counts are replaced by population shares. But the uniform case then has equal shares, so every party has Shapley value \(1/n\); the paper’s power disparities come from finite-sample fluctuations that this limit removes. In the exponential case, the limiting shares are SI, and the paper already connects their power values to the deterministic SI analysis. Keeping the fluctuations would make the question about finite-population sampling, which is the paper’s existing model rather than a continuous-society mirror.

The proposed mirror is sensible as a way to *describe* a large electorate, but that description is already covered by the theorem it claims to mirror. A broader question for unrestricted rational shares might be worthwhile on its own, but it would be the general weighted-voting power computation problem, not a new continuous version of Theorem 6.1. The negative case is strong for this anchor; it cannot rule out every adjacent research question one might formulate.
