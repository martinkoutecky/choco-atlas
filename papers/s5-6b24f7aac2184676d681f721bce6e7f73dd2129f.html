The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\subseteq[0,1]\) of valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and slot classes \(\ell=1,\ldots,q\) with weights \(w_\ell\) and per-capita supplies \(a_\ell\geq 0\), compute an assignment \(x_{t\ell}\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\ell=1}^q \theta_t w_\ell x_{t\ell}\), subject to \(\sum_\ell x_{t\ell}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t\ell}=a_\ell\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite distribution of valuation types \((\theta_t,\mu_t)\); each slot class has a weight and supply that scale with the population. The decision variables \(x_{t\ell}\) are masses assigned from valuation type \(t\) to slot class \(\ell\), and the objective is total welfare.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s welfare-maximizing allocation. It leaves the repeated-game truthfulness and fairness claims, the utility calculations in Theorems 4 and 5, and the simulations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can finite FEDoR allocations be shown to converge to this mass-allocation problem as the population and slot supplies grow proportionally?
  • Can the paper’s ex-ante fairness and truthfulness guarantees be meaningfully recovered in a type-mass model that retains repeated interaction?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper’s social-welfare maximizing allocation has a natural continuous-population version. The paper does not, however, contain a named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its numbered results concern mechanism properties, not whether a problem is in P, hard, or parameterized tractable; it gives no computational formulation that would qualify instead.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 1 (Social efficiency), proved here. It says that, under the paper’s utility \(u_i(d,\theta_i)=\theta_i\sum_j w_j\delta_{i d_j}\), FEDoR’s allocation based on truthful values has expected social utility at least that of any other mechanism under any strategies. Its allocation rule sorts values and assigns the highest values to the highest-weight slots. That is a substantive optimization result, but it is not a complexity classification. So it is a useful basis for a candidate mirror, not a qualifying computational anchor.

Call the candidate Continuous Weighted-Slot Allocation. An instance gives a finite support \(T\subseteq[0,1]\) of normalized value types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and slot-quality classes \(\ell=1,\ldots,q\), with weights \(w_\ell\) and rational per-capita supplies \(a_\ell\), where \(\sum_\ell a_\ell\leq1\). Choose masses \(x_{t\ell}\geq0\), where \(x_{t\ell}\) is the mass of value type \(t\) assigned a slot of quality \(\ell\), subject to
\[ \sum_\ell x_{t\ell}\leq\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t x_{t\ell}=a_\ell. \]
The question is to maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\ell=1}^q \theta_t w_\ell x_{t\ell}. \]
A solution is a feasible assignment of masses attaining that maximum.

The regime could be a large bandwidth or compute pool serving many interchangeable users or processes. In each round, a user’s normalized value \(\theta_t\) describes their value for service, and \(w_\ell\) describes a service tier. If there are thousands of users but only dozens of value classes and a few service tiers, \(n\) is large while the support size \(\tau\) and \(q\) stay moderate. This is a per-round population of realized values; the paper’s fairness across named agents is ex ante, over their repeated independent draws. The supply \(a_\ell\) scales with population size, since keeping a fixed number of slots while \(n\) grows would make their per-capita supply vanish.

This problem should be tractable: it is a transportation LP with \(O(\tau q)\) variables, solvable in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length. Its optimizer assigns higher value mass to higher-weight slots, capturing the allocation logic behind Theorem 1. A large resource pool with replicated slot classes is the co-modification needed to get a meaningful limit; it remains recognizably the paper’s resource-allocation question.

The mirror covers only the welfare-maximizing allocation component. It does not continuize the full FEDoR mechanism: the GoF test, individual histories, incentives, and truthfulness across repeated rounds are outside this problem. Nor does it reveal a hardness result dissolving in the continuum—the paper’s underlying sorting allocation is already simple. Further questions would be whether fairness can be stated in a type-mass model while preserving the paper’s ex-ante guarantee, and whether the repeated strategic mechanism has a tractable high-multiplicity formulation.

My weakest point is also the anchor issue: under the required standard, this paper offers no qualifying named computational result. The allocation mirror is plausible and precise, but it is anchored only in Theorem 1’s optimization claim, not in a complexity result. If the anchor requirement is applied strictly, that is the correct limit of the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the paper’s fit for ChoCo, not a claim that no useful model can be made. The paper has no named computational result: Theorem 1 is a social-welfare dominance claim, not a complexity or algorithmic result about an input-defined computational problem. The allocation rule sorts reported values, but the paper does not study the complexity of finding an allocation, or of bribery, control, or another computational task. Under the programme’s stated gate, that is a decisive mismatch.

The proposed weighted-slot allocation is nevertheless a credible model. One could even improve it by using the paper’s uniform distribution over values directly: choose measurable fractions of each value type for each slot class, subject to per-capita supply constraints, and maximize expected welfare. That preserves the allocation objective, and scaling slot supply with population is an acceptable co-modification. I would not dismiss this mirror because its answer is simple or because it uses fractional mass. The problem is that it supplies a new computational formulation around a welfare theorem; it does not continue a computational problem the paper posed.

Trying to preserve more of FEDoR exposes a separate difficulty. Its fairness and truthfulness claims concern persistent, named players across repeated rounds. A distribution over current values omits those identities and histories. In an atomless model, one player’s deviation has zero effect on aggregate mass, so individual incentive constraints lose their force; fairness across named players is likewise not represented by the value distribution. Restoring identities and histories brings back the finite-agent repeated mechanism rather than a population-level computational problem.

So the negative verdict is strong under ChoCo’s computational-result criterion, but not universal in the mathematical sense. If the programme accepts a newly formulated welfare-optimization problem as a sufficient anchor, the continuous weighted-slot model survives, and I do not have an honest reason to rule it out.
