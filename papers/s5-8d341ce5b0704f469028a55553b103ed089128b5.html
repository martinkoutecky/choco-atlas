The continuous mirror question

Let \(\mu\) describe a unit-mass population with at most \(\tau\) true types \(t=(A_t,v_t)\), where \(A_t\subseteq J\) is public and \(v_t\) is private; the mechanism knows public eligibility-class masses but not masses split by private value. An adversarial stream of \(H\) batches \((j_h,q_h)\), with \(j_h\in J\), arrives online; the mechanism sees current reports, irrevocably assigns up to \(q_h\) mass to unmatched eligible agents, and sets payments immediately, while each individual receives at most one indivisible item. Require ex-post truthfulness for each individual under every random seed, and measure expected welfare against the offline maximum-weight matching of the true population and supplies. What is the minimum worst-case competitive ratio for randomized mechanisms, and can it be achieved in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|J|\), \(H\), and the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A continuous population of mass one over complete types \((A_t,v_t)\), with public eligibility and privately reported values; per-capita supplies arrive in batches, aggregate allocations may be fractional, and each individual’s bundle remains integral. The mechanism receives reports online and must preserve the paper’s individual ex-post truthfulness and prompt-payment requirements.

The objection that survived

The proposed known masses for cohorts defined by private value would reveal private information; the mirror needs to specify that only public eligibility masses are known and private values arrive through individual reports.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the prompt randomized truthful competitive-ratio question for non-myopic agents with public eligibility. It leaves the paper’s myopic, tardy, private-edge, and ex-ante results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(\tau\) and \(|J|\), is the best competitive ratio bounded independently of \(H\)?
  • What is the optimal ratio as a function of the number of complete types?
  • Can a polynomial-time mechanism implement the continuous allocation while preserving individual ex-post truthfulness?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a fluid version of the paper’s prompt, truthful online matching problem. The regime I find convincing is a large advertising market: many advertisers are present throughout a stream of ad opportunities; each can receive at most one, has a publicly known set of eligible ad categories, and privately values receiving one. Advertisers sharing both eligibility and value form a cohort. The market may have millions of advertisers and opportunities but only a small number \(\tau\) of cohorts and a fixed number of ad categories. Cohort masses and opportunity supplies are measured per capita. This keeps the paper’s unit-demand welfare problem and online decisions, while letting the population—and the supply that scales with it—be continuous.

The anchor is Theorem 5 in the published *Algorithmica* numbering (Theorem 4 in the ICALP version). It is proved in the paper: for non-myopic buyers with public graph edges and private values, *every prompt randomized truthful mechanism has competitive ratio at least \(\Omega(\log \nu/\log\log \nu)\), where \(\nu=\min(m,n)\).* Here truthfulness is ex-post, and the lower bound is established using Yao’s principle and the paper’s own Lemma 3. [The published paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-023-01202-3)

I would name the mirror Prompt Truthful Fluid Matching. An instance has a finite set \(J\) of item categories, at most \(\tau\) agent types \(t\), and a society of total mass one. Type \(t\) has public eligible categories \(A_t\subseteq J\), private value \(v_t\), and mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A finite stream has rounds \(h=1,\ldots,H\); at round \(h\), a category \(j_h\) arrives with rational supply \(q_h\), where \(\sum_h q_h=1\). The mechanism sees each arrival and its supply when it occurs, not the future stream. It allocates that supply immediately among unmatched eligible agents and sets any payment at allocation. Each individual still receives either zero or one indivisible item; only the aggregate mass allocated from a cohort can be fractional. Agents know the future stream and have the paper’s ex-post truthfulness requirement: for every realization of the mechanism’s randomness, truthful value reports maximize utility. The public eligibility information and reports arrive according to the paper’s online model.

The benchmark is the offline maximum-welfare matching, expressible as the LP maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}v_t x_{t,h}\) subject to \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le q_h\), \(\sum_h x_{t,h}\le\mu_t\), and \(x_{t,h}=0\) whenever \(j_h\notin A_t\). The problem is to find a randomized online direct mechanism—with an allocation rule and prompt payment rule—that is ex-post truthful and achieves the smallest worst-case competitive ratio against this benchmark. A concrete target is to determine whether, for fixed \(\tau\) and \(|J|\), such a ratio can be bounded independently of \(H\), and whether the mechanism can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau, |J|, H\), and the rational input bit length.

I expect this mirror to be Class A-like for fixed \(\tau\) and \(|J|\): removing scarcity at the level of identical agents should allow a constant competitive guarantee, with the fluid allocation state tracked by only \(\tau\) cohort masses. That is a conjecture about the mirror’s answer, not a consequence of Theorem 5; the paper’s combination of adversarial arrivals, prompt payments, and non-myopic incentives remains central. Further questions are the best achievable ratio, how it grows as the number of complete types grows, and whether finite-population mechanisms converge to a fluid mechanism while preserving ex-post truthfulness.

The weak point is specific: Theorem 5’s lower-bound construction is not itself a fixed-support high-multiplicity example. Although it uses only \(k\) value tiers, each buyer’s desired set is a prefix ending at that buyer’s rank, so the complete agent types proliferate with \(n\). The theorem therefore does not establish its logarithmic lower bound in this mirror. I still think the mirror is one the authors would recognize: it asks their same welfare, online allocation, and truthful-mechanism question for a plausible cohort market, while treating per-capita supply as part of the population continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to this anchor is that Theorem 5’s lower-bound construction does not persist in the proposed high-multiplicity regime. It uses only \(k\) value levels, but each buyer’s eligibility set is a different prefix of the item sequence. Thus the complete buyer types grow with \(n\). With a fixed set of ad categories and a fixed number of eligibility classes, that construction disappears. The theorem gives no reason to expect its \(\Omega(\log \nu/\log\log \nu)\) bound in the proposed market; the mirror’s real question is whether the bound survives after removing the growing neighborhood structure.

There is also a modeling problem in the stated mirror: it assigns each cohort a mass and a *private* value. If the mechanism is given the mass of each value cohort, it is given information the paper treats as private. If those masses are hidden, the mechanism needs a separate model for the stream of reports and for what it knows about the population. Scaling item supplies into positive-mass batches also changes the item-at-a-time process: allocations can now split a batch immediately. Those choices need to be made explicit before this is a precise mirror.

These objections weaken the proposed formulation, but they do not defeat the better version. A plausible repair is a nonatomic population with known masses of public eligibility classes, private values reported by individuals, and per-capita item supplies arriving in batches. That preserves the online welfare and incentive problem, while making fixed-support cohorts sensible for a large advertising market. Whether prompt truthfulness becomes easier there is a meaningful computational question, even if the paper’s lower-bound proof offers little guidance.

So the honest negative case is weak: the anchor’s specific lower-bound witness relies on support that grows with the population, and the proposed model needs sharper information assumptions. I cannot turn those points into a convincing case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The repaired fixed-support problem survives them.
