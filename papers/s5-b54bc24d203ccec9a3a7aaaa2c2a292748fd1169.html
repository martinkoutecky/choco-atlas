The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), predictor scores \(f\), and \(c\in(0,1)\), choose a rule \(\Pi\) that sees \((T,\mu,f)\) and returns a lottery \(q^\Pi\) over types. For unknown type utilities \(u_t\in[0,1]\) with \(\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s>0\), define \(C_\mu(f,u)=\frac{\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s(\mathbf{1}\{f_t>f_s\}+\frac12\mathbf{1}\{f_t=f_s\})}{\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s}\). What is the maximum, over such rules, of the worst-case advantage \(\inf_{T,\mu,f,u:\,C_\mu(f,u)\ge c}\left(\sum_t q_t^\Pi u_t-\sum_t\mu_tu_t\right)\) over uniform selection from the population?

The model it lives in

A large waitlist is represented by patient types carrying mass \(\mu_t\), with type-constant predictor score \(f_t\) and donor-specific utility \(u_t\). One donor still goes to one patient: the policy chooses a type lottery \(q\), and its expected utility is \(\sum_t q_tu_t\); the population-lottery baseline is \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s single-donor, top-candidate allocation problem. The paper proves no NP-hardness, tractability, or parameterized-complexity result. Its best anchors are instead numbered guarantees about what allocation policies can promise from a predictor. If “anchor” is restricted to results in a complexity class, this paper has none.

Consider a national transplant service allocating one standardized donor type among a large waitlist. Suppose the deployed policy uses a finite vocabulary of complete patient profiles: every fact relevant to compatibility, predicted survival, and allocation is part of the profile. Let \(T\) be the profiles present, \(\tau=|T|\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the eligible waitlist with profile \(t\). The regime is \(n\) patients with \(n_t=n\mu_t\) patients per profile and \(n\gg\tau\). A type’s utility \(u_t\in[0,1]\) is its donor-specific restricted graft-survival utility, scaled to the chosen horizon; \(f_t\) is the predictor’s score. One donor still goes to one patient: a deterministic policy selects a type, or a randomized policy chooses a lottery \(q\) over types.

The mirror of C-index is the mass-weighted pairwise ranking score. For profile pairs with \(u_t>u_s\), the pair is weighted by \(\mu_t\mu_s\); it earns full credit if \(f_t>f_s\), half credit for a predicted tie, and none otherwise. Thus \(C_\mu(f,u)=\frac{\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s(\mathbf 1\{f_t>f_s\}+\frac12\mathbf 1\{f_t=f_s\})}{\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s}\). A lottery \(q\) has expected utility \(\sum_t q_tu_t\); uniform selection from the waitlist corresponds to \(q=\mu\), with utility \(\sum_t\mu_tu_t\).

My lead is Continuous C-index Robust Random Allocation, anchored by Proposition 2, proved in this paper’s Appendix B. The proposition says: for any algorithm selecting one candidate based on predictor \(\hat f\), including a randomized algorithm, and any \(c\in(0,1)\), there is a candidate set and predictor with C-index at least \(c\) on which the algorithm cannot guarantee expected utility greater than uniform random selection. The mirror asks whether an allocation rule can guarantee a positive advantage over the population lottery for every continuous society whose predictor has mass-weighted C-index at least \(c\). Formally, for a rule \(\Pi\) mapping observed \((T,\mu,f)\) to a lottery \(q^\Pi\), its worst-case advantage is \(\inf_{(T,\mu,f,u):\,C_\mu(f,u)\ge c}\bigl(\sum_t q^\Pi_tu_t-\sum_t\mu_tu_t\bigr)\). The problem is to find a rule maximizing this value. Uniform selection achieves zero; the mirrored result says no rule can guarantee more, so the optimum worst-case advantage is zero.

There is a direct high-multiplicity construction behind that expectation. Take \(\tau\) equally weighted profiles and replicate each profile across \(M\) interchangeable patients, with \(M\gg\tau\). The support stays at \(\tau\), while the population has \(\tau M\) patients. Put the profile selected most often by the policy at the bottom of the true utility ordering and order the other profiles correctly by the predictor. As \(\tau\) grows, the misordered mass of pairs shrinks while the policy’s worst-case advantage over uniform selection does not improve. This is a transfer of the proposition’s counterexample structure to type masses, not a new claim proved by the paper.

A useful second anchor is Continuous C-index Top-One Approximation, based on Proposition 1, also proved in Appendix B. Proposition 1 says that for every deterministic single-candidate algorithm and every \(c,\rho\in(0,1)\), there is a candidate set and a predictor with C-index at least \(c\) for which the algorithm’s selected utility is at most a \(\rho\) fraction of optimal utility. The mirror asks whether a deterministic type-selection policy can guarantee \(u_t\ge\rho\max_s u_s\) for every society with \(C_\mu(f,u)\ge c\). The equal-mass replicated-profile construction gives the same negative guarantee: no such universal policy exists. This anchor is narrower than Proposition 2 because it treats deterministic policies, but it gives the sharper statement that the approximation ratio can be made arbitrarily small.

Both mirrors fall on the transfer side of the programme’s trichotomy: the negative guarantee already appears on finite candidate sets and survives when each candidate is represented by a large cohort of one type. This is not an NP-hardness transfer; the paper establishes an impossibility of a performance guarantee, not a complexity-class lower bound. Complexity questions follow naturally: for a fixed \((\mu,f,c)\), how hard is it to compute the best robust lottery, and can a mass-based version of the paper’s NDCG guarantee be defined and analyzed for \(k>1\)?

The authors should recognize the lead mirror: it keeps their core decision—one donor, one selected patient—and asks whether a pairwise predictor-quality guarantee can support a policy-quality guarantee. It covers only Propositions 1 and 2’s single-donor C-index results. It does not claim to continuize their censored-data estimators, NDCG bootstrapping, empirical experiments, or sequential donor matching.

The weakest point is the type regime. A finite operational profile vocabulary can support many patients per type, but the paper’s models use rich patient covariates, and exact profile repetition may be scarce if those inputs distinguish nearly every patient. There is also a technical boundary: Propositions 1 and 2 use fully observed candidate utilities. The clean mass-weighted construction applies to the type-level or deterministic-utility subcase; extending it to stochastic within-type survival and right-censored observations is further work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest objection is that this paper has no result about computational complexity. Propositions 1 and 2 are impossibility results about the guarantees a policy can obtain from C-index; Theorem 1 bounds allocation utility using NDCG. None classifies a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized, or analyzes an algorithm’s running time. The paper’s contribution is statistical and decision-theoretic: how predictor quality relates to allocation quality. That is a real computational setting, but it is not yet a ChoCo complexity result to continuize.

The proposed Continuous C-index Robust Random Allocation is a coherent population restatement of Proposition 2. With equal-mass types, repeat each type into a cohort and apply the proposition to the resulting finite candidate list. The individual C-index then equals the mass-weighted type-pair score, and uniform selection from the list becomes the lottery \(q=\mu\). The same construction handles Continuous C-index Top-One Approximation from Proposition 1. So both anchors survive as guarantees about a type-based population model, but neither yields a new algorithmic problem: they transfer the paper’s impossibility statement to weighted notation. They are two benchmarks for the same predictor-quality question, not separate computational contributions.

The maximin formulation does not supply the missing complexity result. Its input to a policy is \((T,\mu,f)\), while the true utilities are hidden and range over an adversarial class. The paper’s propositions already quantify over algorithms and adversarial utilities; turning the conclusion into an optimization over policy lotteries does not by itself define a new runtime or complexity question. A fixed-instance robust-lottery problem could be studied, but it would add a new policy-design task and would need its own formulation and computational anchor.

A stronger mirror from the paper’s NDCG result runs into the same issue. For one donor, NDCG@1 is the utility of the predicted top candidate divided by the best candidate’s utility, so Corollary 1 states that allocation guarantee directly. Duplicating patient types leaves that ratio unchanged. In an atomless population, a single top-ranked patient has zero mass; using a positive-mass rank cutoff can repair the definition, but then the question concerns a lottery over a population segment rather than the paper’s single-donor choice. That may be a worthwhile new model, but the paper gives no complexity result for it.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here: large waitlists and recurring clinical profiles make the population regime credible. The stronger negative case is narrower. The proposed mirrors preserve the paper’s predictor-versus-policy guarantees, but they do not turn those guarantees into the computational questions ChoCo is organized to chart. A dynamic, many-donor type-mass model might create such a question; the paper only gestures toward sequential matching and establishes no result to anchor that extension. Thus the case against a *worthwhile ChoCo mirror* is fairly strong on scope, though weaker against the broader claim that these results could inspire a separate continuous allocation project.
