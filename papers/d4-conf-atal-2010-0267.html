The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no qualifying named computational result: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Its 88–99% efficiency findings are empirical, and its welfare benchmark appears as Equation (1), not as a named result. I can’t honestly supply an anchor in the form you requested.

The strongest positive candidate is a continuous-population version of the paper’s day-ahead welfare benchmark. Consider a large regional grid with many households and small generators, grouped by feeder location and shared demand or generation characteristics. A type would include an agent’s node, buyer or seller role, fixed demand, capacity, and cost-curve parameters. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with those characteristics. With millions of participants and a relatively small set of feeder and device profiles, \(\tau\) could plausibly be far smaller than the population. Line capacities and flows would be expressed in matching per-capita units.

Call the candidate Type-Aggregate Day-Ahead Welfare Maximization. An instance consists of a finite grid, rational type masses summing to one, each type’s node and demand or generation parameters, and the grid’s DC-flow and line-capacity data. Choose aggregate flexible consumption or generation \(Q_t\) by type, voltage angles \(\delta_n\), and line flows \(f_\ell\), subject to type capacities, nodal power balance (including fixed demand), and the DC-flow equations \(f_\ell=(\delta_u-\delta_v)/r_\ell\). For example, a buyer type with mass \(\mu_t\) and per-agent marginal value \(c_t-d_tq\) contributes total value \(c_tQ_t-\frac{d_tQ_t^2}{2\mu_t}\); a seller type with marginal cost \(x_t+y_tq\) contributes cost \(x_tQ_t+\frac{y_tQ_t^2}{2\mu_t}\). Maximize buyer value minus seller costs and the paper’s integrated transmission costs. A solution is an optimal type-level allocation, voltage-angle vector, and secure line-flow vector, or a report that the instance is infeasible.

I would expect this candidate to be tractable for the paper’s convex cost regime, through convex optimization. It raises the programme’s natural follow-up questions: the running time in the grid size, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length; exact versus approximate solutions; and how the aggregate optimum relates to the finite-agent benchmark. But that expectation is an analysis of the proposed mirror, not a result proved in this paper.

The case is plausible because the authors explicitly compute this welfare benchmark and compare their market to it. The paper already makes quantities, flows, and cost curves continuous, but keeps the population of traders discrete; the mirror changes that population dimension. This candidate covers the benchmark only. It does not cover the paper’s main strategic CDA mechanism or online balancing scheme, where order arrival, individual orderbooks, and misreported preferences matter.

That limitation is the weakest point: authors might see the welfare problem as a benchmark for their contribution rather than the contribution itself. A full-market mirror would need to represent trader strategies, arrival patterns, and market state in each type, which could make the support too large and the model less convincing. So there is a concrete, author-recognizable continuous question here, but without a qualifying named anchor it cannot carry the requested anchor-based case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper offers no named computational result to anchor a mirror on. It presents a market mechanism, trading strategies, and empirical efficiency results. Its day-ahead welfare optimization is a benchmark, described as a convex optimization problem and implemented with CPLEX; the paper does not classify its complexity or claim a new algorithmic result for it. So the proposed welfare problem is a new question suggested by the paper, not a continuous counterpart of a computational result the paper establishes.

That leaves the welfare benchmark as the only serious candidate. Its type model is plausible: households and small generators could share types defined by feeder, role, and cost or demand profile, with capacities expressed per capita. Aggregating identical agents into masses preserves the surplus and balance constraints. The paper’s focus on strategic bidding, orderbooks, and balancing does not erase that candidate: one mirror is enough, and the benchmark is part of the authors’ analysis.

The negative case is stronger against a mirror of the paper’s main market mechanism. A mass version would have to represent offer timing, orderbook state, and strategic behavior. Types could include these details, but doing so might make the support approach the number of agents; coarser types could change the market protocol’s behavior. That makes the full-market mirror less direct, though it does not rule out a sensible cohort-based model.

I can therefore defeat the proposed anchor as an anchor to a result in this paper, but I cannot honestly defeat it as a potentially worthwhile continuous question. The paper’s lack of a named computational result is a strong limitation on the case *for* a mirror; it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The universal negative is weak here: the aggregate welfare problem survives, and its likely tractability is not a reason to dismiss it under the programme’s criteria.
