The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a qualifying anchor for a continuous computational mirror. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered Results 1–3 are empirical findings, so treating them as the required computational anchors would misstate what the paper proves.

The closest point of contact is Result 1: over the experiment, people originally in high-contribution groups were more likely to vote for informal sanctions in closed societies than open ones; for people from low-contribution groups, the pattern reversed. That is a meaningful population-level phenomenon, but it is not a computational result.

A possible follow-on would represent agents by complete behavioral types—including their contribution, voting, and migration choices—and assign each type a mass \(\mu_t\). One could then ask how the mass voting for informal sanctions differs between open and closed communities, conditional on the initial distribution of contributions. Large workplaces or municipalities might supply a plausible many-agent setting. But the paper gives no structural model of those choices: it estimates behavior from a laboratory experiment with ten subjects per session. Turning this into a problem for a prover would require adding a behavioral or equilibrium model that the authors do not study. That would be a new research question, not a defensible mirror of a named computational result here.

So there are no anchors and no continuous problem to state *per anchor*. The strongest positive observation is that the paper studies voting and migration at the group level, where population shares could be natural quantities. The decisive weakness is that its contribution is experimental evidence about those quantities, not a computational problem whose complexity changes under high multiplicity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper reports experimental findings, not computational results. Its numbered Results 1–3 describe observed voting, contribution, punishment, and migration patterns. They do not define an algorithmic problem, say what its input and output are, or establish a complexity or algorithmic claim. That is a serious category gap for a programme about the computational landscape.

Result 1 is the best foothold. A natural continuous version would put mass on complete behavioral types and compare the mass voting for informal sanctions in open and closed communities, conditional on initial cooperation. That could fit a large workplace or municipality. But if the types’ behavior is given, the vote share is just an aggregate of their masses. To predict how opening a community changes the vote, one must supply a model of beliefs, migration, and behavior over time. The paper estimates those relationships from laboratory choices; it does not specify that model. Adding one could be worthwhile, but it would be a new theoretical problem rather than a continuous version of Result 1’s empirical estimand.

Results 2 and 3 do not provide a stronger computational anchor. A continuous population could naturally represent average contributions or migration flows, including fractional masses. Yet the reported differences in contributions and the roughly \(20\%\) migration rate are descriptive findings, not optimization or complexity results. Informal punishment also depends on directed actions between group members and their histories. Those details could be captured by richer types or interaction states, so identity alone is not a decisive objection. But doing so still leaves the central gap: the paper supplies no rule for predicting those actions in a large population. Treating observed rates as fixed inputs makes aggregation straightforward; deriving them requires a new behavioral model.

The case against is strongest on fit with this programme, not on the broader claim that no continuous model could be interesting. The paper’s settings and motivation make population shares plausible, and a continuum game of institutional choice and migration could be a sensible research direction. But without a computational result in the paper to mirror, that direction would need its own strategic assumptions, solution concept, and computational question. I would therefore reject a continuous computational mirror of this paper while acknowledging that a related continuum theory might be worth pursuing on its own.
