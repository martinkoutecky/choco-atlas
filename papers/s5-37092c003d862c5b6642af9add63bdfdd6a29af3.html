The continuous mirror question

Given finite rational supports \(V_s,V_b\subseteq[0,1]\) and masses \(\mu_s,\mu_b\), let \(F_s(p)=\sum_{s\le p}\mu_s(s)\). For each buyer type \(b\ge r\), use a fixed tie rule to choose \(p_r(b)\in\arg\max_{p\in[r,b]}(b-p)F_s(p)\); for \(b<r\), do not trade. With type-pair mass \(\mu_s(s)\mu_b(b)\), let \(W(r)\) be aggregate welfare when a pair trades exactly when \(s\le p_r(b)\), and let \(W^*=\sum_{s,b}\mu_s(s)\mu_b(b)\max\{s,b\}\). Find a reserve maximizing \(W(r)\), or one satisfying \(W(r)\ge 0.746W^*\).

The model it lives in

A cohort of independent bilateral-trade desks. Seller and buyer valuation types have rational masses \(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_b\); desks have pair types with product masses \(\mu_s(s)\mu_b(b)\). Each desk uses the paper’s buyer-offering mechanism with a common reserve, and the objective is aggregate welfare.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the §5 guarantee of at least \(0.746\) and the §4 seller-only guarantee of \(0.71004\); it leaves the §4 upper bound of \(0.7159\), the mechanism characterization, and the auxiliary lemmas unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an optimal reserve, or one attaining the paper’s \(0.746\) guarantee, be found in time polynomial in the support sizes \(\tau_s,\tau_b\) and encoding length \(L\), including all offer ties and reserve breakpoints?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a cohort limit of repeated bilateral trades. Imagine a large market made up of independent one-item trading desks. Each desk has one seller and one buyer. Sellers fall into valuation types \(s\in V_s\), buyers into valuation types \(b\in V_b\), and everyone of a given type has the same value and follows the same rules. In the limit, \(\mu_s(s)\) and \(\mu_b(b)\) are fractions of the seller and buyer cohorts. Pair desks so that mass \(\mu_s(s)\mu_b(b)\) has type pair \((s,b)\). This is a deterministic population model: the masses describe how many desks have each pair of types, rather than a lottery over the values of two named people.

The paper does not supply printed theorem numbers, and none of its results establishes a complexity class such as P or NP-hardness. Its relevant anchors are approximation guarantees for a mechanism. I cite the bare theorem declarations by section rather than inventing numbers.

My lead anchor is the Main Result, formalized as the Theorem in §5 and proved here. It says that for every pair of seller and buyer value distributions, there is a reserve \(r\) such that the buyer-offering mechanism achieves expected welfare at least \(0.746\) times first-best welfare. The buyer offers a profit-maximizing price of at least \(r\); the seller accepts when its value is no greater than the offer. The appendix’s factor-revealing LP certifies the \(0.746\) guarantee.

Its continuous mirror is Product-Cohort Reserve Welfare. The input is finite rational value sets \(V_s,V_b\subseteq[0,1]\), rational masses \(\mu_s,\mu_b\), and their encoding length \(L\). Let \(F_s(p)=\sum_{s\le p}\mu_s(s)\). For \(b\ge r\), let \(p_r(b)\) be a profit-maximizing offer in \([r,b]\), with a fixed tie rule; its profit is \((b-p)F_s(p)\). For \(b<r\), there is no trade. The aggregate welfare is

\[ W(r)=\sum_{s\in V_s}\sum_{b\in V_b}\mu_s(s)\mu_b(b) \begin{cases} b,&s\le p_r(b),\\ s,&s>p_r(b), \end{cases} \]

where a no-trade desk contributes \(s\). First-best welfare is

\[ W^*=\sum_{s,b}\mu_s(s)\mu_b(b)\max\{s,b\}. \]

The problem is to find a common reserve \(r\in[0,1]\) with \(W(r)\ge0.746W^*\), or to maximize \(W(r)/W^*\). The theorem supplies the existence guarantee; it does not give a running-time bound for finding \(r\).

I expect this finite-support version to be Class A. With a fixed tie rule, a buyer’s best offer need only be the reserve or a seller-support value. As \(r\) varies, those choices change at reserve/support crossings and buyer indifference points. A finite breakpoint search therefore looks plausible, with running time expressed in \(\tau_s,\tau_b,L\). Proving that bound would be a new result. The mirror stays close to the paper’s problem: the same local mechanism runs at each desk, and aggregate welfare is exactly the integral of the paper’s per-value welfare over the cohort masses.

A second, distinct anchor is the Theorem in §4, proved here. It says that for every seller distribution there is a reserve, chosen using only that distribution, for which the buyer-offering mechanism obtains at least \(0.71004\) of conditional optimal welfare for *every* buyer value \(v_b\), regardless of the buyer’s distribution.

Its mirror is Seller-Only Robust Reserve. Given seller masses \(\mu_s\), choose \(r\) to maximize the worst conditional welfare ratio over \(b\in[0,1]\):

\[ \inf_{b\in[0,1]} \frac{\text{welfare under reserve }r\text{ against buyer value }b} {\sum_s\mu_s(s)\max\{s,b\}}. \]

The question is whether one can return a reserve attaining at least \(0.71004\). I also expect a tractable finite-support version: the seller support partitions the price and buyer-value ranges into finitely many regions, though the breakpoint argument needs to cover the worst buyer value as well as reserve changes.

The cohort regime is concrete: many replicated desks, \(N\) much larger than the number of distinct seller and buyer valuations, with each type cohort sharing its valuation and the common market rules. Rational masses can be represented exactly by a sufficiently large finite cohort. For the main theorem, the product masses preserve the paper’s independent seller and buyer distributions. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their setting aggregated over many identical bilateral markets.

The weak point is that the paper’s distributions describe uncertainty about a single seller and buyer; they do not themselves assert that a large population is present. Replicating independent bilateral markets gives a faithful aggregate interpretation, but adds no cross-market interaction. So this is a credible high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s welfare-approximation question, not evidence that population continuization creates a new hardness landscape here. The paper’s contribution remains its mechanism guarantee; the mirror’s computational question is whether a reserve with that guarantee can be found efficiently from compact type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no theorem about the complexity of an input-encoded computational problem. Its main results in §§4–5 are approximation guarantees: a good reserve exists, and a factor-revealing LP certifies the guarantee. A continuous mirror could add a reserve-finding problem, but that would be a new algorithmic question layered onto a distributional mechanism-design result.

The proposed replicated desks are the strongest population scenario, and they are plausible. With anonymous random matching, each desk faces exactly the paper’s interim model: seller values follow \(\mathcal F_s\), buyer values follow \(\mathcal F_b\), and the buyer chooses an offer using the same expected-profit calculation. Aggregate welfare is then just the sum of the desks’ welfare, so the ratio and reserve guarantee are unchanged as the cohort grows. If pairings are fixed and known, buyers can condition offers on their particular sellers, which changes the mechanism. If sellers and buyers interact in a pooled market, that is a different problem. The faithful cohort model therefore replicates the original problem without adding population-level structure.

For the §5 Main Result, one can still ask how to compute a reserve from finite rational supports. That is a legitimate question, but its difficulty appears to come from a one-dimensional search over reserve values, not from making society continuous. With finite seller support, a buyer’s best offer can be taken from the reserve or the seller support; as the reserve varies, offer choices change at finitely many breakpoints. This suggests a direct finite search. The paper’s \(0.746\) theorem already applies to arbitrary distributions, so the cohort interpretation contributes no new guarantee or population-dependent computation.

The §4 seller-only theorem is even closer to a direct restatement: its guarantee already holds for every buyer value, regardless of the buyer’s distribution. Averaging that pointwise guarantee over a buyer cohort preserves it immediately. Optimizing the reserve for the worst buyer value could be useful, but with finite seller support the offer choices and welfare expressions have finitely many pieces. That again looks like reserve computation for the original model, rather than a computational question created by a continuous population.

This negative case is not airtight. A large market of replicated bilateral trades is a credible setting, and computing a good reserve from an encoded distribution could be worth doing. The case against is that both anchors survive replication unchanged: the aggregate is a rescaling of the paper’s expected welfare, while the plausible new computation is reserve search with no apparent role for high multiplicity or continuous-optimization machinery.
