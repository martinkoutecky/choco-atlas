What the mirror covers

The case anchors only Theorem 2's finite two-player transfer minimization; it leaves the incomplete-information theorems and the mechanism-design impossibility discussion alone. The proposed continuous model does not carry that result over.

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror I believe in is Continuous Fair Dominant-Action Adjustment. A municipal school-placement system is a plausible regime: hundreds of thousands of families, but far fewer complete types, where a type includes a family’s feasible schools, payoff function, and desired school. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of families of type \(t\), and let \(x_{t,a}\) be the mass of type \(t\) choosing school \(a\). The types are complete in the programme’s sense: families in a type are identical in every feature the model uses.

The paper’s strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved here, not cited from elsewhere. For a finite two-player normal-form game and a desired action profile, the adjustment constructed in the proof of Theorem 1 has minimum *infimum* general transfer among adjustments that make the profile strictly dominant while satisfying fairness, individual rationality, and implementability. General transfer is the sum, over action profiles and players, of the absolute adjustments. The paper notes that the feasible transfer values need not form a closed set, so the infimum need not be attained. This is an optimization result, though the paper does not state a complexity class or formal running-time bound.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The instance gives a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), finite action sets \(A_t\), a target action \(a_t^\star\) for each type, and rational piecewise-affine payoff functions \(u_t(a,x)\). The aggregate states are
\[ \mathcal X=\left\{x\geq 0:\sum_{a\in A_t}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\text{ for every }t\right\}. \]
A solution is a finite-description, piecewise-affine transfer schedule \(v_t(a,x)\). It must make \(a_t^\star\) strictly better than every other action for every type and every \(x\in\mathcal X\); an individual deviation leaves the aggregate state unchanged because each agent has zero mass. It must also satisfy pointwise budget balance, \(\sum_{t,a}x_{t,a}v_t(a,x)=0\), and the paper’s fairness and individual-rationality requirements in population form: target-action agents receive nonnegative adjustments at every state and at least as much as agents taking other actions; and each type’s average adjustment over actions and normalized volume on \(\mathcal X\) is equal across types and nonnegative. I would also require zero adjustment at the all-target state, matching the paper’s stated feature that the desired profile itself needs no adjustment. The objective is to find the infimum of
\[ \int_{\mathcal X}\sum_{t,a}x_{t,a}|v_t(a,x)|\,d\lambda(x), \]
where \(\lambda\) is normalized volume on the aggregate-state polytope. A solution reports that infimum and either a schedule attaining it or schedules approaching it arbitrarily closely.

This mirrors Theorem 2’s underlying question—how much gross transfer is needed to make a fixed action profile strictly dominant—while making the population the continuous object. Actions remain discrete; the continuous variables are the type masses and their distribution across actions. The population could be large while \(\tau=|T|\) is comparatively small. This is not a claim that Theorem 2 already solves the continuous problem; it is a concrete extension of the authors’ incentive-adjustment question to a setting in which per-capita payoffs respond to aggregate enrollment.

My provisional expectation is Class C for the full problem as stated: the schedule is function-valued, and the dominance and budget constraints must hold over every aggregate state. That is a reason to investigate continuum-specific hardness, not a result established by this paper. A useful next question is whether restricted payoff classes admit a finite separation problem and move the mirror into Class A.

The weakest point is the change from two players to a nonatomic population. A single family’s deviation has zero effect on aggregate budget accounting, whereas in the paper every player’s transfer is counted at every action profile. The volume-based objective is also a necessary choice for measuring transfer across a continuum of states, not something dictated by Theorem 2. These are real modeling choices, but they leave a recognizable question for the authors: does their fair, budget-balanced minimum-transfer adjustment have a meaningful high-multiplicity analogue, and what is its complexity?

One qualification matters: this paper has no named theorem claiming a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Theorem 2 is the strongest named computational anchor available because it gives a constructive minimum-transfer result; it should not be recast as a formal complexity classification. This case covers that optimization result, not the paper’s incomplete-information theorems or its mechanism-design impossibility discussion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the only anchor is Theorem 2, and its problem has no population axis to relax: it is a minimum-transfer result for a two-player normal-form game. Its variables are entries in two players’ adjustment tables, indexed by joint action profiles. Encoding the players as two large cohorts leaves only two strategic choices; letting members of each cohort choose separately creates a different, many-player game. Replication is not a continuous version of the theorem’s input.

Keeping the theorem’s general payoff model creates a second mismatch. A player’s type would have to describe their payoff against every joint action profile of the other, named players. In a large population, those profiles grow with the population, and there is no reason their payoff tables should collapse to a moderate support of complete types. The school-enrollment example offers a plausible escape: assume utilities depend only on school-level aggregate demand. But that adds an anonymity and aggregation assumption the theorem does not make. It is a sensible model to study; it is not a high-multiplicity relaxation of the result as stated.

The proposed mirror makes that change explicit. It replaces a static table \(v_i(a)\) with functions \(v_t(a,x)\) over aggregate states, then asks for an objective integrated over those states. Theorem 2 supplies no measure over population states: its “general transfer” is a sum over finitely many action profiles. Normalized volume is one possible new choice, but another measure or a worst-case objective would define a different optimization problem. The proponent’s formulation also has to supply population versions of fairness and individual rationality. Those choices may yield a worthwhile mean-field incentive-design problem, but the paper does not provide the bridge from its optimum to that problem.

I would not claim that school placement or commuting can never support a useful continuous model. That is where this negative case is weakest. The narrower objection is that Theorem 2 offers no scalable computational problem for ChoCo to continuize: it gives a minimum-transfer construction for two players, without a complexity classification or a growing population parameter. A continuous study built on anonymous aggregate payoffs could be valuable on its own, but it would need a new model and objective rather than a continuous mirror of this theorem.
