The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper has no qualifying numbered computational result: it contains no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition establishing a complexity classification or a computed formulation. Sections 2.3–2.4 define bankruptcy allocations and fallback bargaining, and Section 3.3 reports the resulting monthly choices, including a Pareto-optimality claim. Those are useful ingredients for a mirror, but they are not named anchors of the kind you require. So there are no anchor-based questions to list, and I would not present any of the paper’s results as one.

The most plausible exploratory mirror is a basin with many water-user associations or local service units bargaining over scarce monthly water and pollution-assimilation capacity. A type would include everything the allocation uses: hydrological reach, sector and crop profile, monthly blue- and grey-footprint claims per unit served, treatment and return-flow characteristics, and the resulting preference over allocation schemes. The mass \(\mu_q\) is the share of claimants of type \(q\); there may be thousands of units but relatively few repeated profiles. Available basin capacity is expressed per unit of claimant mass, and each type receives a per-unit allocation. This continuizes the population while keeping months discrete and the physical resource allocations intact.

A precise candidate question is Type-Continuous Basin Fallback Allocation. For each month in which capacity is scarce, the instance gives a finite type support \(Q\), rational masses \(\mu_q\) summing to \(1\), per-unit claims \(d^B_q,d^G_q\), and available capacities \(A^B,A^G\), with \(A^z<\sum_q\mu_qd^z_q\) for each resource \(z\). For each of the paper’s six bankruptcy rules, extend its allocation formula to the weighted claims, yielding per-type per-unit allocations \(a^z_{q,r}\) that use the available capacity. Rank the rules for each type by how close its satisfaction ratio \(a^z_{q,r}/d^z_q\) is to \(1\), as in the paper’s sustainability-based preferences. The task is to find the smallest fallback depth \(k\) for which some rule is in the top \(k\) for every positive-mass type, and return that compromise rule or rules and their type-level allocations. Run the blue and grey allocation decisions separately, as the paper does. Here the objective is the fallback agreement depth; a solution is the agreement set and its induced allocations.

The regime is conceivable where many local claimants share standardized profiles, but this is an extension of the paper’s six-region setup. Including reach and other hydrological characteristics in the type is essential: otherwise the grouping could erase the very upstream/downstream differences the paper is designed to manage. Under this model I would expect tractability in \(\tau\) and the input bit length: there are six rules, their weighted allocations can be computed from a finite set of claims, and fallback agreement checks a finite set of rankings. That makes a useful algorithmic question, but it offers no discrete hardness result whose difficulty might dissolve under continuization.

The weakest point is that the paper studies a small set of geographically distinct regional stakeholders, not a large population of interchangeable claimants. A referee could reasonably regard the candidate as a scalable extension of its allocation framework rather than the continuous analogue of a result the paper proves. Whether fallback agreement remains meaningful with many claimants, and whether its Pareto claim survives under this type-based population model, are the most important follow-up questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to continuize. It gives a monthly accounting model, applies six bankruptcy rules, and selects among their allocations with fallback bargaining. Its output is a case study for six geographically defined regions. The Pareto claim describes that allocation procedure; it is not a complexity result for a computational problem. So there is no result here whose high-multiplicity version could reveal whether continuous populations change the algorithmic landscape.

The proponent’s Type-Continuous Basin Fallback Allocation is the strongest candidate: keep months and blue and grey resources discrete, put hydrology and all other relevant characteristics in each type, and let masses represent repeated claimants. But that formulation must choose what the population represents. If it is the residents or users whose demands determine water footprints, the paper’s bargaining parties remain the six regions; the new population changes the accounting inputs, not the bargaining problem. If user associations become the parties, the model has changed who bargains and who has a veto. That might be a useful new model, but it is not a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s six-party result.

There is also no natural population limit for the fallback step as written. Its agreement condition is unanimous among stakeholders. Applied to a population distribution, the direct analogue makes every type with positive mass a veto player: a type with mass \(\epsilon\) can matter as much as one with mass \(1-\epsilon\). Weighting agreement by mass, or allowing disagreement by a negligible fraction, would avoid that sensitivity but would replace the paper’s unanimity rule with a different bargaining rule. Treating each type as one collective bargainer is another coherent choice, but then the mass measures claims while the type gets a veto as a group. The paper supplies no reason to privilege that interpretation.

One could study large consortia of similar water users, or define a new mass-weighted bargaining procedure for them. I cannot rule out that such a project would be worthwhile on its own. The negative case is narrower: the paper offers no computational result to mirror, and its best proposed population extension changes the bargaining institution or leaves the original parties intact. That makes it a weak fit for this continuization programme.
