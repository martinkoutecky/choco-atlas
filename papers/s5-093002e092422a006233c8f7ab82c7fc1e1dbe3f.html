The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant case anchored in this paper’s computational results: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. The introduction cites NP-completeness results for coalitional manipulation from other papers; those are not results proved here. This paper’s own results characterize strategy-proof score profiles and bound the fraction of manipulable profiles under IC or ISC.

The nearest foothold is Proposition 2, proved here: for plurality, a profile is \(c\)-collusion-proof exactly when some candidate leads every other candidate by at least \(c+1\) points. One could pose a continuous plurality question for a large electorate with repeated ranking types: given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings and a coalition’s distribution over sincere types, can the coalition redistribute its mass among reported rankings so that every member prefers the resulting winner to the truthful outcome? The types would be complete rankings; the action would be a mass redistribution; and the output would be a feasible redistribution or a proof that none exists. A large electorate with relatively few recurring ballot patterns is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the plurality score constraints suggest this version should be tractable.

But Proposition 2 is a characterization of strategic vulnerability, not a complexity result. This proposed problem also changes the paper’s focus: its results concern fractions of finite profiles as the number of candidates grows, not computation over a continuous population. So this is the strongest adjacent idea I can offer, but it does not satisfy the required anchor criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the decisive point: this paper proves no computational complexity result of its own. Its propositions characterize which score profiles are strategy-proof or collusion-proof; its theorems count such profiles under IC or ISC. The NP-completeness of coalitional manipulation appears as background from other work. There is therefore no complexity result here for a continuous version to mirror.

Proposition 2 is the strongest possible anchor, but I would not count it as one. It takes a plurality score profile from \(n\) truthful voters and characterizes whether \(c\) *new* voters can collude. A faithful high-multiplicity limit makes those \(c\) voters’ mass vanish. Giving the coalition a positive share instead changes the coalition-size regime; and the paper’s central candidate-asymptotic results keep \(n\) fixed, so they have no growing electorate to continuize.

There is a more promising adjacent question: given a distribution of ranking types, how much mass must voters who prefer a target candidate to the current winner misreport to make that candidate win? That is a coherent continuous manipulation problem. But it changes the object from the paper’s characterization of profiles against a fixed number of new voters to an optimization over a positive-mass coalition. The paper does not supply a computational result, or a probability model on continuous societies, that would make this particular optimization its mirror. It may be worth studying on its own; it does not rescue Proposition 2 as an anchor.

So the negative case is strong against a *paper-anchored* continuous mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can arise: the mass-manipulation problem is plausible, but its motivation would need to come from elsewhere.
