What the mirror covers

The paper's named results characterize cones and preference relations; no computational result is covered by a continuous population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchored case for a continuous computational mirror: the paper has no named result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its named results are representation theorems.

The closest candidate is Corollary 4.2, proved here: a reflexive binary relation \(R\) on probability measures satisfies independence if and only if there is a family \(\mathcal U\) of sets of continuous utility functions such that \(p\,R\,q\) exactly when, for every \(U\in\mathcal U\), some \(u\in U\) has \(E_p[u]\ge E_q[u]\). That is a characterization of preferences over lotteries, not a computational result about a population. The lottery space is continuous, but that continuizes the alternatives, not the society.

A plausible population story would be many decision-makers with repeated preference types over lotteries, represented by a distribution \(\mu\). But choosing an aggregation or optimization task for that population would add a new problem; no result in this paper anchors it. Even restricting to finitely many alternatives and types would not supply the missing computational question or make that question the authors’ problem.

So there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover from this paper. The nearest results may be useful mathematical machinery for a future model, but treating them as evidence for a ChoCo mirror would confuse outcome-space continuity with population continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 4.2 is the closest anchor, but it gives no computational problem to continuize. It characterizes when a preference relation \(R\) over lotteries satisfies independence: comparisons can be represented by families of continuous utility functions. The lotteries are alternatives in the relation; they are not a population of agents. Independence concerns mixing a common lottery into both alternatives, not aggregating people’s preferences.

The strongest population story would take many decision-makers, group them by preference type, and describe their proportions by a distribution \(\mu\). But to get a computational question, one must then add a collective rule—say, a threshold for how much mass prefers one lottery to another—and specify how each type’s relation is represented in the input. Corollary 4.2 supplies neither. Its families of subsets of \(C_b(Z)\) need not have any finite or effective encoding. Choosing an aggregation rule and an encoding would define a new problem, not mirror a result in this paper. The same applies if one tries to extract a computational task from the other representation theorems: they characterize cones and relations, rather than classify algorithms or complexity.

The negative case is strongest at that boundary. Outcome-space continuity in the paper is not itself an objection, and a population model could certainly be built around its subject matter. But no such model would be anchored by a named computational result here. I cannot rule out every worthwhile future problem involving populations of decision-makers; I can say that posing one would require adding the central computational question the paper never asks.
