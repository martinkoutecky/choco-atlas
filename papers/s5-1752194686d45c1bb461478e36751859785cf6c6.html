The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), a fixed complete agenda \(\triangleright\) with target \(p\) not last, a base distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types, eligible-cohort capacities \(\nu_t\), and an addition budget \(\beta\), decide whether there are masses \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\) such that \(p\) is not the TSMR winner under the resulting distribution \((\mu_t+x_t)/(1+\sum_t x_t)\).

The model it lives in

A base society of unit mass distributed over ranking types, plus a bounded eligible cohort of each type. The controller chooses added mass \(x_t\); TSMR uses pairwise-majority margins in the combined society, and the objective is to make \(p\) lose while minimizing total added mass or meeting a given mass budget.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 11's DCAV-TSMR hardness result for agendas where the target is not last; it leaves the other control, manipulation, agenda, and partial-information results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Establish finite-population approximation guarantees relating the minimum added mass to DCAV instances with specified cohort sizes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is continuous destructive control by adding voters. It keeps the paper’s TSMR rule and its question—can an outside actor make \(p\) lose by adding voters?—while treating the electorate and the addable cohort as masses of preference types.

The anchor is Theorem 11, proved in this paper by reduction from Red-Blue Dominating Set. It says that DCAV-TSMR is W[2]-hard parameterized by the number of added votes plus the number of registered votes, whenever the distinguished candidate is not last in the agenda. In DCAV, the actor may add at most \(k\) unregistered votes and asks whether the distinguished candidate can thereby be prevented from winning. The reduction’s choices encode a dominating set.

Here is a continuous version of that problem. An instance has a candidate set \(C\), a complete agenda \(\triangleright\) on \(C\), a target \(p\) that is not last, and a base society \(\mu\) over complete rankings \(t\) of \(C\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). It also has an eligible cohort with capacity \(\nu_t\) for each ranking type, measured relative to the base population, and an addition budget \(\beta\). The decision variable is added mass \(x_t\), where \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\). The question is whether some such \(x\) makes \(p\) lose under TSMR in the resulting society, whose type masses are \((\mu_t+x_t)/(1+\sum_t x_t)\). A solution is a feasible vector \(x\); the optimization form minimizes \(\sum_t x_t\) subject to \(p\) losing.

This is plausible in a large member vote or public consultation using a fixed sequential procedure: many participants rank the same modest menu of proposals, while an organizer or campaign can mobilize some fraction of an eligible cohort. The population might number in the millions while the number \(\tau\) of distinct ranking types is far smaller. Types are complete ballot descriptions for everything this model uses. This retains the paper’s control question and TSMR outcome; the continuous quantity is the population being added. That fit is especially credible because TSMR depends on pairwise majorities, and the paper notes that the weighted-majority-graph computation in Lemma 1 applies to weighted elections.

I expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable. For candidates \(a,b\), let \(M_{ab}(x)\) be their pairwise margin in the combined, unnormalized population. It is linear in \(x\), and \(a\) beats \(b\) exactly when \(M_{ab}(x)>0\). The normalization by total population does not change the sign.

There are two ways for \(p\) to lose. First, some predecessor \(c\) in the agenda beats \(p\), so \(p\) is not a source of the forward-arc graph. For each such \(c\), maximize \(M_{cp}(x)\) subject to the mass bounds and budget; a positive optimum gives a defeating addition. Second, some successor \(q\) is a source. For each successor \(q\), test whether the same mass bounds and budget can satisfy \(M_{aq}(x)\le 0\) for every predecessor \(a\) of \(q\). A later source rules out \(p\) as the rightmost source, whether or not \(p\) itself is a source. These are linear programs, at most \(O(m)\) of them, with \(\tau\) variables and rational input. Thus the continuous decision problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

That tractability would not contradict Theorem 11. The theorem’s reduction encodes combinatorics in choosing a subset of individual added ballots. In the mirror, the actor chooses fractions of homogeneous cohorts. The model still has the paper’s candidate and agenda structure, but it no longer requires an all-or-nothing choice for each voter. This is precisely the kind of population-granularity change that can make a discrete hardness result disappear.

I would keep the scope to Theorem 11. Follow-on questions include computing the minimum mobilized mass needed to defeat \(p\), comparing that value with the discrete vote-addition optimum, and adding type-specific mobilization costs.

The weakest point is the change in action granularity: a campaign can add voters, but may not literally choose \(0.3\) of a cohort in a finite election. In a genuinely high-multiplicity regime, though, mobilizing a percentage of a large, nearly homogeneous group is a sensible question, and the paper’s control problem remains recognizable. The mirror should therefore be presented as a population-scale relaxation of Theorem 11, not as a claim that its discrete W[2]-hardness already answers the continuous problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to Theorem 11’s proposed mirror is that it changes what the controller can do. The paper lets an actor choose whole ballots from a fixed list of unregistered voters. A mass variable \(x_t\) instead assumes a scalable pool of eligible voters of each ranking type, with known and stable preferences. In an actual election, eligibility and turnout may not provide such a pool. And a continuous solution can rely on an arbitrarily small addition that no finite electorate can make.

That objection limits the mirror’s institutional interpretation, but it does not defeat it. A large public consultation could have a known pool of potential participants grouped by ranking, from which an organizer mobilizes a share. Rankings are complete types for this problem, and voter identity is otherwise irrelevant. The paper’s TSMR outcome depends on pairwise majorities, so the rule itself carries over cleanly to those type masses. The added-pool assumption is extra modeling work, but it is a plausible setting for the same control question.

I can’t honestly defeat this anchor. Its weakness is the need to justify the eligible pool and fractional mobilization in a particular application; that does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. The negative case is weak here.
