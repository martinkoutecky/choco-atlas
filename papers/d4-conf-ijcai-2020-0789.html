The continuous mirror question

Given finite assignment and resource sets, rational rewards and resource uses, per-capita budgets, a finite horizon \(T\), and an explicit finite rational distribution for each stage’s arrival-mass vector \(\Xi_t\), choose offline assignment masses \(x_e\) before arrivals and online assignment masses \(z_{e,t}\) after observing the current arrivals and past history. Each person receives an integral assignment, while aggregate masses may be fractional; all assignments must obey shared resource budgets at every stage. What is the best competitive ratio achievable in polynomial time against a clairvoyant fluid allocator that sees the full arrival path?

The model it lives in

A finite set of complete worker, task, and assignment types, each carrying mass in a population of scale \(N\); the instance specifies rational per-capita rewards, resource uses and budgets, plus finite distributions over stage-wise arrival-mass vectors whose components may share a common shock. The policy selects offline assignment mass \(x_e\), then observed online assignment mass \(z_{e,t}\), maximizing expected reward under the shared budgets; the benchmark knows the full arrival path.

The objection that survived

Common-shock arrivals do not arise by aggregating the paper’s independent arrivals, and the complexity may depend on how their temporal distribution is represented.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s general-case guarantee and includes Theorem 1’s integral-resource guarantee as a special case; it leaves the empirical comparisons and other proof details outside the main question.

Open questions for a prover

  • What competitive ratio can a polynomial-time policy guarantee as a function of resource sparsity and minimum non-integral per-capita budget?
  • How does the answer depend on whether the common-shock arrival process is given as an explicit scenario tree or a more compact distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a fluid version of their two-stage resource allocation problem. It continuizes the workers and tasks, while keeping the paper’s defining structure: offline and online assignments compete for the same resources, and online decisions are irrevocable.

A type would be a complete assignment-relevant profile. A bike-rebalancing worker type, for example, includes route, qualifications, payment, and the tasks they can perform; a task type includes its source and destination, reward, and resource use. A type’s mass is its share of a large deployment. For a market of scale \(N\), write \(x_e\) for the mass assigned along offline edge \(e\), and let \(\Xi_t(j)\) be the mass of online type \(j\) arriving at time \(t\). The policy sees \(\Xi_t\) when it arrives and chooses assignment masses \(z_{e,t}\), with \(z_{e,t}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_{e\in E^2_j}z_{e,t}\le\Xi_t(j)\). It must respect every resource budget at every stage. Each person receives a whole assignment; only the aggregate mass is divisible.

A concrete instance would give the finite assignment and resource sets, rational rewards and resource uses, a finite horizon, and finite rational distributions over online arrival-mass vectors. The policy chooses \(x\) before arrivals and chooses \(z_t\) from the observed history and current arrivals, without seeing future arrivals. Its objective is to maximize expected total reward. The comparator is the expected reward of an offline allocator that sees the full arrival realization before choosing both phases’ assignments. The input’s type support is finite; the number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the number of types.

This regime is plausible in the paper’s bike-sharing application: many tasks and workers recur across a modest set of station-pair and route types, while bike supplies, station demands, and budgets grow with the deployment. The authors already model \(I,H,J\) as types and allow repeated assignment edges, which supports the high-multiplicity interpretation. That is evidence for the mirror, not a novelty collision: assignments and arrivals in the paper still have discrete units.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, “Performance of \(\alg\) for the general case,” proved in this paper. It says that for TS-RA with integral-resource sparsity \(\ell_1\), non-integral-resource sparsity \(\ell_2\), \(\ell=\ell_1+\ell_2\), and minimum non-integral budget \(B\), the paper’s LP-guided sampling algorithm with \(\eta=\alpha=1/(2\ell)\) achieves competitive ratio at least
\[ \frac{1}{4\ell}(1-\epsilon), \qquad \epsilon=2\max\!\left(\frac{1}{B-2},\ \ell_2\exp(-B/12+1/6)\right), \]
using the benchmark LP. The paper proves that LP’s value upper-bounds the expected offline optimum, so the guarantee also gives a ratio against that optimum. This is an algorithmic approximation result, not a named P/NP-hardness result.

The continuous problem I would hand a prover is Fluid Two-Stage Resource Allocation: given the instance above, determine whether a policy computable in time polynomial in the finite input description can achieve an expected reward at least \(\rho\) times the expected clairvoyant fluid optimum, and characterize the best such \(\rho\) in terms of sparsity and budget scale. The natural benchmark is the paper’s LP with expected arrival masses in place of arrival probabilities. The direct conjecture suggested by Theorem 2 is an \(\ell\)-dependent constant guarantee, approaching \(1/(4\ell)\) as non-integral budgets grow while sparsities remain fixed. I would initially expect a Class A result for such a guarantee: the paper already has a sparse-resource LP benchmark and a policy built around it. That does not imply exact optimization is easy. Further questions include whether the bound improves when type masses are deterministic, how it depends on the maximum cohort size, and whether the LP’s gap is tight for fluid arrivals.

The paper’s Theorem 1, proved here, is the integral-resource special case: with sparsity \(\ell\), the same algorithm using \(\eta=\alpha=1/(2\ell)\) achieves at least \(1/(4\ell)\). It supports the same mirror, but I would not count it as a separate anchor because Theorem 2 is the broader result.

My weakest point is the arrival model. In the paper, each round brings one independently sampled agent; with many independent agents, type proportions tend to become deterministic, and much of the online uncertainty can disappear. To keep an online decision problem in the fluid mirror, I allow stochastic aggregate cohort masses, such as route-type demand shifted by a common citywide shock. That is a plausible high-volume setting and preserves observed, irrevocable arrivals, but it is an extension of the paper’s independence assumptions. If the authors reject that extension, the deterministic fluid allocation problem remains a sensible mirror, though the competitive-ratio question becomes less central.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the one substantive anchor; Theorem 1 is its integral-resource special case. Their shared subject is randomized assignment of individual units under independent arrivals: Phase I rounds edge counts, Phase II samples individual assignments, and the safety checks prevent those discrete choices from exceeding budgets. The proposed mirror keeps the graph and resource constraints but removes the individual units that make those checks necessary.

For the paper’s independent-arrival model, that removal also erases the online uncertainty at population scale. Scale the population and budgets together, as a nontrivial fluid limit requires. The normalized arrival mass then concentrates around the known arrival profile. With that profile known, the planner can compute the assignments for every stage in advance and execute the schedule as the mass arrives. The online policy and clairvoyant comparator no longer differ in what they know. A fluid LP can still describe the allocation, but the competitive-ratio question anchored in Theorem 2 has lost its defining online-information obstacle. That is a degeneration of the object, not an objection that its answer would be easy or that the theorem’s guarantee would change.

The strongest rescue is the proponent’s common-shock version: citywide events could cause large random changes in route-type demand, leaving stochastic mass arrivals even with a continuum of workers. But that uncertainty does not arise by aggregating the paper’s independent individual arrivals. It adds macroscopic dependence among agents, changing the arrival model. If the authors accept that as their problem, it could support a worthwhile stochastic-allocation question—but the complexity would then depend on how the common-shock process is represented, rather than on the high-multiplicity limit behind their theorem. An explicit scenario tree gives a standard stochastic optimization formulation; a compact, complex shock model may create a harder problem, but that would be a new source of complexity.

The high-multiplicity regime itself is credible here: the paper already works with worker and task types and permits repeated assignments. I would not reject the mirror on grounds of identity, implausible multiplicity, or the fact that the paper’s benchmark is an LP. My case against is narrower: the faithful fluid limit removes the stochastic online phenomenon measured by the two theorems, while the most compelling way to preserve that phenomenon changes the arrival assumptions. That makes the universal negative case weak: a common-shock model might still be a valuable continuous problem if these authors would recognize it as theirs.
