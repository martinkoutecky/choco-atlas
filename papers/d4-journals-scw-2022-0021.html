The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the required kind: no theorem or proposition classifies a problem as hard, tractable, FPT, or similar. Its named results concern polling dynamics, cycle existence, convergence, and robustness. So there is no eligible anchor under your rule, and I would not present any of them as a complexity result.

The strongest positive case is that a high-multiplicity polling society is a natural regime. In a large election preceded by repeated polls, millions of people may fall into a comparatively small number \(\tau\) of complete types: each type has the same ranking and follows the same polling heuristic. Represent type \(t\) by its population share \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A state can record the fraction \(p^t_B\) of that type currently casting each sincere approval ballot \(B\); candidate \(a\)’s approval share is then \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{B\ni a}p^t_B\). The paper already works with weighted voter types and, in its continuous-state model, distributions of ballots within each type. This makes the regime plausible to the authors, though the continuous coordinates in that model primarily describe variation in ballot behavior.

The closest results to anchor a follow-on question are Theorem 2 and Corollary 4.5, both proved in the paper. Theorem 2 constructs an Approval election with four candidates, strict preferences, and a Condorcet winner in which voters following the Leader Rule cycle without ever electing that winner; a majority of sincere, expressive initial ballot profiles lead to the cycle. The proof counts \(1353\) of \(2187\) such profiles. Corollary 4.5 applies the paper’s continuous-state framework to that profile: some \(\varepsilon>0\) and an open set of initial states guarantee a periodic winner sequence excluding the Condorcet winner for every dynamics within \(\varepsilon\) of the embedded dynamics. Example 4.8 gives a more specific robustness illustration: in a three-candidate example, a cycle persists when \(15\%\) of voters retain their ballots, provided the relevant margins exceed \(4\%\). These are dynamical results, not computational-complexity anchors.

A precise follow-on problem would be Maximum Inertia for a Bad Polling Cycle. An instance consists of candidates, a tie-breaking rule, finitely many strict preference types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), the Leader Rule, and a specified tie-free cycle \(C\) of winner-runner-up pairs that excludes the Condorcet winner. For each retention rate \(\varepsilon\), define the update for type \(t\) by
\[ p^{t\prime}=(1-\varepsilon)\delta_{\mathrm{LR}(t,r)}+\varepsilon p^t, \]
where \(r\) is the current poll result and \(\delta_{\mathrm{LR}(t,r)}\) puts all mass on the ballot prescribed by the Leader Rule. The question is to compute the supremum \(\varepsilon^\star\) for which there is an attracting periodic orbit with outcome sequence \(C\), and provide a witness orbit. For a specified cycle, I would expect this to be tractable: on each region with the same outcome, the update is affine, and the cycle condition reduces to checking that the resulting orbit remains within the corresponding outcome regions. The broader question—whether *any* bad cycle persists at a given retention rate—would be a separate problem.

This formulation covers the bad-cycle and robustness results, not the paper’s experimental prevalence claims, its one-dimensional convergence theorem, or its chaos discussion. I think the authors would recognize the question: they explicitly study persistence under incomplete updating and give a concrete partial-update example. My weakest point is that the threshold problem is a proposed computational layer over their dynamical results, not a problem they already analyze for complexity; for a fixed cycle it may also be comparatively easy. The paper’s continuous states chiefly distribute ballots within types, while the programme’s central move is to make the population itself the object. So the regime is persuasive, but the complexity case is a research proposal rather than an existing-result mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with a mismatch: Theorem 2, Corollary 4.5, and Example 4.8 state no computational complexity result. Theorem 2 constructs a bad cycle and counts how many finite ballot profiles reach it; Corollary 4.5 proves that a cycle persists under small perturbations; Example 4.8 exhibits an attracting cycle under a particular amount of voter inertia. None classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, there is no computational result here to mirror.

That does not mean the population regime is implausible. The paper’s examples group voters into a few weighted preference types, so representing them by rational population shares is natural. Nor is “the cycle already exists continuously” a sufficient objection: the paper’s continuous framework studies dynamics, not the complexity of computing them. The narrower point is that the proponents’ anchors do not supply an existing complexity question.

Theorem 2’s count of \(1353\) out of \(2187\) profiles does not directly become a continuous prevalence result. In the mass model, a type can split its ballots, giving a simplex of possible states. There is no canonical continuous counterpart to counting the type-level ballot profiles uniformly: Lebesgue volume is an extra modeling choice, while independent per-person ballot choices induce a different distribution over mass states. A continuous basin-measure question could be defined, but the theorem itself does not determine which one to ask.

Corollary 4.5 is the closest anchor for the proposed inertia threshold, but its result is topological: some neighborhood and some perturbation tolerance preserve the sequence of winners. Example 4.8 gives a concrete partial-update rule and verifies a particular cycle. “Compute the largest inertia preserving this cycle” is a coherent quantitative extension. It is nevertheless a new algorithmic question layered onto dynamical results, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper. Supplying the cycle as input also makes the proposed threshold question narrower than the broader task of finding bad cycles.

A stronger formulation could use rational type shares, specify the Leader Rule and an explicit initialization, and ask whether some retention rate admits an attracting cycle that excludes the Condorcet winner. That would be a well-posed computational question about a natural high-multiplicity society. I cannot honestly rule out its being worthwhile. The negative case is strong only under the programme’s narrower requirement that a mirror be anchored in a named computational result; the universal claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists is weak if the programme also welcomes new computational questions built from the paper’s dynamics.
