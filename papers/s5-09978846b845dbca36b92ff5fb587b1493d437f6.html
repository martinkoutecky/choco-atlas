Why no mirror

Theorem 2.11 and Theorem 2.8 are impossibility results about whether voting rules can satisfy specified axioms. The SAT and Lean work helps establish those results, but the named theorems do not assert an algorithm, computational hardness, or a computational task solved through a formulation. The proposed continuous axiom question is intelligible, but it fails bit (a).

What the mirror covers

The proposed distributional question corresponds to Theorem 2.11's axiom package and could also be posed for Theorem 2.8's separate package. It leaves the SAT proof strategy, its Lean verification, and the paper's other axiomatic results without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a near-miss: this paper has no named result that qualifies as a computational-complexity anchor under your definition. Its SAT and Lean work proves axiomatic impossibilities; it does not classify an underlying problem as, for example, NP-hard, polynomial-time, or FPT.

The closest anchor is Theorem 2.11, proved in the paper using SAT. It says there is no voting method on profiles with up to four candidates satisfying anonymity, neutrality, binary \(\gamma\), and quasi-resoluteness. Theorem 2.8 is similar: no method satisfies anonymity, neutrality, binary quasi-resoluteness, binary \(\gamma\), and \(\alpha\)-resoluteness on profiles with up to six candidates, though such a method exists up to five. These are nonexistence results about voting rules, not computational-complexity results.

If that anchor were allowed, the natural mirror would use a large electorate with repeated complete rankings. A type \(t\) is a strict ranking of the candidates; \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population, with \(n\) large and support size \(\tau\) much smaller than \(n\). The unknown is a winner correspondence \(F_C(\mu)\subseteq C\), required to be nonempty and to satisfy the paper’s axioms: anonymity through dependence on \(\mu\), neutrality under candidate renaming, binary \(\gamma\) under restriction to smaller candidate sets, and quasi-resoluteness when pairwise margins are uniquely weighted. The question is whether such an \(F\) exists for all distributions on up to four candidates. There is no optimization objective; it is a feasibility question about a rule.

The impossibility would carry over: each finite profile is an empirical distribution over rankings, so a continuous rule satisfying those axioms would induce a discrete rule forbidden by Theorem 2.11. But this is an axiomatic extension, not a computational mirror in ChoCo’s sense; the continuous population adds no algorithmic problem, and the result fits none of Classes A, B, or C as defined. That is the weakest point—and it is decisive for whether this counts as a ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This paper has no computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 2.11 is an impossibility theorem about whether a voting rule can satisfy four axioms; Theorem 2.8 asks the same kind of existence question for a different axiom package. Its “up to four” and “up to six” bounds count candidates needed for inconsistency. They are not running-time, hardness, or algorithmic bounds. The SAT and Lean machinery proves those impossibilities; it does not classify the complexity of a problem about elections.

The strongest continuous formulation is still easy to state sensibly. Let each ranking \(t\) have population mass \(\mu_t\), and ask whether a winner correspondence on these distributions can satisfy anonymity, neutrality, binary \(\gamma\), and quasi-resoluteness. That is a legitimate axiomatic question, and large electorates with repeated rankings are a plausible regime. But the question remains whether a rule exists. It supplies no input-output task, objective, or algorithmic parameter for ChoCo to study. The fact that normalized finite profiles sit inside the distribution model confirms the correspondence between the two formulations; it does not turn rule existence into a computational problem.

The same problem defeats the Theorem 2.8 mirror. One could also cast the paper’s SAT encoding as a search for a smallest witness to incompatibility, or as a way to find a rule satisfying a weakened axiom set on selected distributions. Those would be new proof-search or rule-design questions, not computational results anchored in this paper. The authors’ stated future direction—optimizing the numbers of voters and candidates in impossibility proofs—does not change that: it is a sharper bound on an axiomatic witness, not a complexity classification for a population-level computational problem.

So the negative case is strong within ChoCo’s scope, though not a claim that continuous voting questions are uninteresting. A continuous axiomatic continuation may be worthwhile in its own right; this paper gives no named computational result for such a mirror to take as its anchor.
