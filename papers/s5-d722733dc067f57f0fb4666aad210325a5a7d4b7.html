The case FOR (proponent)

The anchor audit gives zero results that meet your complexity criterion: the numbered results establish equilibrium existence and uniqueness, market clearing, and welfare bounds; none says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Theorem 8 is the strongest named result to build a positive analogy around, but it is a welfare theorem rather than a qualifying computational-complexity anchor.

The plausible regime is a large population of elastic traffic flows managed in stable cohorts: for example, a traffic manager bids for many flows whose users share the same rate utility. Let \(T\) be the cohort types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. Type \(t\)’s members share utility \(U_t(d)\) per unit of population. They submit a common per-member bid \(b_t\); the network sets price \(p(f)\) by \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t=f p(f)\), where \(f=\sum_t\mu_t d_t\), and allocates per-member rate \(d_t=b_t/p(f)\). Each cohort chooses its bid to maximize \(U_t(d_t)-b_t\), anticipating its effect on price. The planner’s objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t U_t(d_t)-C(f)\), with \(C(f)=\int_0^f p(z)\,dz\). For a population of size \(N\), this means total congestion cost scales as \(N C(f)\).

The lead question is Mass-Type Elastic-Supply Equilibrium Computation. Given rational masses \(\mu_t\), linear utilities \(U_t(d)=a_t d\) with positive rational \(a_t\), and a rational convex piecewise-linear price function \(p\), find a pure Nash equilibrium bid vector and its induced rates. A solution must satisfy the market-clearing and allocation equations above, and no cohort can improve its payoff by changing its bid. The result I would tentatively expect is Class A for this single-link restriction: for a fixed aggregate rate, type responses are determined by the price and its local slope, and the active types are ordered by their utility slopes. That suggests a polynomial search over price segments and active-type thresholds. This is a conjecture about the mirror’s computation, not a result proved in the paper.

Theorem 8, proved in this paper, supplies the welfare anchor: under Assumptions 1–3 and \(U_r(0)\ge 0\), every Nash equilibrium \(w\) has surplus at least \((4\sqrt{2}-5)\) times the surplus of a social optimum \(d^S\). The bound is tight: for every \(\delta>0\), the paper constructs a number of users \(R\), linear utilities, and a piecewise-linear price function with an equilibrium and optimum whose surplus ratio is at most \(4\sqrt{2}-5+\delta\). The cohort game preserves this result exactly: with total cohort rate \(z_t=\mu_t d_t\) and total bid \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\), the paper’s user utility becomes \(V_t(z_t)=\mu_t U_t(z_t/\mu_t)\), and the game and planner’s objective take the paper’s original form. Thus Theorem 8’s guarantee applies to the mirror, including its arbitrarily close tightness examples.

This case covers the paper’s single-link equilibrium and efficiency result, especially Theorem 8; it does not claim a mirror for the network extension or the elastic-to-inelastic limit. Its weakest point is the strategic unit: the mirror treats each cohort’s controller as a player coordinating its mass. If many individual users instead bid independently and each has vanishing influence on price, the continuum limit removes price anticipation; it becomes closer to the paper’s price-taking benchmark. The cohort-controller scenario is plausible for managed traffic, but narrower than the paper’s literal individual-user model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed lead question is not a computational result of this paper. Theorem 8 proves a welfare bound, Theorem 14 extends it to networks, and the existence and uniqueness propositions are analytic. None states an algorithmic or complexity result. “Mass-Type Elastic-Supply Equilibrium Computation” is a new question built around the model, rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The cohort construction also changes who acts. It treats each type as a controller for the entire mass of that type. The rescaling \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\), \(z_t=\mu_t d_t\), and \(V_t(z_t)=\mu_t U_t(z_t/\mu_t)\) does make Theorem 8 apply exactly. But that is a finite game with one strategic controller per type, written in mass coordinates; it does not preserve the paper’s model of individual users choosing bids. If the continuum instead consists of independently acting, infinitesimal users, one user’s deviation has zero effect on the aggregate bid and price. The paper’s price-anticipating strategic effect then disappears. Preserving it requires positive-mass controllers or cohorts, which makes the proposed mirror a coalition or aggregator game. Theorem 14 has the same issue at each link.

The best scenario for the proposal is a network where managers coordinate bids for stable groups of similar flows. That is plausible, and the continuous mass formulation may be useful for that setting. Its weakness is also its limit: the paper studies individual users’ strategic behavior, while the cohort version gives a collective controller the power to move an entire group’s bid. Unless the authors regard that collective decision problem as theirs, the construction does not establish a worthwhile mirror of their results.

The negative case is not airtight. Theorem 8 admits a clean mass-based reformulation, and managed cohorts are a credible scenario. So I would not claim that no continuous model can be written. The stronger case is that the paper supplies no computational anchor, and the proposed mass model preserves price anticipation only by changing the strategic unit.
