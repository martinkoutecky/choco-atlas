What the mirror covers

The anchors cover Proposition 1's three-party legislative equilibrium characterization and Proposition 2's optimal-funding result; the thesis presents these over population shares already, and gives no discrete-electorate computational result to bridge.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is that this is a very natural high-multiplicity *model*: a large proportional-representation electorate can have many voters but only three relevant types. A type is a voter’s ideal policy and voting capability; \(\mu_L,\mu_M,\mu_R\) are the population shares. If middle-type voters coordinate, their vote mass can be split among the three parties, while the extreme-type vote masses stay fixed. The resulting shares determine the legislature; parties bargain over policy \(y\) and a resource allocation \(g\) summing to \(G\). One could ask for the subgame-perfect equilibrium and the value of \(G\) maximizing the resulting social welfare. The regime \(n\gg 3\) is credible, and fractional vote mass is a sensible relaxation of finite vote counts.

But I cannot supply a qualifying anchor under your rule. The closest named result is Proposition 2, proved in the thesis: after normalizing \(d_R=1\), the optimal funding \(G^*\) does not depend on the population’s ideology distribution; for \(0<d_L<1\), \(G^*=d_L^2/2\), while for \(d_L\ge 1\), \(G^*\) is bounded above by \(1/2\). It also states that \(G^*\) is positively related to \(d_L\). Proposition 1, also proved there, characterizes legislative equilibrium outcomes across the three-party configurations. These are analytical equilibrium and comparative-statics results; neither asserts a complexity classification or gives a computational result of the kind required for an anchor.

More seriously, the proposed mirror is already essentially the thesis’s model: it explicitly represents voters by population proportions, lets the middle group coordinate a portion of its votes, and uses fractional party shares. Restating that as a continuous problem would collide with the paper’s existing continuous-population formulation. A finite-voter version with vote allocations restricted to multiples of \(1/n\) could make a distinct high-multiplicity comparison worthwhile, but this thesis supplies no named computational result about that discrete problem to anchor it. So the positive case for a *sensible model* is strong; the case for a new continuous-complexity mirror of this paper is weak, and no expected complexity class is warranted from its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest negative case is that none of the proposed anchors is a named computational result. Proposition 1 characterizes a bargaining equilibrium in a game with three parties; Proposition 2 gives comparative statics for optimal funding. Neither poses a complexity question. The proponent correctly recognizes this, so neither proposition can support the requested computational mirror.

The more fundamental problem is that the thesis already takes the population to be continuous in the relevant sense. It represents voters by shares \((\sigma_L,\sigma_M,\sigma_R)\), lets the middle group divide its votes strategically, and maps vote shares directly to legislative shares. Asking for the equilibrium or welfare-optimal funding in that model would restate what the thesis already analyzes. That is not a novelty collision under the programme’s definition: the thesis does not formulate or solve a computational complexity problem. But it does mean there is no discrete population in these results for a continuous mirror to create.

A finite-electorate version, with vote allocations restricted to multiples of \(1/n\), would be a legitimate new comparison. It still supplies no computational result from this paper to mirror. And if the proposed task is simply to compute equilibrium or optimal funding in the thesis’s three-party model, its input consists of a fixed number of shares and ideological parameters; the thesis already provides the relevant equilibrium cases and funding characterization. A complexity study would need a scalable dimension the paper does not give it.

The stronger escape route is to vary the number of parties or enrich the voter types, then study the complexity of equilibrium computation in a continuum electorate. That could be worthwhile, but it changes the model’s scope and generalizes its results; I cannot rule out that it yields a good project. So the case against is strong for the anchors actually raised, but the universal claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile mirror is weaker.
