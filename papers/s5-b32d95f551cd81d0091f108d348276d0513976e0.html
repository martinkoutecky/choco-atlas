The continuous mirror question

Given an issue set \(PS_n\), a finite support \(T\) of utility types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a satisfiable \(k\)-cube goalbase \(G_t\) with positive rational weights for each type, and a rational threshold \(B\), does there exist a package \(X \subseteq PS_n\) such that \(\sum_{t \in T}\mu_t \sum_{(\varphi,w)\in G_t:\,X\models\varphi} w \ge B\)? Equivalently, compute a package maximizing the population’s average utility.

The model it lives in

A large organization is a society with distribution \(\mu\) over complete utility types. Each type \(t\) has a weighted-goal utility function \(u_t\); the organization chooses a shared package \(X\) to maximize \(\sum_t \mu_t u_t(X)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the MAX-UTIL hardness results in Theorems 5.2 and 5.4; it leaves the expressivity and succinctness results, and the rest of the complexity classification, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which structural parameters beyond support size, such as issue count or goal incidence, yield useful exact or approximation algorithms?
  • How sensitive is the optimal package and its average utility to changes in the type masses \(\mu_t\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large organization choosing a package of binary policy commitments. Its members fall into a small number \(\tau\) of recurring utility types: members of type \(t\) evaluate every package according to the same weighted-goal utility function \(u_t\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the membership, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For a population of \(N\) members, the corresponding finite counts are \(N\mu_t\) whenever \(N\) is a common multiple of the mass denominators. A package is an assignment \(X\subseteq PS_n\) to the \(n\) binary issues, and the organization chooses one package to maximize average member utility:
\[ U_\mu(X)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t u_t(X). \]

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a very large membership can have many people per utility type and relatively few distinct types. A type records the full utility function relevant to the problem. The paper’s conclusion also explicitly points to collective-utility optimization for groups as a direction for these representation languages, which makes this extension recognizable to its authors.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.2. It states that, for every fixed \(k\ge 2\), MAX-UTIL\((k\text{-cubes},\mathbb Q^+)\) is NP-complete, even when every goal formula is satisfiable. Here goals are conjunctions of at most \(k\) literals and have positive rational weights. The paper proves the result by relating it to weighted MAX \(k\)-CONSTRAINT SAT, whose hardness it cites.

The corresponding continuous problem is Mass-Weighted MAX-UTIL\((k\text{-cubes},\mathbb Q^+)\). An instance gives \(PS_n\), a listed support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a goalbase \(G_t\) for each type using satisfiable \(k\)-cubes with positive rational weights, and a rational threshold \(B\). It asks whether there is an assignment \(X\subseteq PS_n\) such that
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \sum_{\substack{(\varphi,w)\in G_t\\X\models\varphi}} w \ge B. \]
A solution to a yes-instance is such an assignment; the optimization version returns an assignment of maximum average utility. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). Setting \(\tau=1\) and \(\mu_1=1\) gives exactly the paper’s problem, so the reduction’s combinatorics over binary alternatives survive in a continuous society. In particular, parameterizing only by \(\tau\) cannot yield an FPT algorithm unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), since hardness already holds at \(\tau=1\).

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 5.4. It states that MAX-UTIL\((k\text{-spcubes},\mathbb Q)\) is NP-complete for every \(k\ge 2\). The paper proves this by reduction from MAX 2-SAT. Here the goals are strictly positive conjunctions of at most \(k\) variables, while rational goal weights may be negative. The matching continuous problem, Mass-Weighted MAX-UTIL\((k\text{-spcubes},\mathbb Q)\), has the same society and threshold formulation as the lead problem, with each \(G_t\) restricted to those strictly positive cubes and signed rational weights. Its solution is again a package meeting the threshold, or a maximum-average-utility package in the optimization version. It too should be Class B by the one-type embedding. This variant matters on its own terms because it retains hardness when goals mention only positive bundles; penalties from negative weights can still create difficult choices.

The mirror covers these MAX-UTIL hardness results, not the paper’s expressivity and succinctness classifications. It also generates questions about how structure beyond \(\tau\)—such as the number of issues or the incidence pattern of goals—supports exact algorithms, and about approximation and sensitivity when the type masses change.

The weakness is that linear utilitarian aggregation lets one combine all types’ goals into a single weighted goalbase by multiplying each weight by its type’s mass. Thus this mirror may inherit the paper’s computational problem almost verbatim, and it does not yet show a new tractability gain from continuous optimization. Still, the explicit mass distribution is a meaningful model of a compressed, high-multiplicity society, the group objective is a direction the authors themselves flag, and the hardness result answers a real continuization question: here, making the population continuous does not dissolve hardness rooted in the alternative space.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection applies to both anchors. Theorem 5.2 and Theorem 5.4 concern one utility function and the problem of choosing one package \(X\). In the proposed society model, the common-package utilitarian objective can be written as a single weighted goalbase:
\[ \widehat G=\biguplus_{t\in T}\{(\varphi,\mu_t w):(\varphi,w)\in G_t\}. \]
Then \(u_{\widehat G}(X)=\sum_t\mu_tu_{G_t}(X)\). This construction has polynomial encoding size. It preserves positive rational weights and \(k\)-cubes for Theorem 5.2; it preserves signed rational weights and strictly positive \(k\)-cubes for Theorem 5.4. Thus the two anchors lead to the same computational question: optimize one aggregate utility. The type distribution supplies a decomposition of that utility, while the algorithmic task can be stated without a population.

I do not count the resulting equivalence or hardness transfer as an objection. The concern is what the mirror asks researchers to study. In this formulation, it asks for no population-level computation: there is one shared package, no type-specific outcome, and no constraint or intervention involving the society. The masses are coefficients used to construct an ordinary MAX-UTIL instance. That makes the proposed mirror look like a social interpretation of the paper’s single-agent problem, rather than a question whose computational object is the continuous society.

A stronger population model could make mass affect the choice—for example, choose a package that maximizes the fraction of members whose utility exceeds a threshold, or allocate a package of issues across members subject to per-capita supply constraints. Those are plausible large-organization questions. But they introduce a new welfare criterion or a coupled allocation problem. Theorems 5.2 and 5.4 do not classify those problems; at most, their one-type cases inherit the original MAX-UTIL hardness. They may be good follow-on questions, yet their computational content would need its own anchor.

That objection has a serious limit: the paper’s conclusion explicitly proposes collective-utility optimization, including utilitarian and egalitarian objectives. A large organization with recurring utility types is also a credible high-multiplicity regime. So I cannot honestly argue that the proponent’s question is nonsensical or outside the authors’ interests. My negative case is that the two anchors provide little population-specific research target; under the programme’s broad recognition gate, that may not be enough to defeat the mirror.
