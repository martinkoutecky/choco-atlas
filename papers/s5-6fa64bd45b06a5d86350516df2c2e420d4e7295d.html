The continuous mirror question

Given an explicitly listed, non-degenerate domain \(X\subseteq D^m\), decide whether there are Borel maps \(g_j:\Delta(D_j)\to D_j\), where \(D_j=\operatorname{pr}_jX\), such that for every population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(X)\), the evaluation \(A(\mu)_j=g_j(\mu_j)\) belongs to \(X\), with \(\mu_j(d)=\sum_{x\in X:x_j=d}\mu_x\), \(g_j(\delta_d)=d\), and \(g_j(\nu)\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\).

The model it lives in

Types are feasible evaluations \(x\in X\), and \(\mu_x\) is their population mass. The collective evaluation is chosen issue by issue from the marginal distributions; the question is whether one unanimous, supportive family of Borel rules works for every population distribution.

The objection that survived

The mirror classifies whether a uniform anonymous rule exists over all distributions, rather than computing an outcome for a particular society; that leaves it farther from instance-level population computation than Theorem 36’s algorithm may suggest.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the supportive IIA impossibility-domain question represented by Theorem 36. It leaves the paper’s gadget characterizations, binary-domain results, idempotent classification, and StrongDem discussion outside the proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is there a finite witness bound for failure of such a rule?
  • Can Borel rules be replaced by a finitely described class without changing feasibility?
  • What is the decision complexity as a function of \(|X|\), \(m\), and \(|D|\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a continuous, anonymous version of supportive evaluation aggregation. A type is a complete feasible evaluation \(x\in X\subseteq D^m\); mass \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of an atomless population holding it. This fits settings with many people but relatively few distinct judgment patterns, such as large surveys on logically linked propositions. The collective outcome remains a single evaluation in \(X\), so the population is what becomes continuous.

Here is the precise problem. Given an explicitly listed, non-degenerate domain \(X\subseteq D^m\), decide whether there are Borel maps \(g_j:\Delta(D_j)\to D_j\), where \(D_j=\operatorname{pr}_j X\), such that for every population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(X)\), the outcome
\[ A(\mu)_j=g_j(\mu_j),\qquad \mu_j(d)=\sum_{x\in X:\,x_j=d}\mu_x, \]
belongs to \(X\). Require unanimity, \(g_j(\delta_d)=d\), and supportiveness, \(g_j(\nu)\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\). The maps are the decision rule; there is no optimization objective. They use only each issue’s population shares, which is the distributional form of IIA.

My anchor is Theorem 36, proved in this paper. It gives an algorithm for deciding whether an explicit \(X\subseteq D^m\) is an impossibility domain under IIA, Supportiveness, and Non-dictatorship, with running time
\[ O\!\left(\operatorname{poly}(|X|,m,|D|)\,|D|^{m|D|^3}\right). \]
The algorithm first checks total blockedness; if \(X\) is not totally blocked, the domain is possible. Otherwise, it checks supportive ternary aggregators and returns possible exactly when it finds a non-dictatorial one. For fixed \(|D|\), this is a single-exponential upper bound in \(m\), not a polynomial-time result.

There is a concrete bridge to that theorem. Any continuous rule above, restricted to equal-mass societies of three voters, defines
\[ f_j(u_1,u_2,u_3) =g_j\!\left(\frac{\delta_{u_1}+\delta_{u_2}+\delta_{u_3}}{3}\right). \]
These operations are symmetric, supportive, and together map every triple from \(X\) back into \(X\). On a non-degenerate domain they cannot be projections onto a fixed voter, so they give a non-dictatorial ternary aggregator of the kind Theorem 36 checks. Thus a continuous “yes” instance must be possible under the paper’s finite test. The continuous question adds the requirement that the rule work coherently at every population share, not just on equal-mass finite profiles.

I think the authors could recognize this as their aggregation problem in an atomless, anonymous regime. The qualification is that classical non-dictatorship is stated for named voters, while a rule that sees only mass distributions has no named individuals to dictate. Anonymity is therefore part of the model, not an equivalent restatement of their condition. The paper itself discusses anonymity, which makes the change legible, but a referee could reasonably judge it a stronger, related problem rather than a direct mirror.

I would provisionally expect continuum-specific hardness (Class C): requiring compatible decisions across all real mass ratios adds constraints that the finite ternary test does not capture. That is a conjecture about this proposed problem, not a hardness result in the paper. The useful follow-up questions are whether continuous feasibility has a finite witness bound, whether Borel rules can be replaced by finitely described rules, and how its complexity depends on \(|X|\), \(m\), and \(|D|\).

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 36 classifies finite non-dictatorial aggregation, while this mirror imposes anonymous mass dependence. Its value is that it states a precise population-level decision problem and gives a checkable bridge back to a named result; its claim to be *the* continuous version of that theorem remains contestable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 36 is the paper’s strongest anchor, and it is a genuine complexity result. But its input is the finite domain \(X\), and its answer is whether \(X\) admits certain finite IIA aggregators. It does not take a society’s distribution \(\mu\) as input. The running time depends on \(|X|,m,|D|\); population size and mass precision do not enter. The classification is a property of the allowed evaluation patterns, unchanged across societies with different proportions of those patterns.

There are two ways to make a population-level question from it. Given one distribution \(\mu\), asking for a supported collective evaluation is easy: any evaluation in \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) has each coordinate in the corresponding marginal support. Issue-by-issue maps can be assigned those outputs at the marginals in question. Requiring the maps to work for *every* distribution avoids that triviality, but then the question is whether \(X\) admits a uniform family of aggregation functions over the whole simplex. No particular high-multiplicity society is being computed on; the continuum enters as the domain of a new universal existence problem.

That universal problem is the best version of the proposed mirror. It is coherent, and a large survey on linked judgments is a plausible setting. A Borel, supportive rule \(g_j\) would induce symmetric supportive aggregators at every finite arity. Theorem 36 tests for a supportive ternary aggregator, so it gives a necessary finite check. It does not settle whether such a rule exists: the rule must also fit all population shares coherently, while the theorem allows finite aggregators that may vary with arity and need not be anonymous. This gap could motivate a new classification, but the resulting question is an extension of the paper’s aggregation axioms rather than an algorithm for acting on a given high-multiplicity society.

The identity issue sharpens that gap. Retaining named identities makes each person a separate type, eroding the repeated-type regime. Collapsing people into evaluation types removes the named individuals whose dictatorship the theorem excludes. One could define a type-level or mass-level substitute for non-dictatorship, but that would be an additional condition. The paper’s discussion of anonymity makes this extension intelligible; it does not make it the same computational problem.

So my case against the anchor is about fit with the continuization programme, not about whether the proposed question is well-posed. Theorem 36 and its idempotent counterpart, Theorem 37, classify finite domains; they provide no population objective, intervention, or instance-specific computational task for continuous optimization to unlock. I would not claim the mirror cannot be worthwhile. The honest weakness in this negative case is that an anonymous, all-distributions aggregation classification is a credible research question; its value depends on whether the programme counts that new axiomatic existence problem as a computational mirror of this paper.
