The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rooted river tree with type masses μ_t that are positive binary rationals summing to one, per-capita supplies e_t, and rational jointly concave piecewise-linear direct utilities g_t, compute the per-capita withdrawals q_t in a continuous ANBS outcome. For each type t, define available water by F_t(q) = ( μ_t e_t + ∑_{s∈P(t)} μ_s(e_s-q_s) )/μ_t, where P(t) is its set of upstream ancestors; impose 0 ≤ μ_t q_t ≤ μ_t e_t + ∑_{s∈P(t)} μ_s(e_s-q_s). Is the resulting ANBS withdrawal vector a maximizer of ∑_t μ_t g_t(F_t(q),q_t), and how efficiently can it be computed?

The model it lives in

A continuum of participants distributed over a fixed finite tree of hydrological types. Type mass scales local supply and aggregate withdrawal; each type has a common per-capita withdrawal and utility, and the ANBS uses mass-weighted utilities and transfers.

The objection that survived

The model must treat same-type participants as sharing pooled water access at a fixed tree position; the paper does not specify that arrangement, and adding distinct positions may increase the number of types with the population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the ANBS water allocation in Theorem 4.4 and the transfer calculation in Theorem 4.5. It leaves aside the comparisons among ATS, strict UTI, and aspiration levels, as well as the ANRS participation-constraint results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the efficient allocation be computed tractably under broader benefit and cost function assumptions from the paper?
  • How well does the mass solution approximate finite basin agreements when participant counts at each type are integral?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow one: the paper has no named complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. It does have named, constructive results about computing its bargaining solution. Those are the anchors I would use; there is no hardness anchor to prefer.

Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper, is the lead. It says that if \((x^*,f^*,t^*)\) is the asymmetric Nash bargaining solution (ANBS), then \(x^*=x^{UW}\) and \(f^*=f^{UW}\): the negotiated water use and inflows equal the utilitarian-welfare-maximizing water allocation. Given the paper’s uniqueness assumption, the physical allocation is independent of the disagreement point and bargaining weights.

Theorem 4.5, also proved here, gives the transfer step. At the efficient water allocation, the transfer-maximizing ANBS has
\( \hat t_i=d_i+\alpha_i\bigl(w-\sum_j d_j\bigr)-u_i(f_i^*,x_i^*,0). \)
Equivalently, agent \(i\)’s final utility is \(d_i+\alpha_i(w-\sum_jd_j)\). This is a closed-form result for dividing the surplus after the water allocation is found.

Here is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for mirroring those results: a basin agreement involving many small irrigation cooperatives or municipal water-user associations, each with a seat in negotiations or ratification. A type records the full details relevant to the model: its position in a fixed, pooled river tree, per-capita local supply, benefit and cost functions, disagreement utility, and bargaining weight. Many associations share each of a small number \(\tau\) of types, so the number of participants can be large while the number of types stays modest. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants of type \(t\). Local supplies and withdrawals scale with that mass. This is an alternative to the paper’s country-level examples, while retaining its river externalities, disagreement points, transferable utility, and unanimity bargaining. The paper itself describes agents broadly enough to include agricultural communities and locations.

For precision, let \(T\) be the nodes of a finite rooted river tree, with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Write \(P(t)\) for the upstream ancestors of \(t\), \(e_t\) for its per-capita local supply, and \(q_t\) for its per-capita withdrawal. Its per-capita available water is
\( F_t(q)=\bigl(\mu_t e_t+\sum_{s\in P(t)}(\mu_s e_s-\mu_s q_s)\bigr)/\mu_t. \)
The type’s direct per-capita utility is \(g_t(F_t,q_t)=b_t(q_t)-c_t(F_t,q_t)\). With a per-capita monetary transfer \(r_t\), its utility is \(v_t=g_t(F_t,q_t)+r_t\), and the transfer budget is \(\sum_t\mu_t r_t\leq0\). Let \(d_t\) be its disagreement utility, and let \(\beta_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\beta_t=1\), be the type’s aggregate bargaining weight. The continuous ANBS maximizes \(\prod_t(v_t-d_t)^{\beta_t}\), subject to the river constraints \(0\leq\mu_tq_t\leq\mu_te_t+\sum_{s\in P(t)}(\mu_se_s-\mu_sq_s)\), individual rationality \(v_t\geq d_t\), and the transfer budget.

The Continuous ANBS Water Allocation problem is: given that instance, compute the water-use vector \(q\) in an ANBS outcome. Theorem 4.4’s mirror predicts that it is a maximizer of
\( W=\max_q\sum_t\mu_tg_t(F_t(q),q_t) \)
over the river constraints. For rational jointly concave piecewise-linear \(g_t\), this is an LP: the masses and the tree preserve the resource constraints, and the piecewise-linear welfare terms have a linear hypograph formulation. I expect this encoded version to be tractable, Class A. For the paper’s full function assumptions, however, Theorem 4.4 alone establishes no polynomial-time bound or convexity; the paper assumes uniqueness of the welfare maximum but does not establish a general complexity classification.

The Continuous ANBS Transfer Allocation problem fixes an efficient \(q^*\) and asks for the per-capita transfers \(r_t\) that maximize the same mass-weighted Nash product subject to individual rationality and the budget. Let \(W\) be the maximum total welfare above. The mass version of Theorem 4.5 predicts
\( v_t=d_t+\frac{\beta_t}{\mu_t}\bigl(W-\sum_s\mu_sd_s\bigr), \)
and therefore
\( r_t=d_t+\frac{\beta_t}{\mu_t}\bigl(W-\sum_s\mu_sd_s\bigr)-g_t(F_t(q^*),q_t^*). \)
This is Class A once \(W\) and \(q^*\) are known: the remaining computation is a closed-form transfer calculation. When participants have equal individual bargaining power, the aggregate type weight is \(\beta_t=\mu_t\), recovering a common per-capita share of surplus.

The continuous object in this mirror is the population of negotiating participants; the water allocation is already divisible in the paper. The authors would plausibly recognize the mirror because it keeps their underlying water-sharing and ANBS questions intact. Its credibility depends on a real regime: many participants must share a fixed type-level river structure, and supplies must scale appropriately with population mass.

The weak point is that the paper’s agents are themselves hydrological locations or countries. Their upstream/downstream relations matter, so preference similarity alone cannot justify aggregation. The mirror only holds where many associations can be represented by a modest, exact type-level tree of pooled reaches. If each participant has a distinct position or network effect, those details become part of the type and \(\tau\) may grow with the population. The country-level treaty cases in the paper are unlikely to be high-multiplicity instances.

Further questions follow directly: when does type aggregation preserve river feasibility; can the paper’s broader cost-function assumptions yield tractable welfare optimization; and how does the mass solution approximate a finite basin agreement with integer participant counts? The positive case is therefore for these particular high-multiplicity water-sharing questions, not a claim that the paper establishes a general complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 4.4 identifies the water allocation in the ANBS with a utilitarian welfare maximizer; Theorem 4.5 gives the transfers once that allocation is known. Neither states a complexity bound or classifies an algorithmic problem. The proposed LP, meanwhile, depends on imposing rational piecewise-linear utilities. The paper’s assumptions do not give that finite encoding or guarantee the joint concavity needed for the LP. This is a plausible new computational extension, but it is not a complexity result from the paper.

The best population scenario is many irrigation associations bargaining over a basin. The difficulty is that the paper’s agents are hydrological positions: each one controls an extraction point, and its effect on others depends on the river tree. A type therefore has to include its position and network relations, not just its preferences and costs. In a fixed basin those relations distinguish the agents. Put many associations at the same point and their withdrawals can be aggregated, but that treats them as one water-control position; it does not create more river agents of the kind the paper models. Grow the population by adding more positions, and the type space must preserve the added network structure. The proposed finite list of types then needs a new spatial aggregation model to capture those relations.

Theorem 4.4’s continuous water-allocation question does not resolve that mismatch. The proposed per-capita model can be coherent if supplies and network structure scale with the population, but scaling local supply with mass represents additional water sources or catchments, not simply more participants at a fixed location. A more faithful model of repeated tributaries would need to specify how their flows join the shared river and how their agents bargain. That could be worthwhile, but it is extra modelling work beyond the paper’s result.

Theorem 4.5 is a weaker independent anchor. Its transfer formula extends naturally if each type’s mass and aggregate bargaining weight are well defined, but then it is a formula for dividing surplus after the water allocation is computed. It does not provide a separate computational result in the paper, and it cannot supply the missing population model for Theorem 4.4.

I would therefore oppose calling this a continuous mirror of the paper’s computational results: those results are not complexity results, and the proposed population interpretation needs a new account of how agents map onto the river network. But I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The paper explicitly allows agricultural communities as agents, and a basin with many similar communities on repeated tributaries is plausible. The negative case is strongest against the claimed anchors as mirrors of this paper; it is weaker against a new, carefully specified high-multiplicity water-sharing model.
