The continuous mirror question

Given finite proposer- and acceptor-type sets \(\mathcal P,\mathcal A\), rational masses \(\mu_p,\nu_a\), strict rankings over counterpart types, and reward values, let \(x^\star\) be the proposer-optimal stable mass flow, with each individual receiving at most one partner. Does there exist a completely uncoupled rule, using only each proposer's own past actions, rewards, and action set, such that for every \(\eta>0\) and \(p<1\) there are \(T,N_0\) for which \(\Pr(\lVert x^{t,N}-x^\star\rVert_1\le\eta)\ge p\) for every \(t\ge T\) and every compatible \(N\)-agent realization with \(N\ge N_0\)?

The model it lives in

A two-sided nonatomic market with finitely many proposer and acceptor types, each carrying a rational mass and a shared ranking over counterpart types. Individuals still receive integral matches; the aggregate decision is a flow \(x_{pa}\), with type supplies imposing capacity bounds. Proposers learn from their own actions and rewards, while acceptor types allocate incoming proposals by their rankings. The target is the proposer-optimal stable flow \(x^\star\).

The objection that survived

The paper selects the proposer-optimal match through rare transitions in each finite-state chain; their probabilities and ordering may change as the population grows, and the theorem supplies no market-size-uniform horizon.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's decentralized convergence claim over type masses; it leaves the simulation and the paper's finite-market proof and auxiliary matching lemmas unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a completely uncoupled learning rule make the type-mass flows converge to \(x^\star\) for every finite-support market?
  • Can the convergence horizon be bounded polynomially in the number of types, encoding length, accuracy, and confidence, uniformly over sufficiently large finite realizations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: Theorem 1, proved in this paper, gives an algorithmic convergence guarantee, though not a conventional complexity result. It says that for every matching market \(M\) and every \(p<1\), there is a learning rule \(\Gamma\) of the form in (1) such that, if all proposers follow it, then at every sufficiently late timestep the action profile corresponds to the proposer-optimal stable match \(\mu^\star\) with probability at least \(p\). The theorem imposes no restriction on market size or preference structure. It gives no bound on how late “sufficiently late” is. The paper has no named theorem asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another standard complexity class; if only such classifications count as anchors, there is no qualifying anchor.

A concrete mirror is Type-Mass POSM Learning. Its input is finite sets of proposer types \(\mathcal P\) and acceptor types \(\mathcal A\), with binary-rational masses \(\mu_p,\nu_a\) summing to one on each side, and complete preference and utility descriptions for each type. A type records everything relevant to matching: its side, its preferences over opposite-side types, and its utility values. In a labor-market scenario, for example, many firms could share a job profile and many applicants a standardized skill profile, with \(|\mathcal P|+|\mathcal A|=\tau\) far smaller than the number \(N\) of agents.

A matching is a mass flow \(x_{pa}\ge 0\), where \(x_{pa}\) is the mass of proposer type \(p\) matched to acceptor type \(a\), with row and column sums bounded by \(\mu_p\) and \(\nu_a\). Each person still receives at most one partner; only the aggregate flow is fractional. Define \(D_p(a)\) as the mass of type \(p\) assigned to a less-preferred type than \(a\), or left unmatched, and \(S_a(p)\) as the capacity of type \(a\) that is vacant or held by less-preferred proposer types. A flow is stable when \(D_p(a)=0\) or \(S_a(p)=0\) for every pair \(p,a\). Take \(x^\star\) to be the proposer-proposing deferred-acceptance outcome for these type masses, splitting mass at a capacity cutoff when necessary.

At each round, proposers choose an acceptor type or remain single. Acceptors use their rankings to accept the most-preferred proposals up to their capacity. The question is whether there is a completely uncoupled rule \(\Gamma\)—each proposer uses only their own past actions, rewards, and action set—whose induced matching flows approach \(x^\star\). More precisely, for every tolerance \(\eta>0\) and confidence \(p<1\), does there exist a horizon \(T\) and scale \(N_0\) such that, in every sufficiently large \(N\)-agent realization of the input masses, for all \(t\ge T\),
\[ \Pr\!\left(\lVert x^{t,N}-x^\star\rVert_1\le\eta\right)\ge p? \]
A solution is a local rule and a proof of this guarantee. A further algorithmic question is whether \(T\) can be bounded polynomially in the number of types, the encoding length \(L\), and the accuracy and confidence parameters.

The regime is credible: the paper itself motivates decentralized learning in massive online markets, and the mirror preserves its central challenge—proposers learn preferences from match rewards without centralized coordination. It is also a type-level matching problem, not a move to fractional outcomes as the goal. I would expect a tractable result in a fixed-support regime, since the aggregate state can be described using finitely many type and learning-state masses. That is a research conjecture; Theorem 1 does not provide a rate or establish it.

The weakest point is that the paper ranks named individuals strictly, while this mirror ranks counterpart types. That groups agents with ties at the type level and can change the stable outcome. Theorem 1’s finite-state and stable-matching arguments do not automatically survive that aggregation. The case remains plausible because shared preferences within a type are exactly the high-multiplicity assumption, and the paper’s large-market motivation fits it directly. The proposed mirror covers Theorem 1’s decentralized convergence result; it does not claim to mirror a hardness result or to settle the paper’s other claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 1, but it is an existence and convergence theorem for a stochastic learning process, not a complexity result. It gives no bound on how long convergence takes as the market grows, on the computation needed to update a strategy, or on any exact or approximate algorithm over a society distribution. That makes it a poor fit for ChoCo’s stated computational gap.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is coherent: group genuinely interchangeable proposers and acceptors into types, give each type a mass, and ask whether local reward-based learning approaches a proposer-optimal stable flow. The problem is what that question becomes. If preferences are supplied, type-capacitated deferred acceptance already computes the target; if preferences remain unknown and learning remains decentralized, the question is about convergence of a mean-field learning dynamic. The latter is a plausible research question, but it belongs to the analytic learning and dynamics tradition the programme distinguishes from its complexity questions. Adding a polynomial convergence-time target could make it computational, but that would be a new finite-time learning result, not a complexity mirror furnished by this paper.

The proposed finite-population approximation does not bridge that gap automatically. The paper proves convergence for each finite market without a horizon bound uniform in market size. Its proof selects the POSM through rare transitions in a finite-state Markov chain; taking a continuum limit changes those transitions and can change the order of the long-time and large-population limits. Establishing whether a polynomial, population-uniform horizon exists would be substantial new work, but it is precisely the extra question the paper leaves open.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit: no result here charts the computational landscape that ChoCo set out to study. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A fixed-support, high-multiplicity learning model is sensible, and its mean-field convergence could be worthwhile in its own right; it simply does not, on this paper’s evidence, fill the programme’s computational gap.
