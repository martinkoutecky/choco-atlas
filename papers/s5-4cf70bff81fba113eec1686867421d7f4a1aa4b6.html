The continuous mirror question

Given rational worker and firm cohort masses, an explicitly listed finite profile set \(\Omega\), payoff tables, information partitions, integer wages, and an initial mass matching, can an algorithm polynomial in the number of supported cohort and information types and the binary input length output a finite sequence of positive rational mass rematches and paper-consistent information updates ending at an individually rational, unblocked, information-stable state?

The model it lives in

Represent each side by masses over complete types, including payoff-relevant information states; represent an allocation by match flows \(x_{h,r,p}\ge 0\) at integer wages, and each step by a type-pair and transferred mass \(\delta>0\). The task is to construct a finite path ending at a state with no feasible positive-mass block and no further information implied by individual rationality and nonblocking; there is no welfare objective.

The objection that survived

The proponent’s basic “find any path” formulation has no runtime or output-size target; on rational grids replication settles existence, so the polynomial-time compressed construction is an added specification rather than a guarantee in Theorem 2.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the path-to-stability results in Theorems 2 and 3 under worst-case blocking and endogenous information updates. It leaves aside Theorem 1’s static equivalence and the alternative Bayesian-stability discussion.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a path be constructed in time polynomial in the number of supported types, \(|\Omega|\), and the binary input length when the common mass denominator may be exponentially large?
  • Does almost-sure finite convergence persist if the process permits arbitrary positive rational mass transfers rather than replicated individual moves?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a mirror of the paper’s path-to-stability process. A caveat first: the paper proves no result of the form “in P,” “NP-hard,” or “FPT.” Its usable anchors are named algorithmic convergence theorems, with no running-time bound. They support a computational mirror, but not a standard complexity classification.

A plausible regime is a large labor market with repeated worker cohorts and employer cohorts: many applicants share the same productivity and other relevant characteristics, and many firms share the same valuations and information. For example, there might be hundreds of thousands of workers and firms but only tens or hundreds of complete types. A firm’s information partition belongs in its type, along with its valuation; firms with the same partition and valuation are interchangeable. That is the high-multiplicity premise, not a distortion of the model.

Here is a mass version that keeps the paper’s central ingredients. Let \(H\) and \(R\) be finite sets of worker and firm cohorts, with rational masses \(\alpha_h\) and \(\beta_r\), respectively. Let \(\Omega\) be an explicitly listed finite set of possible worker-type profiles: in profile \(\omega\), cohort \(h\) has worker type \(w_h(\omega)\). Each firm cohort \(r\) has a common firm type \(f_r\) and a shared partition \(\Pi_r\) of \(\Omega\). The type-dependent payoff tables \(\nu_{wf}\) and \(\phi_{wf}\), the true profile, and an initial allocation are part of the input. An allocation is a mass-preserving matching of worker and firm cohorts, including unmatched mass, with integer wages; cohorts can split across matches and information states. A blocking move transfers some positive rational mass \(\delta\) of workers and firms to a new match at wage \(p\), subject to the paper’s worker-acceptance and firm worst-case-improvement conditions. It releases the displaced mass and updates information when rematching, or when its absence is observed. Stability means individual rationality, no positive-mass blocking move, and no further information implied by individual rationality and the absence of blocking. There is no welfare objective in the paper: the task is to reach such a stable state.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. Under Assumption 1, which restricts permitted payments to integers, start from any state. Whenever the state is blocked, select a blocking combination from a distribution with full support over the available combinations; the distribution may depend on the current state but not on the history. The resulting random learning-blocking path reaches a stable state with probability one, after finitely many rematchings.

That anchors Continuous Random Mass Convergence: given the rational cohort masses, payoff tables, possible profiles and partitions, initial allocation, and a specified state-dependent, full-support rule for choosing legal mass-blocking moves, does the resulting process reach a stable mass allocation with probability one in finite time? A solution is a proof of convergence or a counterexample process; a stronger computational version would also give a bound on the expected number of moves in terms of the support size and input bit length. The random rule must be specified: in a continuum, choosing one named agent changes zero mass. One natural version chooses from type-and-status block labels, then draws a positive rational \(\delta\) with full support over feasible move sizes.

The second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. Under the same integer-payment assumption, from any initial state there exists a finite learning-blocking path to a stable state. It anchors Continuous Mass Path-to-Stability: given the mass-market instance and initial state above, find a finite sequence of legal moves \((h,r,p,\delta)\) and information updates ending in a stable state, or report that no such path exists. The source theorem makes the finite-path guarantee a natural conjecture for the mirror. Indeed, on a rational mass grid one can replicate each mass unit as an individual and use the paper’s path with moves of size \(1/N\), for a common denominator \(N\). That establishes the qualitative guarantee on this grid. It says nothing about how to construct a path efficiently from binary-encoded masses, or about a genuinely fractional mass process.

For both mirrors, my provisional expectation for the *unrestricted fractional-move computational problem* is a Class C candidate: the discrete proof relies on a finite state space, while fractional moves create many more possible allocations and paths. That is a reason to investigate continuum-specific behavior, not a claim of hardness. On the quantized rational grid, the qualitative convergence results should carry over by replication; the open computational question is whether an algorithm can find a path or characterize convergence without expanding a market whose size \(N\) may be exponential in the input bit length. Theorem 1, which equates the paper’s stable outcomes with outcomes supported by a stable state for some information partition, helps justify the terminal condition, but is not itself a complexity anchor.

The mirror covers the paper’s path-to-stability results under worst-case blocking and endogenous information updating. It does not try to continuize every static characterization or the alternative Bayesian-stability discussion. Its weakest point is the process co-modification: the paper rematches named pairs, whereas an aggregate process must say how much mass moves at once. On a mass grid, this is faithful through individual-sized moves; with arbitrary fractional moves, the dynamics may differ materially. The second open question is whether a suitable mass-move rule preserves finite or almost-sure convergence, and whether any convergence bound depends polynomially on the number of types and the mass encoding length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorems 2 and 3 are convergence results, not complexity results. Theorem 2 guarantees that some finite learning-blocking path exists; Theorem 3 shows that a specified finite-market random process is absorbed almost surely. Neither asks how hard it is to find a path, bounds its length, or classifies a computational problem. The proposed mirrors therefore need an extra choice of task—path construction, short-path optimization, or a convergence-time bound—that the paper itself does not make.

For rational cohort masses, the qualitative path question is already answered by replication. Choose a common denominator \(N\), replace each cohort by the corresponding number of individual workers or firms, and apply Theorem 2. Theorem 3 likewise applies to the replicated market’s individual rematching process. This establishes the grid version’s qualitative guarantees, but says nothing about efficient computation: \(N\) can be exponential in the masses’ bit length. Asking for a path without expanding that market could be interesting, but it is a new succinct-witness problem, not a continuous counterpart of either theorem’s claim. The paper gives no reason to prefer finding *any* path over finding a short one or computing a compressed description.

The replication also exposes an obstacle to the proposed support-size model. A worker’s complete type for this paper’s purposes may include their position in a type profile in \(W^I\); a firm’s may include its valuation and its partition over those identity-indexed profiles. Firms observe their own employees’ types, and rematching and its absence update information. Grouping workers by productivity and firms by valuation can therefore merge agents whose information or learning histories differ. Including those distinctions in the types can make the number of types grow with the market, or make the information representation very large. An anonymous, finite-template information structure could restore a bounded cohort model, but that is an additional restriction on the paper’s central information heterogeneity.

The random mirror has a related problem. In the paper, a move is a rematch by a named pair, and the proof of Theorem 3 uses a finite-state Markov chain. A positive-mass move instead coordinates a bloc of agents; it can reveal different information from a sequence of individual rematches. If moves remain atom-sized, the model is the replicated finite market. If moves transfer arbitrary mass, the state space and transition rule change, and “full support” over move sizes needs a new interpretation. The proposed label-and-mass rule is a possible model, but its almost-sure finite convergence would be a new dynamical theorem, not a result inherited from Theorem 3.

These points defeat the two mirrors *as stated*: the path-existence question on rational grids is settled by replication, while the genuinely fractional process requires modeling choices that the paper does not supply. They do not, however, establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A labor market with large interchangeable cohorts and a small set of shared information templates is plausible. For that regime, efficient path construction in a succinct representation could be a meaningful high-multiplicity problem. So the honest negative case is strongest against treating Theorems 2 and 3 themselves as computational anchors; it is weak against the broader possibility of a carefully specified cohort-symmetric research question.
