What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror: Theorems 12–14 concern mechanism structure and exchange-complexity measures, and the paper's other results likewise provide no computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered result about the computational complexity of an underlying problem—such as membership in P, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. So there are no valid anchors, and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The tempting results are Theorem 12, which says minimal mechanisms are graphical \(G\)-mechanisms, and Theorem 13, which says that for \(m>3\), the star, cycle, and complete mechanisms are precisely the strongly minimal ones, with \((\pi,\tau)\) values \((4,2)\), \((2,m-1)\), and \((m(m-1),1)\), respectively. The paper cites [9] for the corresponding classification within \(M_g(m)\) and proves the extension to \(M^*(m)\) here. Theorem 14 says that for any positive \(\lambda,\mu\), the star uniquely maximizes \(\lambda\pi+\mu\tau\) for sufficiently large \(m\). These are structural results about a mechanism’s exchange and price complexity, not results about algorithms or computational complexity.

A plausible population setting would be a large exchange market with many traders grouped by identical admissible offers and market access, represented by masses of trader types. The authors would likely recognize a continuum of traders as compatible with their framework: the paper itself notes that its analysis extends to that setting. But its results classify mechanisms by commodity graphs and properties that do not depend on population multiplicity. Making trader mass continuous therefore supplies no computational mirror of those results. That is the weakest point, and it rules out a stronger honest case here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the level of the paper’s actual results: it contains no computational complexity result of the kind ChoCo studies. Its “complexity” measures are \(\tau_{ij}\), the number of exchange steps; \(\pi_{ij}\), the number of offer components affecting a price ratio; and \(k_i\), the dimension of an offer space. The paper gives no encoded computational problem, input length, runtime bound, or complexity classification to mirror.

The proponent names Theorems 12, 13, and 14 as tempting candidates, and each fails for the same basic reason. Theorem 12 characterizes minimal mechanisms as \(G\)-mechanisms. Theorem 13 classifies strongly minimal mechanisms and reports their \((\pi,\tau)\) values. Theorem 14 compares those measures as the number of commodities grows. A stronger proposed mirror might ask an algorithm to find a minimal mechanism, classify one, or optimize a weighted combination of \(\pi\) and \(\tau\). For a general mechanism, though, the paper supplies no finite encoding of the return maps an algorithm would receive. Restrict the input to a graph-encoded \(G\)-mechanism, and the proposed task becomes a new graph problem, with no trader-type distribution or continuous society specified by these theorems.

There is a credible high-multiplicity setting: many traders can share a type describing their available offers or market access. The authors explicitly say their analysis remains valid with a continuum of traders, so this is evidence that the regime makes sense. But Theorems 12–14 are statements about mechanisms and commodity graphs. To build a computational task around them, one would need to add a population-dependent objective or constraint that the paper does not study. Adding trader utilities, endowments, welfare goals, or strategic choices could supply one, but would define a new problem rather than mirror a result here.

The closest stronger proposal is to compute prices for a continuum of traders. Theorem 9 establishes a unique price ray, and the graphical mechanism gives an equation for prices from aggregate offers. That could motivate a separate market-computation study. The paper, however, treats general mechanisms as abstract maps, without a finite representation for computing their prices; restricting to graphical mechanisms supplies one, but the resulting computational question is an extension of the pricing formula, not a complexity result the paper reports. It does not rescue the proposed anchors.

So the case against a continuous mirror of this paper is strong: the population can naturally be continuous, yet the named results provide no computational question whose complexity the programme could chart. The limit of this argument is the word “any”: a useful adjacent project on market computation is conceivable. The defensible negative claim is narrower—such a project would be new work built around the paper’s model, rather than a continuous mirror of its results.
