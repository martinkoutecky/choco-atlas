The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is shared policy design for a large recommender cohort. Picture a streaming service with millions of users but a much smaller set of recurring preference and response profiles. A type \(t\) would specify everything the problem uses about a user: their response probabilities for each query after each possible history, their stopping behavior, and the utility they receive from the fixed recommender’s terminal recommendation. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of users of type \(t\).

A precise candidate is Continuous-Population Preference-Elicitation Policy Optimization. An instance gives finite query and response sets, a horizon \(T\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), type-specific response kernels \(\rho_t(r\mid h,q)\), and terminal utilities \(u_t(h)\). The decision is a single history-dependent policy \(\pi\), used across the cohort, mapping each partial history to a query. Its value is
\[ F_\mu(\pi)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbb{E}_{\rho_t,\pi}[u_t(H_T)]. \]
The question is whether there is such a policy with \(F_\mu(\pi)\ge K\), for a given rational threshold \(K\); a solution is the policy itself. This preserves the paper’s sequential query-selection problem and its recommendation-utility objective, while treating the deployed user cohort as the continuous object.

The scenario is plausible if many users really do share the same session-level response and utility behavior: for example, a large service with millions of users and hundreds or thousands of recurring profiles. It is less convincing if profiles are merely coarse approximations to highly individual users. The mirror covers the paper’s policy-quality comparisons in §8: in particular, its empirical finding that model-free policies are often comparable to or better than model-based ones, and usually faster at a given search budget. That evidence motivates cohort-level policy design, but it does not establish a complexity result.

There is a decisive limitation under your anchor rule: the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational-complexity result. Its results are empirical comparisons in §8, not named hardness or algorithmic results of the required kind. So there are zero qualifying anchors, and this candidate cannot honestly be presented as a problem *per anchor*. It is an unanchored mirror proposal.

For that candidate, my expectation would be hardness transfers (Class B): finite rational cohort masses can be represented by finite populations, and aggregation alone does not seem to remove the combinatorial difficulty of choosing a contingent query policy. Further questions include how complexity depends on \(|Q|\), \(\tau\), and \(T\); whether additive approximation is efficient; and whether MCTS or learned predictors can give guarantees for aggregate utility.

The soft spot is substantial: §5 already gives the model-based formulation a prior \(P(u)\) over user embeddings, and the paper evaluates policies over sampled users. The cohort objective may therefore look like a population reading of a distribution the paper already uses, rather than a genuinely new computational question. A large-cohort deployment is a reasonable scenario the authors might recognize, but the missing named anchor and the closeness to the paper’s existing prior keep this from being a strong anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the anchor requirement: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting the complexity of a computational problem. Section 8 reports empirical comparisons of learned policies, utility, and runtime. Those results may motivate further work, but they do not provide a named complexity result to mirror. The proponent identified no qualifying anchors, so the proposed candidate cannot make this paper an anchored ChoCo case.

The cohort policy-optimization proposal also adds little population-specific computation. For a fixed shared policy \(\pi\), users’ sessions are independent, and \(F_\mu(\pi)\) is the expected terminal utility of a randomly sampled user. The type distribution can be marginalized into history-dependent response probabilities and expected terminal utilities; optimizing the policy then has the same sequential-planning structure already set out in §§2 and 4 and evaluated over sampled users in §8. The masses change the evaluation distribution, while the policy decisions remain per session. With explicit response and utility tables, policy search can use backward induction over the history tree; with compact models, the complexity depends on those models and the planning problem.

A cohort budget or fairness constraint could couple users and create a real population-level optimization problem. That would be a new extension, though: this paper supplies no result about such constraints to mirror. So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored continuous mirror. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists; a genuinely coupled cohort problem might be worthwhile, but it would need its own motivation and anchor.
