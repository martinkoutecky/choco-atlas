The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify an eligible anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are axiomatic characterizations, not results about computational complexity or algorithms. For example, Theorem 1, proved here, says that a rule satisfies additivity, weak equal treatment of equals, stand-alone pair, and any one of maximum aspirations, non-negativity, or weak upper bound if and only if it is a split rule. Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes families of compromise rules under additivity, weak equal treatment of equals, pairwise reallocation proofness, and one of those bounds. Neither asks whether an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The tempting population mirror would make teams, rather than viewers, the continuum: types would describe clubs with the same relevant audience interactions, mass would be their share of the league, and an audience kernel would describe the revenue generated by games between types. But that changes the paper’s fixed finite set of teams and its audience matrix into a variable-population model. The paper itself leaves variable population for future work. Moreover, the audience is already an aggregate in each matrix entry; taking viewers to a continuum would continuize the wrong object.

So there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous problem to, and no anchor-specific question or expected complexity outcome to state. A continuous league-allocation model might be worth developing separately, but calling it a mirror of a computational result in this paper would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive problem: there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes split rules, and Theorem 2 characterizes compromise rules. Neither poses an algorithmic problem or makes a complexity claim. Naming either theorem does not supply the missing computational question.

The strongest population mirror would treat teams as a continuum, with types encoding clubs’ relevant audience interactions and an audience kernel replacing the finite matrix. That could be a sensible model for a large league. But the paper fixes the team set and its arbitrary pairwise audiences; it explicitly leaves variable-population extensions for future work. Such an extension could ask new axiomatic questions, but those are outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Treating viewers as the continuum would instead continuize the wrong object.

One could invent an algorithmic task around the model, but it would need a new computational problem, input regime, and objective. Evaluating one of the displayed rules is not a computational result of the paper. I can’t rule out a worthwhile project built from these ideas; the negative case is narrower and stronger: no continuous-population scenario turns this paper’s axiomatic characterizations into a computational mirror without adding a different problem.
