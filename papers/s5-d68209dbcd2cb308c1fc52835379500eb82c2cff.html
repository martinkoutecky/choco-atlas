What the mirror covers

No named computational result is available to mirror; the paper’s analytical propositions and numerical comparisons are outside the computational anchor requirement.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that the paper’s setting makes a continuous population entirely natural: it assumes a continuum of homogeneous workers and a continuum of identical firms, with the worker population normalized to one. In the simplest type description, there is one worker type, with mass \(1\). That supports the plausibility of high multiplicity, but it also means continuizing the population would largely restate the model the authors already use.

There is no eligible computational anchor in the paper. Its named results do not classify a problem as tractable, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally complex. The closest is Proposition 1, proved here: under perfectly transferable utility, the Nash, Egalitarian, and Kalai-Smorodinsky solutions yield the same aggregate wage equation, \(w=\frac{1}{2}z+\frac{1}{2}(y+\gamma\theta)\), and thus the same labor market equilibrium. That is an analytical equivalence result, not a computational one; the paper’s numerical exercises compare outcomes rather than analyze algorithmic complexity.

So I cannot honestly name a continuous problem per anchor, or predict its complexity, without inventing an anchor the paper does not contain. Computing equilibria from the model’s equations could be a follow-up question, but this paper does not establish it as a complexity result, and its population is already continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent gives no actual computational anchor. The only named candidate is Proposition 1, and it establishes an analytical equivalence between bargaining rules, not the complexity of a computational problem. That is the strongest point against a mirror here. The fact that the paper already uses a continuum is not, by itself, a novelty collision: this is an analytic treatment, not an existing computational treatment of a continuous society.

The stronger version of a Proposition 1 mirror would ask for an algorithm to compute the equilibrium from model parameters, or to test whether the bargaining rules agree. But the paper’s symmetric model reduces the market state to unemployment and tightness, and its bargaining conditions to scalar wage equations. Under transferable utility, Proposition 1 gives the common wage equation directly; under partial transferability, the paper gives implicit equations and analyzes equilibrium properties. Those could motivate numerical computation, but the paper supplies no finite input encoding for the matching or utility functions, no decision problem, and no complexity measure. Without those, there is no defined complexity question to mirror. Encoding arbitrary functions or adding many kinds of worker and firm would create a computational problem, but its complexity would depend on those new choices.

A more plausible extension would give workers different risk preferences or productivities and describe their proportions by a distribution over types. That makes multiplicity meaningful, but it removes the paper’s homogeneous-worker and symmetric-match assumptions. The resulting task—computing equilibrium outcomes in a heterogeneous search market—could be worth studying in its own right. It is difficult to present it as a mirror of a named result in this paper, though: none of the paper’s propositions concerns a distribution over agent types or an algorithmic task over such a distribution. The paper’s continuum population establishes that mass is natural in its economic model; it does not supply the missing computational target.

So the negative case is strong for a direct mirror and weaker for the universal claim. A heterogeneous computational extension is conceivable. But it would need a new model and a new computational question, rather than extracting a continuous computational problem from the results this paper actually proves.
