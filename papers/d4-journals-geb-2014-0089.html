The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved in the paper; it is an exact structural characterization of the welfare optimum, not a claim that the optimization problem is in P. I would use it as the lead for a positive mirror, while being clear about that limit.

Theorem 2 says that for an integer question budget \(k^*\ge2\), an informationally optimal mechanism exists. In every such mechanism, for some buyer \(i\), the seller asks \(i\) \(k^*-1\) questions and the other buyer one question, in the order \((i,\ldots,i,-i)\). Here “informationally optimal” means optimal welfare under truthful answers. The paper then invokes Fadel and Segal’s Proposition 6 to support implementing the relevant allocation rule with transfers.

My proposed mirror is a continuum of limited-communication bilateral auctions. Imagine a platform operating many one-good auction desks, each with two buyers. A desk’s type \(t\) specifies the two buyers’ independent valuation distributions and its communication costs; let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of desks of that type. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime if a platform runs many similar micro-auctions, with many desks sharing the same pair of valuation laws. It also fits the paper’s motivation of limited communication in settings with many licences or transactions.

The continuous problem is: given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, valuation distributions \(F_{t1},F_{t2}\) with positive densities on \([0,1]\), a maximum \(Q\) of questions per desk, and a pooled budget \(B\in[2,Q]\) questions per unit mass, choose welfare-maximizing mechanisms for the desks. A solution specifies fractions \(x_{t,q,i}\ge0\) of type-\(t\) desks assigned \(q\in\{2,\ldots,Q\}\) questions, with buyer \(i\) questioned first, plus the threshold questions and transfers for each assigned mechanism. It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{q,i}x_{t,q,i}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t,q,i}q\,x_{t,q,i}\le B. \]
Each local mechanism asks \(i\) \(q-1\) threshold questions, then asks the other buyer one final threshold question; it awards the good to the buyer with the higher conditional expected valuation. The objective is to maximize the mass-weighted sum of the desks’ expected buyer-value welfare. The threshold levels remain part of the optimization, so the question is about the auction problem itself, not an encoding of it.

The connection to Theorem 2 is direct at each desk: it supplies the form of an optimal protocol for every chosen \(q\). The mass assignment is the added high-multiplicity layer. In a finite platform with many desks of each type, the \(x_{t,q,i}\) describe proportions assigned to each question budget; the continuous model is the natural relaxation of assigning integer numbers of desks. This mirrors the paper’s total-question-budget result while pooling that budget across a population of auctions.

I would provisionally expect this to be Class A when the per-type, per-budget threshold optimization can be computed efficiently: the population-level allocation is a finite linear program over types and question budgets, and Theorem 2 sharply restricts the local protocols. But Theorem 2 does not establish an efficient algorithm for finding optimal thresholds, particularly when \(Q\) is an input. So the paper supports the structure of the mirror, not a polynomial-time claim for the full problem. The mirror covers Theorem 2’s total-question-budget setting; it does not cover Theorem 1’s fixed per-buyer question quotas.

The weakest point is the choice of population unit. The continuum here is over auction desks, not over buyers inside a single auction. The buyers’ valuations were already continuously distributed in the paper; that alone is not continuization of society. Pooling communication capacity across many desks is a plausible extension—especially given the paper’s discussion of question costs—but it changes the original single-seller, single-good setting. If the authors require the population itself to be the buyers in one auction, this is a weaker mirror. The natural next questions are whether the local threshold problem is efficiently solvable as \(Q\) grows, and how quickly the continuous portfolio optimum approximates finite desk populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the paper’s strongest possible anchor: it characterizes the optimal protocol under a total question budget. But the paper has no complexity result, and its inputs are two buyers’ value distributions, not a population of agents represented by type masses. A buyer’s distribution is uncertainty about that buyer’s value; it is not the fraction of a society with a given type. Nor does the paper specify a finite encoding for arbitrary densities, so a complexity question about computing its optimal thresholds needs an additional input model.

The proposed auction-desk mirror supplies a plausible population by treating independent auctions as units. Its best version would use many repeated bilateral auctions, group desks by their buyers’ distribution laws and communication costs, and pool a per-desk question budget. That makes a coherent portfolio-optimization problem, but the mass variable only allocates question budgets across separate copies. Each desk’s threshold problem is unchanged; once its optimal welfare \(W_t(q)\) is known, the outer problem is a finite linear program over desks’ types and question budgets. Theorem 2 gives the form of each local protocol, but the continuum adds no leverage for finding its thresholds. This looks like a high-multiplicity relaxation of a portfolio problem, not a continuous-population mirror of the paper’s computational core.

The more direct population mirror fares worse. A continuum of buyers competing for the paper’s one indivisible good no longer has the bilateral structure Theorem 2 characterizes. A type distribution records masses, while the outcome must select an individual winner; with atomless types, the highest value may only be a supremum, and type masses do not identify a particular buyer. Moving to many goods or replicated auctions can restore a well-defined aggregate allocation, but that changes the model’s central object and returns to the portfolio construction.

So the strongest negative case is that the only convincing high-multiplicity scenario puts the population outside the auction—at the level of repeated instances—and leaves the theorem’s local optimization untouched. This is a meaningful adjacent question if ChoCo wants to study portfolios of auctions. It is a weak fit for a programme whose target is the computational effect of making the society itself continuous. The case is not airtight: if the programme accepts auction instances as population units, the desk model is coherent. But its main novelty would be pooled resource allocation, not continuization of the paper’s buyers.
