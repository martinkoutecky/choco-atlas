The continuous mirror question

Given \(p,\alpha,\beta\) and an exact finite rational polyhedral subdivision of \(\Delta_{k-1}\times\Delta_{\ell-1}\), with each cell labeled by a subset of candidates, decide whether the simplex vertices can be bijectively labeled by \(X_\alpha=\{x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{p}:\sum_a x_a=\alpha\}\) and \(Y_\beta=\{y\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{p}:\sum_a y_a=\beta\}\) so that at every \((q,r)\) the supplied label equals \(\arg\max_a(\sum_i q_i x^{(i)}_a+\sum_j r_j y^{(j)}_a)\). If so, output the bijections.

The model it lives in

Each bloc has mass \(1/2\), fixed card allotment, and a distribution over integral card-allocation types; the type masses are \(q\) and \(r\). The decision is whether hidden bijections from simplex vertices to allocation types make the supplied response map agree everywhere with aggregate-card maximization. This is a feasibility recognition problem with no separate objective.

The objection that survived

The input is a whole response surface over all \((q,r)\), whereas ChoCo's usual instance is one society distribution; the computational target is therefore rule recognition across societies rather than computation for a single society.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case I believe is narrow: recognition of distributed approval rules when two large, homogeneous voter blocs distribute cards among candidates. The continuous object is the population’s distribution over individual card allocations. Each person still distributes an integral number of cards; only the fractions of people using each allocation are continuous.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that for a given \(k\times\ell\) voting correspondence \(h\), one can decide in polynomial time whether it is a distributed approval voting correspondence and, if so, label its rows and columns by the corresponding card-allocation strategies. When \(\ell\ge k\), the row labeling is unique; when \(\ell<k\), the column labeling is unique. Here \(k=|X|\) and \(\ell=|Y|\), for the strategy sets of two voters with card budgets \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).

A second, closely related anchor is Theorem 8, also proved here. For a voting form \(g\), when \(p\ge3\) and \(\beta\ge2\alpha\), it gives a polynomial-time test for whether \(g\) is a distributed approval voting form. If it is, the algorithm labels rows and columns by strategies, and the row labeling is unique. This is the single-winner version of the recognition question, under the theorem’s stated parameter restriction.

Here is a continuous question for each result. Let
\[ X_\alpha=\{x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{p}:\sum_{a\in A}x_a=\alpha\}, \qquad Y_\beta=\{y\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{p}:\sum_{a\in A}y_a=\beta\}. \]
The society consists of two blocs, each of mass \(1/2\). Everyone in the first bloc has the same type and an allotment of \(\alpha\) cards; everyone in the second has the same type and an allotment of \(\beta\) cards. A bloc’s strategy is a distribution over its members’ integral card allocations. For example, if \(q_i\) is the fraction of the first bloc using allocation \(x_i\), then \(\sum_i q_i=1\). The candidate’s total card mass is proportional to
\[ S_a(q,r)=\sum_i q_i\,\phi(i)_a+\sum_j r_j\,\psi(j)_a, \]
where \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) assign the pure allocation strategies to the vertices of the two distribution simplices. The factor \(1/2\) from the blocs’ population masses can be omitted because it does not change the winners.

Continuous DAV Correspondence Recognition (lead): the input is \(p,\alpha,\beta\) and an exact, finite rational polyhedral subdivision of \(\Delta_{k-1}\times\Delta_{\ell-1}\), with each cell—including tie faces—labeled by a subset of candidates. The simplex vertices are initially anonymous. Decide whether there are bijections from those vertices to \(X_\alpha\) and \(Y_\beta\) such that, at every pair of population distributions \((q,r)\), the supplied label is exactly
\[ \arg\max_{a\in A} S_a(q,r). \]
A yes solution includes the two bijections. This is the population analogue of Theorem 3: it asks whether a supplied correspondence is generated by distributing cards and selecting every candidate with maximum total card mass.

Continuous DAV Form Recognition is the same instance type, but cells are labeled by single winners. In the parameter range \(p\ge3,\ \beta\ge2\alpha\), decide whether vertex bijections exist for which the label at every \((q,r)\) is one of the candidates maximizing \(S_a(q,r)\). A yes solution again includes the bijections. This mirrors Theorem 8’s form question, preserving arbitrary tie-breaking among tied candidates.

A plausible setting is a large association with two sizable constituencies voting on a finite list of proposals. Members within each constituency share the same allotment and rules, but may distribute their cards differently. The number of people can be large while the number of types remains two. The authors already study normal forms of voting schemes, correspondences, and forms; these questions preserve that object and its card-summing winner rule. They extend the profile domain to mass distributions, while keeping individual actions integral. That makes this a plausible high-multiplicity analogue of their recognition problems, rather than a change to continuous outcomes or to a different voting rule.

I would expect both problems to be Class A, though that is a conjecture, not a consequence of the theorems. The maps are built from linear aggregate scores, so a finite polyhedral description exposes the winner regions. The open computational issue is whether one can recover the hidden allocation labels efficiently from that geometry, as the paper recovers labels from discrete rows and columns. The follow-up questions include the complexity in the size of the polyhedral input, whether the restricted parameter condition in the form problem can be relaxed, and how much of the continuous map is determined by its restriction to pure-strategy profiles.

The main weakness is that the paper’s central examples have two strategic voters, while my scenario turns each into a large bloc. The authors may regard the continuum of bloc distributions as a new recognition problem rather than the high-multiplicity form of their original one. Also, these anchors are polynomial-time recognition results, not hardness results: this paper offers no named hardness anchor from which to ask whether population continuity dissolves discrete hardness. The case rests instead on the close fit between its recognition task and a natural population extension of the same voting scheme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 and Theorem 8 are genuine computational results, so I would not dismiss them as merely axiomatic or claim that high multiplicity is implausible for approval voting. The problem is that both theorems recognize a normal-form table: its rows and columns are the possible actions of two strategic voters, and the algorithm asks whether the table entries match the winners produced by each pair of actions. The input is the whole table, not a society whose winner or vulnerability is to be computed.

The best population version is coherent: treat card allocations as voter types, let \(q\) and \(r\) describe their population shares, and choose winners by aggregate card mass. But for one given society \((q,r)\), that is simply winner determination. To preserve the recognition question, one must instead supply an entire function over all \((q,r)\), such as the proposed polyhedral subdivision, and ask whether it has the required representation. That is a new inverse problem about encoding and recognizing a response surface. Its input is a family of hypothetical societies, not the society instance that ChoCo’s computational questions take as input.

Calling \(q\) and \(r\) distributions over strategic actions does not close the gap: that makes them mixed-action profiles of two blocs. Calling them distributions over fixed allocation types gives a legitimate population model, but then the recognition algorithm is classifying a rule across many possible societies. Neither interpretation carries Theorem 3’s or Theorem 8’s table-recognition task into the programme’s population-instance setting. Theorem 8 has the same obstacle as Theorem 3; its tie-breaking and parameter restrictions are secondary.

This is a real weakness in the negative case: recognizing voting rules over distributional societies could be a worthwhile adjacent project, and the proposed construction is much closer to one than a superficial objection would suggest. But the paper’s results do not themselves provide that population-input problem. The case against rests on this mismatch between *what the theorems take as input* and *what continuization makes the object*; it is a strong scope objection, not a proof that no author would recognize the broader question.
