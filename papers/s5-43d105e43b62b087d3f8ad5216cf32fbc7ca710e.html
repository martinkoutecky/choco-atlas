What the mirror covers

The proposed winner computation follows directly from the definition of utilitarian voting; the paper’s normative justifications, axiomatic discussion, and examples are not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

Hillinger’s paper contains no numbered computational result of the required kind: no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition classifies a problem as hard, tractable, FPT, or similar. The “Flaw of PV” claims and examples concern voting-rule outcomes, while the paper’s discussion of Arrow and May is theoretical and axiomatic. None is a qualifying anchor, so I cannot honestly give a mirror per anchor.

The strongest unanchored candidate is the computation of a utilitarian winner. In a large election, let a type be a voter’s complete score vector \(v\in S^J\), and let \(\mu_v\) be the fraction of society with that ballot. Given finitely many supported types with rational scores and masses, the problem is to return every candidate maximizing
\[ U_\mu(j)=\sum_v \mu_v v_j. \]
The many agents are voters sharing identical full ballots; the instance has \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of supported ballot types. A solution is the set of maximizing candidates. This is a faithful population mirror of the paper’s definition of utilitarian voting: it aggregates voters’ scores and chooses the highest-scoring alternative.

But it is not a mirror of a named result in the paper. Direct summation solves it in \(O(J\tau)\) arithmetic operations, and Hillinger does not study its complexity. The authors might recognize it as computing a utilitarian-voting outcome, but the paper’s contribution is a normative argument for the rule, not a computational question about finding its winner. That is the positive case’s weakest point—and it prevents this paper from supplying the requested hardness anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s weighted winner computation is the strongest candidate, but it is not a mirror of a result in this paper. Hillinger defines utilitarian voting by aggregating scores and selecting a maximum; he does not study the complexity of computing that outcome. The paper’s claims concern normative justifications, axiomatic conditions, and examples of voting-rule behavior. None is a computational classification.

A continuous population of voters with identical complete score vectors is entirely sensible here. With \(\tau\) supported ballot types and \(J\) candidates, its scores are \(U_\mu(j)=\sum_v\mu_v v_j\). Computing the winners is direct aggregation over the input support. That may be a well-posed computational question, but it is an algorithmic restatement of the paper’s definition, not a computational question the paper raises or answers.

The closest stronger candidate is a continuous model of strategic voting or winner robustness. The paper discusses strategic incentives, but supplies no formal misreporting model, costs of changing ballots, coalition objective, or optimization problem. Adding those could produce a worthwhile study, but it would require specifying the central problem rather than continuizing one from the paper.

So the negative case does not rest on the absence of a plausible high-multiplicity election regime. It rests on the absence of a computational result to mirror. That makes the case strong as a judgment about this paper’s fit for the programme; it is weaker as a claim that no new computational work could be built from utilitarian voting.
