The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. Its named results do not establish that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally tractable or hard.

The closest results are Theorem 2, which says that a gradual mechanism implementing a strategy-proof social choice function is incentive compatible if and only if it is reaction-proof; Proposition 9, which says that the open-by-two ascending-price auction is incentive compatible and transparent; and Proposition 10, which says that the RDA mechanism for any hierarchical exchange rule is incentive compatible and implements that rule. These are strategic characterizations and mechanism results, not complexity results. Theorem 2 gives a condition for incentive compatibility, but does not analyze the complexity of checking it.

The strongest potential seed for a continuous mirror is Proposition 9’s information-design question: how much information can bidders see while truthful bidding remains dominant? One might try a society with value distribution \(\mu\), where mass at each value represents the share of bidders with that value, and ask for the most informative disclosure policy that preserves truthful incentives. But the auction’s incentive test concerns an individual bidder’s unilateral deviation. In an atomless population, one bidder has zero mass and cannot affect the allocation or price, so that test risks becoming vacuous. Treating an entire value cohort as one strategic actor avoids that, but changes the paper’s question. The auction also uses bidder order and a “two bidders stay” threshold, neither of which carries over directly to mass.

So I do not have a precise continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. A distribution over bidder values is an imaginable extension, but it is not an honest mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes incentive compatibility by reaction-proofness; Proposition 9 proves incentive compatibility and transparency for one auction; Proposition 10 proves incentive compatibility and implementation for RDA mechanisms. None defines an algorithmic problem and establishes its complexity. The paper gives no input encoding, running-time bound, or hardness result. One could invent problems such as deciding whether a gradual mechanism is reaction-proof or finding the most informative incentive-compatible disclosure policy, but those would be new computational questions, not continuous mirrors of results in this paper.

The deeper mismatch is that the paper’s incentive criterion is pointwise: truth-telling must be dominant for each agent across every relevant type profile and deviation. A distribution over types does not enter that test. In a mass-only continuum, an individual’s deviation has zero effect on aggregate outcomes, so the strategic test can become vacuous. Keeping the test meaningful requires positive-mass cohorts acting as coalitions, or distinguished agents with positive influence. Either choice changes the unit of strategic action or leaves finite agents at the center.

That applies to Theorem 2 as much as to the applications. Its reaction-proofness test compares histories arising from an individual agent’s actions and another agent’s possible reactions. In a continuum, either those actions have no effect on the aggregate, or the test still concerns selected pivotal agents and the structure of the game tree. A complexity study of checking reaction-proofness could be interesting, but its computational object would be the mechanism representation, not a distribution over a large population.

Proposition 9 has the paper’s best large-population story: an auction may have many bidders drawn from a small set of values. But it allocates one indivisible item, and its “open-by-two” rule depends on a finite count and bidder order. In an atomless model, each bidder’s allocation probability vanishes in the limit; replacing “two stay” with a mass threshold changes the disclosure rule, while treating a whole value cohort as one actor changes individual incentives into coalition incentives. A model with one focal bidder facing a continuous population could preserve pivotal incentives, but the focal bidder remains an atom, and optimizing disclosure there would be a newly formulated problem. Scaling up the supply could avoid vanishing per-capita allocation, but would change the second-price auction into a market or multi-unit setting.

Proposition 10 faces a sharper identity problem. HER and RDA track who owns each object, who designates whom, and which trading cycle forms. If type means only a preference ranking, agents with different roles are incorrectly grouped; if type includes the relevant ownership and inheritance role, the number of types may grow with the number of agents. A replicated market with recurring roles and proportionally scaled object supplies is conceivable, but fractional type-level assignments and mass trading cycles would define a new market mechanism. The paper’s dominant-strategy result remains a finite-agent, distribution-free statement.

So the case against a ChoCo mirror is strong on scope, though not a proof that no related continuous-market question could be worthwhile. Large auctions and replicated exchange markets are plausible settings for new work. The difficulty is that those questions would require new computational targets and, often, new incentive criteria. The paper itself supplies no computational result whose population axis can be continuized.
