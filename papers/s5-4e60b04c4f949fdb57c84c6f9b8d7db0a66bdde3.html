Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 1 — survived: A repeated-component formulation preserves simple girth-\(\geq 3\) hypergraphs and gives a valid continuous population analogue.

The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many rooted component types \(H\), each a hypergraph or multi-hypergraph of girth at least \(3\) with multiplicity at most \(2\), additive valuations, and rational masses \(\lambda_H\) of independent copies, compute a finite-support distribution \(x_{H,\sigma}\) over complete integral allocations \(\sigma\) of each component, with \(\sum_{\sigma}x_{H,\sigma}=\lambda_H\), such that every positive-mass pair of bundles satisfies \(v_a(B)\geq \frac{2}{3}v_a(B'\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in B'\).

The model it lives in

A component-local high-multiplicity model: agent types are component-and-vertex-role pairs, good supplies are local edge roles, and \(x_{H,\sigma}\) assigns mass to integral allocations of repeated components while enforcing \(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX pointwise on positive support.

The objection that survived

The proposed global type-level supply model makes endpoint types share \(q\rho_g\) physical goods, violating the paper's simple or multiplicity-two premise; only a component-local repair avoids this.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's EF2X result and Theorem 4's \(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX result, while leaving the EF3X and \(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\)-EFX results and auxiliary lemmas unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the component-configuration formulation be solved in polynomial or pseudo-polynomial time when component types and valuations are given succinctly?
  • Can one characterize when mixtures of integral component allocations preserve exact EFX rather than only \(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity fair-division problem, not a version in which an individual receives fractional goods. The goods remain indivisible; what becomes continuous is the population of agents and the empirical distribution of their integral bundles.

Let \(A\) be a finite set of complete agent types. A type \(a\) includes its valuation \(v_a\), its hypergraph neighbourhood, and all information relevant to fairness. Let \(\mu_a\) be the mass of type \(a\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\). Let \(G\) be a finite set of good types, with per-capita supply \(\rho_g\). A good type includes its endpoint type-set and its values for every agent type. In a multi-hypergraph, two good types with the same endpoint set are twins.

An individual agent still receives an integral bundle \(B\) of goods. The continuous allocation variable is \(x_{a,B}\), the mass of type-\(a\) agents receiving \(B\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_B x_{a,B}=\mu_a \]

for every agent type \(a\), and

\[ \sum_{a,B}x_{a,B}n_g(B)=\rho_g \]

for every good type \(g\), where \(n_g(B)\) is the number of copies of \(g\) in \(B\). Fairness is imposed pointwise on the support of \(x\), not in expectation: whenever \(x_{a,B}>0\) and \(x_{a',B'}>0\), type \(a\) must be EFX or EF\(k\)X towards \(B'\) according to its valuation \(v_a\).

This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime. For a denominator \(q\), \(q\mu_a\) agents of type \(a\) and \(q\rho_g\) copies of good type \(g\) give a finite instance; its empirical bundle frequencies are \(x\). Conversely, every finite allocation projects to such frequencies. Thus the continuous object is the population distribution, while each realized agent still receives an indivisible bundle.

The natural scenario is a large institution, housing market, or collection of repeated districts: \(q\) is very large, while the number of valuation/incidence types and good types is small. For example, many residents may fall into a few preference and eligibility types, while there are repeated units of a few kinds of shared workspace. The type-level hypergraph retains the paper’s girth and twin conditions. The multiplicity restriction applies to distinct good types in this quotient; their physical copies are the high-multiplicity supply.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, the paper’s \(\mathrm{ThmHyperTwo}\) and statement labelled \(\ref{thm:hyper2}\). It is proved in this paper. Its full content is: “Instances on multi-hypergraphs of girth at least 3 and multiplicity 2 under additive valuations admit a \(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX allocation that can be computed in pseudo-polynomial time.” The running-time claim is supported by the paper’s Lemma 16, which states that its Hyper2 algorithm runs in \(\mathrm{poly}(n,m,2^k)\) time, where \(k\) is the number of bits used to represent valuation values.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

HM-\(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX in Twin Hypergraphs. Given rational type masses \(\mu\), rational per-capita good supplies \(\rho\), a type-level multi-hypergraph of girth at least \(3\) in which each endpoint set has multiplicity at most \(2\), and additive valuations \(v_a\), output a mass allocation \(x_{a,B}\) satisfying the supply equations above and, for every positive-mass pair of bundles, the inequalities

\[ v_a(B)\geq \frac{2}{3}v_a(B'\setminus\{g\}) \]

for every good \(g\in B'\). The output is a finite-support distribution over integral bundles; fractional mass may be assigned to a bundle, but no individual bundle contains a fractional good.

I would expect this to be a Class A problem in the bounded-local regime, with a pseudo-polynomial algorithm in the general numerical representation and possibly a polynomial configuration-LP algorithm when bundle size and type incidence are bounded. The paper’s paired-twin construction suggests treating twin goods as local configurations, while the mass variables naturally express how many agents of each type receive each configuration. The main additional computational question is whether the support constraints can be handled by column generation: the pricing problem would search for an integral bundle that violates \(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX or improves the current mass allocation.

This question remains recognisably the authors’ problem. It keeps additive valuations, indivisible bundles, envy after removing any one good, hypergraph locality, girth, and twin goods. It does not replace population continuity by divisible outcomes. It also generates useful follow-up questions: whether the \(\frac{2}{3}\) guarantee survives with exact rational masses; whether the continuous optimum exceeds \(\frac{2}{3}\); whether exact EFX is attainable for some type systems; and whether failure of a configuration algorithm would reveal genuine continuum-specific hardness.

A second, broader anchor is Theorem 1, the paper’s \(\mathrm{ThmEFtXsimple}\), labelled \(\ref{thm:girth3}\). It is proved here, although the paper explicitly presents it as a simpler polynomial-time reproving of Kaviani et al.’s Theorem 5.7. Its full content is: “Instances on hypergraphs of girth at least 3 with general monotone valuations always admit an EF2X allocation that can be computed in polynomial time.”

The matching continuous problem is:

HM-EF2X in Simple Hypergraphs. Given rational agent-type masses \(\mu\), rational good-type supplies \(\rho\), a simple type-level hypergraph of girth at least \(3\), and normalized monotone valuations \(v_a\), output a mass allocation over integral bundles such that, whenever \(x_{a,B}>0\) and \(x_{a',B'}>0\),

\[ v_a(B)\geq v_a(B'\setminus D) \]

for every \(D\subseteq B'\) with \(|D|=2\), whenever such a two-good deletion is possible. This is exactly the paper’s EF2X condition applied to every positive-mass agent pair.

I would also put this in Class A for explicitly represented local valuations and bounded local bundles, but I would be more cautious than for the additive anchor. With unrestricted value-oracle access to arbitrary monotone valuations, the configuration/pricing problem could itself become continuum-specifically hard. That would not undermine the mirror; it would be an interesting Class C boundary. The paper’s algorithm gives a credible starting point because it relies mainly on local relevance, single-good comparisons, girth, and the fact that each pair of agent types shares at most one good type.

The weakest point is that the paper’s hypergraph is fundamentally a structure on named agents and named goods. Its algorithms also use a distinguished agent \(0\), whereas a continuum has no canonical individual of positive mass. A faithful continuous version therefore needs a type-level pivot or a limiting construction using a vanishing anchor mass. It is not automatic that the paper’s proof survives that change. Likewise, if the authors insist that repeated physical copies of a good invalidate the original multiplicity condition, the type-level quotient must be defended as the intended high-multiplicity regime rather than as a literal unchanged hypergraph.

That caveat is real, but it does not erase the positive case. The paper’s results are about envy comparisons and local incidence patterns, not about the identities of particular agents. Large populations with repeated valuation and incidence types are a sensible regime for those questions. The strongest mirror is therefore a constructive, pointwise fair allocation of indivisible bundles to a continuous distribution of agent types, with Theorem 4 as the lead problem and Theorem 1 as the broader general-monotone extension.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirrors fail for a structural reason: the paper’s restrictions are on physical agent–good incidences, while high multiplicity repeats those incidences.

Fix a finite type system and realize rational masses with denominator \(q\). If a good type \(g\) has per-capita supply \(\rho_g>0\), the realization contains \(q\rho_g\) physical copies of \(g\). If \(g\) is incident to agent types \(a\) and \(b\), then every \(a\)-agent and every \(b\)-agent share all \(q\rho_g\) copies, because identical types must have identical hypergraph neighbourhoods. Hence the pair shares \(q\rho_g\) goods.

For the paper’s simple hypergraphs, this violates simplicity as soon as \(q\rho_g>1\). For the multi-hypergraph theorem, it violates multiplicity at most \(2\) as soon as \(q\rho_g>2\). For a twin pair, the problem is even clearer: two type-level twin goods with positive per-capita supplies become \(2q\rho_g\) physical goods, not two goods. Calling them one “good type” does not change what the paper counts as a good; EF\(k\)X and the multiplicity hypothesis concern physical goods.

This defeats the proposed lead anchor, \(\mathrm{ThmHyperTwo}\). Its continuous formulation has only three possible repairs:

  • Scale the goods with the population: then the realized instance has unbounded multiplicity and is no longer an instance of the theorem.
  • Keep only \(O(1)\) goods per type pattern: then only \(O(1)\) agents receive nonempty bundles, so the positive-mass limit consists almost entirely of empty bundles and the EF3X question disappears from the support.
  • Give different copies different local neighbourhoods: then agents who see different copies are different complete types. Repeated copies of a component therefore produce many types rather than a high-multiplicity population.

Exactly the same trilemma defeats the broader EF2X theorem for simple hypergraphs. A “simple type-level hypergraph with per-capita good supplies” is not a simple hypergraph in any finite realization with positive density of goods; it is a hypergraph with many parallel edges. Keeping the goods finite causes the same collapse to null-mass recipients.

The proponent’s configuration distribution \(x_{a,B}\) does preserve indivisibility inside each bundle, but it silently changes the incidence model. It permits a positive mass of identical agents to access a positive density of copies of the same local goods. That is a natural new fair-division problem, perhaps, but it is not the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s bounded-multiplicity hypergraph problem. Conversely, making goods private or locally matched preserves the paper’s graph structure only by encoding location or component membership into the agent type, which removes the claimed repetition.

Thus the obstruction is not that the continuous answer might be easy, hard, or different. The obstruction is that the paper’s interesting sparse-incidence regime and a nondegenerate finite-type population limit cannot coexist. One can study unbounded-multiplicity hypergraphs, spatially indexed agents, or fractional goods, but each abandons a defining feature of the anchored results. The honest negative case is therefore strong for both proposed anchors, though it does not rule out a worthwhile *new* high-multiplicity fair-division model unrelated to these theorems.
