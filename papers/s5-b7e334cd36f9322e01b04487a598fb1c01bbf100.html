The case FOR (proponent)

Under the brief’s anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. Propositions 1–6 establish equilibrium existence, properties of absorbing states, equivalence to strong Nash equilibrium, and almost-sure convergence of the stated finite-population dynamics. None classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. Proposition 1’s existence proof uses a potential function; that is not a claim that finding an equilibrium is in P. Propositions 2, 4, and 6 are convergence results, not complexity results.

There is a strong reason to think a continuous population model could make sense here: the paper already studies identical people whose payoffs depend anonymously on club size. Large populations forming local-public-good jurisdictions are a credible high-multiplicity regime. But turning that into a continuous mass-transfer dynamic would be a new model and result. The paper’s convergence arguments rely on finite strategy profiles, individual updates, and indivisible club sizes—the features a continuum would change. So I would not present a precise continuous problem as a mirror of one of these results: there are no anchors to support it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s main point is decisive for a mirror of this paper’s results: Propositions 1–6 contain no complexity classification. They prove existence, identify equilibria, and establish almost-sure convergence. The paper supplies no named computational problem whose complexity could be charted for a continuous society.

The high-multiplicity setting is real: a large jurisdictional economy of identical people is a credible population regime. But it yields one exogenous agent type, while club sizes and locations are endogenous. Its best apparent computational question—constructing the equilibria in Propositions 4 and 6—is already given by the club-size arithmetic under the paper’s single-peaked assumption: an NCE exists exactly when \(n\) is divisible by \(s^\star\), and the ANCE leaves the remainder \(n-s^\star\lfloor n/s^\star\rfloor\). A mass version either lets clubs have positive population shares, in which case divisibility and the leftover-player cycle disappear, or keeps \(s^\star\) fixed in headcount, in which case each club’s mass vanishes as the population grows. The first erases the paper’s central indivisibility result; the second requires retaining the finite club counts the continuum was meant to replace.

A continuous best-reply or coalition process could still be studied. That would be a new mean-field dynamics question, and the paper gives no complexity input for it: its utility and cost functions are abstract, and the number of locations grows with the population. A computational treatment would need extra choices about how those functions are encoded and what output is sought. Those choices may lead to worthwhile research, but they do not furnish a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

So the case against is strong on programme fit, though it cannot establish that no related computational problem could ever be worthwhile. The paper’s high-multiplicity regime supports a continuous economic model; it does not, by itself, give ChoCo a complexity question to mirror.
