The continuous mirror question

Given finite support \(T\), rational masses \(p_t>0\) with \(\sum_t p_t=1\), claims \(c_t\ge 0\), a rational per-creditor endowment \(e\in[0,\sum_t p_t c_t]\), and a fixed admissible gain-loss function \(\phi\), compute a maximizing family of award distributions \((\nu_t)_{t\in T}\), where each \(\nu_t\) is supported on \([0,c_t]\), subject to \(\sum_t p_t\int x\,d\nu_t(x)=e\), maximizing \(\sum_t p_t\int [x+\phi(x-c_t)]\,d\nu_t(x)\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic society has types \(t\) with rational masses \(p_t\), claims and reference points \(c_t\), and a common admissible \(\phi\). Decisions are award distributions \(\nu_t\) over \([0,c_t]\); their mass-weighted mean must equal the per-creditor endowment \(e\), and their mass-weighted utility is maximized.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2’s utilitarian optimum when \(r=c\) and gain-loss functions are common. It leaves the other reference-point cases, maxmin results, heterogeneous gain-loss functions, and the remaining properties and extensions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an exact optimizer be represented by fully awarding lower-claim mass, assigning zero awards to higher-claim mass, and splitting at most one cutoff type; what is the resulting running time in \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the utilitarian welfare result for claimants who use their claims as reference points. The paper has no numbered result that explicitly classifies a problem as NP-hard, in P, or similar. It does, however, give a named, constructive optimization result that supports an algorithmic mirror.

My lead anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says that for any bankruptcy problem \((c,E)\), when claimants have reference-dependent preferences with reference points \(r=c\), the Small Claims First (SCF) rule maximizes utilitarian welfare. SCF orders claimants by increasing claims, satisfies lower claims first, and allocates what remains to the next claimant; ties are broken randomly. The paper’s result does not state a running time, but its rule gives a directly computable optimizer.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a failed institution with many small creditors whose claims fall into a modest number of standardized amounts. A type \(t\) consists of a claim \(c_t\), reference point \(r_t=c_t\), and the common gain-loss function. Its mass \(p_t\) is the fraction of creditors of that type. The regime is \(n\) large and the number \(\tau\) of distinct types much smaller than \(n\). This is an instance-specific case for high multiplicity, not a claim about bankruptcy problems generally.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: SCF\(_\infty\) Utilitarian Welfare Maximization. An instance gives finite support \(\{(c_t,p_t):t\in T\}\), with positive rational masses summing to one, and a rational per-creditor endowment \(e\) satisfying \(\sum_t p_t c_t\ge e\). Fix a common gain-loss function \(\phi\) satisfying the paper’s assumptions. Choose, for each type, a distribution \(\nu_t\) over awards in \([0,c_t]\). It describes how that type’s mass is assigned awards; it can also represent an anonymous random priority rule. Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_{t\in T}p_t\int x\,d\nu_t(x)=e. \]
The question is to return a feasible family \((\nu_t)_{t\in T}\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}p_t\int\bigl(x+\phi(x-c_t)\bigr)\,d\nu_t(x). \]
A solution is the award distribution for every type, with the aggregate resource constraint met and utilitarian welfare maximized.

The continuous analogue of SCF is to allocate losses to the largest claims first: fully satisfy the lower-claim types, then use a fraction of the cutoff type’s mass, leaving the higher-claim mass with zero awards. Equivalently, if \(D=\sum_t p_t c_t-e\) is the aggregate loss, assign zero awards to mass \(z_t\) among the largest claims until \(\sum_t z_t c_t=D\); everyone else of type \(t\) receives \(c_t\). This is the continuum version of concentrating losses on the claimants best able to bear them under the paper’s utilitarian criterion. Sorting the \(\tau\) supported claims and computing the cutoff gives a tractable Class A problem, with running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). The solution does not depend on the detailed form of the fixed \(\phi\), provided it meets the assumptions.

This mirror continuizes the population and the endowment’s scale to a per-creditor quantity. The resource was already divisible in the paper; outcome-space continuity is not the proposed contribution. A finite roster with \(n_t\) creditors of each type maps to masses \(p_t=n_t/n\), and its award vector maps to empirical award distributions within types. The continuous model permits arbitrary mass splits where that finite roster permits only person-by-person assignments. For the SCF cutoff, that is a natural high-multiplicity relaxation. The paper’s own discussion of random tie-breaking and Ex-Ante Equal Treatment of Equals makes this assignment among identical creditors plausible to the authors, rather than an unrelated alteration of the allocation problem. The paper studies finitely many claimants, so this is not a novelty collision; high-multiplicity optimization is supporting context.

The scope is deliberately narrow: Proposition 2’s utilitarian objective, common gain-loss function, and claims-as-reference-points case. I would not stretch it to the paper’s maxmin results, other reference points, or heterogeneous gain-loss functions.

The main weakness is that the finite problem is already easy to solve by sorting claimants, so this mirror does not show discrete hardness dissolving in a continuum. Its computational contribution is compression: when masses are given by type and the underlying number of creditors is very large, the algorithm works over \(\tau\) types rather than \(n\) people. Also, Proposition 2 is an optimization theorem, not an explicit complexity classification; if the anchor requirement is read as demanding a named theorem that literally asserts membership in P or another complexity class, this paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 2 is a welfare characterization, not a computational result in ChoCo’s sense. The paper identifies the SCF allocation as utilitarian-optimal, but does not pose an encoded optimization problem, analyze its complexity, or develop an algorithmic method. A continuous version can certainly be formulated, but that makes it a new computational question attached to the paper’s result, rather than a mirror of a computational contribution the paper actually makes.

The proposed type model is the best version of that question: claim amounts define types, rational masses replace claimant counts, and the per-capita endowment preserves the bankruptcy constraint. It is a coherent high-multiplicity regime. But for Proposition 2, its output can be represented by a cutoff and the mass assigned on either side of it. The input has merely changed from a list of claimants to weighted claim classes; the paper’s priority rule already supplies the allocation principle. That is a reason to doubt the mirror’s value to a programme charting computational complexity and optimization methods, though ease of solution alone would not disqualify a well-posed question.

The fairness angle does not make the objection stronger. A continuum can represent random tie-breaking within a claim class, and the paper itself recognizes ex-ante equal treatment. Nor does the fact that creditors may have different identities defeat a utilitarian mirror: identity can be included in types if the problem uses it, while the narrow Proposition 2 setting treats claimants with the same claim as interchangeable for the objective.

So the negative case rests on a boundary of scope: this paper offers a constructive economic allocation rule, but no named complexity result for a computational problem. I would argue that ChoCo should not count its mass-weighted restatement as a priority mirror. The case is weak against the broader claim, however. Standardized creditor cohorts are plausible, the population is genuinely continuized, and the paper’s result gives the mirror a natural anchor. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario; I can only argue that this is a low-value fit for a programme focused on computational questions.
