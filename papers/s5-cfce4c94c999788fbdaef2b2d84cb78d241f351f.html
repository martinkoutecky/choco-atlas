The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchor-based case for a continuous computational mirror here. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, characterizes SW-efficiency: a lottery is SW-efficient iff it is ex post efficient and uninteresting. Theorem 2 further characterizes it as ex post efficiency and degeneracy when no two alternatives are Pareto indifferent. Corollary 1, attributed to Dogan, Dogan, and Yildiz, gives the corresponding conclusion for strict-preference assignments. These are characterizations of an efficiency notion, not results about complexity or algorithms.

A continuous-population problem about recognizing SW-efficiency could be proposed, with types as rankings and masses as population shares. But it would be a new computational question derived from an axiomatic characterization, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. Under your criteria, there are no qualifying anchors and therefore no anchor-specific continuous problems to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the one the other analyst identified: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes SW-efficiency; Theorem 2 gives a special-case characterization; Corollary 1 applies that conclusion to strict-preference assignments. None asks an algorithmic question or establishes a complexity bound. By ChoCo’s stated standard, they are not computational anchors.

The best attempt to build one would be a recognition problem: given a society distribution \(\mu\) and lottery \(p\), decide whether \(p\) is SW-efficient. Theorem 1 supplies the test: check whether each alternative in \(p\)’s support is Pareto optimal, and whether the support is uninteresting. For an explicit alternative set, these are pairwise checks over alternatives and the positive-mass preference types. The masses themselves do not affect the test; only which types have positive mass does. Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 lead to the same kind of property check. That is a possible new algorithmic question, but it is not a complexity result from this paper, and the continuum adds no mass-dependent computational structure to the characterization.

Making the alternative space implicit could make related tasks harder—for example, searching for a Pareto improvement among assignments. But then the computational problem depends on how that alternative space is represented and searched. The paper supplies no complexity result for that task either. One could also pose optimization questions about selecting among SW-efficient lotteries, but those would add objectives absent from the paper.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here: repeated preference types in large elections are a sensible regime. The weakness is narrower. The paper gives no computational result, and its characterizations do not make the masses matter. That makes the case against a *mirror of this paper* strong. It does not prove that no useful computational project could be built around SW-efficiency in any setting; that broader claim is not established by the paper.
