The continuous mirror question

Given a finite course catalog \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita course capacities \(q_g\), and \(\{1,c\}\)-valued submodular valuations \(v_t\) for integer \(c \ge 2\), choose masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) assigned to bundles \(S\subseteq G\), subject to \(\sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_g\) for every course \(g\). Lexicographically maximize \(\left(\sum_{t,S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S},\ \sum_{t,S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\right)\), where each individual receives an integral bundle and \(x\) records the mass assigned to each bundle.

The model it lives in

A continuum of students split across \(\tau\) complete valuation types, with mass \(x_{t,S}\) assigned to each integral course bundle and per-capita course capacity \(q_g\); the objective first maximizes positive-utility mass, then weighted log welfare.

The objection that survived

Scaling course supplies and imposing at most one seat per category per student adds a capacity model not analyzed by the theorem, so the paper's path algorithm may not extend; the proponent acknowledges the pricing gap but does not show that the algorithm transfers.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is for large course allocations with repeated valuation types. Imagine a large university system assigning seats in a fixed catalog of courses. Many students share the same complete course valuation—for example, students in the same program and year follow one of a modest number of priority templates. A type is that full valuation function, including every bundle preference. The regime is \(n\) students with \(n\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of supported types.

Let the catalog be \(G\), with \(m\) course categories. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and valuation \(v_t\). After scaling, require \(v_t\) to be a \(\{1,c\}\)-valued submodular valuation for an integer \(c\ge2\), as in the paper’s divisible-ratio results. Course supply scales with enrollment: \(q_g\) is the number of seats in category \(g\) per student in the population. This co-modification keeps the course-allocation problem meaningful as the population grows. Each student still gets an integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\); only the mass of a type assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

Represent an allocation by masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), subject to \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_g\). The \(x_{t,S}\) are the solution: they say what fraction of each type receives each bundle. For a discrete realization, scaling the masses and supplies by a common denominator gives counts of students and seats.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It says that for finite indivisible goods and agents with \(\{1,c\}\)-SUB valuations, where \(c\ge2\) is an integer, Bivalued Yankee Swap with the stated MNW gain function computes a max Nash welfare allocation. MNW first maximizes the number of agents receiving positive utility, then maximizes the product of their utilities. The paper’s Proposition 5.6, also proved here, gives a polynomial running-time bound for the framework when valuation and gain-function computations are efficient; Section 6.4 explains the constant-time gain computation for its listed objectives.

The continuous question mirroring that anchor is Continuous MNW Course Allocation. Given the types, masses, course supplies and valuations above, find feasible \(x\) that lexicographically maximizes
\[ \left( \sum_{t,S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S}, \quad \sum_{t,S:v_t(S)>0}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S) \right). \]
The first term is the population fraction receiving positive utility. The second is normalized log Nash welfare among positive-utility assignments, so it is the continuous counterpart of maximizing the finite product after maximizing the number of positive utilities.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, though the paper’s theorem does not itself prove a bit-polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) for this continuous instance. The objective and feasibility constraints give a two-stage linear optimization problem over the bundle masses: the first stage maximizes positive mass, and the second maximizes a weighted log-utility sum. There are exponentially many possible bundles, so a prover would need to control the pricing problem over \(S\subseteq G\), not simply write down the LP. The Yankee Swap path structure is a reason to expect tractability; whether it yields efficient pricing or a direct mass-transfer algorithm is the main question. Further questions include whether an optimum has a compact rational representation, and whether it is exactly realizable in a sufficiently large scaled finite instance.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 6.2, also proved here. Under the same finite-agent and \(\{1,c\}\)-SUB assumptions, Bivalued Yankee Swap with gain function \(\phi(X,i,d)=-(c+1)v_i(X_i)+d\) computes a leximin allocation: it lexicographically maximizes the sorted vector of individual utilities. Its mirror is Continuous Leximin Course Allocation on the same feasible \(x\). List the distinct attainable utility levels in increasing order, \(u_0<u_1<\cdots<u_K\). For each \(j<K\), let \(F_j(x)=\sum_{t,S:v_t(S)\le u_j}x_{t,S}\). Find feasible \(x\) that lexicographically minimizes \((F_0(x),F_1(x),\ldots,F_{K-1}(x))\). This compares the lower tails of the population’s utility distribution and is the continuum version of lexicographically maximizing the sorted utility vector.

I also expect this problem to be Class A. It is a finite sequence of linear objectives over bundle masses, with a finite utility range; the unanswered computational issue is again whether the exponentially many bundle choices admit efficient separation or a path-based algorithm with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Comparing its optimum with the limit of discrete leximin allocations is a natural additional question.

The paper’s §6.5 does report NP-hardness for MNW in a different regime, citing Akrami et al. [2022b]: bivalued additive valuations with a nondivisible value ratio. But it is not a numbered hardness result of this paper, so it cannot serve as one of the required anchors. It is useful boundary evidence: the divisible-ratio restriction in Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 matters. This case therefore does not claim a discrete hardness barrier dissolves in the continuum; its possible contribution is a high-multiplicity complexity analysis in \(m,\tau,L\), rather than dependence on an expanded population.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. Course valuations can be highly individual, and if almost every student has a different complete valuation then \(\tau\) grows with \(n\) and the population compression buys little. The scenario is convincing only where repeated full valuation templates are plausible. I am mirroring the MNW and leximin computation results, not extending the paper’s MMS guarantees or EF1 findings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is a fork: if we keep the paper’s finite stock of indivisible goods, the population limit collapses; if we scale supply with the population, we need a materially different allocation model.

With \(m\) fixed goods and \(n\) agents, at most \(m\) agents can receive positive utility. So the positive-utility mass behind Theorem 6.1 tends to zero as \(n\) grows. The utility distribution behind Theorem 6.2 tends to a point mass at zero. In that literal limit, both continuous objectives lose their fairness content.

The course proposal avoids this by giving each course category supply proportional to population. But the paper assigns each good once, and every item has positive marginal value. Replicating seats as goods would let a student gain value from multiple copies of the same course; forbidding that needs additional per-person constraints or a different valuation model. A category-capacity version is plausible, but it makes the mirror a new capacitated allocation problem. Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 do not establish that its configuration pricing problem is tractable—or that it is hard. This is a serious gap in the case that the paper’s results themselves point to a tractable continuous mirror.

The course scenario also requires repeated *complete* valuation functions, not merely students who share a program or priority template. That may be a narrow regime, though planned curricula could plausibly produce it.

These objections hit MNW and leximin together: they are both about the population’s utility distribution, and both survive if scaled course capacities and repeated full valuation types are accepted. The programme explicitly allows per-capita supply as a co-modification, and the mass assignment can still give each person an integral bundle. I therefore cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The negative case is strongest against the literal fixed-supply limit; it does not defeat the authors’ best capacity-scaled version.
