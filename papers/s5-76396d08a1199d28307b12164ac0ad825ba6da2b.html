What the mirror covers

The proposed reading leaves the paper’s domain classification, constructions, and asymptotic counting results without a population-computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. Its numbered results concern the structure and size of preference domains, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest candidate is Proposition 10, proved here. It derives the asymptotic size of domains from the even set-alternating scheme: for even \(n\), \(a(n)\sim \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}(2+2\sqrt{2})^{n/2}\); for odd \(n\), it gives the corresponding asymptotic formula. This is a result about a constructed family’s cardinality, not the complexity of computing or optimizing over such domains. Proposition 1, also proved here, says every set-alternating scheme generates a copious, maximal Condorcet peak-pit domain; it likewise makes no complexity claim. The paper’s finite computational searches do not supply a named complexity anchor either.

A continuous population mirror is therefore hard to defend as a mirror of the paper’s results. The paper studies which sets of rankings preserve transitive majority and how large those sets can be; population masses are not its object. One could add a distribution over rankings, but that would not turn these results into computational questions about a continuous society. Under the requested standard, there is no anchored continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the main obstacle: this paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its results characterize preference domains, prove structural properties, and count rankings. That makes a continuous mirror hard to anchor in the paper itself.

Proposition 10 is the closest candidate, but its quantity \(a(n)\) is the number of rankings in a constructed domain. A distribution over those rankings cannot change that count. One could ask for a uniform random ranking’s probability of belonging to the domain, or maximize entropy over distributions supported on it; the former needs a chosen reference distribution, and the latter simply recovers \(\log |D|\). Neither makes population mass part of the result. Asking for an algorithm to compute or maximize \(f_n(A)\) would be a computational question, but its variables are permutations and sets \(A\), not a continuous society.

Proposition 1 is a stronger-looking candidate because Condorcet domains are defined through majority voting. For a population distribution \(\mu\), pairwise margins are weighted sums over rankings. Requiring every such distribution supported on \(D\) to yield a transitive majority relation is a natural continuous restatement of the domain property. But it does not produce a computational result from this paper: the proposition already proves the property for its constructed domains, while checking triple restrictions on an explicitly listed domain is direct. An implicit-input version could pose a new algorithmic problem, but the challenge would be representing or generating the domain, not handling its masses.

The better adjacent proposal is to measure how much population mass must move to rankings outside \(D\) before a majority cycle becomes possible. That could be a sensible robustness problem. It needs a baseline population, a way to measure or price changes, and a choice of which outside rankings are available. The paper supplies none of these. Its maximality result says that adding any ranking destroys the domain property; it does not quantify a mass threshold. The threshold question may be worthwhile in its own right, but it is not a continuous mirror of Proposition 1.

So neither named candidate survives as an anchored mirror, even under a more favorable formulation. The universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous question is harder to defend: a population-level robustness study of these domains is plausible. I would reject the proposed anchors as mirrors of this paper, while treating that robustness study as a new problem rather than pretending the paper’s results already pose it.
