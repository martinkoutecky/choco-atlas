The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses μ_t summing to \(1\), entitlements \(w_t>0\), additive values \(v_{tj}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) for \(M\) item categories, find a finite-support assignment λ_{t,b}≥0 to integral bundles \(b∈\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^M\), with ∑_b λ_{t,b}=μ_t and ∑_{t,b}λ_{t,b}b_j=q_j\). Require that for every two positive-mass recipient classes \((t,b)\) and \((s,d)\), if \(d\ne 0\), some category \(k\) with \(d_k>0\) satisfies \(v_t(b)/w_t\ge v_t(d-e_k)/w_s\). Can such an assignment always be computed in time polynomial in \(M\), the number τ of types, and the binary input length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

The population is represented by type masses μ_t, with each type specifying its full additive value vector and entitlement \(w_t\). The decision variables λ_{t,b} give the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(b\); category supplies \(q_j\) scale with the population, and WEF1 is checked across every pair of positive-mass recipient classes.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the allocation-construction results in Theorems 3.3 and 4.6. It leaves the paper’s other fairness guarantees, counterexamples, and experiments outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the WEF1 assignment in the stated model be computed in time polynomial in \(M\), τ, and \(L\), without expanding rational masses into individual agents and item copies?
  • For the WEF1–Pareto-optimal extension, what precise mass-level comparison captures Pareto improvements among the same individuals, and what is its compressed computational complexity?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.3, proved in the paper: for any number of agents with additive valuations and arbitrary positive real weights, a picking-sequence protocol computes a complete strong WEF1 allocation in polynomial time. The paper also proves Theorem 4.6: with the same valuation and weight assumptions, a complete allocation that is both strong WEF1 and Pareto optimal always exists; for integer-valued inputs, one can compute it in time polynomial in \(m,n,v_{\max},w_{\max}\), which is pseudo-polynomial in the numerical values. Theorem 4.6 is proved here by adapting Barman et al.’s algorithm. I find no named hardness result in this paper to use as an anchor. These theorem numbers and statements appear in the [full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1909.10502).

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large public-benefit or aid allocation: millions of households receive bundles assembled from standardized item categories, and households fall into relatively few profiles of needs, valuations, and publicly accepted entitlements. A type \(t\) is the complete pair of its additive value vector and entitlement \(w_t\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of households of that type. The number of households can be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of types. The authors themselves discuss groups as a setting for weights, which supports this kind of application. Here, though, type mass \(\mu_t\) records how common a type is, while \(w_t\) continues to mean entitlement. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1909.10502)

Here is a precise shared model for the two mirrors. There are \(M\) item categories, with interchangeable but individually indivisible copies within each category. The input gives rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational entitlements \(w_t>0\), additive values \(v_{tj}\geq 0\) for each type \(t\) and item category \(j\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\geq 0\). An individual receives an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^M\), with value \(v_t(b)=\sum_j v_{tj}b_j\). An allocation is a finite-support mass assignment \(\lambda_{t,b}\geq 0\), where \(\sum_b\lambda_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}\lambda_{t,b}b_j=q_j\) for each category \(j\). Thus bundles remain integral for every person, even though the type-level allocation can have fractional masses.

Continuous WEF1 allocation, the mirror of Theorem 3.3, asks for such a complete allocation whose every positive-mass pair \((t,b),(s,d)\) satisfies strong WEF1: if \(d\neq 0\), there is a category \(k\) with \(d_k>0\) such that
\[ \frac{v_t(b)}{w_t}\geq \frac{v_t(d-e_k)}{w_s}, \]
where \(e_k\) is one copy of category \(k\). A solution is the finite list of positive \(\lambda_{t,b}\) and their integral bundles. This checks fairness between actual bundle recipients, not between type-level average bundles.

Continuous WEF1–PO allocation, the mirror of Theorem 4.6, asks for the same output with one further condition: there must be no other feasible assignment to the same population that weakly improves almost every individual’s value and strictly improves a positive mass of individuals. That is the continuum Pareto criterion; the mirror asks whether every rational instance admits such an allocation and, if so, how to compute one. Its output has the same finite-support representation.

These models have a direct high-multiplicity bridge. A finite instance with \(N\) agents and item counts \(g_j\) gives \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(q_j=g_j/N\); its allocation induces a mass assignment, and the WEF1 inequalities carry over exactly. Conversely, rational masses and supplies can be scaled by a common denominator to form a finite replicated instance. This makes the continuous questions recognizable as the paper’s allocation problems, with the population described by proportions and supplies scaled per capita. The paper’s group interpretation supports the scenario, and the model keeps each person’s bundle integral.

For the WEF1-only mirror, I expect a tractability result after type compression, though the finite theorem’s polynomial running time in \(n\) does not itself give a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau,M\), and binary input length \(L\): denominator scaling can make \(N\) large. For the WEF1–PO mirror, I also lean toward tractability, perhaps through compressed price and MBB structures, but with less confidence. The continuum Pareto condition allows mass reallocations that do not appear in a single finite replica, so that extension needs real proof.

The weakest point is precisely that continuum Pareto condition. Replication transfers the WEF1 constraints cleanly, but it does not by itself establish that a discrete Pareto-optimal allocation remains Pareto optimal against every measurable reallocation of mass. Also, the paper’s stronger algorithmic result is pseudo-polynomial, so the continuous mirror’s hoped-for gain is type compression, not a consequence already supplied by the theorem. My case covers these two construction results; it does not claim that the paper’s other fairness notions or experiments need a population mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is methodological. Both results are about individual integral bundles. To preserve their meaning in a continuum, the model must retain bundle types and check WEF1 between every pair of positive-mass recipient classes. The one-item exception remains one indivisible copy. Replacing bundles with type-level average bundles would lose the fairness condition, so the natural mirror does not gain a simpler fairness test from taking a limit.

That leaves a credible but narrower scenario: for example, a large aid programme distributing standardized kits to households with a small number of shared valuation and entitlement profiles. Give each household an integral bundle, represent profile frequencies by masses, and scale item supplies per capita. This is plausible and preserves the problem the authors study. Rational masses and supplies can be scaled into a finite replicated instance, and a finite instance can be summarized by its type-bundle masses. The mirrors are therefore recognizable high-multiplicity versions of the paper’s questions.

For Theorem 3.3, that replication gives existence and an algorithm on the expanded instance. Its running time may still depend on the number of replicated households and item copies, which can be exponential in the binary input length. The theorem does not settle whether there is an algorithm polynomial in the number of types and input bit length. That gap is real, but it is the computational question the mirror would ask.

Theorem 4.6 has the same opening. Its algorithm is pseudo-polynomial in the expanded instance parameters, and Pareto optimality against positive-mass reallocations deserves its own formulation and analysis. The paper’s discrete theorem does not answer that compressed problem. The proponent’s caveat about the stronger continuum Pareto condition points to work still needed, rather than a reason the question is ill-posed.

So the defensible negative is that these mirrors may offer less direct access to continuous-optimization methods than the programme hopes: WEF1 retains discrete bundle comparisons, while the main prospective gain is compression of repeated agents and item copies. That makes the payoff uncertain. It does not establish that the questions lack value, or defeat either anchor in the standardized-aid regime. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; both anchors survive the strongest objection I can make.
