This is close to the paper’s objective and decision variable; rational empirical distributions also embed finite multisets of rankings, so the expected complexity direction is hardness transfer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper fails the requested anchor test: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its complexity statements appear in prose and its algorithms are numbered, but neither supplies the kind of named result the proponents were asked to mirror. That is the strongest paper-specific case against their anchored argument.

Their fallback mirror is harder to dismiss. Put the rankers on a fixed candidate slate into a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, and minimize expected Kendall–Tau disagreement subject to the MANI-Rank constraints. A large body ranking the same slate, with many members sharing a modest number of preference types, is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Calling the rankers “inputs” rather than a population would not answer that case.

The best objection is that \(\mu\) affects only the objective. The fairness constraints still concern the fixed candidates, and the output is still a permutation. In fact, the objective can be written using only the weighted pairwise-precedence matrix induced by \(\mu\); the full distribution over ranking types is unnecessary. So this mirror mainly changes how ranker preferences are encoded, while leaving the permutation problem intact. That makes its added value look narrower than a mirror that opens new continuous-optimization structure.

But that limitation does not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s stated standard: a high-multiplicity version that turns out to retain the same combinatorial difficulty can still be a worthwhile result. A candidate-population mirror is less promising: each strict ranking gives candidates distinct positions, so preserving the paper’s preference data generally leaves no repeated candidate types to aggregate. Still, that does not rule out the ranker-population mirror.

So the negative case is strong on the missing-anchor point, but weak on the universal claim. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario; the ranker-distribution formulation survives the strongest objection.
