What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the welfare-threshold assignment problem of Theorem 3.4 in a repeated-profile regime. It does not address the paper’s approximation result, its tractable special cases, its price-of-diversity bounds, or its lottery analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Mass-AssignTC, a continuous-population version of the paper’s welfare-maximizing assignment problem with type-block quotas.

The anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper: the decision version of AssignTC is NP-complete. An instance has agents divided into types, items divided into blocks, a nonnegative utility for each agent-item pair, an upper capacity for each type-block pair, and a welfare threshold \(U\). It is a yes-instance if there is a partial matching—each agent and item used at most once—that respects all type-block capacities and has total utility at least \(U\). The proof reduces Bounded Color Matching to AssignTC; it is not merely a citation to an earlier hardness result.

A plausible regime is a large school district assigning tens of thousands of applicants to seats, with a few hundred recurring combinations of eligibility, priority, and school preferences. Each complete profile occurs many times. A profile \(\theta\) includes the student’s quota category and full utility vector over seat categories, along with any other traits that affect assignment. Seats are grouped by school and program; identical seats form an item class. The number of applicants is then much larger than the number of profiles, and the number of seat classes is manageable. The same setup could describe a large housing intake with recurring household and preference profiles.

Formally, let \(\Theta\) be the finite set of complete profiles, with population masses \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\) summing to one. Let \(g(\theta)\) be a profile’s quota category. Let \(J\) be the item classes, \(b(j)\) the block containing class \(j\), and \(\sigma_j\) the supply of that class per unit of population. The input also gives utilities \(u_{\theta j}\), quota masses \(q_{pb}\), and a welfare threshold \(W\). For example, a percentage quota \(\alpha_{pb}\) can be represented as \(q_{pb}=\alpha_{pb}\sum_{j:b(j)=b}\sigma_j\).

The question is whether there is a mass allocation \(x_{\theta j}\ge 0\) with welfare at least \(W\), subject to
\(\sum_j x_{\theta j}\le\mu_\theta\) for every profile, \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta j}\le\sigma_j\) for every item class, and \(\sum_{\theta:g(\theta)=p}\sum_{j:b(j)=b}x_{\theta j}\le q_{pb}\) for every quota category and block. Here \(x_{\theta j}\) is the mass of profile \(\theta\) receiving an item from class \(j\), and welfare is \(\sum_{\theta,j}u_{\theta j}x_{\theta j}\). The optimization version maximizes that welfare. Each individual still receives at most one whole seat or flat: \(x\) records how a nonatomic cohort is divided among item classes, not fractional bundles for named people.

This is a direct LP with polynomially many variables and constraints in \(|\Theta|\), \(|J|\), and the input bit length, so I expect the continuous problem to be Class A, tractable. Its object is still the paper’s underlying decision problem: maximize utilitarian welfare under hard type-block caps. The model makes the population continuous and scales seat supplies and quotas with it; it does not switch to lotteries or fractional outcomes. Given the paper’s housing and school-seat settings and its focus on welfare loss under hard diversity constraints, this seems like a question its authors could recognize as a continuous form of their assignment problem.

The scope is specifically Theorem 3.4’s constrained welfare assignment, in a repeated-profile regime. It does not mirror the paper’s lottery analysis or its price-of-diversity bounds. The weakest point is that Theorem 3.4’s reduction uses edge-agents with utilities tied to individual edges and gadget items. Those agents generally have distinct complete profiles, so the hard instances need not have small support \(\Theta\). This mirror therefore does not show that the theorem’s unrestricted hardness disappears; it asks what happens in the large-population regime where many people really do share full profiles. I would also avoid an overly narrow example where everyone in a quota category has the same utility vector: the more interesting case has several recurring preference profiles within each quota category.

The follow-up questions are whether finite instances built by replicating these profiles admit a rounding guarantee relative to Mass-AssignTC, and how small the profile support can remain while discrete hardness persists. Those would give a sharper boundary between the paper’s hard individualized instances and its continuous repeated-cohort regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Mass-AssignTC is that Theorem 3.4’s hardness comes from highly individualized utility vectors. Its reduction makes each graph edge an agent, with utilities encoding that edge’s particular incident vertices and profit. The reduction therefore supplies no evidence that hard instances have small support over complete utility profiles. In the paper’s housing and school examples, exact preferences over named blocks or schools may also make profiles nearly unique. In those regimes, the proposed continuum has little compression and is less representative of the paper’s NP-hard instances.

That objection weakens against the better mirror the proponent gives: many applicants can share a complete profile, while seats retain their own identities and the type-block caps still aggregate across profiles. This is a coherent high-multiplicity assignment problem. Its mass LP has polynomial size in the profile and item supports, and the fact that each person receives a whole item does not prevent allocating different fractions of a cohort to different item classes. Per-capita supplies and quotas are also an allowed co-modification. So the mirror remains well-posed, and a tractable answer would be a Class A result of the kind the programme seeks.

I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The paper’s NP-completeness theorem gives no guarantee that its hard instances survive in a repeated-profile regime, but survival is not required: the continuous problem asks what happens in that regime. The paper’s objective is utilitarian, not dependent on tracking named agents, and the text gives no existing continuous-population treatment that already answers this computational question. My case against the mirror is therefore limited to whether the relevant repeated-profile regime is common enough to merit priority; it does not support the claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
