The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu\) summing to \(1\) over the six strict rankings of \(x,y,z\), decide whether the strict pairwise-majority relation cycles. Equivalently, for \(\Delta_X=\mu_{PX1}-\mu_{PX2}\) and analogously \(\Delta_Y,\Delta_Z\), test whether all three differences have the same strict sign and each satisfies \(|\Delta_i| < (|\Delta_X|+|\Delta_Y|+|\Delta_Z|)/2\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass society distributed over six complete ranking types; aggregate type masses determine pairwise majorities, and the output is whether those majorities form a strict cycle.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 1’s cycle-recognition criterion with counts replaced by type masses; it does not cover Propositions 2 and 3’s asymptotic probabilities for finite random electorates.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a continuous version of cycle recognition. But this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor it, so it cannot support the requested kind of case.

The candidate mirror is: given rational masses \(\mu\) summing to \(1\) over the six strict rankings of \(x,y,z\), decide whether the mass-weighted pairwise majority relation contains a strict cycle. A solution is a yes/no answer. The type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. This fits a large electorate with only six relevant preference types. For the paper’s labels, set \(\Delta_X=\mu_{PX1}-\mu_{PX2}\), and define \(\Delta_Y,\Delta_Z\) analogously. Proposition 1’s criterion carries over directly: there is a cycle exactly when the three differences have the same strict sign and each absolute difference is less than \((|\Delta_X|+|\Delta_Y|+|\Delta_Z|)/2\). Checking this is plainly tractable.

That is a plausible infinite-population counterpart to the paper’s pairwise-majority question. The bridge is especially clear in the paper’s probability results: if voters are independently drawn from type probabilities \(p\), the empirical type shares approach \(p\) as the electorate grows. Proposition 2 says the probability of no cycle tends to \(1\) if the type-pair differences have both signs, or if one absolute difference exceeds half their total absolute value. Proposition 3 says the probability of a cycle tends to \(1\) when all three differences have the same strict sign and each absolute difference is less than half their total. Both are proved here: Proposition 2 has a sketch proof, and Proposition 3 is stated to follow analogously.

Those propositions are not computational-complexity anchors, though. Proposition 1, attributed to Nicholas Miller, characterizes when a finite anonymous profile cycles; it does not state a complexity classification. Propositions 2 and 3 are asymptotic probability results, not claims that a problem is in P, hard, or has some parameterized complexity. The paper names no result of the required computational kind.

The weakness is substantial: the proposed continuous decision problem is elementary, and it answers whether the limiting mass distribution cycles, not the paper’s central question about the probability of cycles in a finite random electorate. The authors would likely recognize it as a deterministic limit counterpart, but it is not a strong computational continuation of their work. So this is a sensible continuous object and a useful bridge to their results, rather than a qualifying, anchored computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The high multiplicity regime is perfectly sensible here: a large electorate can be represented by masses on the six rankings. The difficulty is that the paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes cycles, and Propositions 2 and 3 give asymptotic probability results; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

The proposed cycle-recognition mirror is the closest fit, but it has almost no computational content. With three options, compute the three mass differences and apply Proposition 1’s inequalities. If the number of options varies, sum the masses supporting each pairwise comparison, form the directed majority graph, and test it for a cycle. This takes polynomial time in the number of supported types, the number of options, and the input bit length. The paper’s central question is instead what happens to the probability of a cycle when voters are randomly drawn. Replacing the electorate by its limiting masses turns that probability into a deterministic cycle test.

One could ask for the limiting cycle probability as a function of the type distribution. Away from tie boundaries, the law of large numbers makes the answer \(0\) or \(1\), according to the same mass-level cycle test. At boundaries, a nontrivial limit can depend on Gaussian fluctuations in the finite electorate. That is a natural extension of the paper’s probability analysis, but it brings back sampling noise around the continuous society; for the paper’s three-option setting, the relevant calculation is also fixed-dimensional. It does not reveal a substantial computational landscape for the continuous population itself.

A more involved question, such as the minimum mass that must be moved to create a cycle, could be worth studying. But that adds an intervention objective and a cost model absent from the paper; it is a new robustness or control problem, not a mirror of one of its results. So the strongest case against is narrow but firm: the population can sensibly be continuized, yet its direct mirror is elementary, while making it computationally richer changes the question. The universal claim is hard to establish against every possible extension, but this paper supplies little reason to spend the programme’s time on a faithful continuous mirror.
