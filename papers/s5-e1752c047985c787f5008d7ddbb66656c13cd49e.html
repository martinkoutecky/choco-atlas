The case FOR (proponent)

The honest bottom line is that this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results are structural characterizations of mechanisms, not results stating NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), parameterized complexity, or an algorithm. The only explicit computational statement is the literature-review remark attributing hardness of finding Pareto-optimal roommate matchings to AbMa04; it is neither a numbered result nor proved here. Thus, under the programme’s strict anchor rule, no complete positive case can be made from this paper.

The strongest honest near-miss is Theorem 7, proved in this paper:

If \(k\geq 2\), all group strategy-proof and Pareto efficient multiple-assignment mechanisms are sequential dictatorships.

This is not a computational theorem, but it gives a very natural continuous-population question.

Consider a high-multiplicity multiple-assignment market. Let \(H\) be a finite set of house types, \(k\geq2\), and let \(\mathcal B_k(H)=\{S\subseteq H:|S|\leq k\}\). A voter type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking over \(\mathcal B_k(H)\). The society is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of agents of type \(t\), with \(n\gg |T|\) in the corresponding finite market. Each house type \(h\) has per-capita supply \(q_h\), representing \(nq_h\) interchangeable copies in a finite approximation.

The continuous decision variable is \(x_{t,S}\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:h\in S}x_{t,S}\leq q_h\) for every \(h\). Thus individuals still receive indivisible bundles; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

The problem is:

Continuous Multiple-Assignment Mechanism Problem. Given \(H\), \(k\), \(q\), and a finite type distribution \(\mu\), characterize or compute measurable allocation rules \(F\) that map reported preference profiles to feasible assignments, are Pareto efficient, and are group strategy-proof against every positive-measure coalition. In particular, determine whether every such rule has a continuum analogue of sequential dictatorship, such as an ordered sequence of positive-mass priority cohorts choosing preferred feasible bundles.

This is a credible mirror. The agents are many households with repeated preference types; the underlying conflict is exactly the paper’s multiple-assignment conflict over scarce objects; and the mass variable is not an artificial relaxation of each person’s bundle. The only co-modification is the natural one: finite house supplies scale with population. The authors should recognize the question as asking whether their \(k\geq2\) characterization survives in the high-multiplicity regime.

My expectation is that this is a boundary problem rather than an immediate Class A or B result. The finite proof relies heavily on a first individual dictator and induction on the number of agents. In a nonatomic society, one individual has zero mass and cannot consume a positive supply. If priority is represented by positive-mass cohorts, sequential-dictatorship ideas may survive and the resulting type-level feasibility problem may be amenable to LP methods. Under anonymity, however, genuinely new continuum mechanisms—or a collapse of meaningful strategy-proofness—may appear. That makes continuum-specific behavior, Class C, a serious possibility.

The further questions are whether positive-measure group strategy-proofness is the right continuum analogue, whether anonymous rules can be nontrivial, whether a finite priority-cohort representation is complete, and whether rational \(\mu\) gives a two-way high-multiplicity dictionary with the finite problem.

The weakest point is decisive: Theorem 7 is not a computational result. Moreover, “group strategy-proofness” must be reformulated because individual deviations are null events, and the resulting continuum mechanism is therefore not literally the mechanism studied in the paper. So this is a strong case that the paper supplies a sensible continuous-population research question, but not a valid anchored case under the programme’s computational-anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s entry gate: this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Every numbered theorem is an axiomatic or structural characterization of mechanisms. Theorems 1–7 classify strategy-proof, group-strategy-proof, Pareto-efficient, or sequential-dictatorship mechanisms; they do not state membership in \(P\), hardness, an approximation guarantee, or an algorithmic problem. The paper’s references to computational hardness in the roommates literature are background remarks, not results established by the paper. Thus there is no named computational result whose continuous-population counterpart ChoCo would be mirroring.

The proponent’s best candidate, Theorem 7, does not repair this defect. The theorem says that for \(k\geq2\), every group-strategy-proof and Pareto-efficient multiple-assignment mechanism is a sequential dictatorship. The proposed question—classifying or computing measurable continuum mechanisms—is a potentially interesting follow-on project, but it is not a computational mirror of that theorem. It has no specified finite input, output, encoding, decision problem, or complexity measure. The variables \(x_{t,S}\) describe one aggregate assignment for one reported type distribution; Theorem 7 concerns a rule \(f:P^N\to C\), namely a coherent mapping across every individual preference profile. Passing from \(x\) to such a rule is precisely the missing mechanism-design problem.

There is also a substantive difficulty with the proposed continuum version. Sequential dictatorship obtains its force from an individual agent who chooses first and thereby changes the feasible options of later named agents. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass, so their choice does not alter positive per-capita supplies. A sequence of individual dictators therefore has no aggregate effect. To preserve the phenomenon, one must introduce positive-mass priority cohorts. But a cohort is not an individual dictator: one must specify whether its members choose jointly, whether it may split across bundles, how within-cohort conflicts are resolved, and which coalitions may coordinate reports. Those are new primitives, not consequences of the paper’s model.

Retaining individual priority labels does not solve the problem cleanly. If priority is part of a type, then agents with the same preferences but different priorities are different types for purposes of the mechanism. If one insists on finitely many types, priority must be coarsened into cohorts, producing the new model just described. If one instead imposes anonymity, the paper’s sequential-dictatorship conclusion is no longer even the natural target, because sequential dictatorship is intrinsically nonanonymous. This is not an objection that the answer changes; it is an objection that the theorem’s defining object does not have a canonical mass-only representation.

The proposed model is therefore caught between two cases. Preserve named individuals and the high-multiplicity limit does not produce the relevant continuous object. Compress to masses and the mechanism, manipulation notion, and priority structure require substantial independent design. A cohort-based theory could be worthwhile, but it would be a new continuous mechanism-design programme motivated by Theorem 7, not a computational mirror supplied by this paper.

I cannot honestly prove that no researcher could make that cohort model valuable. That is the weak point of the negative case. But under ChoCo’s stated standard, it is decisive: the paper contains no named computational theorem to continuize, and its strongest structural theorem yields only a plausible new question whose computational content and continuum mechanism have yet to be defined.
