What the mirror covers

The proposed population evaluation aggregates the detector experiments; the attack procedures and detection rates have no qualifying named computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper an anchor-compliant positive case: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational complexity or algorithmic classification. Functions 1–3 describe attack procedures, and Tables 1–2 report CNN detection rates; neither is the kind of named result the brief requires. For example, Table 1 reports detection rates for \(N=5\) of 89%, 99%, and 93% for the naive, basic, and advanced attacks. That is empirical evidence, not a complexity result.

The closest honest continuous mirror is a population version of those detection experiments. Fix \(n\) alternatives and one of the paper’s attack families \(a\). A type is \(t=(C_t,y_t)\), where \(C_t\) is a reciprocal pairwise-comparison matrix and \(y_t\) records whether it is clean or was generated using attack \(a\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the share of experts with that type, and let \(F_a\) be the paper’s trained detector for \(a\). Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) and a threshold \(\eta\), Population Attack-Detection Evaluation asks whether

\[ \operatorname{Acc}_a(F_a,\mu) =\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[F_a(C_t)=\mathbf{1}[y_t=a]\right] \ge \eta, \]

and returns the exact confusion masses. A discrete panel with \(n_t\) experts of each type is recovered by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\).

A plausible regime is a large organization repeatedly using the same AHP comparison templates, alternatives, and rating conventions, so many experts share complete judgment types and \(N\gg\tau\). In that setting, mass means the fraction of the panel represented by each matrix-and-label type. The authors could plausibly recognize this as the population-level counterpart of evaluating their detector on generated samples. For a fixed detector, the evaluation is tractable by running it on each supported type and summing the masses, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the detector’s size.

That is a deliberately narrow case: it covers the paper’s detection experiments, not the attack algorithms as optimization problems. Its weakness is also decisive for the ChoCo programme: the continuous question aggregates classifier performance, but creates no substantial computational problem whose complexity might change under continuization. Training or testing detectors against adaptive attacks could yield harder questions, but those would be new research questions, not results anchored in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent already identifies: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Functions 1–3 describe attack procedures; the tables report CNN performance on generated samples. Neither gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic result for a computational problem. So the proposed detection metric is not a mirror of a result in the paper.

That metric is well-defined for a population of matrix-and-label types, but it only computes a weighted test score: run the fixed detector on each supported matrix, then sum the masses for which its predictions are correct. The population introduces no interaction, intervention, or resource constraint; it does not make detection itself a new computational task. A plausible setting with many experts using repeated, quantized comparison templates would give the metric a sensible population interpretation, but would not change that. In the paper’s generated data, random matrix values also make exact repeated types unlikely, so the proposed support-based representation would generally offer little compression.

A stronger candidate would model a group AHP decision: aggregate many experts’ matrices and ask for the least mass of manipulated experts needed to change the group ranking while evading the detector. That is a coherent population-level computational question, and it could be worth studying. But it adds a group aggregation rule and an adversarial objective that the paper does not analyze. Its computational challenge would come from those additions, not from continuizing one of the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: its only computationally adjacent result is empirical detector evaluation, and the natural population version is just weighted evaluation. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise in any scenario. A group-decision extension could be worthwhile; on the evidence here, it is a new problem inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of it.
