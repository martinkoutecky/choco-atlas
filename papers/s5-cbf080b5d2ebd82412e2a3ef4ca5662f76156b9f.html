The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives and a sparse distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), compute the paper’s MFP refinement of the Borda co-winner set. Let \(G_\mu=\{\sigma\in S_m:\sigma\cdot\mu=\mu\}\), \(W_B(\mu)\) be the Borda co-winners, and \(F(\mu)=\{c\in W_B(\mu):\sigma(c)=c\text{ for every }\sigma\in G_\mu\}\). Choose a permutation \(\sigma^*\) maximizing the relabelled histogram \(\sigma\cdot\mu\) lexicographically; if \(F(\mu)\ne\varnothing\), return its candidate whose image under \(\sigma^*\) is highest under the fixed priority order, and otherwise return the highest-priority Borda co-winner.

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings \(R\in L_m\), with masses \(\mu_R\) given in sparse binary-rational form. The output is one candidate: select from the Borda co-winners fixed by every mass-preserving alternative permutation, using the lexicographically canonical relabelling to break ties; use the priority-order Borda co-winner as backup when no eligible candidate exists.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4 in the full-ranking, single-winner Borda setting; it leaves the ANR impossibility characterizations in Theorems 1 and 2 and the paper’s other settings unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove an exact runtime polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\) for computing this rule.
  • Formalize the paper’s most-equitable guarantee for rational mass distributions and verify that the sparse implementation preserves it.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s computation of an equitable tie-break for a single-winner election with full rankings. The paper gives no named computational hardness result; its direct complexity anchor is an algorithmic one.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for any polynomial-time-computable backup tie-breaking rule \(f\), and any Common Setting with full-ranking preferences \(E=L_m\), Algorithm 1 computes the most-favorable-permutation tie-break \(MFP_f\) in polynomial time. The paper’s proof bounds the computation polynomially in \(m\), the explicitly listed electorate size \(n\), and the size of the decision set \(D\). In the single-winner case, \(D=L_1\) and \(|D|=m\).

My lead mirror is Continuous MFP-Borda Winner. An instance has \(m\) alternatives, a sparse distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, given by the \(\tau\) rankings with positive mass and their binary rational masses summing to \(1\), and a fixed priority order \(\prec\) used for the backup tie-break. A type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. Let \(W(\mu)\) be the Borda co-winners, with Borda scores computed by averaging each ranking’s scores according to \(\mu\).

For a permutation \(\sigma\) of alternatives, let \(\sigma\cdot\mu\) be the relabelled distribution, so \((\sigma\cdot\mu)_{\sigma(R)}=\mu_R\). Define \(G_\mu=\{\sigma:\sigma\cdot\mu=\mu\}\), the symmetries that leave the society unchanged. The eligible equitable winners are
\[ F(\mu)=\{c\in W(\mu):\sigma(c)=c\text{ for every }\sigma\in G_\mu\}. \]
Order rankings lexicographically using \(\prec\), and choose a permutation \(\sigma^*\) for which \(\sigma^*\cdot\mu\) is lexicographically greatest. If \(F(\mu)\) is nonempty, return its candidate whose image under \(\sigma^*\) is highest in \(\prec\). If \(F(\mu)\) is empty, return the \(\prec\)-highest Borda co-winner as the backup. The answer is one candidate. The choice from \(F(\mu)\) is independent of which maximizing permutation \(\sigma^*\) is used: two such permutations differ, on the original society, by a symmetry in \(G_\mu\), which fixes every candidate in \(F(\mu)\).

The objective is to select a Borda co-winner that preserves anonymity and neutrality wherever a resolute choice can do so; when the society’s symmetries make that impossible, the backup still returns a winner. The MFP construction gives a canonical way to make the selection across relabelled societies. This is the same underlying decision as the paper’s tie-breaking problem, with population counts represented as shares.

I expect this continuous problem to be tractable in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. The paper proves polynomial time for an explicitly listed finite profile. The proposed support-based adaptation need not process each individual: Borda scores are sums over the \(\tau\) supported rankings; a most-favorable permutation can be found by considering the supported rankings of maximum mass and mapping each to the canonical ranking; and the stabilizer can be found by testing the permutations that map one supported ranking to another of equal mass. There are at most \(\tau\) candidates for the image of any chosen supported ranking. Exact rational comparisons add polynomial dependence on \(L\). This gives a concrete route to a sparse-input algorithm, though the paper itself does not prove that extension.

The regime is a large ranked-ballot election or public consultation over a modest slate. Millions of voters can submit complete rankings, while many submit the same ranking; the relevant scale is the fraction holding each type, with \(\tau\) substantially below the electorate size \(n\). This is a population-continuous model: the type space is still rankings, the outcome is still one alternative, and the tie-breaking question remains the authors’ question. It does not make the outcome fractional or introduce a noise model. Because every rational distribution can be realized by some finite electorate, the change is chiefly to the population representation and input scale.

I would expect the authors to recognize this as their problem’s continuous form: it preserves full rankings, the Borda co-winner set, and the aim of refining an anonymous and neutral irresolute rule. Existing high-multiplicity election work also supports treating this regime as sensible. My case covers Theorem 4’s full-ranking, single-winner setting; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s characterizations of when the ANR impossibility holds.

The weakest point is that Theorem 4’s input is a finite profile, and its polynomial bound is stated for that explicit input. It does not itself establish a binary-rational, support-compressed continuous algorithm. The proposed extension rests on the structure of Algorithm 1, which appears to use rankings’ multiplicities, their equality, and their order under relabelling—all quantities available from a sparse mass distribution. A prover should therefore establish the support-based runtime and the continuous ANR interpretation, rather than cite Theorem 4 as already proving them. Further questions include formally extending the paper’s most-equitable guarantee to rational societies, and whether the same support compression works for multi-winner decisions or partial-ranking types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is about research yield, not whether the mirror is well-posed. Theorem 4 is the paper’s computational anchor, but it gives an algorithm for computing a tie-break on an explicitly listed profile. A continuous version would not introduce a new objective or a search over possible societies: it would replace the profile by a sparse, rationally weighted histogram and compute the same MFP refinement of the Borda co-winner set. The main contribution might therefore be a succinct-input analysis rather than a new use of continuous optimization.

That skeptical argument has a clear limit. For support \(\tau\), the proposed problem is precise: compute the equitable Borda winner from \(\tau\) complete-ranking types with binary-rational masses. The finite-profile theorem does not establish a runtime polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\), so there is a real result to prove. And the route looks concrete: identify maximum-mass rankings, test the at most \(\tau\) relabellings they induce against the support, compute Borda scores and fixed-point co-winners, and compare the resulting sparse histograms. These operations appear to take time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

I can’t turn the modest scope of that extension into a sound objection to the mirror. Rankings are sensible complete types for a large ranked-ballot electorate; normalizing their multiplicities to masses preserves the relevant symmetries, ties, and co-winners. The paper supplies a named computational result, and the proposed continuous input changes the population representation while keeping the authors’ question intact. Theorem 4 does not prove the continuous algorithm, as the proponent correctly says, but it gives a strong anchor for it. I don’t find a defensible case that this mirror is not worthwhile. The negative case is limited to arguing that the contribution may be narrow and technically direct; that does not defeat the anchor.
