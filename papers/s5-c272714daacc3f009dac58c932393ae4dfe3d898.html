What the mirror covers

The proposed task covers only the price-implementation side of Proposition 1; it leaves the w-value allocation in Proposition 2 and the cooperative-game stability claims aside. Neither cited proposition supplies a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. No numbered theorem, lemma, or proposition classifies an algorithmic problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. The abstract’s claim of “computational efficiency” has no stated complexity bound behind it, and the four-agent numerical example is not a complexity result.

The closest mathematical hook is Proposition 1, proved here: the Sub-game 1 equilibrium has all agents form a grand coalition and implement optimal equilibrium prices to achieve Pareto-optimal pollution control. This is an analytical equilibrium characterization, not a computational result. Proposition 2, which identifies the final welfare of each agent with its \(w\)-value under Price Constraint I, is also analytical and does not qualify as an anchor.

A plausible follow-on mirror would concern many similar polluters—for example, facilities sharing a technology, regulatory regime, and damage profile, with \(N\) facilities but only \(\tau\ll N\) distinct types. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s population share, \(q_t\) its per-capita emissions, and \(S=S_0+\sum_t\mu_tq_t\) the per-capita pollution stock. For rational quadratic revenue and damage functions satisfying the paper’s concavity and convexity assumptions, define the problem Type-Continuum Price Implementation: find \(q^*\) maximizing
\[ \sum_t\mu_t\bigl(u_t(q_t)-d_t(S)\bigr), \]
and return the implementing marginal-damage price \(p^*=\sum_t\mu_t d_t'(S^*)\). The output must satisfy \(u_t'(q_t^*)=p^*\) for every type, together with the resulting stock \(S^*\). This is a continuous-population version of the paper’s Pareto-efficient price-implementation question. For this quadratic input class, I would expect it to be tractable by solving the resulting convex optimization problem.

That candidate covers only the price-implementation side of Proposition 1; it does not cover the \(w\)-value allocation or the full cooperative game. Its weakest point is the \(w\)-value’s dependence on the fully noncooperative emissions benchmark: in an atomless population, an individual’s effect on aggregate pollution vanishes, so the paper’s finite-agent benchmark cannot simply be replaced by type masses. Extending the \(w\)-value needs a defensible continuum benchmark. The proposed price-implementation problem is therefore a plausible research question, but it is not a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 characterizes an equilibrium and its prices; Proposition 2 identifies an equilibrium welfare allocation. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or even a computational problem with an encoded input. The abstract’s claim of “computational efficiency” is unsupported by a stated method or runtime, and the four-agent example supplies neither. The paper also leaves the revenue and damage functions unrestricted as computational inputs, so there is no basis for a bit-complexity claim. These are the only plausible anchors the proponent raises, and neither qualifies.

The proposed type-continuum price problem is coherent: similar facilities can share types, and the population shares can enter aggregate emissions and welfare. I would not dismiss it because mass is an unnatural quantity or because the resulting optimization might be easy. But it is a newly specified optimization problem, with a new input class such as rational quadratic functions. The paper’s proposition says which prices support a Pareto optimum; it does not study the computational task of finding that optimum from encoded functions. That task could be useful in climate policy, but the paper does not provide a computational result for it to mirror.

The stronger attempt to mirror the \(w\)-value runs into a different problem. Under the proposed per-capita scaling, one agent’s effect on aggregate pollution is proportional to its population share. In an atomless limit, that effect vanishes. Since the paper assumes strictly increasing emission revenue and gives no emission cap, an individual facing a fixed aggregate stock has no finite best response in the noncooperative game. Adding a cap makes the benchmark collapse to maximum emissions; treating each type as a strategic bloc instead changes the strategic unit and coalition structure. A revised continuum game may be worth studying, but its \(w\)-value would need a new benchmark rather than a continuization of the one defined here.

So I would reject the paper as a source of computational mirrors for this programme. The universal claim that *no* worthwhile continuous research could grow from it is harder to defend: the price-implementation problem is a plausible follow-on. Its weakness is that it is follow-on optimization work, not a computational result of this paper.
