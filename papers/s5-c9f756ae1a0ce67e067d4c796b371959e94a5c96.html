The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a related complexity classification.

The main results are axiomatic characterizations. Theorem 1 (proved here) says that on the common-object-ranking domain, a rule satisfies efficiency, strategy-proofness, individual rationality, and no subsidy if and only if it selects a minimum-price equilibrium allocation at every profile. Theorem 2 (also proved here) gives the same characterization on a common-tiered-object domain when \(2\le k<m+1\) and the pivotal tier \(T_{l_0}\) is a singleton. Neither theorem concerns the computational complexity of a problem. Facts 1 and 2 establish equilibrium existence and a lattice property, but are attributed to earlier work and do not provide complexity results either.

A plausible setting for a population-continuous market would be a large housing allocation: types would be complete preferences over houses and payments, mass would be each type’s share of households, and the decision would assign mass to houses at prices while satisfying supply and demand. But computing such an equilibrium would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. A continuous version of the theorems would instead continue their axiomatic question, so I would not present it as an in-scope anchor for ChoCo.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s contribution is axiomatic, not computational. Theorem 1 characterizes the minimum-price rule on the common-object-ranking domain; Theorem 2 does so on a restricted common-tiered-object domain. Neither states a complexity result, and the equilibrium existence and lattice facts are background results attributed to prior work. A continuous restatement of either theorem would continue an axiomatic question, which is outside ChoCo’s scope. That is the strongest reason against treating this paper as a mirror candidate.

The housing-market scenario is plausible as a high-multiplicity setting: households with identical complete preferences can form a type, and supplies could scale with population. But that produces a new computational question—perhaps how to compute a minimum-price allocation from finitely represented type preferences and scaled supplies. The paper does not formulate that problem or specify a finite representation of its broadly defined preference relations. Choosing, for example, piecewise-rational utility functions would supply the missing computational model, but it would be a new research direction, not a continuous counterpart to either theorem.

There is a further modeling gap to bridge. The paper assigns distinct, indivisible objects, while a nontrivial population limit requires supplies to scale or objects to be grouped into types. That co-modification is allowed by the programme, so it is not a scope objection by itself. But after making it, one still needs to define the continuum mechanism and its incentive constraints: a negligible agent’s report does not change the population distribution, whereas the paper’s strategy-proofness concerns an individual’s deviation in a finite-agent rule. Those choices could lead to worthwhile work, but they do not rescue a computational anchor in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for the requested classification: neither characterization supplies an in-scope result to continuize. Its limit is that a new computational study of scaled assignment markets could be worthwhile in its own right; the paper simply does not establish that as a mirror of its contribution.
