The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational result. The closest is Theorem 2, proved here. It says that an SRS \(R\) satisfies WIVIP, IWS, and SSI if and only if it is lexicographic excellence, \(R^L\): \(x\) is ranked at least as high as \(y\) exactly when the vector \(\theta(x)\)—whose coordinate for each equivalence class counts the coalitions in that class containing \(x\)—is lexicographically at least \(\theta(y)\). Theorem 3, also proved here, says that \(R\) satisfies NT, WIVIP, TO, and SI if and only if it is plurality, \(R^P\), which ranks individuals by how many coalitions in the top equivalence class contain them. Theorem 1, cited from [17], characterizes IIS through NT, WIVIP, IWS, IBS, and INUI. These are axiomatic characterizations, not claims about algorithms, running time, or hardness. Proposition 2 establishes logical independence of axioms, also not computational complexity.

There is a suggestive structural seed: one could imagine an atomless workforce, measure-valued groups, and ranking people by lexicographic masses of higher-ranked groups containing them. But the paper’s \(\theta\) counts coalitions in each class; it supplies no natural measure over coalitions to replace that count. Choosing one would change the model. The input is also a ranking of coalitions, not a distribution over agent types. Turning this into a continuous computational problem would therefore require inventing a new task, rather than mirroring a computational result in the paper.

So there is no prover-ready continuous problem per qualifying anchor, and no grounded A/B/C expectation to give. The strongest nearby anchor is Theorem 2, but treating it as computational would overstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that there is no computational result in this paper to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes lexicographic excellence, Theorem 3 characterizes plurality, and Theorem 1—cited from earlier work—characterizes IIS. They say which social ranking solution satisfies specified axioms. They do not define an algorithmic problem, an input encoding, or a complexity claim. Proposition 2’s logical independence result is axiomatic too. Turning any of these into a computational question would start a new project rather than continuize a result of this paper.

Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate for a mirror. Its score \(\theta_{\succ}(x)\) counts the coalitions in each equivalence class that contain individual \(x\). A proposed mass version might rank types by the measure of coalitions containing their members. But the model gives no measure over coalitions: population mass measures subsets of \(X\), while \(\theta_{\succ}\) counts members of classes in \(2^X\). Normalizing the finite counts or choosing a probability law over coalitions would add a modeling choice that can change the ranking. In a continuum, individual membership also has measure zero, so distinguishing one person requires a conditional or type-level construction that the theorem does not provide.

Theorem 3 has the same gap: plurality counts top-ranked coalitions containing each individual. Theorem 1’s IIS rule instead depends on how far an individual lies in the intersections of initial coalition classes. Both can inspire new mass-based axioms, but neither supplies a population distribution over rankings—the central object in ChoCo’s continuization. Here \(X\) is the set of people being ranked, and the input is a ranking of their coalitions. Adding a continuum of assessors would introduce a new population absent from the model. Grouping the ranked people into types would change the output from rankings of individuals to rankings of types, or tie members of each type.

That leaves room for a worthwhile new theory of ranking measurable groups, or of ranking types from a distribution over coalitional assessments. The negative case is weaker as a literal claim that no conceivable scenario could be useful: such a theory can be invented. Its force is that none of the paper’s named results grounds that theory as a continuous computational mirror, and the paper gives no computational problem whose complexity the programme could chart.
