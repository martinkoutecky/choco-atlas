The continuous mirror question

Given a finite shared agenda of temporal preference rules over binary attributes and a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) over user types, where each type is a theory on that agenda, form \(\Phi_\mu=\{\phi:\sum_{t:\phi\in\Phi_t}\mu_t>1/2\}\). If \(\Phi_\mu\) is acyclic and every rule lies in \(TPref^*\), decide whether \(\Phi_\mu\) is consistent over all finite sequences; a no-instance has a finite improvement cycle as a witness. The sequences remain discrete; the population of users is continuous.

The model it lives in

Types are complete temporal preference theories on a shared agenda, with mass \(\mu_t\); aggregate each rule by strict majority, then test the resulting theory for consistency. The decision output is consistency or a finite improvement cycle.

The objection that survived

Majority over rule syntax can vary under semantically equivalent encodings, so the instance needs a shared rule agenda and elicitation format; the proponent specifies the threshold but not that agenda.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3's consistency test for binary attributes, acyclic \(G(\Phi_\mu)\), and rules in \(TPref^*\). It leaves optimal-sequence generation, the other preference formalisms, and consistency outside those restrictions untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity when the majority theory has a cyclic dependency graph or includes rules with both past and future conditions?
  • When can consistency be guaranteed for an issue-by-issue majority theory without Proposition 3's acyclicity restriction?

The case FOR (proponent)

The most credible mirror here is a crowd-sourced version of the paper’s temporal-preference consistency problem. Imagine a large streaming or festival service asking users for rules about the order in which films or events should appear. A type is a complete temporal preference theory: the same attributes, domains, and conditional preference rules. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users with that exact theory. In a high-multiplicity regime, millions of users may share a comparatively small number \(\tau\) of preference templates.

For each rule \(\phi\), let \(w_\phi=\sum_{t:\phi\in\Phi_t}\mu_t\). The service publishes the strict-majority theory \(\Phi_\mu=\{\phi:w_\phi>1/2\}\). The continuous problem, which I would call Majority-TPref Consistency, is: given the binary-rational masses and the type theories, decide whether \(\Phi_\mu\) is consistent over all finite sequences. Consistency has the paper’s meaning: the transitive closure of the one-position improvements licensed by the accepted rules is irreflexive. The answer is yes or no; for a no-instance, a finite improvement cycle is a witness. The sequence remains a sequence of discrete objects—the population of users is what has become continuous.

My anchor is Proposition 3, stated in this chapter and not attributed there to an outside result; it is presented as following from the chapter’s earlier consistency criteria. It says that for a temporal preference theory \(\Phi\), if its dependency graph \(G(\Phi)\) is acyclic and every rule lies in \(TPref^*\) (its condition uses only past and present components), then \(\Phi\) is consistent exactly when, for every sequence \(\kappa\) of length at most \(\ell(\Phi)\), the associated non-temporal theory \(\Gamma_k(\Phi,\kappa)\) is locally consistent. If all variables are binary, the chapter gives a consistency-checking time proportional to \(|\Gamma|^2|V|2^{\ell(\Phi)}\). Here \(\ell(\Phi)\) is the chapter’s rule-length bound.

For the mirror, restrict the aggregate theory \(\Phi_\mu\) to that same fragment, require \(G(\Phi_\mu)\) to be acyclic, and keep all variables binary. The proposition then gives an FPT-style algorithm in \(\ell(\Phi_\mu)\), with a polynomial factor in the stated theory and attribute sizes; aggregating rule masses adds polynomial work in the input size and rational bit lengths. This is not a claim of polynomial time when rule length grows. It is a concrete tractability question for a continuous population, with parameters that include \(\tau\), the number of attributes, input bit length, and temporal-rule length.

The scope is deliberately limited to checking consistency of the majority theory. I would not claim that the chapter’s results on producing optimal sequences have been mirrored here. Further questions include what happens without the acyclicity promise, with future conditions, or when the support threshold changes; and whether the majority theory can be represented compactly enough for sequence optimization.

The weak point is substantial: the chapter models one decision maker, not a society, and supplies no aggregation rule. Issue-by-issue majority is my proposed co-modification, not a result of the paper. Authors might accept it as a natural multi-user extension of their consistency problem, especially in their film-program setting, but a referee could reasonably judge it to be a new collective-preference problem rather than the continuous form of their original one. The case rests on preserving their rule semantics and consistency question while making repeated users the population—not on claiming that the paper already studies groups.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is a real computational anchor: it gives a consistency test for a restricted class of temporal preference theories. The strongest objection is that the proposition’s input is one decision maker’s theory \(\Phi\), not a society. Its rules describe that person’s local improvements between sequences. Duplicating the person changes nothing; assigning mass to different people matters only after adding a rule for combining their preferences.

The proposed strict-majority rule supplies that missing model, but it changes the question. It takes a vote over syntactic rules, then asks whether the resulting theory is consistent. Semantically equivalent preferences can be written with different rules, so the result can depend on how preferences were elicited or encoded. Fixing a common rule agenda could address that, but the agenda and voting rule would be substantive parts of a new collective-preference problem, not consequences of the chapter’s model.

A stronger mirror could aggregate users’ induced comparisons between sequences instead. That avoids voting on syntax, but it introduces a social preference relation over the sequence space, with choices about how to handle incomplete individual orders and collective cycles. Other approaches, such as maximizing total satisfaction, add a social objective. These are plausible research questions, but the chapter’s algorithms do not select among them or supply the collective semantics. Its stated further work stays with consistency, dominance, and optimal sequences for a single elicited theory.

So the negative case is that the population axis is not latent in Proposition 3: it has to be added along with an aggregation model, and that addition may take the work outside what these authors would recognize as their problem. But this is not airtight. A platform that elicits rules from a large user base and tests the majority theory’s consistency is plausible, and the chapter’s consistency machinery would apply once that model is fixed. If the authors accept that as their problem, the mirror survives; the case against depends on treating the aggregation step as a new research question rather than a continuization of this one.
