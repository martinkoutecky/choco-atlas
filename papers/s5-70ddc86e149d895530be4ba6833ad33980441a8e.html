The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many item categories \(J\), common per-item values \(w_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), recipient masses \(\mu_3,\mu_6\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) with \(\mu_3+\mu_6=1\), and per-capita supplies \(\nu_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) satisfying \(\sum_j\nu_j=3\mu_3+6\mu_6\), does there exist a mass assignment \(x_{s,b}\ge 0\) to integral bundle patterns \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\), where \(\sum_j b_j=s\in\{3,6\}\), such that \(\sum_b x_{s,b}=\mu_s\), \(\sum_{s,b}b_jx_{s,b}=\nu_j\), and every pair of positively used patterns \(b,c\) satisfies \(w\cdot b\ge w\cdot c-\max_{j:c_j>0}w_j\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of recipients with quota types \(3\) and \(6\), each receiving an integral bundle pattern over finitely many item categories. The decision variables are the masses \(x_{s,b}\); feasibility must match each quota mass and per-capita item supply while ensuring EF1 between all bundle patterns used with positive mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers EF1 feasibility with identical valuations and fixed quotas of three or six; it leaves heterogeneous utilities, other quota regimes, minimum exchange counts, and worst-case exchange bounds aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the polynomial-time feasibility method extend when quota values are part of the input or the number of bundle patterns is exponential?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would put the positive case on Theorem 3.8, proved in this paper. It says that REFORMABILITY is strongly NP-complete for an arbitrary number of agents, even when all agents have identical additive utilities. Here REFORMABILITY asks whether an EF1 allocation exists with a specified size vector. The theorem’s reduction starts from 3-PARTITION: for \(q\) agents with quota \(3\), one agent with quota \(6\), and a common valuation, it uses goods worth \(x_1,\ldots,x_{3q}\) and six goods worth \(K/5\). There is an EF1 allocation exactly when the \(x_j\) can be partitioned into \(q\) triples each summing to \(K\). That is a particularly useful anchor: its hard instances already have only two agent types, distinguished by quota.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large network of depots receiving indivisible supplies. Depots use a shared, public schedule for valuing supply categories, but have different fixed storage quotas—for example, three-slot and six-slot depots. With millions of depots and only a few quota classes, the number of agents can greatly exceed the number of types. The type is the complete pair of common valuation and quota. The six-slot class need not be a singleton in the scenario; both classes can contain many depots.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: EF1 Allocation for a Continuum of 3- and 6-Slot Recipients. An instance gives finitely many supply types \(j\in J\), a common per-item value \(w_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), recipient masses \(\mu_3,\mu_6\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) with \(\mu_3+\mu_6=1\), and per-capita supplies \(\nu_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), with \(\sum_j\nu_j=3\mu_3+6\mu_6\). Each individual recipient must get exactly three or six indivisible items, according to their type. A bundle is an integer vector \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{J}\), with \(\sum_j b_j\) equal to that quota.

The decision variable is \(x_{s,b}\), the mass of quota-\(s\) recipients receiving bundle \(b\). A solution must satisfy \(\sum_b x_{s,b}=\mu_s\) and \(\sum_{s,b}b_jx_{s,b}=\nu_j\) for every \(j\). In addition, every pair of bundles used with positive mass must satisfy EF1: \(w\cdot b\ge w\cdot c-\max_{j:c_j>0}w_j\). The question is whether such a mass allocation exists. Its solution is a finite list of integral bundle patterns with rational masses. Masses can be fractional; an individual bundle cannot. This is a feasibility question, with no separate optimization objective.

This is recognizably the authors’ problem. Their REFORMABILITY question is equivalent to finding an EF1 allocation with the prescribed size vector: exchanges preserve bundle sizes, and any two allocations with the same size vector are reachable from one another. The paper also explicitly treats that existence question as meaningful under space constraints. The mirror keeps EF1, additive common valuations, and each individual’s integral quota intact. It makes the population continuous and records inventories per capita, as needed to describe supply at that scale.

For this restricted mirror, I expect Class A: tractable. With quotas fixed at three and six, there are only \(O(|J|^6)\) possible bundle patterns. There is also a useful simplification of the EF1 condition. Let \(\lambda\) be the least value of any bundle used with positive mass. EF1 holds exactly when every used bundle \(b\) satisfies \(\lambda\le w\cdot b\le\lambda+\max_{j:b_j>0}w_j\). The lower bound is the definition of \(\lambda\); the upper bound follows by comparing \(b\) with a least-valued used bundle. Conversely, those bounds make every used bundle EF1 toward every other one. One can enumerate possible \(\lambda\) and a bundle attaining it, restrict the bundle variables to this value band, and solve the resulting rational feasibility LP with the mass and inventory constraints. The fixed quotas keep the number of patterns and candidate thresholds polynomial in \(|J|\). This is a proposed algorithmic route for the mirror, not a result claimed by the paper.

So the mirror covers Theorem 3.8’s fixed-quota, identical-utility regime. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s results on heterogeneous utilities, arbitrary quotas, or the minimum number of exchanges. Follow-up questions include whether the LP method extends to quotas that are part of the input, what pricing methods help when the bundle patterns become exponential, and how the continuous feasibility question relates to exact discrete instances beyond the direct normalization.

My weakest point is that Theorem 3.8’s reduction uses just one six-slot agent, whose normalized mass is \(1/(q+1)\), and its hardness is driven by partitioning the goods. The theorem therefore does not itself exhibit two repeated quota cohorts, and the continuous mass assignment may erase the exact partition requirement. That is a real limitation of the anchor as evidence for a hardness result in the continuous setting. It does not undermine the mirror’s plausibility: the question remains the paper’s EF1 feasibility problem with its size constraints, in a credible high-multiplicity regime. The possible disappearance of the discrete hardness is precisely what makes this a worthwhile continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.8 is a genuine computational result, so the objection cannot be that the paper lacks an anchor. The strongest case against starts with the reduction itself: it has \(q\) recipients with quota \(3\) and exactly one with quota \(6\). As \(q\) grows, the six-slot recipient has mass \(1/(q+1)\), which tends to zero. The reduction’s partition constraint is carried by assigning individually valued goods among the three-slot recipients; the exceptional recipient acts as a rigid numerical sink. In the nonatomic limit, that recipient disappears. Keeping a singleton six-slot recipient would require a distinguished atom alongside the population, which is a hybrid model rather than the proposed distribution over quota types.

The proponent’s stronger version avoids that problem by giving both quota classes positive mass. It is also a credible allocation scenario, and it preserves the paper’s EF1 feasibility question. Its computational route, however, is exceptionally small: with quotas fixed at \(3\) and \(6\), there are \(O(|J|^6)\) integral bundle patterns. The proposed minimum-bundle-value characterization then reduces feasibility to enumerating candidate thresholds and solving rational LPs. That looks like a routine finite configuration formulation; the theorem’s strongly NP-complete partition structure has not survived. This makes Theorem 3.8 weak evidence for the programme’s intended complexity boundary, even if the resulting feasibility problem is clean.

But that does not defeat the mirror under the programme’s stated standard. A tractable answer is still worthwhile, and the authors explicitly treat fixed-size EF1 feasibility as their problem. The proposed model’s fractional aggregate supplies are also an admissible consequence of scaling the population while keeping every individual bundle integral. Extending the model to input-sized quotas or richer utility types might restore substantial complexity, but that would be a stronger, still-recognizable version of the same problem—not a reason to dismiss it.

So the honest negative case is limited: the theorem’s own hard instances degenerate in the high-multiplicity limit, and the rescued mirror may be routine rather than a rich complexity study. I cannot honestly rule out the positive anchor. The mixed-quota EF1 feasibility question remains a plausible continuous mirror; the material here does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
