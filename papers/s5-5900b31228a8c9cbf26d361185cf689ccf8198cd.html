The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is only one plausible anchor here: Appendix A’s numerical search for responsive equilibria. It is not a complexity result. It searches the fixed \(N=5\), three-signal treatments, and the authors explicitly warn that it can miss equilibria. The paper’s main findings are experimental; Definition 1 defines efficiency, while Theorems 2 and 3 are results cited from Goertz [3]. So there is no named computational result in this article that a continuous mirror could directly continue.

Still, take the strongest proposed mirror: compute equilibria for a large committee whose members have signals \(a,b,c\). In the atomless limit, a type’s mass in state \(z\) is \(\phi_z(t)\). Given strategies \(\sigma\), the vote share for \(X\) is \(q_X(z)=\sum_t\phi_z(t)\sigma_X(t)\). A single voter has measure zero and cannot change those shares or the winner. Every strategy is therefore a best response: equilibrium no longer selects or predicts behavior. And the paper’s signal condition gives \(\phi_z(z)>\sum_{t\ne z}\phi_z(t)\), so informative voting elects the correct alternative in every state. The finite committee’s pivotal calculations, strategic mixing, and imperfect efficiency—the substance of the paper’s result—disappear.

A finite-\(N\) sequence can retain pivotality, but that points back to a finite game. Its incentives depend on near-ties and finite-sample fluctuations, which the limiting type masses alone erase; preserving them requires extra structure, such as an explicit finite population or a model of second-order fluctuations. That could support worthwhile asymptotic game theory, but it is a different computational question from the atomless mirror, and the paper itself flags large-election models as theoretically different. Continuity does not by itself turn this search into a tractable population-level algorithm.

The other tempting rescue is to take a continuum of five-person committees. That preserves the strategic interactions, but the population units are now whole committees. Computing aggregate efficiency amounts to averaging outcomes across committee instances; the five-voter equilibrium problem remains inside each unit. It supplies no new computational object from this paper. Likewise, adding costs or interventions would create a problem the article never studies.

This is a strong case against a direct mirror, not proof that no author-recognized extension could be worthwhile. A large-election model with a carefully specified pivotality mechanism might be valuable. The honest weakness is that such a project cannot be ruled out universally; the point is that the natural continuous population limit here erases the very strategic problem the article investigates, while the paper offers no computational result to anchor a more elaborate one.
