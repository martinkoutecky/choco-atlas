The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita bounds \(q_t\), strict preferences over a finite set of activity templates \(E\), rational contributions \(a_{te}\), and template capacities \(\kappa_e\), determine the complexity of outputting a rational stable allocation \(x_e\). Feasibility requires \(x_e\leq\kappa_e\) and \(\sum_{e:\,t\in e}a_{te}x_e\leq\mu_tq_t\); every unsaturated template must have a participating type whose bound is exhausted by activities that type weakly prefers to it. In particular, is there an algorithm polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A finite type set \(T\) with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita bounds \(q_t\), complete type-level preferences and contributions, and finite activity templates with rational capacities. The decision variables \(x_e\) are activity masses; each type has aggregate capacity \(\mu_tq_t\), and stability requires every unsaturated activity to be dominated by a type whose aggregate capacity is filled by activities it weakly prefers.

The objection that survived

The type-mass instance can be represented directly as a finite stable allocation problem with type bounds \(b_t=\mu_tq_t\), so Theorem 13 already implies solvability and the remaining runtime question is close to the paper's own open question about Scarf's algorithm.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 13 and its stable allocation special case, Theorem 12. It does not cover the core and matching results or the discrete hospitals-residents problem with couples.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(|T|\), the number of activity templates, and the input bit length that outputs a stable allocation?
  • When does a continuous stable allocation admit a rounding to a stable discrete schedule?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper. It states: “Every stable allocation problem with contributions is solvable.” In the paper’s model, a finite hypergraph has a strict preference order over incident activities at each vertex, a capacity for each activity, a bound for each vertex, and a contribution amount for each vertex–activity pair. A solution assigns nonnegative intensities to activities, respects all bounds and capacities, and is stable: every activity below capacity has some participant whose bound is fully used by activities that participant likes at least as much. The paper says the Scarf algorithm finds such an allocation; the proof of Theorem 13 is omitted because the same proof technique as for Theorem 12 applies.

Here is the continuous mirror I would ask a prover to study: Continuous Type-Mass Stable Allocation with Contributions. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Each type \(t\) has a per-agent capacity \(q_t\), a strict preference order over the activities it can join, and a contribution coefficient \(a_{te}>0\) for each such activity \(e\). An activity \(e\) specifies its participating types and has a rational capacity \(\kappa_e\), measured in population-mass units. The question is to output a rational allocation \(x_e\geq 0\) satisfying
\[ x_e\leq \kappa_e \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{e:\,t\in e} a_{te}x_e\leq \mu_t q_t \quad\text{for every }t. \]
It must also be stable: for every unsaturated activity \(e\), with \(x_e<\kappa_e\), there is a participating type \(t\in e\) such that
\[ \sum_{\substack{f:\,t\in f\\ f\succeq_t e}} a_{tf}x_f=\mu_t q_t. \]
Here \(f\succeq_t e\) means type \(t\) likes \(f\) at least as much as \(e\). The task has no scalar objective: any allocation satisfying feasibility and stability is a solution.

The regime I find plausible is a large, recurring project or staffing market with many participants but relatively few complete types. For example, a multi-institution research programme could have thousands of researchers and students classified by their full preference order over standardized project roles, available time, and contribution to each role. The type count \(\tau=|T|\) could then be far smaller than the population. The paper itself motivates contributions with research projects and declared work time, though its small-department example is not by itself a high-multiplicity regime.

The mirror preserves the paper’s underlying stable-allocation question: activities can use different amounts of different participants’ capacities, and stability is evaluated using their preferences over activities. It changes the population representation by replacing named agents with masses of agents identical in every respect the model uses. That distinction matters because the paper’s fractional activity intensities already concern a finite set of players; fractional allocations alone do not make the population continuous. I would expect the authors to recognize the type-mass formulation as a continuous version of their problem.

I use one anchor because it carries the case on its own. The paper mentions NP-hard stable matching problems in its prose, but does not give a numbered in-paper hardness theorem to anchor a continuous hardness mirror. Theorem 13 is a named constructive solvability result, not a polynomial-time bound. My tentative prediction for the mirror is Class A, because the Scarf construction gives a direct route to finding a solution. That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 13: the paper establishes solvability but does not establish polynomial running time in \(\tau\) or the input bit length.

The mirror covers Theorem 13’s stable allocation problem with contributions, including its stable allocation special case in Theorem 12. It does not cover the paper’s other results on core nonemptiness, half-integral matching, or the discrete couples problem. Useful next questions are whether the Scarf construction or another method gives a polynomial-time algorithm for rational type-mass instances; when aggregating identical agents preserves stability in both directions; and when a continuous stable allocation can be rounded to a stable discrete schedule.

The weakest point is the gap between “a solution can be found” and “a solution can be found efficiently.” Theorem 13 establishes the former, not the latter, and the type compression only helps when many real participants genuinely share complete types. That gap limits the complexity claim, but it does not undermine the mirror itself: it leaves a precise, author-recognizable continuous computational problem whose complexity is still open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 13 is the strongest anchor, but its claim is solvability, not a complexity bound. The paper guarantees a stable allocation and points to Scarf’s algorithm; it gives no running time in the number of players, activity types, or input bit length. So the proposed mirror would ask a new question about algorithmic complexity, rather than continuize a complexity result established in the paper.

The proposed type-mass model has a particularly short route back to Theorem 13. Make each type a vertex, each activity template a hyperedge, and give type \(t\) the bound \(b_t=\mu_t q_t\). The template’s contributions and capacity supply the remaining coefficients. That is already a finite stable allocation problem with contributions, so Theorem 13 and Scarf’s algorithm apply directly. The masses enter as bounds; they do not create a new pricing or optimization problem. The mirror may still be semantically different from the paper’s named-player model, but its solvability result follows immediately from the paper’s theorem.

There is a genuine research question left: how quickly can one find such an allocation as the number of types and templates grows? Yet that is close to the paper’s own open question about the complexity of Scarf’s algorithm. A type-mass formulation would make the input size and population interpretation clearer, but it would need a further algorithmic idea to do more than reparameterize that question. The proponent also needs to count the activity templates. If templates refer to specific projects or pairings, their number may grow with the population and erase the apparent compression. If they are a small fixed menu of standardized roles, the proposed model is clean, but Theorem 13 already applies to it.

A recurring project market with interchangeable participants is a plausible, author-recognizable scenario. Complete types can preserve preferences, contributions, and capacities, and aggregate masses can then represent the available labor. That makes it difficult to claim the mirror is incoherent or that identity necessarily defeats it. The strongest case against is narrower: the paper’s result already covers the type-level allocation problem, while the remaining complexity question is largely the complexity of its existing algorithm on a compactly described instance.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case airtight here. The mirror is well-posed and plausible; the case against its worth rests on how much new algorithmic structure the type-mass formulation would reveal beyond Theorem 13 and the paper’s stated open problem.
