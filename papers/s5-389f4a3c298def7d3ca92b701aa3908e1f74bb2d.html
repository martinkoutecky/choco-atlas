The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of household types \(\mathcal{T}\) with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), sizes \(s_t\), ranked acceptable hospitals, and hospital priority tiers, plus per-capita hospital capacities \(q_h\), find a feasible assignment \(x_{t,h}\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}s_t x_{t,h}\). Each household still receives an integral assignment or remains unmatched; \(x_{t,h}\) records the mass of identical households assigned to \(h\). Call an assignment occupancy-stable if there are no type \(t\), hospital \(h\), and positive mass \(\delta\) of type \(t\) preferring \(h\), together with removable masses \(y_u\) of types ranked below \(t\) at \(h\), such that \(0\leq y_u\leq x_{u,h}\), \(\sum_u s_u y_u\leq \delta s_t\), and \(\sum_u s_u x_{u,h}-\sum_u s_u y_u+\delta s_t\leq q_h\). Common master orders over types may be imposed as a restriction.

The model it lives in

A continuous mass assignment for many households drawn from a finite support of complete types. Type masses and capacities are rational and normalized per household; household sizes and bundles remain integral. Hospital priorities order types, with identical copies tied, and occupancy blocking allows a positive mass to move while displacing lower-priority mass whose total size does not exceed the incoming mass.

The objection that survived

The paper uses strict hospital rankings of named agents, whereas repeated complete types require copies to share a priority tier; that can change which agents a hospital may displace in a block, and the paper’s hardness reduction does not carry over automatically.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s maximum-size occupancy-stable matching problem. It leaves the approximation theorem, guaranteed existence result, generalized-master-list results for classical stability, and first-coalition stability results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is a maximum-value occupancy-stable mass assignment computable in time polynomial in the number of hospitals, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • Does the problem remain tractable under common master orders over types, or does hardness persist when \(\tau\) grows with the instance?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Max-Occupancy Stable Mass Assignment. It takes the paper’s maximum-size occupancy-stable matching problem into a high-multiplicity regime: many households share a small set of complete types. A type \(t\) records a household’s size \(s_t\), its ranked acceptable hospitals, and its priority class at each hospital. For example, a large refugee-placement or childcare system might have many households but relatively few combinations of family size, location preferences, and policy priorities. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of households of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(q_h\) be hospital \(h\)’s capacity in slots per household. Both are binary rationals. Capacities scale with the population. Each household still goes whole to one hospital or remains unmatched; fractions describe how the many identical households are distributed. The sizes can remain in \(\{1,2,3\}\), as in the hardness result, and the model can retain common master lists on the two sides.

A solution is a matrix \(x_{t,h}\geq 0\), where \(x_{t,h}\) is the fraction of type \(t\) assigned to \(h\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t s_t x_{t,h}\leq q_h\). Its value is \(\sum_{t,h}s_t x_{t,h}\), matching the paper’s definition of matching size: total matched household size.

For stability, use the population-scale version of occupancy blocking. Write \(x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_hx_{t,h}\). A type \(t\) has mass \(d_{t,h}=x_{t,\bot}+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}\) that would prefer \(h\) to its current assignment. A block exists if some \(\delta\in(0,d_{t,h}]\) can move to \(h\), while the hospital removes masses \(y_u\) of types \(u\) it ranks below \(t\), such that
\[ 0\leq y_u\leq x_{u,h},\qquad \sum_u s_u y_u\leq \delta s_t,\qquad \sum_u s_u x_{u,h}-\sum_u s_u y_u+\delta s_t\leq q_h. \]
The first inequality on removed mass preserves occupancy; the second keeps the hospital within capacity. The assignment is occupancy-stable if no such \(t,h,\delta,y\) exists. The problem asks for a rational, maximum-value occupancy-stable assignment; its threshold version asks whether one of value at least \(K\) exists.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: *Given an HRS instance, computing a maximum-size occupancy-stable matching is NP-hard, even if every agent has size at most \(3\), and there is a master-list ordering on both agents and hospitals.* The authors prove this with a reduction from Vertex Cover on 3-regular graphs; their construction makes an agent-perfect occupancy-stable matching exist exactly when the graph has a vertex cover within the given bound. They also note that earlier work implies the hardness, but this paper proves its stated result with its own reduction.

This is a credible mirror because the paper itself describes agents as groups such as refugee families or siblings, whose members share preferences and must be assigned together. In the mirror, the families remain indivisible; only the distribution of many identical families across hospitals becomes fractional. The authors could reasonably recognize this as their problem in a large intake with standardized household categories and capacities proportional to intake. The result I expect for this continuous version is Class A, plausibly through a linear or flow formulation using the common master lists and divisible type masses. The Vertex Cover reduction’s all-or-nothing choices may become fractional choices over types, so its discrete NP-hardness does not by itself transfer. Whether the resulting optimization is actually polynomial in the number of hospitals, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length remains the question for a prover.

The scope here is just Theorem 2’s maximum-size occupancy-stability result, including its master-list restriction. Two follow-up questions are whether an optimal mass assignment can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and whether it gives useful additive approximations or preprocessing for large finite instances. The reduction has \(\tau\) growing with the graph, so it does not itself establish hardness in a regime where the population is much larger than the number of types.

The weakest point is the stability translation. An individual household has vanishing mass in a continuum, so I define blocking by a positive mass of households moving together and allow the displaced mass to be fractional. That retains the paper’s occupancy and capacity tests at population scale, while removing the discrete granularity of choosing whole households to displace. The paper’s authors might see that as a meaningful limit of their notion, or as a change to one of its defining features. The case for the mirror rests on precisely this being an admissible high-multiplicity model of their family-assignment problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 2, the NP-hardness of finding a maximum-size occupancy-stable matching. The paper plainly has a computational result to mirror, so a negative case cannot rest on a lack of suitable theorems. Its weakness is that the proposed high-multiplicity model smooths away information the paper’s stability test uses.

In this paper, both sides rank acceptable partners strictly. A hospital’s rank of an agent affects whether it can displace that agent in a blocking pair. Consequently, two agents with the same acceptable hospitals cannot be the same complete type: each hospital they share ranks them differently. Every agent with at least one acceptable hospital therefore has a distinct type, so \(\tau\) grows with the number of active agents. The proposed model’s “priority class” avoids this only by replacing strict rankings with ties. That is a consequential change: a hospital may displace a strictly lower-ranked agent in the paper’s model, while it cannot strictly prefer one copy over another in the tied type. The paper’s hardness construction uses strict orders, including under master lists, so it does not supply a high-multiplicity regime that resolves this mismatch.

The best repair is to keep priority classes and define blocking for positive mass, as the proponent does. That is coherent, and the paper’s family and sibling examples make repeated household types plausible. But the repaired question concerns occupancy stability with tied, type-level hospital priorities; it is no longer the paper’s strict-priority problem. Alternatively, preserve strict priorities by including rank position in each type. Then copies cease to be identical, or the input must become a distribution over a continuum of rank profiles rather than the paper’s finite-support distribution over types. Either route may lead to worthwhile research, but neither is an obvious high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 2.

So the strongest case against is that this paper’s central matching notion is unusually dependent on agent-specific priority information: its natural family-size grouping does not create repeated complete types. I would use that to argue against prioritizing this paper for ChoCo. I would not claim it proves that no worthwhile mirror exists. If the authors accept tied priority tiers as a natural population version of their model, the proposed mirror remains plausible, and the negative case is weak at that boundary.
