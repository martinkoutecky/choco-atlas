The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it has no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1, proved here, characterize rule families satisfying unanimity, strategy-proofness, group-wise anonymity, and fairness. Theorem 3 gives an extreme-point characterization. These are structural results about which social choice rules have certain properties; none states a complexity bound or solves an algorithmic problem. Lemmas 1 and 2 are cited characterizations from earlier work, and Section 7’s constructions give no running-time result.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large city choosing a public-facility location, with residents grouped by neighborhood and represented by masses over complete single-peaked rankings. But a rule on those masses would be a new axiomatic model, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. In particular, individual strategy-proofness needs rethinking: an individual has zero mass in an atomless society, so unilateral reports cannot change the outcome. Replacing it with incentives for positive-mass blocs changes the paper’s question.

The paper’s random outcomes are lotteries over alternatives; that is continuity in the outcome space, not in the population. Treating the lottery as the continuous object would miss ChoCo’s scope. So there is no eligible anchor and no honest per-anchor prover question to state. The possible mass-based rule characterization is a follow-up direction, but calling it a computational mirror would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed continuous society is sensible: a large city could be represented by masses over complete single-peaked preference types, with group identity included in each type. I would not reject the mirror on the grounds that groups have no multiplicity. But that gives the paper a population model, not a computational result to continuize.

Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 characterize which rules are probabilistic fixed group ballot rules; Theorems 2–5 characterize deterministic rules and random mixtures, including their fairness constraints. Theorem 6 supplies examples of representative functions, and Section 7 constructs rules for special cases. None gives an algorithm or a complexity bound. The paper says these characterizations may help identify algorithmically tractable rules, but leaves that analysis for later. The strongest possible anchors in the proponent’s account therefore remain axiomatic characterizations after the population is changed.

One could ask for a continuous-population characterization, or ask an algorithm to find a fair strategy-proof rule from group mass distributions. The first is outside ChoCo’s computational scope. The second needs a specified rule representation, input, and design objective that the paper does not supply; with arbitrary ballot maps over mass vectors, the rule space is infinite-dimensional. Individual strategy-proofness also needs a deliberate treatment in an atomless model, where one person’s report has no effect. Choosing block incentives or approximate incentives might produce a worthwhile new problem, but it would add a central modelling choice rather than mirror a result here.

The proponent raised no computational anchor, so there is no per-result continuous question to defeat. Their city example is the best case for a plausible high-multiplicity setting; it still does not furnish the missing computational target. I can make a strong case that this paper is not a ChoCo mirror candidate. I cannot honestly claim that no useful computational project could grow out of its characterizations.
