The case FOR (proponent)

There is no result in this paper that meets the requested computational-complexity anchor standard: it gives no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, parameterized-complexity, or similar result. The strongest nearby anchor is Theorem 1, which is proved here in a simplified form; the paper notes that a more systematic version appeared in Grandi and Endriss (2013). It says that, for an odd number of individuals, majority rule is collectively rational with respect to an integrity constraint \(IC\) if and only if \(IC\) is equivalent to a conjunction of clauses of size at most \(2\). That is a structural characterization, not a complexity classification.

The most convincing continuous counterpart is Continuous Majority-Paradox Recognition. An instance is a set of \(m\) binary issues and an integrity constraint \(IC\). A type is a complete ballot \(x\in\{0,1\}^m\) satisfying \(IC\). A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). For societies with no issue tied at exactly half, aggregate each issue by strict majority. The question is whether there exists such a society whose majority ballot violates \(IC\). A “yes” solution is a support and rational masses witnessing the paradox; a “no” solution means every such society has a rational majority outcome.

This is a natural population mirror: the agents are a large electorate deliberating over a fixed agenda, and people with the same complete ballot are the same type. A high-multiplicity setting is plausible when many participants share a relatively small number of coherent positions. The action is issue-by-issue majority aggregation; the objective is to find a paradoxical society, not to optimize an outcome. The authors would likely recognize the question: it preserves their issues, rationality constraint, aggregator, and definition of paradox, replacing counts with population fractions.

Theorem 1’s characterization carries over to this tie-free distribution setting. If \(IC\) is equivalent to clauses of size at most \(2\), a violating majority would require two literals in some clause each to be false on more than half the population, which is impossible for a society whose types satisfy the clause. If \(IC\) is not equivalent to such a formula, the theorem’s three-person construction gives a continuous witness by assigning mass \(1/3\) to each of its three rational ballots. Thus any paradox has a witness with at most three types. This is an exact finite-to-continuous correspondence, not evidence that continuous optimization makes the problem tractable.

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its general collective-rationality question; it does not need to separately mirror each example in the paper. Its expected result is that the same structural boundary holds in the continuum. A further question is the complexity of recognizing that boundary under a chosen encoding of \(IC\); the paper does not answer it.

The weakness is central: this is a credible continuous-population version of the authors’ problem, but it is not a computational-complexity result of the kind the programme is meant to chart. The theorem answers when paradoxes exist, but offers no hardness or algorithmic classification for deciding that property from a succinctly encoded constraint.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Its sole anchor, Theorem 1, is an axiomatic characterization of when majority preserves an integrity constraint. Continuous Majority-Paradox Recognition is a sensible question, but it adds a complexity problem about the encoding of \(IC\); the theorem itself does not state or study that problem.

The proposed question also has no society as input. If a distribution \(\mu\) is the input, one can sum the masses for each issue, form the majority ballot, and evaluate \(IC\). If the question is whether *some* society causes a paradox, the paper’s theorem already gives the structural boundary, and its proof supplies a witness with at most three ballot types and masses \(1/3\). Any remaining complexity is about recognizing whether the encoded Boolean relation is equivalent to a \(2\)-CNF formula. That may be worth studying, but its difficulty depends on how \(IC\) is represented; it comes from the constraint language, not from continuous population mass.

A stronger proposal might ask how much mass must change to create or avoid a paradox. But the paper specifies no baseline society, change costs, or intervention objective. Choosing those would define a new problem rather than continuize one of its results.

So this anchor does not make a strong case for ChoCo: the paper has a legitimate continuous-population restatement, and the three-type witness is a clean bridge, but the candidate computational question does not make population continuity do meaningful computational work. The negative case is limited, though: it does not show that the restatement is illegitimate, only that this paper is a weak fit for a programme centered on computational problems over continuous societies.
