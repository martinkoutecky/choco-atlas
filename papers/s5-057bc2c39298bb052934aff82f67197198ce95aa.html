The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational-complexity anchor, so I can’t make a strong case under the programme’s stated standard.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: under Assumption 1, each agent’s log-belief for each hypothesis converges almost surely to the average across agents of a log Dirichlet likelihood ratio, evaluated at the true signal distributions and the agents’ finite prior evidence. Proposition 2 and Corollary 3, also proved here, show that finite empirical models can make a wrong hypothesis look better with positive probability, and that a conventional update can then drive belief in the true hypothesis to zero. These are meaningful statistical and convergence results, but none classifies a problem’s computational complexity or gives an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm.

The most plausible population mirror would take a type to include an agent’s signal distributions, finite calibration evidence, and communication role; mass would be the fraction of agents of each type. The decision process would be the paper’s uncertain-likelihood belief update, with limiting type-level beliefs as the quantity of interest. A population of many repeated sensors or analysts with shared calibration and communication characteristics is a plausible regime. But the resulting question—whether the belief dynamics converge to an integral analogue of Theorem 1—is an analytic continuum-agent question. It also needs a continuum communication model that the paper does not provide. Treating it as the requested computational mirror would mistake the paper’s convergence analysis for a complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Equation (1) defines a finite-hypothesis inference objective; it does not classify the complexity of solving a decision problem. Theorem 1 proves almost-sure convergence of a specified update rule. Proposition 2 and Corollary 3 establish that finite empirical models can cause a conventional update to discard the true hypothesis with positive probability. They are substantial results, but none gives a complexity bound or an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm.

A population of repeated sensors or analysts with shared signal models, calibration evidence, and communication roles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. I would not reject it on the ground that agents must be unique. But its natural mirror of Theorem 1 is an integral version of the convergence result: the finite average of log-likelihood ratios becomes an average over agent types. Mirrors of Proposition 2 and Corollary 3 similarly become claims about uncertainty across evidence types. Those are population-continuum questions, but they remain statistical or analytic questions of the kind the programme distinguishes from computational complexity.

One could add a computational task—for example, choosing where to collect calibration data or how to design the communication network. The paper’s future-work discussion makes those topics adjacent. But each adds an optimization objective the paper neither studies nor proves a result about. The most credible population model therefore yields analytic continuizations of the named results; obtaining a computational mirror requires adding a different problem. That is a strong negative case for this paper as written, though it cannot rule out every worthwhile future problem one could build around it.
