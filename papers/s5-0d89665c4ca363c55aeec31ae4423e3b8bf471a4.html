What the mirror covers

The paper covers empirical performance and bias analyses of WMV, EXP4, MetaCMAB, and ExpertiseTree on simulated groups of human advisers; no named computational result is available to anchor a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper’s *setting* has a plausible continuous population analogue. But it has zero eligible anchors: the supplied text contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition establishing a computational complexity result. Algorithm 1 is a procedure, and Sections 2.1 and 2.3 report empirical comparisons and statistical analyses. The paper’s findings about ExpertiseTree and MetaCMAB are not complexity classifications proved here. So I cannot provide a continuous problem, trichotomy prediction, or hardness-transfer claim *per anchor* without inventing one.

A plausible unanchored mirror would model a large, recurring fact-checking platform. A type would be a complete headline-conditioned advice rule, including the contexts and responses the aggregator uses; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the adviser population with that rule. On each headline, an aggregation policy would weight types’ advice, then update after the true label is revealed, with the aim of maximizing cumulative accuracy. A platform with many advisers but relatively few stable advice types could make \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\), and the authors would likely recognize the question as a population version of their adaptive aggregation setting.

The weakness is that the paper gives no evidence that such complete advice types are few: its experiment used 2,005 participants, with simulated groups of at most 36, and advice patterns could be highly individual. More fundamentally, even if the continuous problem is sensible, it is a new computational question rather than a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no result to mirror in the programme’s requested sense. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its claims about ExpertiseTree’s accuracy and bias mitigation are empirical findings from simulations on participant data; Algorithm 1 describes that evaluation. So there are zero eligible result anchors here.

The proposed large-platform mirror is the best counterexample to that negative case. It is plausible to model a recurring fact-checking service as a distribution over complete adviser policies, then ask how an aggregation algorithm should weight their mass as labels arrive. But the paper gives little support for the needed type structure. A type defined by responses across headline contexts and histories preserves the individual calibration and correlated errors that MetaCMAB and ExpertiseTree use; in this setting, those complete policies could be numerous, leaving \(\tau\) close to the number of advisers. Grouping people by demographics or a few headline categories would make \(\tau\) smaller, but could merge advisers with different advice and error patterns. A computational problem would also need choices the paper does not make, such as the headline stream, feedback model, and policy class. That would be a new online-learning formulation rather than a continuous version of a named result in this paper.

That is a meaningful case against treating this paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: a platform with many advisers following a small number of stable, complete policies could make the population formulation sensible. The honest negative verdict is therefore narrow. There is no anchored computational result here, and the proposed unanchored mirror needs substantial additional modeling; the universal claim that no scenario could make it worthwhile remains weak.
