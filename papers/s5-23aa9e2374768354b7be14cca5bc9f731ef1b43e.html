The continuous mirror question

Given projects \(P\), applicant types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq T\times P\) with each type accepting at most two projects, and rational exact quota shares \(\rho_p\), choose project-opening variables \(z_p\in\{0,1\}\) and assignment masses \(x_{tp}\ge0\) to maximize \(\sum_{(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\), subject to \(\sum_{p:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}=\rho_pz_p\) for every project. Determine the best polynomial-time approximation ratio as a function of \(|P|\); the hard instances have \(q\) projects, \(q^2-r\) equal-mass types, and \(\rho_p=q/(q^2-r)\), with optimum \(q\alpha(H)/(q^2-r)\) for the source graph \(H\).

The model it lives in

A bipartite incidence model of projects and applicant types: each type carries rational mass \(\mu_t\) and accepts at most two projects; each project is open or closed and, when open, must receive its exact per-capita quota \(\rho_p\). Variables \(x_{tp}\) allocate type mass subject to at most one project per unit of mass, and the objective is total assigned mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 2 and 11 for unit utilities and exact project quotas, including the project-count inapproximability barrier. It does not mirror the paper's algorithmic results for restricted quotas and treewidth, or its bounds in raw applicant count and integer upper quota.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of wmlq itself. In a large student–project placement round, an applicant’s type records their acceptable projects and utility for each one; applicants with identical records are indistinguishable for the problem. Let \(T\) be the positive-mass types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and let \(P\) be the finite set of projects. Project quotas are fractions of the intake: \(\ell_p\) and \(u_p\). For acceptable type–project pairs, choose assigned masses \(x_{tp}\ge 0\) and project-opening indicators \(z_p\in\{0,1\}\), subject to
\[ \sum_{p\in N(t)}x_{tp}\le \mu_t,\qquad \ell_p z_p\le \sum_{t:p\in N(t)}x_{tp}\le u_pz_p. \]
Maximize \(\sum_{t,p} w_{tp}x_{tp}\). Each unit of mass represents applicants who each receive at most one project; \(x_{tp}\) records how much of their cohort goes to project \(p\). Projects remain open or closed, as in the paper.

This is a plausible regime for large centralized placement systems: many applicants, relatively few recurring preference and eligibility types, and project capacities or minimum viable group sizes that scale with intake. For example, taking \(K\) applicants of each type gives \(N=K\tau\) people over \(\tau\) types. The quotas must scale too, so their per-capita shares remain meaningful. That is the co-modification needed for the population mirror, and it fits the paper’s project-allocation setting.

My lead is HM-MLQ(2,3): for unit utilities, each type finds at most two projects acceptable and each project is adjacent to at most three supported types; maximize assigned mass under rational lower and upper quota shares. The expected result is hardness that transfers, in ChoCo’s Class B sense.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: \(\mathrm{mlq}(2,3)\) is APX-complete. Here \(\mathrm{mlq}\) is the unit-weight version of wmlq, with each applicant applying to at most two posts and each post receiving at most three applications. The paper proves membership in APX using its greedy approximation result, and proves hardness by an approximation-preserving reduction from maximum independent set on cubic graphs; the source APX-completeness is cited externally.

The continuous version preserves that reduction exactly. Given a cubic graph \(H\) with \(r\) edges, make a project for each vertex and a type for each edge. Each edge type accepts its two endpoint projects and has mass \(1/r\). Give every project the exact quota \(3/r\). In a discrete realization, this means \(K\) applicants per edge type, \(Kr\) applicants total, and quota \(3K\) at each project. An open set \(S\) of projects needs mass \(3|S|/r\). The total mass of types incident to \(S\) is only \((3|S|-|E(S)|)/r\), where \(E(S)\) is the set of graph edges with both endpoints in \(S\). Thus an internal edge leaves too little mass to fill the quotas. Conversely, if \(S\) is independent, all types incident to \(S\) can be assigned to their unique endpoint in \(S\). Even though type mass may be split, the optimum is exactly \(3\alpha(H)/r\), where \(\alpha(H)\) is the maximum independent-set size. The discrete combinatorics survive the continuous assignment variables.

This also gives the regime: there are \(Kr\) people but only \(r\) types. Choosing \(K\) large makes every type a substantial cohort while retaining the hard choice among projects. The reduction’s combinatorics lie in the project graph, rather than in one-person granularity, which is why continuization does not remove this hardness. Further questions include whether other quota patterns admit tractable type-mass algorithms, and when fractional splitting makes the continuous optimum differ from the scaled discrete optimum.

A second useful question is HM-MLQ exact-quota approximation: the same mass-allocation problem with unit utilities, at most two acceptable projects per type, and \(\ell_p=u_p\) for every project; ask for the best polynomial-time approximation as a function of the number of projects. The expected result is again hardness transfer, now with a stronger approximation barrier.

Its anchor, Theorem 11, is proved in this paper: for every \(\varepsilon>0\), \(\mathrm{mlq}\) is not approximable within \(|P|^{1-\varepsilon}\), \((\sqrt{|A|})^{1-\varepsilon}\), or \(u_{\max}^{1-\varepsilon}\), unless \(P=NP\), even when every post has equal lower and upper quotas and every applicant has degree at most two. The paper’s reduction and theorem are proved here, using externally cited maximum-independent-set inapproximability.

For the mirror, take a graph with \(q\) vertices and \(r\) edges. The paper’s construction yields singleton-project types and one type per graph edge, with at most \(q+r\) types; duplicate every applicant \(K\) times. There are then \(K(q^2-r)\) people, while the type masses are independent of \(K\). Give each project exact quota \(q/(q^2-r)\). For any open vertex set \(S\), its quota demand is \(q|S|/(q^2-r)\), while the distinct applicant mass available to it is \((q|S|-|E(S)|)/(q^2-r)\). So the open projects must form an independent set, and the maximum assigned mass is \(q\alpha(H)/(q^2-r)\). The project-count inapproximability therefore transfers to this continuous problem.

The other two factors in Theorem 11 depend on the *raw* discrete applicant count and integer quota. They do not survive normalization as intrinsic continuous guarantees: duplicating everyone leaves the mass instance unchanged. That limits what I would claim from this anchor, but its \(|P|^{1-\varepsilon}\) barrier remains meaningful. Further questions include approximation bounds in intrinsic parameters such as support size \(\tau\), and whether bounded support or restricted project overlap changes the classification.

Both questions are recognizably about the paper’s underlying problem: applicants with acceptable posts, at-most-one assignment, project opening, lower and upper quotas, and a maximum-weight objective. Neither replaces the society with fractional outcomes or asks an axiomatic question. The paper’s student–project motivation makes the high-volume cohort regime particularly legible to its authors.

The weakest point is that the reductions are constructed worst cases, and their project quotas must scale with cohort size. A referee might regard those instances as less representative than the paper’s motivating examples. Still, the mirror does not alter the assignment problem to manufacture hardness: repeated applicants share complete types, project capacities scale per capita, and the paper’s exact-quota constraints remain in force. It is a credible high-multiplicity regime, even if it is not every placement market.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t see an honest way to sustain the universal negative. Theorem 2 survives the strongest version of its mirror. In the cubic-graph construction, each edge can be a voter type of mass \(1/r\), and each project has quota \(3/r\). Opening projects then requires an independent set, even though each type’s mass can be split. Repeating every type \(K\) times gives \(Kr\) applicants over only \(r\) types, with quota \(3K\) per open project. That is a coherent high-multiplicity regime for a large placement round, and it mirrors the paper’s assignment problem directly.

Theorem 11 survives too, at least for its project-count approximation barrier. Its singleton-project and edge types have support at most \(q+r\); duplicating each type preserves the normalized instance while making the applicant cohorts arbitrarily large. The \(|P|^{1-\varepsilon}\) barrier remains meaningful in that mass model. The raw-\(|A|\) and \(u_{\max}\) bounds should not be claimed as intrinsic continuous guarantees, since duplication changes those quantities without changing the society. But that limitation does not defeat the surviving project-count anchor.

The strongest skeptical point is about priority, not validity: both constructions tune quotas to the compatibility graph so project opening encodes independent set. They show that this continuous population model can retain hard combinatorics; they do less to show when fractional mass enables new algorithms in realistic placement systems. Still, the programme explicitly wants to identify hard boundaries, and the paper’s project-allocation setting admits plausible recurring applicant types and capacities that scale with intake. The paper has no continuous computational result that already answers that question.

So I can offer that caveat as a reason to seek a more revealing mirror first, but not as a case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Neither the exact hardness transfer nor the shared high-multiplicity literature is a sound objection under the programme’s stated criteria.
