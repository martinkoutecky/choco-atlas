The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no anchor, and I don’t see a named computational problem in the paper that could supply one. Its headline results are empirical rates over the Scottish and NSW datasets. The strongest candidate is Section 5.2: under the proportional completion model, winner sets change in 130 elections, affecting 3.5% of seats. But that is a data-dependent finding, not a complexity result about a problem the paper defines.

The best attempt to turn it into a mirror would be to give partial ballot types masses and ask for the least mass of missing rankings that changes a winner set. That is a coherent sensitivity problem, and the authors might recognize it as related to their concern about exhaustion. But it changes the task: the paper tests particular completion models; it does not study optimizing over completions or give an algorithmic claim to continue into the continuous setting. The other likely anchors fare no better: the exhaustion and quota rates are descriptive measurements, not computational problems.

So the case against a *mirror of this paper* is strong: a continuous completion optimization might be a worthwhile follow-up, but it would be a newly posed problem, not a continuization of one of the paper’s computational results. The negative case is weaker if “mirror” is stretched to include any new algorithmic question motivated by the paper; then the completion problem remains a plausible candidate.
