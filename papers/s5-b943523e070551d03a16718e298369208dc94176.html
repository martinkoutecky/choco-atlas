The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(j\in[L]\), buyer types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(s_j\in[0,1]\), and explicitly listed nonnegative monotone valuations \(v_t(B)\) for bundles \(B\subseteq[L]\), decide whether there are prices \(p\ge0\) and assignment masses \(z_{tB}\ge0\) such that \(\sum_B z_{tB}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:j\in B}z_{tB}=s_j\), and every bundle assigned positive mass maximizes \(v_t(B)-\sum_{j\in B}p_j\) for its type.

The model it lives in

A unit mass of buyers is distributed over finitely many valuation types; each buyer receives an integral bundle, while assignments across each type's mass may split. Goods supply scales with the population, and the decision is whether prices and assignments support a market equilibrium.

The objection that survived

The explicit-table atomless model appears to reduce to a generic welfare LP, leaving little theorem-specific algorithmic content; this limits the mirror's depth, but not the coherence of the question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the bundle-market equilibrium existence question anchored in Theorem 2. It leaves aside Theorem 1's equivalence, the multi-unit auction results, and the matching-core results in Theorems 3 and 4.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is on the buyer side of the paper’s bundle auctions. Picture a procurement market with many small firms buying from a shared stock of standardized inputs. Each firm has one of a small number of production or valuation schedules over bundles of those inputs. The type set is those schedules, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of firms of type \(t\). The stock of each input scales with the firm population. Each firm still receives an integral bundle; only the allocation across a mass of firms is fractional.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for a bundle auction \([f_i]\) whose maximum value function \(V\) is weakly monotonic at \(e\)—that is, \(V(e+e_j)\ge V(e)\) for every \(j\), and \(V(2e)>V(e)\)—market equilibrium exists if and only if \(V(e)\) equals the optimum of the LP
\[ \max \sum_{x\in C(2e)}\alpha(x)V(x) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{x\in C(2e)}\alpha(x)x=e,\qquad \sum_{x\in C(2e)}\alpha(x)=1,\qquad \alpha(x)\ge0. \]
Here \(C(2e)=\{0,1,2\}^L\). The paper introduces this as a consequence of Theorem 1 and a result from Lahiri (2006), and gives an independent proof in its appendix. This is a computationally useful characterization, though the paper does not claim a running-time bound.

The precise continuous question I would hand to a prover is Atomless Bundle Market Equilibrium (lead). An instance has \(L\) goods, a finite set \(T\) of buyer types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(s_j\in[0,1]\), and, for each \(t\), an explicitly listed nonnegative monotone valuation \(v_t(B)\) for every bundle \(B\subseteq[L]\). Decide whether the market has an equilibrium; a solution is a price vector \(p\ge0\) and masses \(z_{tB}\ge0\) such that
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq[L]}z_{tB}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni j}z_{tB}=s_j, \]
and every bundle with \(z_{tB}>0\) maximizes \(v_t(B)-\sum_{j\in B}p_j\) for its type. Thus \(z_{tB}\) is the fraction of the whole population that is type \(t\) and receives \(B\), not a fractional bundle received by any individual. Allowing the zero price vector follows the standard equilibrium convention; restricting to nondegenerate instances with a positive scarcity price matches the paper’s nonzero-price convention.

I expect this mirror to be tractable for the stated explicit input: the welfare-maximizing allocation is an LP with \(|T|2^L\) allocation variables, and its dual supplies equilibrium prices. Its size is polynomial in the explicitly listed valuation table, not necessarily in \(L\) for a succinctly represented valuation. With succinct valuations, the relevant pricing task—maximizing \(v_t(B)-p(B)\) over bundles—may itself be hard. That is a natural follow-up, not a tractability claim.

The authors should recognize the question: it keeps their goods, buyers, bundle choices, prices, and profit-maximizing demand, while making the buyer population the continuous object. The paper also explicitly relates bundle auctions to multi-unit auctions, which supports scaling inventory with population. The mirror covers the market-equilibrium existence question behind Theorem 2; it does not continue the paper’s matching-core equivalence.

The soft spot is that Theorem 2’s named statement has finitely many buyers and one unit of each good, whereas this mirror scales supply with population and groups buyers by valuation type. That is a co-modification needed for a meaningful high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s multi-unit discussion makes it plausible, but it means this is not a literal restatement of Theorem 2. A second open question is how accurately the continuous equilibrium approximates a finite market when type proportions and supplies must be rounded to whole firms and items.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent’s anchor is that Theorem 2 is thinner computationally than the proposed mirror suggests. It characterizes equilibrium existence by comparing \(V(e)\) with an LP optimum over \(C(2e)\), but gives no input encoding or running-time result. The mirror’s explicit valuation tables then produce a generic welfare LP: one variable for each type and bundle, with equilibrium prices supplied by the dual. That is a clean computation, but its complexity follows from the chosen table representation; with succinct valuations, the new question becomes a bundle-demand or pricing problem whose representation the paper never specifies.

That weakens the connection to the theorem’s particular contribution. The paper studies when a finite allocation of indivisible goods among buyers can be supported by prices. The proposed mirror gives each type’s population mass permission to split across bundles, while supply scales with population. Those are defensible co-modifications, and the standardized-firm scenario makes them plausible. They also leave no theorem-specific computational obstacle in the explicit-input version. A harder succinct-input version could be worthwhile, but choosing that encoding supplies a new research question rather than resolving a difficulty identified by this paper.

Nearby literature makes the novelty case less sharp, though it does not settle it. Azevedo, Weyl, and White study continuum-agent markets with indivisible goods and quasilinear utility, proving equilibrium existence under broad preferences ([paper record](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/1btvv-6pd54)). Paes Leme and Wong give algorithms for Walrasian equilibrium from aggregate demand, with complexity independent of the number of buyers in that model ([paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-018-1334-9)). The former is an analytic continuum treatment; the latter does not give the proposed rational type-mass formulation. Under the stated prior-art standard, neither is a collision.

So the negative case is weak. Theorem 2 does not state a complexity classification, and the proposed explicit-table mirror risks being a routine LP exercise. But the mirror is coherent, population multiplicity is sensible for repeated buyer types, and scaling supply is supported by the paper’s multi-unit model. I cannot honestly claim that these objections defeat the anchor: its continuous computational question remains well-posed, even if its payoff may be a straightforward Class A result.
