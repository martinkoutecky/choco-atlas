The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\), and a rational distribution \((\mu_A)\) over supported approval types \(A\subseteq C\), decide whether \(W\) satisfies continuous PJR. For every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every choice of coalition masses \(x_A\in[0,\mu_A]\), if \(\sum_A x_A\ge \ell/k\) and all types with \(x_A>0\) share at least \(\ell\) approved candidates, must their approval union contain at least \(\ell\) members of \(W\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over approval-set types, with each supported type \(A\) carrying mass \(\mu_A\); a coalition selects mass \(x_A\) from each type. The committee remains a discrete size-\(k\) subset of \(C\), and the test checks the PJR quota and representation condition using population mass. Because the condition depends on the selected types’ common approvals and union, a violating coalition can be represented by the subset of types it uses and their total mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers PJR testing from Theorem 1 and its bounded-approval algorithmic case from Theorem 4. It leaves the committee-construction result and the paper’s EJR discussion outside the stated questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of continuous PJR testing in the support size \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\)?
  • How does tractability change as the maximum approval-set size \(a\) grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The natural mirror is testing PJR for a society described by approval mass. It fits a large committee election or public consultation: there may be millions of participants, but many share the same approval ballot. A type is an approval set \(A\subseteq C\), and \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of the population with that ballot. The input lists only the \(\tau\) supported types and their binary rational masses. The committee \(W\) remains an ordinary \(k\)-candidate set. Only the population becomes continuous.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: given an approval profile \(\vec A\), committee size \(k\), and a committee \(W\) of size \(k\), deciding whether \(W\) satisfies PJR is coNP-complete. The proof reduces Balanced Biclique to the complement of the testing problem.

The corresponding question is Continuous PJR Testing. Given \(C\), \(k\), a size-\(k\) committee \(W\), and a rational distribution \((\mu_A)\) over the supported approval types, decide whether \(W\) satisfies this condition: for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every choice of coalition masses \(x_A\in[0,\mu_A]\), if \(\sum_A x_A\ge \ell/k\) and the types with \(x_A>0\) have at least \(\ell\) commonly approved candidates, then their union of approved candidates contains at least \(\ell\) members of \(W\). A “no” solution can give \(\ell\) and the violating mass vector; the question is whether any such violation exists.

I expect this problem to be coNP-complete, with hardness transferring (Class B). Any violating coalition can be enlarged to include the full mass of every type it uses: its common intersection and union stay the same, while its mass only increases. Thus a certificate for failure can simply list the selected types and \(\ell\), with rational mass sums checked exactly. For hardness, turn any discrete profile with \(n\) voters into masses \(\mu_A=\#\{i:A_i=A\}/n\). A discrete violating group gives a continuous violation, and a continuous violation expands to a group of whole ballot types that violates the discrete condition. This also gives the authors a direct two-way dictionary between their problem and its high-multiplicity form. The continuous version preserves the problem’s central choices and constraints; it does not soften PJR or fractionalize the committee.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here: if the maximum approval-set size \(a=\max_i|A_i|\) is bounded, testing PJR is polynomial-time solvable, with stated running time \(O(m^{a+1}m^2n)\). Its mirror is Bounded-Approval Continuous PJR Testing: the same mass-distribution input and question, restricted to types with \(|A|\le a\) for a fixed constant \(a\). I expect this version to be in P. For each \(\ell\le a\), enumerate a common set \(S\subseteq C\) of size \(\ell\) and a set \(R\subseteq W\) of size at most \(\ell-1\). Sum the mass of types satisfying \(S\subseteq A\) and \(A\cap W\subseteq R\). If that sum reaches \(\ell/k\), those types witness a PJR violation; otherwise none exists. With fixed \(a\), the enumeration and exact rational arithmetic take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). This is a useful tractable boundary for the same question, though it preserves a discrete tractability result rather than showing a new gain from continuization.

The authors should recognize both mirrors: PJR already reasons about how much of the electorate a cohesive group represents, using the quota \(\ell n/k\). Replacing voter counts by population mass gives \(\ell/k\), while leaving ballots, candidates, committees, and the representation requirement intact. The scenario is plausible for large electorates with recurring approval patterns, and the bounded-\(a\) case fits settings where each participant endorses only a small, fixed number of proposals. The paper’s result about constructing a PJR committee is outside this mirror; these questions concern testing a given committee.

The main weakness is that the lead mirror’s hardness is an exact high-multiplicity transfer, not a new use of continuous optimization. Moreover, Theorem 1’s reduction does not show hardness when \(\tau\) is small relative to the electorate; its support can grow with the number of voters. So the mirror establishes a clean, computationally meaningful problem and a Class B boundary, but it does not settle how the problem behaves in the most compressed population regimes. Natural next questions are whether the obvious search over type subsets can be improved, what parameterized bounds apply in \(\tau\), and how tractability changes as the approval-size bound \(a\) grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirrors have little specifically continuous computation in them. In a PJR violation, once you choose which approval types participate, every constraint depends on their common approvals and the union of their approvals within \(W\). Taking more mass from any selected type cannot hurt those constraints, so a witness can always use that type’s full mass. The apparent fractional coalition choice therefore collapses to selecting a subset of supported types and summing their weights. That is a weighted combinatorial test, not a problem whose difficulty comes from optimizing over fractional mass.

That limits the novelty of the Theorem 1 mirror. For rational masses, clearing denominators gives a finite profile with the same PJR violations; conversely, normalizing a finite profile gives such a mass distribution. The paper’s coNP-completeness therefore transfers, and a failure certificate can list the selected types and \(\ell\). This makes the result look like a succinct, high-multiplicity version of the existing test, rather than a new continuous-optimization phenomenon. But that is not a decisive objection under ChoCo’s criteria: the high-multiplicity regime is part of the programme, and an exact discrete-to-continuous correspondence can itself be a useful result. It does not make the mirror a collision.

Theorem 4’s bounded-approval mirror is even more direct. For each \(\ell\le a\), enumerate a common candidate set \(S\) of size \(\ell\) and a set \(R\subseteq W\) of size less than \(\ell\). Sum the mass of types \(A\) with \(S\subseteq A\) and \(A\cap W\subseteq R\). A sum reaching \(\ell/k\) witnesses a violation; otherwise none exists. For fixed \(a\), this is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). It is a sensible mass-based test, but its tractability follows almost immediately from the same bounded-set structure as the paper’s algorithm.

So the negative case is one of priority, not validity: neither mirror seems likely to expose much new continuous-optimization machinery, and the second largely carries over an existing tractability result. I cannot honestly turn that into the requested universal claim. The paper has named computational results, approval types are a natural population description, PJR already uses a population quota, and neither mirror loses information needed by the test. Theorem 1 in particular survives as a well-posed continuous high-multiplicity question. Under the stated standard, the best case against is weak.
