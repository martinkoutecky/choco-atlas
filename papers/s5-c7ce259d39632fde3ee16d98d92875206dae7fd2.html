The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(p\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported complete rankings of \(C\), and a rational deletion budget \(\rho\), can one choose deleted masses \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\rho\) such that, for every \(c\ne p\), \(\sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)\bigl((m-\operatorname{pos}_t(p))-(m-\operatorname{pos}_t(c))\bigr)>0\)?

The model it lives in

The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) supported complete rankings and a rational deletion share \(\rho\). Choose deleted masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), with \(\sum_t x_t\le\rho\), to maximize the minimum Borda score margin of \(p\) over its opponents. This is a linear program in the \(\tau\) mass variables and one margin variable.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's complete-vote CCDV-Borda problem; it leaves the candidate-control results, vote-addition results, and top-truncated-vote results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an optimal fractional deletion be rounded to a discrete deletion set with a bounded loss in \(p\)'s winning margin?
  • Does \(W[2]\)-hardness persist when ballot types have large multiplicities and deletions are restricted to the corresponding \(1/n\)-mass grid?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s vote deletion result. Its Theorem 1, proved in this paper by a reduction from DOMINATING SET, states that CCDV-Borda is W[2]-hard when parameterized by the number \(\ell\) of deleted votes. The underlying problem is: given candidates \(C\), a distinguished candidate \(p\), a multiset \(V\) of complete rankings, and \(\ell\), can at most \(\ell\) votes be deleted so that \(p\) becomes the unique Borda winner? The paper presents this as settling an open question of Liu and Zhu.

A continuous version keeps that question’s ingredients and changes the population scale. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking of \(C\), and let \(\mu_t>0\) be its fraction of the electorate, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The input is the support of \(\mu\), its binary rational masses, \(p\), and a rational deletion budget \(\rho\in[0,1]\). A solution is a vector of deleted masses \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\rho\), such that \(p\) is the unique Borda winner of the remaining society. In particular, for every \(c\ne p\),
\[ \sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)\bigl((m-\operatorname{pos}_t(p))-(m-\operatorname{pos}_t(c))\bigr)>0. \]
Call this problem Fractional Borda Vote-Deletion Control. Its decision is whether a permitted mass deletion exists; its output is the deletion vector \(x\).

The regime is a large election with a modest candidate slate and many repeated ballot types: for instance, an election with a fixed small number \(m\) of candidates has at most \(m!\) complete rankings, while the number \(n\) of voters may be much larger. A type is a ranking because, in the paper’s model, voters with the same ranking contribute the same Borda scores and each deleted vote has the same unit cost. Removing a fraction of one type is then the aggregate form of deleting some of its ballots. This is a mirror of the authors’ control problem, not a move to fractional outcomes or a probability model.

The finite-to-continuous dictionary is explicit. If a discrete election has \(n_t\) ballots of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); deleting \(d_t\) such ballots corresponds to \(x_t=d_t/n\), and a limit of \(\ell\) deletions corresponds to \(\rho=\ell/n\). Every discrete solution gives a continuous one on this \(1/n\) grid. The continuous problem also permits masses between grid points, so it is a relaxation rather than an identity with the discrete decision problem.

This mirror should be tractable. Its feasibility is a linear program over the \(\tau\) type masses: introduce a margin variable \(z\), maximize \(z\), and require each displayed score difference to be at least \(z\). A positive optimum is exactly the condition that \(p\) can be made a unique winner. The LP has size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. So Theorem 1’s parameterized hardness would not carry over to the fractional population problem as stated; the combinatorial choice of whole ballots has become a continuous allocation of deleted mass.

I would keep the scope to Theorem 1. The paper’s candidate addition and deletion theorems concern changing the candidate set, not the population, and are not part of this mirror. Its truncated-vote algorithms could motivate separate mirrors, but they are not needed to make the strongest case here.

The weakest point is the granularity change. Theorem 1 proves hardness in \(\ell\), the number of indivisible ballot deletions; the LP allows fractional deletion and measures its budget as a population share. When \(\ell\) stays fixed while \(n\) grows, \(\ell/n\) tends to zero, so the theorem alone says little about the fixed-share regime. Also, the reduction in the theorem does not establish a many-voters-per-type setting. These are real limits on what the anchor demonstrates, but they do not make the mirror foreign to the paper: the Borda rule, target, deletion operation, and unique-winner requirement all remain intact. They make this a useful test of whether its hardness depends on ballot indivisibility.

Two natural follow-up questions are whether a fractional solution can be rounded to a discrete deletion set with a bounded winner-margin loss, and whether W[2]-hardness persists when ballot types have large multiplicities and deletions are restricted to the corresponding \(1/n\)-mass grid.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes the parameter that makes Theorem 1 notable. The theorem is W[2]-hard in \(\ell\), the number of individual votes deleted. In a population of size \(n\), keeping \(\ell\) fixed makes the corresponding share \(\rho=\ell/n\) vanish; keeping \(\rho\) positive instead makes \(\ell\) grow with \(n\). So the paper’s reduction does not establish hardness in the large-electorate, fixed-share regime. The mirror’s LP answers a related per-capita control question, but it does not continue the theorem’s parameterized hardness result.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror. With rankings as types and deletion budget measured as a population share, one can ask how the best attainable Borda margin varies with that share. This remains the paper’s control action and winner condition, and the explicit support makes it a linear optimization problem. For a discrete election, rounding deleted masses down to multiples of \(1/n\) changes each pairwise margin by at most \(\tau(m-1)/n\); thus a continuous solution with a sufficient margin gives a discrete one with controlled loss. Large elections with repeated rankings are also a natural high-multiplicity regime.

I find no sound objection based on identity, degeneracy, or prior continuous work here. The honest negative case is therefore narrow: this mirror should not be presented as preserving Theorem 1’s W[2]-hardness parameterization. That is a useful qualification, but it does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The per-capita version still appears to be a credible mirror of the paper’s vote-control problem.
