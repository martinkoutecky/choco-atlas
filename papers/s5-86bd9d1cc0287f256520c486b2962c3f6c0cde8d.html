Theorem 2, proved in this paper, extends existence to \(m\) groups of prescribed sizes \(k_1,\ldots,k_m\). Neither gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic result. The two-group protocol is described without a numbered result; the no-finite-protocol statement for three or more groups is attributed to Stromquist (2008), not established as a numbered result here.

The most plausible population mirror would be a setting such as a busy public facility assigning many attendees to a few time slots, where attendees fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of preference types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of attendees of type \(t\), let \(\alpha_j\) be the required fraction in group \(j\), and let \(x\) specify the contiguous cake partition. Choose assignment masses \(y_{t,j}\) with row sums \(\mu_t\), column sums \(\alpha_j\), and \(y_{t,j}>0\) only when type \(t\) prefers piece \(j\) at \(x\). For rational masses and quotas, this is the high-multiplicity version of Theorem 2 by replication. But the paper supplies no computational anchor for asking whether this problem is tractable, and its abstract preference functions would need a finite input representation before that could be a prover-ready complexity question.

So the honest positive case is only that Theorem 2 concerns a population and group quotas that have a natural high-multiplicity interpretation. It does not support a continuous-mirror claim about computation. The paper’s central results remain existence results, and the cake’s divisibility alone is outcome-space continuity, not population continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is simple: this paper proves existence, not computational results. Theorem 1 is the standard existence theorem for envy-free cake division; Theorem 2 extends it to groups of prescribed sizes. Neither states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm. The paper’s own two-group protocol is an unnumbered special case. So there is no computational result here for ChoCo’s complexity programme to carry over.

The best population mirror is easy to formulate. Suppose a large facility divides its day among groups with prescribed population shares. Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of each preference type, let \(\alpha_j\) be group \(j\)’s share, and let \(x\) describe the cuts. Assignment masses \(y_{t,j}\) must sum to \(\mu_t\) by type and \(\alpha_j\) by group, and positive assignment to \(j\) requires that type \(t\) prefer piece \(j\). This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; the paper’s small basketball example does not rule it out. For rational masses and quotas, replication turns it directly into a finite instance of Theorem 2. That yields an existence statement, not a complexity result.

To ask a computational question, one must also specify how preferences are represented. The paper permits arbitrary hungry preferences with closed preference sets. That is not a finite input description, even when there are only a few types. A computable mirror would need to choose a valuation language, such as rational piecewise-constant densities, and then study how to find cuts satisfying those valuations. Once cuts are fixed, the assignment of type masses is a flow problem; the substantive search is over the cake partition and the chosen valuation model. That could be a useful project in its own right, but its complexity question comes from added modeling choices rather than from a computational result in this paper.

The cited impossibility of finite protocols for three or more players does not supply the missing anchor either. It concerns computation under very general preference assumptions, and it is attributed to Stromquist rather than established here as a complexity result for the group theorem. Making the population continuous does not provide the finite preference representation needed to turn that protocol question into an algorithmic complexity landscape.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as written: it contains no named computational result whose continuous mirror fits the programme. It is weaker as a literal claim that no related continuous-computation question could ever be worthwhile. A typed, finitely represented population model could motivate one; it would be a new algorithmic study of cake allocation, not a computational mirror of this paper’s results.
