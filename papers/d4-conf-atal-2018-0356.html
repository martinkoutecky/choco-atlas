The strongest candidate I can offer, explicitly not an anchor, is a continuous version of the paper’s undesirable-influence minimisation problem. Consider a large platform whose users fall into a modest number of complete archetypes: users of a type share the interests, capacity, trust characteristics, and initial message state that the model uses. Let \(\mu_a\) be the fraction of users of type \(a\), and retain their social contacts through a fixed type-to-type interaction matrix. Given the AMID update rules, a target undesirable message, a finite time horizon, and a budget \(B\) measured as a fraction of the population, choose masses \(x_{a,r}\) of each type to seed with each permitted message \(r\), with \(0\le x_{a,r}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_{a,r}x_{a,r}\le B\). The induced stochastic dynamics determine a distribution of user states over time. Minimize the expected cumulative active attention paid to the target message, using the paper’s AICD measure. The solution is the seed-mass allocation \(x\) and its resulting objective value.

This keeps the paper’s computational question—where to seed which influence to suppress another—and continuizes the population: millions of users can share a small number of types, and a seed allocation is naturally expressed as a population fraction. Time and the discrete message opinions remain as in the paper. The interaction matrix is a necessary part of this candidate because AMID’s local network contacts drive its dynamics; a distribution over user types alone would discard that structure. The authors would likely recognize the optimization goal, though they might regard the continuum dynamics as a new analytical model rather than a computational continuation of their agent-based study.

I would treat the candidate’s complexity as open, with continuum-specific hardness a plausible possibility: the choice of seed masses acts through interacting, time-dependent stochastic states, and the paper gives no structural or algorithmic result that settles the resulting optimization. The weakest point is decisive for this assignment: the paper supplies no named computational anchor, and its network-dependent dynamics make a credible population model substantially more involved than simply replacing counts with fractions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would vote against a green for this paper: it offers no named computational result to mirror. Its contributions are a diffusion model, an update algorithm, and experiments. The seed-selection procedure is greedy, but the paper gives no complexity or approximation result for it; Equation 18 states an objective, not an algorithmic classification. So there is no eligible anchor among the paper’s results.

The proposed seed-mass problem is the strongest replacement, but a type-to-type contact matrix does not by itself preserve AMID’s dynamics. Users read messages from particular neighbours; trust is learned from directed user pairs; and capacity limits determine which messages stay active. Two users with the same interests and capacity can therefore behave differently because their neighbours and histories differ. To model that exactly, one may need to include those local contexts in the types, potentially losing the useful compression. A finite-block or graphon model could restore a continuous population and make seed-mass optimization well posed, but it would require new assumptions about contacts and a new state representation. That makes it a plausible research direction, not a continuation directly supplied by this paper.

That is the strongest case against, but it does not establish the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror. A large platform with repeated behavioral types and a defensible block structure is plausible, and its continuous seed-allocation problem could be computationally interesting. The honest negative is narrower: this paper supplies no computational theorem to anchor such a mirror, and the best candidate needs substantial new modeling before it is clearly the authors’ problem.
