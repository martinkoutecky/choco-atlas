The continuous mirror question

Given total population mass \(N\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete valuation types with \(\sum_t \mu_t=1\), and a Robertson–Webb oracle for each type's normalized additive nonatomic valuation, what is the worst-case number of cut and evaluation queries needed to output a finite list \((t_j,q_j,X_j)_{j=1}^S\)? Require \(\sum_{j:t_j=t}q_j=N\mu_t\), \(\sum_j q_j\mathbf{1}_{x\in X_j}=1\) almost everywhere, and \(v_{t_j}(X_j)\ge v_{t_j}(X_k)\) for every pair of cohorts \(j,k\).

The model it lives in

A society of total mass \(N\) has type distribution \(\mu\) over complete valuation oracles. Decision variables are cohort masses \(q_j\) and actual cake pieces \(X_j\); the objective is to minimize the worst-case Robertson–Webb query count subject to full resource allocation and envy-freeness.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1's \(\Omega(n^2)\) query lower bound; it leaves the paper's discussion of proportional division and other cake-cutting results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does worst-case query complexity depend on \(N\), support size \(\tau\), mass bit length \(L\), and output size \(S\)?
  • For fixed \(\tau\), does the unbounded query difficulty known for finite-player envy-free cake cutting persist when valuation oracles are shared across a continuous population?

The case FOR (proponent)

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper: in the Robertson–Webb model, achieving an envy-free cake allocation has query complexity \(\Omega(n^2)\). The bound is against algorithms with no restriction beyond that query model; the counted queries are cuts and evaluations. This is a concrete query lower bound, not an NP-hardness result or a running-time lower bound. It is the one anchor I would use.

My lead mirror is Mass-RW Envy-Free Cake Cutting. A plausible regime is a large public resource allocation—say, many beneficiaries dividing a heterogeneous pool—where complete valuation profiles recur because beneficiaries fall into a modest number of standardized classes. Let \(N\) be the population mass, with \(N\gg\tau\), and let \(\mu\) give the fractions of that mass in the \(\tau\) types. A type \(t\) is the complete valuation \(v_t\): an additive, nonatomic valuation of cake intervals, normalized by \(v_t([0,1])=1\). The application only fits this regime if people within a type really share that valuation, not merely a rough label.

Here is the problem precisely. Take \(N\in\mathbb N\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and, for definiteness, instances with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb N\). Each type has a Robertson–Webb oracle for its valuation. The algorithm may ask that oracle for an interval’s value or for a cut attaining a specified value. It must output a finite list \((t_j,q_j,X_j)_{j=1}^S\): mass \(q_j>0\) of type \(t_j\) receives the actual piece \(X_j\), a finite union of intervals. Require \(\sum_{j:t_j=t}q_j=N\mu_t\) for every type, and require \(\sum_{j:x\in X_j}q_j=1\) for almost every cake point \(x\), so the cake is fully allocated. Envy-freeness is ex post: for every two positive-mass cohorts \(j,k\), \(v_{t_j}(X_j)\ge v_{t_j}(X_k)\). In particular, this does not replace each person’s bundle with an expected bundle; \(\lambda\), or equivalently the list, records population mass assigned to each actual piece. The computational question is the worst-case number of type-oracle queries needed to produce such a list, as a function of \(N,\tau\), the mass bit length \(L\), and the output support \(S\).

This is a mass population version of the paper’s allocation problem: it retains the cake, the type valuations, full resource use, and the same envy comparisons. It changes named agents into divisible cohorts that can be split across pieces. That is the high-multiplicity move. A natural setting is a large allocation program with many beneficiaries but relatively few recurring, complete valuation templates. The discrete bridge is explicit when \(N\mu_t\) are integer counts; the relaxation comes from allowing a type’s mass to divide into subcohorts.

For the intended regime \(N\gg\tau\), I expect the quadratic \(N\) dependence to weaken: cut and evaluation information can be reused across everyone sharing a type. My tentative prediction is an output-sensitive algorithmic result—Class A—with query cost governed roughly by the number of cohorts produced and \(\tau\), rather than by \(N^2\) independent agent-type interactions. I am not claiming that bound follows from Theorem 3.1. The paper’s theorem motivates the question; it does not settle it. Further questions are whether \(S\) can be bounded by \(O(N+\tau)\), whether a succinct output avoids a population-sized list, and whether the query complexity can be polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\) when \(N\) is binary encoded.

The weakest point is that the anchor’s lower bound relies on separately queryable valuations for named agents, while the mirror deliberately shares one oracle across a type and lets its mass split. The proof therefore does not transfer as a lower bound, and an explicit output may itself require many cohorts. The case survives as a precise, author-recognizable question about whether their envy-free allocation barrier comes from population size or from the number of distinct valuation types; it does not claim that this paper already answers that question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is a genuine computational anchor: it proves an \(\Omega(n^2)\) lower bound in the Robertson–Webb query model. So I can’t dismiss the paper for lacking a computational result. Nor can I claim the question is already answered continuously: the query-complexity work I checked is still framed around finite players, as in [Brânzei and Nisan’s study](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/f7a7bb369e48f10e85fce85b67d8c516-Abstract-Conference.html).

The strongest objection is about whether a low-\(\tau\) population is a convincing regime for this particular model. A type here must be the agent’s entire valuation function over cake intervals. With arbitrary additive, nonatomic valuations, exact duplicates are a strong condition; a coarse label such as “prefers mornings” does not suffice if people with that label value some intervals differently. The paper’s broad oracle model permits many distinct valuations, but the proposed mirror’s hoped-for gain depends on many people sharing a small number of complete valuation functions. A public set of exact valuation templates is possible, but feels tailored to this setting: make the templates sufficiently rich to preserve the paper’s problem, and their repetition becomes harder to motivate; make them simple and known, and the question moves toward a special-purpose allocation model.

Even granting repeated, hidden templates, the best mirror is coherent: ask how many Robertson–Webb queries suffice for exact envy-free allocation with \(N\) units of population mass and \(\tau\) valuation oracles, while respecting single-cake capacity. But this changes the source of difficulty from learning many independently queried valuations to reusing a few oracles. The theorem’s lower-bound argument supplies no evidence about the resulting complexity; it exploits agents’ independent valuations. And the mass formulation still needs a large output: if each listed cohort receives a positive-value piece \(X_j\), cake capacity forces its mass \(q_j\le 1\). With total mass \(N\), an explicit allocation therefore needs at least \(N\) cohorts. A query reduction could still matter, but it would not give a succinct allocation or a general runtime improvement. Compressing the output into an allocation kernel would raise a different issue: the paper’s arbitrary valuation oracles provide no finite description for such an object.

That is the best negative case I can make, but it does not defeat the mirror outright. The exact-template scenario is admissible, the mass query problem is well-posed, and the authors could reasonably recognize it as their problem. My case is that its natural regime and payoff are less persuasive than for papers whose types are already short, recurring descriptions. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
