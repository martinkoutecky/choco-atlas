What the mirror covers

The paper’s empirical rankings, Monte Carlo estimates, and comparisons of member influence are not covered by a continuous computational mirror anchored in a named result.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. It reports empirical Banzhaf rankings and describes geometric enumeration and Monte Carlo estimation; its statement that exact calculation is infeasible for the committee studied is not a named complexity result. So there is no valid anchor to restate, and I cannot give the required anchor-based continuous problem without inventing a result.

The strongest possible lead is an unanchored one: model a large delegation as a distribution of interchangeable members over policy-position and attendance types, then ask how a spatial Banzhaf notion of pivotality behaves as the delegation grows. But the paper’s power index counts individual members who are critical in minimal winning coalitions. In a continuum, an individual has zero mass, and there is no canonical uniform probability over the resulting continuum of coalitions. Defining a continuous index would therefore require a substantive new choice, not just replacing voter counts with proportions.

That makes the potential mirror an interesting research direction, but not a case anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The main weakness is that the paper studies a specific 46-member committee and empirical rankings, rather than a computational complexity question in a high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that there is no computational theorem here to mirror. The paper reports a power ranking and estimates it by Monte Carlo over attendance and substitution configurations for a fixed committee of 46 seats. The \(4\times10^{26}\) figure describes that finite roster problem; it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result. The other analyst is right that an anchor would have to be invented.

The best broader proposal is a large membership body whose members have ideology, agricultural affinity, and attendance types, with a question about how spatial Banzhaf power changes as the population grows. That is a plausible high multiplicity setting. It runs into the definition of the paper’s index, though: \(\eta_i\) counts finite winning convex coalitions in which deleting individual \(i\) makes the coalition lose. In a continuous population, deleting one person changes neither the mass of a coalition nor its support up to measure zero. Treating an entire type as pivotal instead makes that type a voting bloc, while the paper explicitly does not assume coordinated bloc voting. A power density based on infinitesimal mass changes could be defined, but it would need new choices about which policy rectangles to weight and how to normalize influence. It would be a new index, not a continuous version already specified by the paper.

A stronger computational proposal might ask for the least mass and cost needed to make a policy coalition win. That could be a sensible bribery or control problem, but the paper gives no costs or mass-transfer operation, so it adds a new problem rather than continuing one of its results. Its finite-support geometry may also admit direct enumeration of rectangle boundaries; the paper’s large computation comes from named-seat substitutions, which disappear when those seats are replaced by population mass.

I would not claim that a continuous influence-density study could never be worthwhile. The negative case is that it would require inventing both the computational problem and its power measure, while the paper itself supplies no computational result to continue. That makes this a weak source for a continuous mirror, even though a separate project could develop a useful theory of mass-based influence.
