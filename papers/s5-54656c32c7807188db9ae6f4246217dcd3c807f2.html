Why no mirror

The paper presents a straightforward procedure for computing the fractional approval scores, but it has no numbered result asserting a computational claim. The continuous approval-mass ranking is a faithful analogue, yet it cannot pass the required named-result gate.

What the mirror covers

The mirror captures the fractional-vote score calculation and ranking over approval ballots; it does not cover the axiomatic discussion or the empirical comparisons.

The case FOR (proponent)

The continuous-population model is natural here. Let \(W\) be the wines, and let each type be an approval set \(S\subseteq W\). An instance is a finite support \(\mathcal S\) with rational masses \(\mu_S\ge 0\) summing to \(1\); the empty approval set is allowed. For each wine \(w\), compute
\[ \phi_\mu(w)=\sum_{\substack{S\in\mathcal S\\w\in S}}\frac{\mu_S}{|S|}, \]
with empty sets contributing zero, then return the wines ordered by \(\phi_\mu\), retaining ties. This is the paper’s fractional-vote rule applied to a distribution of judges: when \(\mu_S=n_S/N\), each score is the finite contest’s amount of votes divided by \(N\), so the ranking is unchanged.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large blind consumer tasting or membership poll with many evaluators but repeated approval ballots over a modest list of wines. The evaluators sharing a type have the same ballot for the ranking task; the number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots. The exact scores and ranking are computable by summing rational masses over the support, in time polynomial in the input size. This mirrors the paper’s scoring and ranking procedure without simplifying its ballot rule. It does not involve optimization: the decision is the resulting score vector and order.

There is, however, no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the paper asserting a computational result. The paper says the Shapley values are straightforward to compute and presents the procedure, but that claim is not a numbered anchor. Its Shapley characterization is attributed to Shapley (1953), and the paper points to Ginsburgh and Zang (2003) for the proof of the special computation. I cannot honestly supply the required named anchor or present this continuous ranking task as one.

So the positive case is that the population mirror is clear and faithful for the paper’s score computation. Its weakest point is also decisive for the requested anchor-based case: the paper offers no numbered complexity result whose hardness might dissolve under continuization. The mirror covers computation of the fractional approval scores and ranking, not the paper’s axiomatic discussion, comparisons with other voting rules, or tasting simulations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its main claims are an axiomatic characterization and empirical comparisons. The Shapley characterization is attributed to earlier work; the paper’s own score calculation is presented as a straightforward procedure, not as a theorem about an algorithmic problem.

The proposed approval-set model is faithful. In a large poll with repeated ballots, let \(\mu_S\) be the share of judges choosing approval set \(S\); then wine \(w\)’s score is
\[ \phi_\mu(w)=\sum_{S\ni w}\frac{\mu_S}{|S|}. \]
For a finite contest, this is exactly the judge-by-judge calculation rewritten using the empirical distribution of ballots. So multiplicity is plausible, and I would not object to the model itself. The problem is that this mirror restates the scoring procedure; it does not continue an algorithmic result the paper proves.

A more substantial proposal would let mass move between approval ballots at a cost, then ask for the cheapest change that makes a target wine win. That could be a worthwhile continuous bribery problem. But the paper specifies no ballot-change operation, cost model, target intervention, or robustness question. Choosing those would define a new problem around the paper’s rule, rather than mirror a result in the paper. The same is true of adding a stochastic model for the simulated approvals.

So the negative case is narrow, and it does not establish that continuous populations are implausible here. It is that the faithful mirror is only the rule’s direct score calculation, while richer computational questions require adding the model that the paper does not study. If “worthwhile mirror” includes analyzing that calculation as a high-multiplicity aggregation task, the case against is weak and the proposed mirror survives.
