The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result, and it makes no P, NP-hardness, FPT, or similar claim. So there are no qualifying anchors to quote or restate. The closest evidence is its computed election allocations, such as Table 6 for 2024; those are illustrative results, not complexity results. I can offer a plausible continuous mirror, but cannot honestly present it as anchored to a named result of this paper.

The most credible candidate is Continuous Voter-Mass Biproportional Apportionment. Let \(D\) be the three constituencies and \(P\) the parties. An instance gives constituency population shares \(q_d\), and a distribution \(\mu_{d,p}\) of valid party votes: a type is a voter who lives in constituency \(d\) and votes for party \(p\). The masses satisfy \(\sum_{d,p}\mu_{d,p}=1\); they can be binary rationals, with support size \(\tau\). For the paper’s 75-seat example, set each constituency’s seat total to
\[ h_d=1+A^{SL}_{36}(q)_d+A^{SL}_{36}(\sqrt q)_d, \]
where \(A^{SL}_k\) apportions \(k\) seats by Sainte-Laguë. For barrier \(\alpha\in\{0.01,0.03\}\), set each party’s adjusted mass to
\[ \bar v_p=\max\left\{0,\sum_d\mu_{d,p}-\alpha\right\}, \]
then allocate the 75 party seats \(s_p\) by D’Hondt on \(\bar v\). The requested solution is the integer seat matrix \(x_{d,p}\) returned by Sainte-Laguë biproportional apportionment of \(\mu\), with row sums \(h_d\) and column sums \(s_p\), using fixed tie-breaking. This is a rule-computation problem: \(x\) is the decision variable, but the paper supplies no objective to optimize, so I would not invent one.

The regime is plausible: a regional election can involve millions of participating voters but only three constituencies and a handful of party choices, so \(\tau\) is at most \(3|P|\). This type definition captures everything the proposed allocation uses. The paper itself works with a constituency-by-party vote table, updated population shares, global party vote totals, and biproportional seat margins. Its 2024 calculations report that the 1% barrier gives the leading party two additional seats and gives Elkarrekin representation. That makes the authors likely to recognize this as their allocation procedure applied to voter masses. The article’s “continuous barrier,” however, is a vote-reduction rule; it does not by itself mean the population has been continuized.

I would expect this candidate problem to be tractable, a Class A style question, since it is built from standard divisor apportionments and the biproportional procedure cited in the paper. That is a reasoned expectation, not a result proved here. It raises further questions about exact complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), handling zero vote cells and ties, and how allocation changes under small perturbations of voter mass.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s contribution is a reform proposal and worked allocation calculations, not a computational problem or complexity result. Its data are already aggregated by constituency and party, and replacing counts with normalized masses may change little beyond rounding conventions. So this is a plausible population mirror of the proposed rule, but the paper does not provide the named computational anchor the requested case requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This paper gives no computational result to mirror. It proposes a reform, calculates allocations for three elections, and argues that the reform improves representativeness and governability. It states no complexity bound or algorithmic result for a computational problem. Table 6 is a worked allocation, not such a result. By the criterion for this task, that is decisive: a continuous version of a computational result cannot be anchored here.

The strongest candidate is the proposed Continuous Voter-Mass Biproportional Apportionment problem. Its types—constituency and party vote—are a sensible high-multiplicity model: many voters can share each type, and the allocation uses no further individual details. But the paper’s computation is simply to apply its prescribed apportionment procedure to an aggregate vote table. Rewriting the entries as rational masses poses no complexity question the paper raises or answers. The article reports outcomes; it does not study the computational task of producing them.

A stronger follow-on might ask for the least mass of voters who must switch party choices to change a seat allocation or create a specified discordance. That is a coherent continuous-population question. But it requires adding a perturbation objective and rules for which changes are allowed; the paper models neither voter interventions nor robustness. Generalizing the number of constituencies and seats to expose a broader complexity question would move still further from its specific reform proposal. Either could be a worthwhile new project, but neither mirrors a computational result in this article.

So the negative case is strong under the requested standard: none of the proposed mirrors is anchored to a named computational result. It is weaker as a literal claim that no related continuous study could interest the authors. The mass-level perturbation question is plausible; the honest objection is that it extends the paper rather than continuizing one of its computational results.
