The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of strong implementability, anchored in Theorem 5. Proved in this paper, it says that deciding whether a given social choice function is strongly implementable is in PSPACE when the input explicitly gives the finite agent set, alternatives, each agent’s finite type space, rational valuation functions, a rational common prior over type profiles, and the function \(f\) on those profiles. The proof uses polynomial-size payment and elimination certificates, then checks strategy profiles using polynomial space. The paper notes that this input can already be exponential in the number of agents. It conjectures PSPACE-completeness, but proves no hardness result.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Mass Strong Implementability. The input has a finite set \(T\) of agent types and a finite outcome set \(X\). A type is a complete payoff and information class: agents of type \(t\) have the same preferences, costs, and private information. A society is a population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society of type \(t\). The target rule \(f:\Delta(T)\to X\) is given by a finite rational polyhedral partition of the simplex, with one outcome assigned to each cell. Each type’s rational valuation \(v_t(x,\mu)\) is affine in \(\mu\). A common prior has finite rational support on possible population distributions \(\mu^1,\ldots,\mu^r\); assume every type occurs with positive marginal probability.

A mechanism has a finite message set \(S\), an outcome rule \(g:\Delta(S)\to X\), and transfers \(P(s,\rho)\in\mathbb Q\) depending on an agent’s own message \(s\) and the aggregate reported distribution \(\rho\). For a finite input representation, require \(g\) to be constant and \(P\) affine on cells of finite rational polyhedral partitions. The question is whether some such mechanism strongly implements \(f\).

The equilibrium variable is \(q_{t,s}\), the fraction of type \(t\) agents sending message \(s\), with \(\sum_s q_{t,s}=1\). This allows a type’s mass to split across messages; it does not assume all agents of that type behave identically. At population state \(\mu\), the reported distribution is \(\rho_s(\mu,q)=\sum_t\mu_tq_{t,s}\). A type-\(t\) agent’s posterior over population states is \(\pi_t(j)\propto\pi_j\mu^j_t\). Since each agent has zero mass, an individual deviation does not change \(\rho\). Thus \(q\) is an equilibrium exactly when every message used by a positive fraction of type \(t\) maximizes that type’s expected utility, \(\sum_j\pi_t(j)[v_t(g(\rho(\mu^j,q)),\mu^j)+P(s,\rho(\mu^j,q))]\). The mechanism must have at least one equilibrium, and every equilibrium \(q\) must satisfy \(g(\rho(\mu,q))=f(\mu)\) for every \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). There is no cost-minimization objective in the paper’s problem: the designer’s decision is whether a mechanism exists with this all-equilibria guarantee.

The regime is a large platform, public agency, or service provider dealing with many small users but relatively few complete preference and information classes. For example, millions of customers may fall into a small number of valuation and information types, while the aggregate composition is uncertain. The mass \(\mu_t\) is their share of the population; the finite prior models uncertainty about that aggregate composition. The paper’s prior over finite type profiles does not itself make its society continuous. This mirror makes the population distribution the state on which the target social choice rule is defined.

I think authors working on computational mechanism design could recognize this as a serious continuous analogue: it retains their finite outcome set, private information, valuations, transfers, a specified social choice function, Bayesian incentives, and the requirement that *every* equilibrium produce the target outcome. The mirror covers Theorem 5’s decision problem, not weak implementation or the whole paper. I would not use Theorem 7 as a second anchor: its polynomial-time result is for a single selfish agent, and replacing that person with an atomless population would change the strategic problem. Theorem 5 is the better anchor, though it is an upper bound rather than hardness; the paper has no proved hardness result to prefer.

My tentative expectation is Class C, with low confidence. The finite problem’s strategy profiles form a discrete set; this mirror has real-valued mass splits \(q_{t,s}\), and strong implementation must rule out every equilibrium in that continuum. That could introduce continuum-specific real-algebraic complexity. The paper’s PSPACE proof does not establish this: it relies on finite-agent strategy profiles and selective-elimination certificates.

The main further questions are whether an augmented revelation principle and finite elimination certificates survive for mass-splitting equilibria; how complexity depends on \(|T|\), the rule’s partition size, and the input bit length; and whether the finite-agent problem embeds into this one or instead admits additive approximations for large discrete populations.

The soft spot is substantial: in an atomless society, a single agent cannot change the aggregate report, whereas pivotal individual deviations are central to the paper’s finite-agent setting. The finite-piece mechanism representation is also an added modeling choice needed to state a finite-input problem. A referee might reasonably call this a new continuum mechanism-design problem rather than a direct replacement of the paper’s model. The case for the mirror is that the changed strategic effect is a consequence of making the population continuous, while the target of the computation—strong implementation of a given social choice rule—remains intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a real computational result, so I would not argue that the paper has nothing to mirror. The problem is that its PSPACE theorem concerns an explicitly represented, agent-indexed game. The input gives valuations \(V_i\), a prior over the product space \(\Theta\), and an arbitrary social choice function on labeled type profiles. Agents with identical preferences can still occupy different roles in those tables. To compress them into a mass \(\mu_t\), one must restrict the problem to instances where those distinctions disappear—typically anonymous valuations and a target rule depending only on type frequencies. That is a plausible problem, but it is a restricted, newly specified version of Theorem 5, not a high-multiplicity encoding of the general theorem.

The proposed model also puts its continuous strategic question in two different places. Incentives are evaluated only at the finitely many population states in the prior’s support, while implementation is required at every \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). Off-support, the equilibrium condition imposes no incentive constraints; it merely asks the outcome rule to agree with \(f\) along the reports induced by an equilibrium strategy. One can repair this by giving the prior full support and a finite representation such as a rational density. But then the input and the computational problem depend on a new choice of how that continuous prior is encoded.

There is a more basic change in the strategic primitive. In the paper, an individual deviation can change the outcome and payments. The selective-elimination argument uses that fact: a flag is a unilateral deviation that can dislodge a bad equilibrium while preserving truthful reporting as an equilibrium. In an atomless population, one person’s deviation does not change the aggregate report. The proposed \(q_{t,s}\) instead describes fractions of a type choosing each message. Ruling out every bad \(q\) is a coherent aggregate-equilibrium question, but it is not the limit of the paper’s unilateral-deviation argument. Restoring influence requires positive-mass or coalition deviations, which changes the equilibrium concept.

This change also leaves the proposed decision problem without a natural finite witness bound. The mechanism may have any finite number of messages and any finite partition complexity, while the condition ranges over a continuum of mass splits. The paper’s PSPACE proof obtains finite certificates by enumerating finite strategy profiles and eliminating bad equilibria one at a time. That argument supplies no bound for the proposed model. Imposing one might make a well-formed computational problem, but the choice of bound would become part of the new problem rather than a consequence of the paper.

The strongest charitable repair is a symmetric nonatomic mechanism-design problem for a large platform or public agency, with a prior over population compositions and explicit bounds on the representation of mechanisms. That could be worthwhile research. But its complexity would depend on those modeling and encoding choices, and its connection to Theorem 5 would be conceptual rather than a continuous version of the theorem’s computational question. So the negative case is strongest against calling the proposed model a mirror of this paper. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile scenario exists: a symmetric, aggregate-based implementation problem is plausible, and the paper’s subject is close enough that its authors might recognize it as their problem. The universal “no mirror” claim is therefore weak.
