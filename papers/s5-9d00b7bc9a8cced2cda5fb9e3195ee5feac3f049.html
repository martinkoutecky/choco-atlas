The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named complexity-theoretic result here: no theorem says a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The strongest computationally relevant anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that for submodular welfare functions \(G,H\), Algorithm 1 is well-defined and, up to taking a subsequence, its proposal masses and associated waiting-time vectors converge. Every such limit yields a generalized equilibrium matching. The algorithm repeatedly chooses welfare-maximizing masses subject to availability constraints, then updates availability by subtracting rejected mass. The theorem gives convergence and existence, but no iteration bound or bit-complexity result.

The strongest follow-on question I can make from that anchor is Rational Polyhedral Aggregated Deferred-Acceptance Equilibrium. An instance consists of finite worker and firm type sets \(X,Y\); rational utility matrices \(\alpha,\gamma\); and explicit rational piecewise-linear descriptions of \(G,H\), promised to be submodular welfare functions with the paper’s compactness and domain conditions. A type groups agents identical in all respects represented by the model, including their relevant preferences or utility distributions. Mass \(\mu_{xy}\) is the amount of type-\(x\) workers matched with type-\(y\) firms. The task is to output an exact equilibrium \((\mu,U,V)\) satisfying, coordinatewise,
\[ \max\{U_{xy}-\alpha_{xy},\,V_{xy}-\gamma_{xy}\}=0, \qquad \mu\in\partial G(U)\cap\partial H(V). \]
There is no single objective being minimized: the solution is an equilibrium. The algorithm’s proposal and disposal phases do have welfare-maximization objectives.

A plausible regime is a large hiring market with many applicants and positions but comparatively few applicant and employer categories, where members of each category share the preferences and other attributes the model uses. A market with millions of participants and tens or hundreds of categories is the intended kind of high-multiplicity setting. The paper itself gives passengers and taxis as an example of clustering agents into observable categories.

For that explicit piecewise-linear version, I would tentatively expect tractability: each proposal or disposal optimization can be written as a linear program. But Theorem 5 does not bound the number of rounds, so this is a research conjecture, not a consequence of the theorem. Natural follow-up questions include whether exact polynomial-time computation is possible, whether the algorithm admits an iteration bound, and how its equilibrium relates to finite markets with the same type proportions.

This case covers only Theorem 5’s aggregated deferred-acceptance result. The paper’s other numbered theorems chiefly establish comparative-statics and convex-analytic properties, not computational complexity results.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already models populations through masses over types and gives an aggregate deferred-acceptance algorithm. So this is a credible computational-complexity follow-up to the paper, but it is not a fresh continuization of one of its discrete computational results. The continuous-population model is already there; what remains open is its computational classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is Theorem 5, but its central object is already continuous. The paper takes worker and firm populations with type masses \(n_x,m_y\), tracks matches as masses \(\mu_{xy}\), and runs Algorithm 1 by proposing, rejecting, and updating quantities. Theorem 5 proves convergence of that aggregate algorithm and existence of a generalized equilibrium. The paper even describes it as a continuous extension of deferred acceptance and applies it to random-utility matching.

That makes “Rational Polyhedral Aggregated Deferred-Acceptance Equilibrium” a possible complexity question about the paper’s existing model, but not a continuous mirror of a discrete result. Choosing rational piecewise-linear inputs supplies an encoding for a new algorithmic classification; it does not create the continuous population. The theorem itself is stated for general submodular welfare functions and gives no iteration or bit-complexity bound. So the proposed question may be worthwhile on its own, but it does not supply the missing continuization the programme is looking for.

A stronger version might start from finite markets whose type counts grow together, then ask whether the finite deferred-acceptance outcomes converge to the paper’s mass equilibrium, with exact or approximate computation guarantees. That could be a valuable discrete-to-continuous bridge. But the paper already treats small and large markets, gives an aggregate algorithm, and identifies the one-person-per-category case with standard deferred acceptance. The proposed bridge would extend the paper’s existing continuous/discrete connection; it would not establish that the continuous model is a new mirror.

The other numbered results do not yield a better mirror. Theorems 1–4 characterize exchangeability and monotone comparative statics for convex functions and sets already defined over continuous quantities. One could ask how to compute those comparative statics under a particular finite encoding, but that would again be an algorithmic question about an already continuous theory. None of these theorems classifies the complexity of a discrete computational social-choice problem that could then be continuized.

The negative case is weakest if “mirror” is expanded to mean any complexity study of an existing continuous model. Under that broader remit, exact equilibrium computation for rational polyhedral welfare functions could be a sensible project, and Theorem 5 leaves its complexity open. But on the stated remit—finding a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper—the anchor does not survive: the paper has already made the population aggregate and continuous.
