The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that the paper’s *subject matter* has a natural population interpretation. In a large membership vote or referendum, many people could share the same payoff profile and induced ranking. A type \(t\) would record that complete payoff and preference information; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the electorate with that type. For voting method \(M\) and frame \(f\), let \(y_{t,b}\) be the mass of type \(t\) casting legal ballot \(b\). Summing those masses gives the approval or plurality totals and hence the winner. This could express the paper’s observed shifts in support and approvals as changes in population shares.

But this is only a representational fit, not a case anchored in the paper’s named computational results. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Tables 3 and 4 report probit regression estimates of experimental voting behavior; they do not classify an underlying problem as tractable or hard. The paper’s empirical findings are relevant context, but they cannot serve as the required anchors.

So I cannot give one continuous problem per anchor, or responsibly predict whether such a problem is tractable, inherits discrete hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard. If type-specific ballot behavior is supplied as input, aggregating ballot masses to find the winner is straightforward. Making the behavioral response itself the computational question would require adding a model the paper does not provide.

The regime is plausible in a larger election with many voters and relatively few shared payoff types, even though the paper’s experiment used six-person groups with three equally represented types. That supports the idea of a population-scale *reanalysis*. It does not supply the missing computational result. The weakest point is therefore decisive: this paper has no named computational anchor for a continuous mirror of the kind the programme asks us to assess.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper studies behavioral effects, not a computational problem. Its claims concern how framing changes individual choices and approval rates, and whether those changes appear in election outcomes. Tables 3 and 4 report statistical estimates; the paper defines no algorithmic task, optimization objective, or complexity result for a mirror to classify.

The natural population reading is coherent: in a large membership vote, people with the same payoff profile could form types, and \(x^f_{t,b}\) could represent the mass of type \(t\) casting ballot \(b\) under frame \(f\). Then approval totals are \(S^f_c=\sum_{t,b}x^f_{t,b}\mathbf{1}[c\in b]\), with plurality obtained by restricting \(b\) to single-candidate ballots. This makes the paper’s behavioral shifts expressible in population shares. But once the ballot masses are given, finding the winner is just aggregation. In the paper’s four-alternative, three-type setting, it is especially small. The difficult behavioral question—how the frame produces those masses—is what the paper estimates experimentally; it supplies no computational model for deriving them.

A stronger candidate would ask how sensitive the winner is to uncertainty in type-specific responses, or how much response mass must shift to change the winner. That could be a sensible new robustness problem. But it needs a specified uncertainty set, perturbation measure, or intervention cost, none of which the paper defines. Adding one would create the missing computational problem rather than continuize a problem the paper studies. With observed response masses alone, comparing the two frames’ winners remains a tally; with response uncertainty, the substantive model comes from elsewhere.

The proponent is right that a large electorate with repeated payoff types is plausible, and that population shares can summarize ballot behavior. That is the negative case’s weak point: it cannot rule out a future computational study that joins a behavioral response model to optimization. It does support a narrower conclusion: this paper offers a plausible continuous-population reanalysis, but no evident computational mirror for the programme to classify.
