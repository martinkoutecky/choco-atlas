The continuous mirror question

Given a rooted directed graph on role types, positive rational masses μ_t summing to one, strict rankings over incoming edges, and edge costs in {0,1,∞}, find a minimum-cost popular arborescence or report that none exists. Each role chooses one incoming edge for its cohort; popularity is determined by the mass-weighted pairwise preference margin, and total cost is the mass-weighted sum of the chosen edges' costs.

The model it lives in

The vertices are role types with masses μ_t; each type includes its incoming-link options, ranking, and per-member costs. A decision is an arborescence over those roles, so all members of a role make the same link choice. Minimize ∑_t μ_tγ(A(t)) subject to ∑_t μ_t(1[A(t)≻_t A′(t)] − 1[A′(t)≻_t A(t)]) ≥ 0 for every competing arborescence A′; infinite-cost choices are excluded.

The objection that survived

The role model does not capture independent member-level delegations, whose aggregate choices alone may not determine whether the expanded network is acyclic; the proponent already limits the question to blocs that choose together.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the minimum-cost hardness result in Theorem 1.4. It leaves the polynomial-time existence algorithm, the common-base generalization, the popular colorful forest polytope result, and the other results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-hardness persist for arbitrary binary-rational masses, or does it rely only on the equal-mass subcase?
  • Can a meaningful mass-splitting delegation model preserve network feasibility when members of one role choose different links?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a Mass-Weighted Minimum-Cost Popular Role-Arborescence problem. Consider a federation with many members grouped into delegation roles. Each role is a cohort whose members share the same incoming-link options, strict ranking over those links, and per-member link costs. The directed graph is over roles: choosing an arborescence chooses one incoming delegation link for each role, and every member of that cohort is represented by the role’s choice. This is plausible when branches or regional units delegate as a bloc. The number \(N\) of members can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of roles, while the graph and preferences remain the operative instance.

Precisely, an instance has a rooted directed graph \(G=(T\cup\{r\},E)\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) over each role’s incoming edges, and costs \(\gamma:E\to\{0,1,\infty\}\). For a rooted arborescence \(A\), write \(A(t)\) for the edge entering \(t\). Define its mass-weighted popularity margin against another arborescence \(A'\) by
\[ \Delta_\mu(A,A')=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[A(t)\succ_t A'(t)]-\mathbf{1}[A'(t)\succ_t A(t)]\bigr). \]
Call \(A\) popular if \(\Delta_\mu(A,A')\ge 0\) for every \(A'\). Its per-capita cost is \(C_\mu(A)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\gamma(A(t))\); an edge of infinite cost rules out that choice. The task is to return a minimum-cost popular arborescence, or report that none exists. The input size is measured in \(|T|\), \(|E|\), and the bit length \(L\) of the masses and other numeric data.

My anchor is Theorem 1.4, proved in this paper. It states that given a rooted directed graph in which each vertex has a strict ranking over incoming edges and an edge-cost function \(\mathrm{cost}:E\to\{0,1,\infty\}\), it is NP-hard to compute a minimum-cost popular arborescence. The paper proves this by reduction from Vertex Cover. The proposed mirror asks the same underlying optimization question for role cohorts whose votes and per-member costs contribute according to their population masses.

The authors have good reason to recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime. Popularity is already defined by comparing how many agents prefer each of two structures, so replacing headcounts with cohort masses is a direct population continuization. The paper also notes existing work on vertex-weighted popular branchings [27], which supports the legitimacy of weighted popularity as a model choice. A concrete regime is a large federation with many members in each of \(\tau\) local units, each unit sharing a delegation policy and selecting one link in a cycle-free hierarchy.

I expect hardness transfers for this mirror. Set \(\mu_t=1/\tau\) for every role. Then every popularity margin is the paper’s unweighted margin divided by \(\tau\), so its sign is unchanged; the per-capita cost is the original total edge cost divided by \(\tau\), so its minimizers are unchanged as well. Theorem 1.4 therefore embeds directly. This says the reduction’s combinatorics live in the role graph, not in the number of members occupying each role. Further questions include whether arbitrary binary-rational masses change the complexity, and whether allowing members within one role to choose different links yields a tractable mass-splitting version.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the minimum-cost popular arborescence hardness result, not the paper’s polynomial-time existence algorithm, its common-base generalization, or its colorful-forest polytope result. The weak point is that the mirror keeps one chosen link per role; the population mass changes the aggregate comparison and cost, but does not split a role’s action among its members. That is a credible regime for organizations that delegate as units, and it preserves the paper’s structural question. A model where each individual independently delegates and the resulting continuum of links must remain a rooted arborescence would be a stronger, separate mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.4 is a genuine computational anchor, and the proposed equal-mass embedding is mathematically sound: it preserves both popularity comparisons and minimum-cost choices up to a common scaling. So the objection cannot be that the paper has no result to mirror, or that the mirror’s answer is already known.

The difficulty is that each vertex here is both a voter and a structural position in the arborescence. Its options are incoming edges whose tails identify other vertices, and the choices must fit together without cycles. Agents with the same ranking are therefore not necessarily the same type: their locations and available edges matter too. The role model handles this by giving each cohort one vertex and letting all its members make that vertex’s choice together. That is a coherent model of blocs, but it changes the object from an arborescence over individual agents to an arborescence over organizational roles.

Allowing members to choose separately does not fix the mismatch using just masses over roles. For example, aggregate choices \(A\to B\) and \(B\to A\) could arise from individual edges \(A_1\to B_1\), \(B_1\to A_2\), and \(A_2\to r\), which form no cycle. The same aggregate pattern could arise from an actual cycle. Thus role-level edge masses alone do not determine whether the expanded graph is feasible. One must either impose bloc choices, add identity-level structure that grows with the population, or define a new fractional network model. The first returns to the proposed role problem; the others are not yet a clear high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 1.4.

That is a substantial objection to treating the role model as the paper’s ordinary high-multiplicity version. It does not, however, defeat the proponent’s scenario. A federation in which each region really does choose a delegation link as a bloc is plausible, and the paper’s cited work on vertex-weighted popular branchings supports the weighted-preference model. That work is not a continuous computational collision, and the equal-mass hardness embedding remains valid. My negative case is therefore weak at its most important point: it challenges whether the mirror represents individual-level population growth, but it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous question for cohesive blocs.
