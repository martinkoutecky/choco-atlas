The continuous mirror question

Given finite-domain variables \(V\), a finite outcome space \(X\), and \(\tau\) preference types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each with rational factorized cost \(f_t(x)=\sum_j \phi_{tj}(x|_{S_{tj}})\), compute the set of outcomes minimizing \(S_\mu(x)=\sum_t \mu_t f_t(x)\).

The model it lives in

A constituency is a distribution over complete preference-cost types. The decision is a complete outcome \(x\); its objective is the mass-weighted total cost \(S_\mu(x)\). For partial assignments \(a\), SC* evaluates the weighted costs of each type's best completion, \(\sum_t\mu_t f_t(\operatorname{BPA}_t(a))\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 2 transfers by scaling type scores and clearing the rational mass denominators, so it yields no population-specific runtime or structural complexity guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's winner-correctness guarantee for sum-based cardinal aggregation over weighted types. It leaves the complexity and runtime of SC*, the Condorcet-rule results, and the experiments outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of minimizing \(S_\mu\) as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • Which restrictions on the factor scopes, such as bounded treewidth, yield fixed-parameter or polynomial-time algorithms?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s cardinal-preference problem: choose a combinatorial outcome that minimizes the population’s weighted penalty. It preserves the paper’s central difficulty—the outcome is a complete assignment over many interdependent variables—while making the population a distribution over preference types.

A type \(t\) is a complete cardinal preference model: for example, a soft-constraint cost function \(f_t\) over outcomes. Let \(V=\{X_1,\ldots,X_m\}\) be finite-domain variables and \(X=\prod_i D_{X_i}\) the finite outcome space. An instance of Continuous Type-Weighted Cardinal Winner gives \(\tau\) types, positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and compactly represented costs. A concrete version uses factorized soft-constraint costs,
\[ f_t(x)=\sum_j \phi_{tj}(x|_{S_{tj}}). \]
The question is to return every outcome minimizing
\[ S_\mu(x)=\sum_t \mu_t f_t(x). \]
The solution is an ordinary discrete outcome; only the population is continuous.

A plausible regime is a large constituency choosing a bundle of interdependent policies, where millions of people use a moderate number of standardized preference models. The mass of a type is its share of the constituency. This is a credible high-multiplicity instance when people assigned to a type really do share the same costs and preferences. The type count is then the number of distinct models, not the number of people.

My anchor is Theorem 2, asserted as a result of this paper; its proof is omitted in the proceedings version, which points to a longer version with full proofs. It says: if the cardinal evaluation function \(F_{\text{Cardinal}}\) is admissible, SC* returns the set of winners under the chosen aggregation rule \(\circ\). The relevant premise is supplied by Theorem 1: when \(\circ\) is non-decreasing in each argument and commutative, \(F_{\text{Cardinal}}\) is admissible. These are exactness results, not claims that the problem is in P.

For the continuous problem, use
\[ F_\mu(a)=\sum_t\mu_t f_t(\operatorname{BPA}_t(a)), \]
where \(\operatorname{BPA}_t(a)\) is type \(t\)’s minimum-cost completion of partial assignment \(a\). Positive weights preserve the lower-bound property. And if the masses are rational, clearing denominators gives integer multiplicities \(q_t\); repeating each type \(q_t\) times multiplies \(S_\mu\) by a common positive constant and leaves its minimizers unchanged. So the weighted-type version is a direct high-multiplicity form of the problem Theorem 2 computes.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers, rather than a tractability result. The paper’s soft-constraint language can express a 3-CNF formula’s number of violated clauses as the cost of a single type. With \(\tau=1\), a minimum-cost outcome satisfies every clause exactly when the formula is satisfiable. That places the hard combinatorics in the number of outcome variables, not in population size. This hardness observation is a reduction from the model, not a hardness theorem proved by the paper. The continuous question still makes sense even if the answer is negative: it identifies a boundary where continuizing the population does not remove the outcome-space difficulty.

This case covers Theorem 2’s cardinal aggregation result for the weighted-sum rule. It does not extend the paper’s Copeland or Minimax sections, whose named results concern non-admissibility of their evaluation functions, or its max-aggregation experiments.

The weakest point is that the anchor establishes correctness, not an efficient running time in \(m,\tau,L\). The rational-mass dictionary also makes the mirror close to repeated finite profiles. Thus the paper supports the legitimacy and exact formulation of this continuous problem, but gives no bound showing that SC* or another algorithm avoids exponential search. Further questions include whether particular factor-graph structures yield algorithms parameterized by treewidth, and how the continuous runtime compares with high-multiplicity discrete input when \(\tau\) is small.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about the result the proponent anchors on. Theorem 2 proves that SC* returns the cardinal-rule winners when its evaluation function is admissible; Theorem 1 supplies that condition for non-decreasing, commutative aggregators. These are computational correctness results, but they give no bound on runtime, search nodes, or preference-evaluation calls. SC* may still visit the whole outcome space, and the experiments do not establish a worst-case guarantee. So the paper offers little basis for a continuous *complexity* result.

The proposed population model is nevertheless coherent. A constituency whose members share a moderate number of complete, factorized policy-cost models is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. For masses \(\mu_t\), its winner minimizes
\[ S_\mu(x)=\sum_t \mu_t f_t(x). \]
The objective does not need individual identities, and the high-multiplicity literature supports treating this as a sensible regime.

The difficulty for the proponent is that Theorem 2’s exactness guarantee transfers by direct substitution. Regard each type as one agent with score \(g_t(x)=\mu_t f_t(x)\). Since \(\mu_t>0\), scaling does not change that type’s best completion, and SC*’s cardinal evaluation becomes \(\sum_t\mu_t f_t(\operatorname{BPA}_t(a))\), exactly the proposed evaluator. For rational masses, clearing denominators gives an equivalent replicated finite profile whose scores and evaluator are scaled by a common positive constant. The theorem therefore yields continuous exactness without a new structural result. It says nothing about whether this computation is efficient.

A follow-up could study the complexity of minimizing the aggregate compact cost over exponentially many outcomes. That is a well-posed question, but its computational substance would come from the outcome representation and its interactions; this paper provides no complexity result for it. My case against is therefore that this paper is a weak anchor for ChoCo, not that the mirror is ill-posed. I cannot honestly defeat the best cardinal mirror as a worthwhile research question: the population model survives, and the paper’s exactness result applies to it. The negative case is weak precisely at that point.
