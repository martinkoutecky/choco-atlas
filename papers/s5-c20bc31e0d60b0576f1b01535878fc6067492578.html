The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete agent types \(T\), rational population masses \(\mu_t\), time endowments \(w_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_a\) of activity slots, and an explicit list of feasible team templates \(j\) for each activity class \(a\), find project masses \(x_{aj}\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{a,j}x_{aj}\), subject to \(\sum_{a,j}d_{ajt}x_{aj}\leq \mu_t w_t\) for every type \(t\) and \(\sum_j x_{aj}\leq\beta_a\) for every activity class \(a\).

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete agent types, each with its time endowment and relevant technology and payoff attributes. Activity slots have per-capita supplies \(\beta_a\); feasible team templates specify type-level time use \(d_{ajt}\). The decision variables \(x_{aj}\) are project masses per person, and the objective is maximum total project density under type-specific time and activity-slot capacities.

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly applying the anchor rule, this paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or similar. The closest anchor is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. It says that, under Assumption \(v0\), in the myopic team-wise dynamics with either the uniform destructive or uniform perturbation scheme, the stochastically stable states are exactly the feasible states with the maximum number of completed projects: \(SS=L\). That is a structural equilibrium-selection result, not a runtime claim.

The strongest positive mirror I see is a continuous version of the maximum-project question that Proposition 3 reduces stability to. Consider a large research workforce in which people share a moderate number of complete types: for example, combinations of research role, time endowment, project eligibility, and any relevant payoff parameters. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of researchers of type \(t\). Assume project opportunities come in recurring activity classes \(a\), with \(\beta_a\) opportunities per person. For each class, list the feasible team templates \(j\), including their required type composition and type-specific person-time use \(d_{ajt}\). A template is part of the technology, just as feasible teams are in the paper.

I would call the resulting question Maximum Stable Project Density. Given rational \(\mu_t\), \(w_t\), \(\beta_a\), and an explicit list of templates, find an allocation \(x_{aj}\geq 0\) of project mass per person that maximizes total project mass, subject to

\[ \sum_{a,j} d_{ajt}x_{aj}\leq \mu_t w_t \quad\text{for each type }t, \qquad \sum_j x_{aj}\leq\beta_a \quad\text{for each activity class }a. \]

A solution is an optimal allocation \(x\), together with its maximum value \(\sum_{a,j}x_{aj}\). Here \(\mu_t\) is population mass, and \(x_{aj}\) is project density. Each type has a strictly positive marginal utility from participating in any feasible project involving it, preserving the behavioral premise of Assumption \(v0\).

This is a credible fluid counterpart of Proposition 3’s selection criterion. In a population scaled by \(q\), with \(q\mu_t\) agents and \(q\beta_a\) opportunities, Proposition 3 selects states with the largest project count. Normalizing by \(q\) gives the project-density objective above; with explicit templates and pooled type capacities, the continuous optimization is a linear program. The finite-to-continuous limit and its implementation as team assignments are part of the proposed mirror, not results established in the paper. With templates listed explicitly, I expect the optimization to be tractable. If templates are implicit or exponentially numerous, the further question is whether the pricing problem is efficient.

The regime is many researchers per type, with \(n\) large relative to \(\tau\), and project opportunities scaling with the workforce. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their resource-constrained formation of overlapping teams: it preserves types of teams, scarce time, activity technology, and the paper’s main stochastic-selection criterion. The scope is Proposition 3’s maximum-project result, not the paper’s other stability results.

The weak point is substantial: the paper treats activity labels as distinct projects, each performed at most once. Replacing them with recurring activity classes whose supply scales with population is a co-modification of the model. It fits the programme’s allowance for per-capita supplies, but an author could reasonably ask whether the resulting project-density optimization still captures the stochastic dynamics they study. And under the strict anchor requirement, this remains a proposed mirror around a named structural result; the paper supplies no named computational-complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is the anchor. Proposition 3 is a characterization of stochastically stable states, not a computational result: it says \(SS=L\), but the paper neither formulates a complexity problem nor studies an algorithm, running time, or approximation guarantee. “Find a maximum stable project density” is a possible new problem inspired by the proposition; it is not the continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

There is also a real gap between that proposed problem and the model Proposition 3 analyzes. In the paper, each activity can be performed only once, so the number of projects is bounded by \(|A|\), regardless of population size. With the paper’s activity set held fixed, maximum project density tends to zero as the population grows. The proposed fix—replicating activity opportunities in proportion to population—is a reasonable co-modification, but it changes the supply of projects and the mutation process that drives the proposition. The paper’s resistance calculation counts individual projects that must be destroyed; it does not specify a continuum of project opportunities or a mutation law over them.

One can define that extension cleanly: with a fixed list of team templates and per-capita supplies of project slots, maximizing project density is a fractional packing LP. That makes the proposal coherent, and a scaled finite model may approximate it. But it also shows the limit of the case for this particular paper: the computational question comes from adding a new optimization problem and new project-supply assumptions, not from a computational question the paper posed. If the templates are explicit, the resulting LP is standard; if they are implicit, the difficulty lies in whatever arbitrary technology is used to generate them, and the paper gives no structure that makes pricing tractable.

I would not claim the population regime is implausible. Large research workforces with repeated project opportunities and a modest number of complete agent types are conceivable, and the authors might recognize such an application. That leaves this negative case weak against the broad claim that no worthwhile continuous model could be built. It is strong, though, against treating this paper as a good ChoCo complexity mirror: its named results concern equilibrium selection and stability, and the proposed LP’s computational content is supplied by the extension rather than by the paper.
