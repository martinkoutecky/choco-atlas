The continuous mirror question

Given finite supported resident and hospital types with binary-rational masses, a resident-to-hospital population ratio, strict rankings over counterpart types, and integral per-unit quotas, consider atomless populations of those types. A feasible assignment must assign each resident unit at most once, assign every resident unit with lower quota one, and give each hospital unit an integral bundle meeting its quotas; type-level bundle frequencies may be fractional. Residents vote by comparing assigned hospital types, and each hospital unit compares its padded bundles using the paper's slot-correspondence rule. Does every feasible instance admit a popular assignment, and can one be computed in time polynomial in the supported types and input bit length?

The model it lives in

Use atomless resident and hospital populations represented by finite rational type masses, with hospital supply measured relative to resident mass. A hospital type's assignment is a distribution over integral bundles of resident types; a finite rational description must also realize those bundle frequencies as measurable assignments. Popularity is the paper's aggregate majority comparison, with resident votes integrated over resident mass and hospital slot votes over hospital mass.

The objection that survived

The paper ranks named agents strictly, while the mirror makes agents within a type interchangeable and therefore indifferent; this can change popularity margins, and the proponent acknowledges but does not resolve that fidelity concern.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every feasible type-mass instance admit a popular measurable assignment under the aggregate vote rule?
  • Can a popular assignment and its integral bundle-pattern distribution be computed in time polynomial in the number of supported types and the encoding length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is an atomless, type-compressed version of the paper’s two-sided lower-quota matching problem. Both sides of the market become populations: residents and hospital-program units. The point of continuity is that a large matching market can be described by the fractions of each kind of resident and program, rather than by a list of named individuals.

My anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that for every HR2LQ instance that has at least one feasible matching, a matching exists that is popular among all feasible matchings, and such a popular matching can be computed in polynomial time. Here “popular” has the paper’s two-sided voting meaning: there is no feasible rival matching that wins by a positive vote margin under any allowed correspondence for comparing the residents assigned to a hospital. This is an algorithmic result, not a cited theorem. The paper does not give a comparable named hardness result, so this case rests on one anchor.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Atomless HR2LQ Popular Assignment. An instance gives finite supported sets of resident types and hospital types, each with a binary-rational mass, summing to one across both sides. A type specifies its acceptable counterpart types, its strict ranking of those types, and its quotas. Each resident unit has upper quota one and lower quota zero or one; each hospital unit has its own integral lower and upper quotas. A type is complete for this model: all its members have the same rankings and quotas. The mass of a type is the fraction of the combined population belonging to it.

Represent each type by an atomless interval of that mass. A feasible matching is a measurable assignment between the two sides: almost every resident is assigned to at most one acceptable hospital, each hospital unit receives between its lower and upper quota of residents, and every resident unit whose lower quota is one is assigned. The hospitals remain individual units with integral local quotas; only their populations and assignment flows are described by mass.

To define popularity, compare any two feasible assignments \(M,N\). A resident unit votes for the assignment it prefers, comparing the types of its assigned hospitals and treating unmatched as \(\bot\). For each hospital unit, pad each assignment to its upper quota with vacant slots. Pair slots across \(M\) and \(N\), fixing residents assigned to that same hospital in both and allowing any correspondence for the remaining slots, as in the paper. The hospital’s vote is the sum of its slot votes, comparing resident types. Integrate resident votes over resident mass and hospital-slot votes over hospital mass. Call \(M\) popular if there is no feasible \(N\) and measurable choice of these correspondences for which the aggregate vote for \(N\) is positive. A solution is a finite, rational description of a measurable matching, such as masses of type-labeled assignment patterns and the measure-preserving pairings that realize them.

The computational question is: given such an instance, promised to have a feasible assignment, must a popular assignment exist, and can one be produced in time polynomial in the explicit type description and the bit lengths of the masses and quotas? The desired running time is in the number of supported types and encoding size, not in the possibly enormous number of people represented by the masses.

A plausible regime is a large, recurring residency match: many residents, including groups with mandatory placement, and many program units with minimum staffing requirements. Residents may share rankings over program types, while program units share rankings over resident types and the same quotas. One could have tens or hundreds of supported types representing a much larger population. That is a credible high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s own application, not a claim that every hospital-residents instance has this structure.

I would expect tractability, though that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 4. The paper reduces popularity to stability in an expanded instance and proves popularity using a weighted-matching LP and a dual certificate. Those are structural ingredients that might admit a type-level or measure-valued analogue. The theorem’s stated polynomial bound, however, is in the size of the individually listed finite instance. Its reduction creates copies in numbers tied to the counts of lower-quota agents. It does not establish a polynomial algorithm in the number of types and mass bit lengths. A prover would need to show that the reduction or its certificate can be compressed.

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s existence and computation result, not every construction or observation in the paper. The main soft spot is the precise meaning of popularity after individuals become atomless. In particular, allowing residents to compare hospital types and be indifferent among members of a type, while extending hospital correspondences measurably, is a genuine co-modification. It preserves the paper’s matching and voting question as far as I can see, but the authors might object that identity-level preferences or the exact correspondence rule are essential. If they accept the type-level interpretation, the continuous question is a sensible computational analogue; whether it is tractable in high-multiplicity input size remains open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real computational anchor: it proves both existence and polynomial-time computability of a popular feasible matching. So the case against cannot rest on the paper lacking a result to mirror.

The strongest objection is that the proposed type compression does not preserve the paper’s strict-preference model. Preferences are rankings of named individuals, and popularity depends on those rankings: a hospital’s vote can turn on which particular resident it prefers. If a type records everything needed to determine those votes, residents who occupy different positions in a hospital’s strict ranking are different types. If types record only preferences over categories, the mirror makes residents within a category tied. The proposed measurable correspondence between assignments cannot recover the omitted identity-level rankings; it only pairs the residents being compared.

There are two plausible repairs, but each changes the object. Keep the strict rankings by adding rank positions or identity coordinates to types, and the finite-support compression may disappear as the market grows. Collapse individuals into categories and treat members of a category as tied, and the result is a natural matching problem, but not the paper’s strict-preference popularity problem. The reduction and dual certificate in Theorem 4 establish no complexity result for that modified model. To preserve integral quotas as well, an aggregate formulation would need to track distributions over hospitals’ discrete bundle patterns, rather than just flows between type labels. That looks definable, but it adds another modeling step.

This is a meaningful objection to the proposed mirror: its population-level voting rule has not yet shown that it preserves the decisive preference information in the anchor. It is not, however, a convincing universal case against a mirror. A large market with repeated resident and program categories, category-level preferences, and integral bundles is plausible; the resulting type-level popularity question is computational and looks like a co-modification the authors could recognize. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile scenario exists. The negative case is weak at that final step: it challenges the fidelity of this formulation, but the category-based mirror remains standing.
