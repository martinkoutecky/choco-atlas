The continuous mirror question

Given task classes \(J\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), values \(u_j\), budget \(\beta\), and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over worker types \((A,c)\), compute an anonymous randomized allocation and payment rule from the type masses. For replications \(N\) with integral type counts \(N\mu\) and task supplies \(Nq_j\), require each outcome to respect eligibility, task capacity, and total budget \(N\beta\); achieve welfare at least \((e-1)/(2e-1)\) times the cost-aware optimum; and retain Theorem 3’s large-market truthfulness guarantees as \(\theta_N=u_{\max}/\mathrm{OPT}_N\to0\), where a unilateral report changes that worker’s type mass by \(1/N\).

The model it lives in

Workers have types \((A,c)\), where \(A\subseteq J\) is the eligible task set and \(c\) is the private unit cost; the society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over these types. Assignment variables \(x_{A,b,j}\) send reported type mass to eligible task classes, with per-capita capacities \(q_j\); type-level payments obey the per-capita budget \(\beta\). Individual incentives are evaluated through finite replications: one worker’s deviation moves \(1/N\) mass, and their assignment and payment follow from the corresponding type-level allocation.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror here is budget-feasible crowdsourcing matching with a continuous worker population. I would lead with Theorem 3: it already studies large markets and fractional matchings, so it gives the strongest bridge to a population described by masses.

Let \(J\) be a finite set of task classes. A class \(j\) contains interchangeable unit tasks, each worth \(u_j\) to the requester, with supply \(q_j\) per worker. A worker’s public eligibility set is \(A\subseteq J\); the worker can do at most one task and has a private minimum cost \(c\), the same for every eligible task. The population is a rational distribution \(\mu_{A,c}\) over these worker types, with total mass \(1\) and support size \(\tau\). The requester has budget \(\beta\) per worker. In a market of \(N\) workers, this represents \(N\mu_{A,c}\) workers, \(Nq_j\) tasks of class \(j\), and budget \(N\beta\).

The mechanism sees reported costs \(b\), giving a reported mass distribution \(\hat\mu_{A,b}\). It must return an assignment flow \(x_{A,b,j}\) and payments. Feasibility requires \(x_{A,b,j}=0\) for \(j\notin A\), \(\sum_j x_{A,b,j}\leq\hat\mu_{A,b}\), and \(\sum_{A,b}x_{A,b,j}\leq q_j\). Total payments per unit population must be at most \(\beta\). A worker with true cost \(c\) should prefer reporting \(c\): if \(z_A(b)\) is that worker’s assignment probability (or assigned proportion) and \(p_A(b)\) their expected payment, then truthfulness requires \(p_A(c)-c z_A(c)\geq p_A(b)-c z_A(b)\) for every alternative report \(b\). The welfare is \(W=\sum_{A,b,j}u_jx_{A,b,j}\); the benchmark \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\) maximizes this welfare subject to the same task capacities and true-cost constraint \(\sum_{A,c,j}c x_{A,c,j}\leq\beta\).

These are masses of workers assigned whole tasks, not fractional jobs handed to an individual. A fractional \(x\) says what proportion of a worker type is assigned to each task class. The model keeps the paper’s matching constraints, private costs, budget, and welfare objective; it changes the population representation and scales task supplies and budget with it.

Lead question — Large-Market Mass Matching-2.58. Given the task classes, supplies and values, budget, and a finite-support reported worker-mass distribution, construct a computable mass-allocation and payment rule with those feasibility constraints. For a sequence of finite replications whose task values satisfy \(\theta_N=u_{\max}/\mathrm{OPT}_N\to0\), require welfare at least \(\frac{e-1}{2e-1}\mathrm{OPT}\), individual rationality, budget feasibility, and the paper’s large-market truthfulness guarantees: the extra utility from a profitable misreport vanishes, and beneficial reports approach the true cost as \(\theta_N\to0\).

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved by the authors with its proof deferred to the longer version. It states that TM-RANDOMIZED is budget feasible, individually rational, truthful in large markets, and has approximation factor \((2e-1)/(e-1)\approx2.58\) against the offline optimum with access to true costs. I would expect the mass version to be tractable, likely Class A: assignment feasibility becomes a flow over worker and task types, while the paper’s randomized-order construction already produces a fractional matching. The open computational question is whether its guarantees can be obtained directly from the compact mass input in time polynomial in the number of task classes, \(\tau\), and the input bit length, without expanding the population. This also raises whether aggregation can improve the approximation factor or make exact truthfulness possible.

A second, useful question exposes the incentive tradeoff rather than blending it into the lead. Threshold-IC Mass Matching-3 asks for a deterministic mass-allocation and payment rule on the same instances, with budget feasibility and individual rationality, exact dominant-strategy truthfulness, and welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/3\). Its anchor is Theorem 2, an author result whose proof is also deferred to the longer version: TM-UNIFORM with the modified non-uniform threshold payment rule is budget feasible, individually rational, truthful, and 3-approximate against the offline optimum with true costs. I would also expect a Class A result, but the threshold-payment rule and its dependence on a fixed ordering of workers make the type-compressed algorithmic question less immediate. A further question is whether the continuous version can keep exact truthfulness while matching Theorem 3’s better ratio.

These are credible mirrors because the authors’ core problem is preserved: a requester buys work from cost-reporting workers, assigns eligible workers to heterogeneous tasks, obeys a budget, and is judged against the cost-aware optimum. The regime is a recurring translation or labeling market with many workers but relatively few combinations of skill bundle and minimum rate—for instance, workers grouped by the topic-language tasks they can do and by standard piece-rate bands. Here \(N\) can be large relative to \(\tau\), while the mix of eligible workers and task supply matters to the assignment. Workers still have private costs; the mirror does not replace the mechanism-design problem with an offline allocation LP.

The paper contains no named hardness result, so these are its strongest named computational anchors. The scope is its one-to-one crowdsourcing mechanism results, Theorems 2 and 3—not the experiments, the many-to-many extension, or the surrounding online-matching work.

The weakest point is that Theorem 3 already relies on a large-market condition and computes a fractional matching. That makes a continuous-population formulation feel close to the paper’s own analysis. But the paper’s matching is still over a finite set of workers; the fractional object is an allocation outcome, not a society represented by a distribution over worker types. The mirror’s question is whether the mechanism can be formulated and computed on that population distribution itself. Theorem 3 is therefore the sturdier anchor: averaging over worker orderings gives a natural route to mass allocation. The fixed-worker ordering used by Theorem 2 makes its compressed version more vulnerable to identity and tie-breaking details.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirrors put the population continuum in the wrong place for the results’ hardest guarantee: workers’ individual incentives. I would not argue that this market lacks plausible repeated types. Workers with the same eligibility set and cost can be interchangeable, and repeated translation or labeling cohorts make a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Nor is there a lack of named computational results: Theorems 2 and 3 are clear anchors.

The problem is that a distribution over reports does not, by itself, say what happens when one worker changes their report. In an atomless society, that deviation changes no mass. Yet Theorem 3’s guarantee is explicitly about what an individual gains by deviating in finite markets as \(\theta\to0\); it is not simply a property of a limiting allocation flow. A well-posed continuous version must therefore specify how an individual’s report affects their treatment—perhaps by defining a sequence of finite replications, or an add-one-worker perturbation. Without that extra semantics, “truthful in large markets” has been replaced by a mass-allocation problem that does not state the incentive claim it purports to mirror.

This is a real weakness in the proposed Theorem 3 mirror, but not a decisive objection to every better version. One could scale task supplies and budget with population size, compute a limiting allocation from the type masses, and require that finite replications converge to it while retaining the paper’s deviation bounds. That is a coherent question. The negative case is that the continuous optimization alone would not answer it: the hard part may remain the finite-agent incentive analysis. But a limit characterization that does answer it could still be useful, so this narrows the mirror rather than defeating it.

The same issue is sharper for Theorem 2. Its threshold payment is defined for a particular worker: how far that worker can raise their bid and remain selected. Under the fixed worker ordering, thresholds can differ even among workers with identical eligibility and cost. Aggregating them into one type mass loses those distinctions. A stronger mirror could replace the fixed order with an anonymous priority rule and seek a type-level allocation and payment schedule. That would avoid literal identity dependence, but it would be a redesigned mechanism whose exact truthfulness and budget feasibility need to be established; they do not follow just by replacing counts with masses. Still, that is a plausible research question, not a fundamental impossibility.

So the honest negative case is limited: the proposed formulations move quickly from fractional allocation to incentive guarantees, while those guarantees require a precise account of individual deviations. For Theorem 3, finite replication is already part of the claim; for Theorem 2, type-level threshold payments need justification. But the natural repairs are recognizable continuizations, and both could yield substantive results. I cannot make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists.
