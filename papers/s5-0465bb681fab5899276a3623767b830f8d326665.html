What the mirror covers

The proposed Mass-UES extension concerns the allocation rule in Theorem 2; it does not cover the characterizations in Theorems 1 and 3 or the Baiyangdian example.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational result in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes LRS and identifies it with the Shapley value of the stand-alone game and a core allocation; Theorem 2 does the same for UES and the upstream-oriented game; Theorem 3 does so for DES and the downstream-oriented game. These are axiomatic and game-theoretic results, not named claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. The Baiyangdian calculations are examples, not numbered complexity results. So I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem per qualifying anchor.

The strongest positive candidate is a continuous-population version of UES. Picture many small firms or villages in a river basin, with only a few recurring hydrological roles. A type records an agent’s position in a finite rooted type tree, its upstream/downstream relations, and its per-capita cleanup cost; agents sharing a type are identical in those respects. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participating agents of type \(t\), and \(c_t\) is their per-capita local cost. The regime has many agents but relatively few types. That scenario fits the paper’s stated examples of firms and villages, and its concluding remarks explicitly contemplate weights based on characteristics such as population.

Call the resulting problem Mass-UES Allocation. Given the rooted type tree, rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational costs \(c_t\), output the exact per-capita charge \(z_t\) for each type. Let \(D(t)\) contain \(t\) and its downstream types, and let \(A(j)\) contain \(j\) and its upstream types. A natural mass version of the paper’s UES rule is

\[ z_t=\sum_{j\in D(t)}\frac{\mu_j c_j}{\mu(A(j))}, \qquad \mu(A(j))=\sum_{i\in A(j)}\mu_i. \]

Thus each type’s aggregate local cost is divided equally across the mass of agents responsible for it. The output is the per-type charge vector; budget balance requires \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t=\sum_t\mu_tc_t\). There is no optimization objective here: the paper’s question is how to allocate costs fairly, not how to minimize an objective.

The nearest named result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: UES is the unique method satisfying Additivity, Independence of Upstream Costs, Upstream Symmetry, Independence of Irrelevant Costs, and Efficiency; it is also the Shapley value of the upstream-oriented game and lies in that game’s core. That gives this mirror a clear conceptual anchor, but it does not make Theorem 2 a computational-complexity result. The mass formula suggests a direct tractable algorithm, using \(O(\tau^2)\) rational operations for \(\tau\) types. Further questions would be whether a mass-weighted version of the axioms still characterizes the rule, and how its answers relate to finite instances with repeated types.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s agents are individual nodes in a finite tree, while Mass-UES treats recurring network roles as types and replaces equal counts with mass. That is a plausible population-weighted extension of the authors’ allocation rule, but the paper neither states nor proves it. Even if accepted as their continuous analogue, its computation appears straightforward; it does not reveal a complexity landscape or dissolve a discrete hardness result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no computational result to continue. Theorem 2 characterizes UES by axioms, identifies it with a Shapley value, and proves core membership. Theorems 1 and 3 make parallel claims for LRS and DES. None states the complexity of a computational problem; the paper does not pose an algorithmic task with a complexity claim to mirror.

Mass-UES is a plausible extension. Many firms or villages could share recurring river-network roles, with mass representing their population share. But asking for their per-type charges turns the paper’s allocation rule into a new input-output problem; it does not make Theorem 2 a computational result. Asking whether mass-weighted axioms characterize the rule would stay axiomatic, which is outside ChoCo’s target. Other computational questions—say, about a weighted compromise among allocation methods—could be worthwhile, but they would be new problems prompted by the paper, not continuous mirrors of its results.

I would not reject Mass-UES because its population regime is nonsensical. The paper’s examples and discussion of population-based weights give that scenario some footing. The negative case is narrower: no matter how convincing the population model is, changing the scenario cannot supply the missing computational anchor. As a claim that *no related computational question could be worthwhile*, this case is weak; as a claim that this paper offers no worthwhile continuous mirror of a named computational result, it is strong.
