The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational complexity result: it proves no NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity classification. The closest named anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It studies \(K^\star\), the congress size maximizing the probability that the \(k\) most competent voters make the correct decision by strict majority, when their independent correctness probabilities are \(p(i)=(n+1-i)/(n+1)\). It proves
\[ (3-2\sqrt{2})n-O(1)\le K^\star\le \tfrac12 n+O(1). \]
So the result establishes that even when competence levels follow the expected order statistics of a uniform distribution, an optimal congress has size proportional to the population. This is a mathematical result about an optimization problem, not a complexity classification; it is the paper’s strongest anchor for a continuous mirror.

A natural mirror is Rate-Optimal High-Multiplicity Congress. Its input is a finite set of competence types \(t\), each with rational competence \(p_t\in(0,1)\) and rational population mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For a chosen fraction \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), select the mass \(\nu^\alpha\) consisting of the most competent types first, with \(\sum_t\nu^\alpha_t=\alpha\) and \(0\le\nu^\alpha_t\le\mu_t\); a tie at the cutoff may be split. Every selected voter still casts a binary vote, independently correct with probability \(p_t\).

The objective is the exponential rate at which the selected congress makes a wrong decision as the population scale \(N\) grows. Define
\[ J_\mu(\alpha)=\sup_{\lambda\le0}\left\{\frac{\lambda\alpha}{2} -\sum_t\nu^\alpha_t\log(1-p_t+p_te^\lambda)\right\}. \]
This is the large-deviation rate for the probability that at most half the selected voters are correct. The problem is: given \((p_t,\mu_t)\) and \(\epsilon>0\), return a fraction \(\alpha\) whose \(J_\mu(\alpha)\) is within \(\epsilon\) of the maximum over \([0,1]\), together with the selected mass \(\nu^\alpha\). Mass is the fraction of the population in each competence type; the optimization chooses how much of the highest-competence population to include. The continuization is of the population, while votes and the correctness event remain discrete.

A plausible regime is a very large pool of independently responding classifiers grouped into a modest number \(\tau\) of validated accuracy tiers, with many classifiers per tier. The paper itself points to classifier ensembles, and its model already assumes that competence determines each member’s probability of a correct vote. A fixed tier is therefore a complete type for this version of the problem; \(N\gg\tau\). The uniform competence profile behind Theorem 1 is the limiting profile this mirror approximates: its expected order-statistic competences are a discrete quantile grid for \(\mu=U[0,1]\). Repeated tiers are a reasonable high-multiplicity extension of that profile, though Theorem 1 does not prove a bound for the extension.

I would expect the approximation problem to be Class A: after sorting the \(\tau\) types, there are at most \(\tau\) cutoff intervals, and evaluating the objective uses a one-dimensional rate-function optimization. Whether the outer optimization admits a clean polynomial-time approximation guarantee in \(\tau\), bit length, and \(1/\epsilon\) is a further question, not a result of this paper. The scope here is the choice of congress size for the paper’s top-expert, majority-accuracy objective; it does not cover the empirical congress-size regression or the comparison with direct democracy.

The weak point is that Theorem 1 optimizes the exact finite-population correctness probability, while the mirror optimizes its asymptotic error exponent. This preserves the reliability objective at exponential scale but can lose distinctions determined by subexponential terms, so its optimal fraction need not equal a limit of \(K^\star/n\). The paper’s profile also assigns a different competence to each voter, whereas the proposed regime has many voters per competence type. Those are real gaps; the case for the mirror is that the paper already asks which top fraction should form a congress, and the classifier-tier setting makes that same selection question a sensible high-multiplicity problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor: Theorem 1 gives asymptotic bounds on the best congress size, but no algorithm or complexity classification. It does not establish a computational result of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror. That leaves room to formulate a new optimization problem, but the proponent’s rate objective is not the theorem’s objective.

The paper maximizes the finite electorate’s probability of a correct decision. For its uniform competence profile, the continuum of competence values is uniform on \([0,1]\). The top fraction \(\alpha\) then has mean competence \(1-\alpha/2\). For every fixed \(\alpha<1\), the law of large numbers makes that fraction’s majority correct with probability tending to \(1\). The limiting accuracy therefore cannot distinguish the fractions that Theorem 1’s finite-size analysis distinguishes. The proposed error exponent \(J_\mu(\alpha)\) avoids this collapse, but it changes the criterion: it asks which fraction makes errors decay fastest, a question the paper does not pose.

The most faithful alternative is to retain the finite majority probability at population scale \(N\), with \(N\mu_t\) voters of each competence type. That does preserve the paper’s objective, but \(N\) is essential: the population distribution alone cannot determine the finite-vote probability. The result is a high-multiplicity computation over a finite ballot process, rather than a population-only continuous optimization.

Even the exponent version offers little of the optimization structure ChoCo is meant to study. With finitely many competence tiers, selecting the top \(\alpha\) mass gives a single cutoff. Between successive tier-mass totals, \(J_\mu(\alpha)\) is a supremum of affine functions of \(\alpha\), hence convex; its maximum on that interval occurs at an endpoint. The proposed task thus reduces to checking the tier cutoffs and evaluating a one-dimensional rate function. That may be a valid statistical optimization problem, but it does not expose the kind of algorithmic landscape illustrated by exponential-variable LPs and pricing problems.

This is not a decisive case against every mirror. Large populations of classifiers grouped into reliable accuracy tiers are plausible, and the fraction selected is a meaningful quantity. The rate objective is also a defensible way to retain asymptotic information. So the negative case is weak at its hardest point: it challenges whether this is a worthwhile ChoCo complexity mirror, but it cannot honestly rule out a useful mirror in that regime.
