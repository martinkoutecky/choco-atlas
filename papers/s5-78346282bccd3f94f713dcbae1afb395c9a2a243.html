The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) divisible bads, a finite type set \(\Theta\) with rational mass vector \(\mu\), common per-capita entitlement \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^m\), and complete SPLC disutilities \(d_\theta\), compute positive prices \(\pi\) up to scaling and bundles \(x_\theta\) such that \(x_\theta\in\arg\min_{x\ge0}\{d_\theta(x):\pi\cdot x\ge\pi\cdot q\}\) for every \(\theta\), while \(\left|\sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{\theta j}-q_j\right|\le\varepsilon q_j\) for every bad \(j\).

The model it lives in

A finite set of complete SPLC disutility types carries masses \(\mu_\theta\); positive bad-price magnitudes \(\pi\) and one demand bundle \(x_\theta\) per type are chosen so each type minimizes disutility subject to its price-budget inequality, while weighted aggregate demand clears approximately.

What the mirror covers

It covers Theorem 29's PPAD-hardness for \(1/\operatorname{poly}(n)\)-approximate CEEI in all-bad SPLC Fisher markets, including the bounded-segment restriction; it leaves the Lemke algorithm, fixed-dimension strong-polynomial bound, structural equilibrium results, and experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(\tau\)-type version become tractable for fixed \(\tau\), or does PPAD-hardness persist with bounded type count?
  • Can exact equilibrium computation over \(\mu\) be separated algorithmically from inverse-polynomial approximate clearing?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a single mirror anchored in the paper’s hardness result.

The lead anchor is Theorem 29, proved in this paper. It states that finding a \(1/\operatorname{poly}(n)\)-approximate competitive equilibrium for a Fisher market with equal budgets (CEEI), when all items are bads and utilities are SPLC costs, is PPAD-hard. The hardness remains even when every utility function has at most three segments with \(O(1)\) costs. Their approximate equilibrium requires each agent to receive an exactly cost-minimizing bundle at the prices, subject to her budget constraint, while each bad is only required to be approximately market-cleared.

A continuous version is:

\[ \textsc{CEEI-Bads-SPLC}_{\infty}(\varepsilon). \]

An instance consists of \(m\) divisible bads, a finite type set \(\Theta\) with \(|\Theta|=\tau\), a rational distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{\Theta}\), and a common per-capita entitlement vector \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^m\). Type \(\theta\) is specified by its complete vector of SPLC disutility functions

\[ d_\theta(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_{\theta j}(x_j), \]

where every \(d_{\theta j}\) is nondecreasing piecewise-linear convex; in the hardness subcase, each has at most three segments and bounded cost parameters. The mass \(\mu_\theta\) consists of agents with exactly that cost profile. Every type has the same entitlement \(q\), so at positive bad-prices \(\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^m\) its budget is \(\pi\cdot q\). The positive prices \(\pi\) are the magnitudes of the paper’s negative prices for bads.

The required output is a price vector \(\pi\), up to scaling, and one bundle \(x_\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^m\) for each type such that

\[ x_\theta\in \arg\min_{x\ge0} \left\{ d_\theta(x): \pi\cdot x\ge \pi\cdot q \right\}, \]

and, for every bad \(j\),

\[ \left| \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta x_{\theta j}-q_j \right| \le \varepsilon q_j. \]

For an exact equilibrium, replace the last inequality by equality. The action is therefore not a planner’s welfare maximization: it is the computation of competitive prices and type-level demands satisfying individual optimality and aggregate market clearing.

This is a genuine population continuization. The paper already makes the *items and bundles* divisible, but its society is still a finite list of named agents. Here the society is the distribution \(\mu\); \(\mu_\theta x_{\theta j}\) is the mass of bad \(j\) assigned to type \(\theta\). Any within-type variation can be averaged, since the type’s feasible set is convex and its disutility is convex. Thus the type-level formulation preserves the paper’s equilibrium question rather than replacing it with a different objective.

A plausible regime is a very large recurring allocation system for unpleasant but divisible obligations: for example, a national network of standardized facilities sharing environmental-remediation tasks, maintenance duties, or service obligations. Facilities with the same technology and operating conditions have the same marginal-disutility curves for the \(m\) tasks. There may be millions of facilities but only tens or hundreds of cost types. Equal entitlements are a plausible CEEI fairness rule. The type is the full vector of cost curves, so no relevant agent-specific information is discarded.

I would expect this mirror to fall in Class B: hardness transfers. Given a finite instance from Theorem 29, group identical agents into types and set

\[ \mu_\theta=\frac{\#\{\text{agents of type }\theta\}}{N}. \]

The normalized aggregate demand condition is exactly the original supply-demand condition divided by \(N\), and assigning one common bundle to every copy of a type preserves individual optimality. Conversely, a type-level solution can be copied back to all agents of that type. The construction can also be replicated \(K\) times, leaving \(\tau\) unchanged while making the actual population \(K N\), so it admits a genuine high-multiplicity interpretation with \(N\gg\tau\).

The honest limitation is that Theorem 29’s reduction does not make hardness arise from population multiplicity: its combinatorics live mainly in the number of bads and the collection of cost types. Thus it does not show continuum-specific hardness, nor does it show that hardness persists for constant \(\tau\). It shows something narrower but still valuable: the authors’ exact CEEI-with-SPLC-bads problem remains meaningful when agents are represented by a population distribution, and its discrete PPAD-hardness survives that passage. The natural follow-up questions are whether exact equilibrium computation is easier than inverse-polynomial approximation, whether fixed \(\tau\) yields a tractable regime, and whether the paper’s fixed-\(m\) algorithmic results extend to the weighted type-level formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 29 does not actually exhibit a population-driven phenomenon. Its reduction uses \(N=6n^2+6n+2\) agents, and the agents are largely distinguished by their complete utility and endowment data: price-regulating agents are indexed by ordered item pairs, while row and column agents encode separate payoff comparisons. Once type completeness is respected, the hard instance has \(\tau=\Theta(n^2)\) distinct types and \(N/\tau=O(1)\). The reduction therefore supplies no genuine high-multiplicity regime.

Replicating every agent \(K\) times does not repair this. It makes the physical population \(KN\), but leaves the distribution over types, equilibrium equations, and computational instance unchanged. This is merely cloning a finite hard market. It shows that the model is closed under replication, not that replacing the population by masses creates a new or useful computational object. A more serious mirror would require hardness with small or fixed \(\tau\), or at least a regime where population multiplicity is the source of compression. Theorem 29 proves neither.

The proponent’s formulation also needs a correction before it can be claimed as the mirror: a type must include the entire transformed SPLC cost curve, including the zero-cost segments introduced in the paper’s exchange-to-Fisher conversion, as well as the entitlement data. With that correction, however, the formulation becomes almost exactly a quotient of the finite problem. Identical agents have the same convex demand set, so their allocations can be averaged into one type-level bundle; conversely, that bundle can be copied back to every member of the type. Market clearing and approximate clearing are preserved. This establishes that the mirror is well-defined, but also shows that it adds no population-level structure beyond grouping identical agents.

One could ask for a fixed-\(\tau\) version, but the paper gives no reason to expect its reduction to survive: its independent payoff inequalities are carried by the many distinct agent types. Encoding those inequalities into longer utility descriptions or more items would move the information out of the population and into the type data or item dimension. That may still be a worthwhile open problem, but it is no longer evidence supplied by this paper for a population continuization.

This is not a valid objection that the answer is unchanged, nor is existing high-multiplicity work a collision; both would be mistakes. The difficulty is narrower: the only anchor is a valid and natural high-multiplicity restatement, but its hard family is not high-multiplicity in the meaningful computational sense, and its suggested replication is vacuous. I therefore cannot honestly defeat this anchor universally. The negative case is weak: it can downgrade the claimed motivation and novelty, but it cannot establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
