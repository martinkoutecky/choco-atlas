The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is a high-multiplicity panel of experts: many advisers with repeated, complete profiles of goal opinions, tolerances, and priorities. Give each profile \(t\) a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For example, a large service network might draw on thousands of local advisers using a small set of standardized planning templates, so the number of advisers \(N\) is much larger than the number of distinct profiles \(\tau\). This makes the *population of experts* continuous; the paper’s \(\alpha\)-cuts remain a way to represent uncertainty about their goals.

There is a strict limitation: the paper has no named, numbered computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. Section 4.1 proposes a Type-2 fuzzy goal-programming model, and Section 5 describes solving crisp LPs at the four \(\alpha\)-cut boundaries. Section 6 gives a numerical example. None is a numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a computational property. The \(\alpha\)-cut method is the closest computational contribution, but presenting it as a qualifying anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

As an unanchored positive candidate, I would ask Expert-Mass Type-2 Goal Programming at a Fixed Cut. An instance has a feasible polyhedron \(P=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^m:A'x\le b'\}\), goal functions \(G_k(x)\), a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) expert profiles with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), and each profile’s Type-2 fuzzy aspiration for each goal. For a supplied \(\alpha\), let \(b^q_{tk}(\alpha)\) be one of that profile’s four cut-boundary values, indexed by \(q\). For each boundary \(q\), find a shared decision \(x_q\) and deviations \(d^-_{tkq},d^+_{tkq}\ge0\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_k \mu_t(d^-_{tkq}+d^+_{tkq}) \]
subject to
\[ G_k(x_q)+d^-_{tkq}-d^+_{tkq}=b^q_{tk}(\alpha) \quad\text{for every }t,k,\qquad x_q\in P. \]
The solution is the optimal decision and average deviation for each boundary. This keeps the paper’s goals, fuzzy aspiration profiles, deviation objective, and LP computation, while making the expert population the continuous object. With explicit support, the resulting LPs are polynomial in the input dimensions and bit length, so I would expect Class A. Follow-up questions include whether average deviation is an acceptable aggregation of expert views, and what happens when the profile support is implicit and requires a pricing problem.

My weakest point is also the central one: replacing fuzzy aggregation of a finite expert panel with mass-weighted average deviations changes the model’s interpretation. The proposed scenario makes that choice plausible, but the paper neither studies that regime nor proves a result about it. So it is a defensible mirror candidate, not a paper-anchored case under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a paper anchored mirror is decisive: the paper states no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary about the computational complexity of a problem. Sections 4.1 and 5 propose a model and a procedure that solves LPs at \(\alpha\)-cut boundaries; Section 6 gives an example. The proponent acknowledges this, so their expert-mass formulation is a new candidate problem, not a continuous version of a result established here.

That candidate is plausible. A large panel of advisers using repeated, complete profiles of goals and tolerances could sensibly be represented by masses. But the proposed weighted-deviation objective makes the panel’s disagreement an expected loss: a large group can compensate for a minority’s unmet goals. The paper instead represents uncertainty in aspirations through Type-2 fuzzy sets and their bounds. A more faithful population model could aggregate each profile’s fuzzy memberships or cut boundaries, but the aggregation rule would be an additional modeling choice, and the paper neither defines nor studies it. That weakens the claim that this is *the paper’s* problem continued to a population limit.

The negative case is therefore strong against using this paper as an anchored example, but weak as a universal claim. The text does not rule out a worthwhile continuous model of expert populations, and repeated expert profiles are a credible high-multiplicity regime. The most defensible conclusion is that the other analyst’s candidate may merit separate study, but it cannot supply the paper-anchored case the prompt calls for.
