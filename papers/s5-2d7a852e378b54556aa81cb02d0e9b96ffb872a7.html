Theorem 1 characterizes when an efficient, individually rational, obviously strategy-proof rule satisfying EUA exists on a rich domain. Theorem 2 proves that no such rule satisfies EBA on the single-peaked domain when \(n\geq4\). Theorem 3 establishes properties of the Designator rule. These are results about mechanism existence, compatibility, and axioms; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The named propositions likewise do not supply such an anchor.

There is a conceivable population-continuum analogue of Theorem 2: let types record both an agent’s single-peaked ranking and her endowed object; let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of each type; and let an assignment flow \(x_{t,o}\) distribute that mass across object types while matching total supply. One could ask whether an efficient, individually rational, obviously strategy-proof mechanism can satisfy a mass version of EBA when exactly two endowment types are acclaimed. But this is not a valid anchored mirror here. The paper’s EBA concerns two named agents receiving their favorite active objects, and its OSP condition applies to individual agents’ choices. In an atomless population, an individual’s report may have no effect; treating whole type groups as strategic players would change the mechanism model. The paper gives no result that settles this continuum question.

So the strongest honest conclusion for the programme is limited: a plausible high-multiplicity setting can be sketched, but this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor to mirror. Its contribution is axiomatic mechanism design, not a complexity landscape for computational social choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with the missing anchor: none of the paper’s named results classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 characterizes when an efficient, individually rational, obviously strategy-proof rule satisfying EUA exists. Theorem 2 proves an incompatibility among mechanism properties, and Theorem 3 verifies properties of Designator. They are existence and axiomatic results, not algorithmic results over encoded instances.

Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate for a mirror. A natural high-multiplicity model would group agents by preference and endowed object, then represent trades among object classes by flows. But each endowed object is part of an agent’s complete type, so the literal model has at least \(n\) types as the market grows. Repeated types become possible if objects are replaced by interchangeable classes or copies. That is plausible, yet it exposes the more fundamental mismatch: OSP compares what an individual can receive after choosing a strategy. In an atomless population, one person’s report has zero mass, so a rule determined by the population distribution cannot respond to it; individual OSP becomes vacuous. Keeping OSP consequential requires finite, positive-mass strategic players. Treating whole cohorts as those players would make the theorem about coalition or group incentives, rather than the individual exchange incentives in the paper.

EBA also has no unique mass translation. With repeated object classes, the mass of claimants may exceed the supply of their favored class. A continuum version must decide how to ration that supply and what fraction of a claimant type must receive its favorite class. Those choices can be reasonable, but they add a fairness axiom and allocation model the paper does not supply. The obstacle is not that an EBA mirror would have a particular answer; it is that preserving the paper’s strategic unit and fairness requirement does not yield a population-distribution problem.

Theorem 1 has the same issue. Its richness and single-peakedness conditions describe a domain of rankings, independently of the population’s masses. Replacing profiles by distributions does not create a computational task from that characterization; moving to object classes changes the domain and again leaves individual OSP’s meaning unresolved. Theorem 3 offers the closest thing to computation, but Designator is a finite procedure with at most \(n\) departures. With explicit rankings, its described operations admit a straightforward polynomial implementation. A mass-flow version would need new rules for ordering copies and assigning members of cohorts, and the paper gives no optimization objective or separation problem that would make that translation a ChoCo complexity question.

So a continuous strategic-exchange project is conceivable, but it would first need a nontrivial incentive concept for an atomless population and a chosen mass version of EBA. That could interest mechanism-design researchers; it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The negative case is strongest as a verdict on this paper’s fit for ChoCo. It cannot prove that every future cohort-based exchange model is unworthy; it shows that making one requires substantive new theory rather than continuizing these results.
