The continuous mirror question

Given finite supported applicant types \(A\), recipient types \(B\), positive masses \(\mu_a\) with \(\sum_{a\in A}\mu_a=1\), complete preference preorders, and a fixed-support clone-symmetric scaled laminar quota function \(q\) on \(E\subseteq A\times B\), choose \(x_e\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(x(S)\le q(S)\) for every \(S\subseteq E\). For \(e=(a,b)\), let \(W_e\) be type \(a\)'s unmatched mass plus its mass on other assignments weakly below \(e\), and let \(S_e\) be the mass strictly below \(e\). The recipient side weakly accepts \(e\) if, for some \(\epsilon>0\), either \(x+\epsilon\mathbf{1}_e\) is feasible for its quota constraints or \(x+\epsilon\mathbf{1}_e-\epsilon\mathbf{1}_f\) is feasible for some occupied \(f\) with \(e\succeq_H f\). It strictly accepts if direct addition is feasible or such an exchange exists with \(e\succ_H f\), matching the paper's addition and exchange cases. Does there exist a feasible \(x\) with no edge \(e\) for which \(W_e>0\), the recipient side weakly accepts, and either \(S_e>0\) or the recipient side strictly accepts; if so, construct such an \(x\)?

The model it lives in

A clone-symmetric two-sided hiring market with applicant-type masses \(\mu_a\), recipient priorities, and scaled laminar quotas represented by a polymatroid rank function \(q\). The assignment variable \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of type \(a\) assigned to type \(b\); feasibility enforces applicant mass limits and \(x(S)\le q(S)\), while stability uses type-level preference support and feasible recipient additions or priority exchanges.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Strongly Stable Matching of Masses under Scaled Matroid Quotas, mirroring Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Theorem 1 says the proposed algorithm correctly decides existence and finds a matching: if it outputs null, no strongly stable matching exists; otherwise its output is strongly stable. The paper gives no numbered hardness result, so this is the appropriate sole anchor. The paper’s cited polynomial results for special cases are useful context, but they are not additional anchors here.

The mirror is a large placement market, such as a national public-service hiring round, in which many applicants and positions recur in a modest number of complete preference and eligibility types. A doctor type records the applicant’s full ranking of program types and every relevant qualification; a recipient type records the position’s admission priority and quota role. The regime is millions of applicants and positions but, say, hundreds or thousands of distinct types. This is a scenario claim, not a claim that every hiring market has few types.

Let \(A\) be the applicant types, \(B\) the recipient types, and \(E\subseteq A\times B\) the feasible type pairs. Type \(a\) has rational mass \(\mu_a\), with \(\sum_{a\in A}\mu_a=1\), and a complete preference preorder \(\succeq_a\) over its feasible recipients and being unmatched. Each edge is strictly preferred to being unmatched, as in the paper. The recipient side has a preorder \(\succeq_H\) over \(E\) and a rational, mass-scaled matroid rank function \(q\), representing per-capita capacity constraints; laminar regional and specialty quotas are a concrete case.

The decision variable is an assignment of mass \(x_e\ge 0\) to each \(e\in E\), with unmatched mass \(u_a=\mu_a-\sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\). Feasibility requires \(u_a\ge0\) and \(\sum_{e\in S}x_e\le q(S)\) for every \(S\subseteq E\). Ask whether there is a feasible \(x\) with no strongly blocking type pair, and, if so, return such an \(x\). There is no optimization objective: this preserves Theorem 1’s existence-and-search question.

Here is the blocking condition precisely. For \(e=(a,b)\), the mass of type \(a\) that can weakly consider moving to \(b\) is \(u_a+\sum_{b'\ne b:(a,b)\succeq_a(a,b')}x_{ab'}\); the strictly improving mass is \(u_a+\sum_{b'\ne b:(a,b)\succ_a(a,b')}x_{ab'}\). On the recipient side, \(e\) weakly accepts an infinitesimal addition if some \(\epsilon>0\) either makes \(x+\epsilon\mathbf 1_e\) satisfy its rank constraints, or permits \(x+\epsilon\mathbf 1_e-\epsilon\mathbf 1_f\) to satisfy them for some \(f\) with \(x_f>0\) and \(e\succeq_H f\). It strictly accepts if such an exchange exists with \(e\succ_H f\). A pair blocks when positive mass can weakly consider the move, the recipient weakly accepts it, and either positive mass strictly prefers it or the recipient strictly accepts it. This follows the paper’s strong-blocking rule: weak support on both sides, strict support on at least one. On a discrete grid of cloned agents, the mass exchange corresponds to the paper’s unit-level addition or matroid exchange.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The paper’s proof is built around matroid intersection, rank functions, and submodular minimization, all natural objects for mass assignments. A target algorithm should run in time polynomial in the number of supported types, the bit length of the masses and quota data, and the rank-oracle cost—not in the expanded number of cloned agents. Theorem 1 does not establish that bound: it solves the explicitly represented finite market. The continuous theorem would need to show that its structure survives aggregation and rational mass scaling.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it retains their two-sided preferences with ties, the same matroid feasibility and exchange logic, and the same existence-and-finding task. It continuizes the population; quota scaling is the accompanying change needed to describe a large market in per-capita terms. The scope is just Theorem 1’s many-to-one strongly stable matching result, not the paper’s individual technical lemmas or its proposed many-to-many extension.

My weakest point is that fractional strong stability under matroid constraints is not uniquely canonical. In particular, the paper defines blocking for individual edges, while the mirror defines it through feasible infinitesimal mass exchanges. That definition is deliberately close to the paper’s addition/exchange rule, and it agrees on expanded discrete instances, but the authors could still dispute whether it captures the right limiting notion. A useful follow-up is to prove exactly when a rational mass-stable assignment lifts to a strongly stable assignment of finitely many clones—and when it does not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The obvious decisive objection is unavailable: Theorem 1 is a named computational result, giving an existence test and a strongly stable matching when one exists. The paper’s problem is therefore a real anchor.

The strongest objection to the proposed mirror is that its type description does not yet capture the paper’s full structure. A recipient’s role is determined not just by its quota and priority, but by how all its incident edges sit in the global matroid: which circuits they belong to, which exchanges are feasible, and how those exchanges compare in priority. Two recipients that look identical locally may not be interchangeable in an arbitrary independence-oracle matroid. A faithful type would have to preserve that whole exchange structure. In generic instances, that could make types nearly unique and leave little population to continuize.

That objection weakens considerably in the better version of the mirror: restrict to a clone-symmetric market with repeated applicant and recipient types and scaled laminar quotas. The aggregate rank constraints can then encode the matroid structure, and infinitesimal exchanges give a plausible mass-level analogue of the paper’s one-edge exchanges. At rational masses, scaling to sufficiently many clones can recover a finite matching model. This is a sensible instance regime, and the paper’s authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem.

There is also a genuine modeling choice around blocking. In the finite theorem, one agent can block; in a normalized continuum, an individual has zero mass. Requiring positive mass to support a block can therefore lose blockers involving only a vanishing share of the population. But the proposed rule treats any positive amount of a type as able to move an infinitesimal amount, which addresses the issue when types have positive limiting mass. Rare types whose population share tends to zero fall outside that regime rather than refuting it.

So the negative case does not defeat the best mirror. It shows that the proponent needs to state the clone symmetry and mass-level exchange assumptions precisely, and that the continuous stability notion may not preserve every vanishing-share obstruction in finite markets. Those are substantial qualifications, but a clone-symmetric laminar-quota hiring market remains a credible continuous mirror of Theorem 1. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
