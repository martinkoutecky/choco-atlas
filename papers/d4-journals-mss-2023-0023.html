The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T=\{t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\}\), masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), entitlement weights \(w_t>0\), a finite set of indivisible good classes \(G\) with per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and a matroid \(M_t=(G,\mathcal{I}_t)\) for each type, compute masses \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) of type \(t\) receiving each integral independent bundle \(B\in\mathcal{I}_t\), subject to \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\le q_g\), maximizing \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}w_tf(r_t(B))\). When \(f(0)=-\infty\), first maximize positive mass, then apply a fixed type-order refinement; otherwise apply the refinement after weighted-welfare maximization.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity configuration LP over population types. The variables \(x_{t,B}\) describe mass assigned to integral matroid-independent bundles, while the objective is weighted concave welfarism. Pricing at dual prices \(p_g\) reduces to \(\max_{B\in\mathcal{I}_t}\bigl(w_tf(r_t(B))-\sum_{g\in B}p_g\bigr)\), solvable by trying each cardinality and using matroid greedy optimization.

The objection that survived

The named-agent lexicographic tie-break is not shown to be the exact high-multiplicity limit of the paper's rule, so the configuration LP mirrors an anonymous extension of \(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tie}}_f\) rather than the theorem's rule verbatim.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper's polynomial-time allocation result in Theorem 4.4. It does not automatically cover the monotonicity and group-strategyproofness theorems, their exact finite-agent tie-breaking, or the appendix counterexamples.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration LP, including the anonymous tie-breaking and the \(f(0)=-\infty\) case, be solved exactly in polynomial time under a precise rational encoding of \(f\), \(w_t\), \(\mu_t\), and \(q_g\)?
  • What rounding or additive-approximation guarantees relate a rational mass solution to finite realizations with \(M\mu_t\) agents of each type and integral bundles?
  • Do resource-, population-, weight-monotonicity, and group-strategyproofness have valid type-mass analogues under the anonymous refinement?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s main algorithmic result, Theorem 4.4, which is proved in this paper. It states:

Under matroid-rank valuations, for every concave strictly increasing function \(f\), Algorithm 1 computes an \(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tie}}_f\) allocation in polynomial time.

Here the input has finitely many agents with positive weights, finitely many indivisible goods, and matroid-rank valuation oracles. The rule maximizes \(\sum_i w_i f(v_i(A_i))\), applies the paper’s special handling when \(f(0)=-\infty\), lexicographically maximizes the utility vector, and removes redundant goods. The algorithm processes goods one at a time, using exchange graphs and shortest augmenting paths; the proof gives running time \(O(mn(m^2+n))\), under the paper’s value-oracle convention.

My mirror is therefore:

Continuum Weighted Matroid-Welfarist Allocation. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a positive entitlement weight \(w_t\), and a matroid-rank valuation \(r_t\) for each type. There is also a finite set \(G\) of standardized indivisible good classes, with per-capita supply \(q_g\). A solution is a collection \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\), subject to

\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\le q_g \]

for every good \(g\). We may restrict to independent bundles \(B\) of the matroid \(r_t\), because redundant goods never improve welfare. The objective is

\[ \max_x\ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B}x_{t,B}w_t f(r_t(B)). \]

When \(f(0)=-\infty\), the rule first maximizes the mass receiving positive utility, with a fixed type-order analogue of the paper’s index tie-breaking, and then maximizes weighted welfare and lexicographically refines the type-average utilities.

This is genuinely the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocation problem. A finite realization with \(M\) agents contains approximately \(M\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), \(Mq_g\) copies of good \(g\), and gives each individual an integral bundle. The variables \(x_{t,B}\) are only type-level proportions; they do not give fractional bundles to individual agents. Thus the mirror preserves indivisibility, weights, matroid-rank preferences, concave welfarism, and the paper’s tie-breaking logic.

A convincing regime is a large university system allocating many copies of course seats to students. A type records a student’s degree programme, eligibility constraints, entitlement weight, and course-demand matroid. Thousands of students may share exactly the same type, while the number of degree/eligibility profiles is much smaller. The supply of seats scales with the student population. Public-housing allocation with many households sharing eligibility and entitlement profiles gives a similar scenario.

The continuous problem is likely in Class A. Its configuration formulation is a linear program with exponentially many bundle variables, but its pricing problem is tractable. Given dual prices \(p_g\), type \(t\)'s pricing problem is

\[ \max_{B\in\mathcal I_t} \left(w_t f(|B|)-\sum_{g\in B}p_g\right), \]

where \(\mathcal I_t\) is the independent-set family of the matroid. For each possible cardinality \(k\), a minimum-cost independent set of size \(k\) can be found by the matroid greedy algorithm. Trying all \(k\) therefore gives polynomial-time pricing, enabling column generation or ellipsoid optimization under standard rational-encoding assumptions. This is precisely the kind of exponential-variable LP whose tractability depends on a solvable pricing problem, and it retains the paper’s underlying problem rather than replacing it with additive valuations or divisible individual bundles.

The authors would plausibly recognize this as their problem: the continuous model is obtained by grouping agents who are identical in every relevant respect and taking the high-multiplicity limit of the same weighted welfare objective. The matroid structure is not weakened. Indeed, the continuum makes the paper’s repeated-agent regime explicit and exposes a natural optimization formulation that complements their exchange-graph algorithm.

This mirror covers Theorem 4.4 emphatically, but not every theorem in the paper. The monotonicity and group-strategyproofness results suggest further continuous questions, such as whether increasing \(q_g\) weakly increases every type’s average utility, or whether no positive-mass coalition can benefit from misreporting its matroid type. I would not use those as anchors here because their continuum tie-breaking and coalition definitions require additional modelling choices.

The weakest point is that the paper’s algorithm is for named finite agents and its proof relies on individual exchange paths. The configuration-LP argument is therefore a new justification for the continuum result, not a direct limiting version of Algorithm 1. A referee could also object that the type-level lexicographic tie-break is not uniquely forced by the finite rule. Those are real qualifications, but they do not undermine the mirror itself: high multiplicity is exactly the regime in which type-level mass allocations and configuration LPs are the natural mathematical object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is a fidelity objection. The paper’s actual rule \(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tie}}_f\) is not anonymous: its lexicographic tie-breaking depends on the named-agent order. Since “type” must include every feature relevant to the problem, exact types would have to include that identity information. With \(M\) copies of an ostensibly identical agent, the type count could therefore still be \(M\), eliminating the high-multiplicity compression.

The proposed repair changes the rule. Replacing agent-order tie-breaking by type-order or average-utility tie-breaking defines a new anonymous mechanism. Likewise, replacing the paper’s named goods by per-capita supplies of parallel good classes imposes substantial symmetry absent from the original model. Theorem 4.4 does not establish this compressed problem: its exchange-graph algorithm pays explicitly for every agent and every good copy. The configuration LP and matroid-greedy pricing are a new algorithmic development, not a limiting form of the paper’s algorithm.

This is a respectable objection to calling the construction a direct continuization of Theorem 4.4. The continuous LP also optimizes a distribution of bundles for each type, whereas the theorem outputs a particular allocation to named agents. Its polynomial-time claim additionally needs rationally represented \(f\)-values and a careful treatment of the anonymous tie-breaking, neither of which is supplied by the paper.

But this does not defeat the best version of the mirror. The programme explicitly permits a natural co-modification forced by population scaling, and parallel course seats or housing units provide a credible regime. The variables \(x_{t,B}\) still assign every individual an integral bundle; they only describe how an atomless mass is divided among such bundles. Rational masses can be expanded into finite high-multiplicity instances exactly. The pricing problem really is polynomial for matroid types, so the resulting configuration LP is a genuine Class A question rather than outcome-space fractionalization.

Thus the negative case can show that the proponent overstated fidelity to \(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tie}}_f\), but it cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Once identity-sensitive tie-breaking is replaced by a recognized anonymous refinement, the course-allocation or housing interpretation supplies a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the continuous computational problem survives.
