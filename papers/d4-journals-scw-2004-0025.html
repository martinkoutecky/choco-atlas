What the mirror covers

A mass formulation could extend the distribution rankings and tax characterizations in Theorems 3 and 5. The paper’s inequality measures, normative criteria, and tax axioms provide no computational guarantee or hardness result; an optimal-tax optimization would require additional constraints not supplied here.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that the paper has a natural population-as-mass version, but it has no named computational result to anchor the continuous problem to.

A plausible regime is a large tax jurisdiction with many people but relatively few opportunity classes and responsibility-choice categories. Represent each complete cell \((O,w)\) by its population mass \(\mu_{O,w}\); income is \(g(O,w)\), and a tax schedule acts on income and opportunity class. The opportunity comparisons then aggregate the cells by \(O\), just as the paper aggregates people into opportunity types. That seems a sensible high-multiplicity setting for its questions about income-tax design and opportunity inequality.

The closest named results are Theorem 3, proved in the paper, and Theorem 5, also proved there. Theorem 3 says that for equal-mean income distributions with the same type partition, opportunity Lorenz dominance is equivalent to obtaining the more equal opportunity distribution through a finite sequence of opportunity-equalizing transfers, and to its being ranked at least as well by every opportunity-egalitarian social evaluation function. Theorem 5 says that a tax policy is opportunity redistributive exactly when it satisfies the paper’s horizontal-equity and vertical-equity axioms. These are mathematical characterizations, not results about computational complexity or algorithms.

So I would not present either theorem as a qualifying anchor or invent a per-anchor computational problem. The main limitation is that the paper’s formal population is finite; its continuous income distributions concern the outcome variable. A mass-based tax-design question may be a credible extension, but this paper does not establish a named computational result for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 3 characterizes agreement between opportunity-Lorenz dominance, opportunity-equalizing transfers, and a family of social evaluations. Theorem 5 characterizes opportunity-redistributive taxes through horizontal- and vertical-equity axioms. Theorem 6 gives a normative comparison with a proportional tax. None poses a computational problem or claims an algorithm, complexity bound, or hardness result.

A population-as-mass model is quite plausible here: a large jurisdiction could have mass distributed across a few opportunity classes and income or responsibility profiles. That supports the *regime*; it does not supply the missing computational result. A continuous-population version of Theorem 3 would ask whether its normative equivalence persists for mass distributions. A version of Theorem 5 would characterize admissible mass-based tax schedules. Both are recognizable extensions of the paper’s economics, but neither is a computational mirror of the theorem it extends.

One could make a computational project by asking for an optimal tax schedule under a specified revenue target, feasible tax family, and additional constraints. That may be worthwhile, but those choices add a new optimization problem; they are not supplied by Theorems 3 or 5. Calling such a project their mirror would make the anchor too loose to distinguish a mirror from a follow-on research question.

So I would reject the anchors as support for a continuous *computational* mirror, while conceding that a continuous-population extension is sensible. The case is weaker if the claim is literally that no scenario could yield any worthwhile adjacent computational project: this paper’s policy-design subject offers plausible starting points. The defensible negative verdict is narrower: none of the named results anchors such a project under ChoCo’s computational criterion.
