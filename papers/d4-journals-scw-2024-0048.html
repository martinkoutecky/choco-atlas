The case FOR (proponent)

There is a natural population-continuous reading of the paper’s central object. A type is a complete ranking \(v\in D\); its mass \(\mu_v\) is its share of society. For a distribution \(\mu\) over \(D\), define the pairwise margin
\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{v\in D}\mu_v\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_v b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_v a]\bigr). \]
The strict majority relation has \(a\succ_\mu b\) when \(M_\mu(a,b)>0\).

A precise candidate problem is Continuous Maximal Condorcet-Domain Recognition: given alternatives \(A\) and a listed set of rankings \(D\subseteq L(A)\), decide whether every distribution \(\mu\) over \(D\) produces an acyclic strict majority relation, and whether \(D\) is inclusion-maximal with that property. A “no” answer could be witnessed by a cyclic distribution or by a larger domain that retains the property. A plausible regime is a large electorate with repeated complete rankings: for fixed or modestly sized \(A\), the population \(N\) can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings present.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for every \(K\subseteq[2,\ldots,n-1]\), the generalized Fishburn domain \(F_K\) is a maximal Condorcet domain. Its content carries over to the candidate problem: \(F_K\) is maximal when arbitrary real masses are allowed. A strict majority cycle under real masses would persist under a sufficiently close rational approximation, and rational masses can be scaled to integer voter counts. Conversely, every finite society gives rational masses. Thus allowing continuous shares does not change which domains have the universal acyclicity property. Theorem 1, also proved here, identifies \(D(S_K)=F_K\); Corollary 1 states that every \(F_K\) is single-peaked on a circle, citing Karpov (2023). These are structural results, not computational classifications.

That distinction is decisive for the requested anchors: the paper contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Theorem 2 is a strong structural foothold for a continuous analogue, but it does not qualify as a computational anchor. For the \(F_K\) instances it gives a yes answer to the domain property; it supplies no algorithmic complexity result for recognizing arbitrary domains.

The weak point is therefore substantial: the mirror is recognizable as the paper’s Condorcet-domain question, but its population weights extend a universal structural property, not a computational problem the paper studies. The continuous formulation appears to preserve the discrete answer exactly, with no complexity landscape established here. This is a plausible structural mirror, and not a compliant anchored case under the stated rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 proves that every \(F_K\) is a maximal Condorcet domain; Theorem 1 identifies \(F_K\) with the necklace construction. Theorem 3 concerns connectivity, and Theorem 4 rules out circular single-peakedness for single-crossing maximal domains. These are structural results about sets of rankings. None classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem.

The proposed Continuous Maximal Condorcet-Domain Recognition is a sensible problem to formulate, but its computational task is supplied by the analyst. The paper does not study recognizing arbitrary domains from an input list, or give an algorithm or complexity bound for that task. For the particular \(F_K\) family, Theorem 2 settles maximality uniformly; turning that proof into a recognition problem does not make it a computational result of the paper.

The population model itself is plausible: voters sharing a ranking are a natural type. And the continuous formulation is faithful to the domain property. If a distribution over rankings produces a strict majority cycle, sufficiently close rational shares preserve that cycle; rational shares can be scaled to a finite electorate. Thus the universal property over real masses agrees with the finite electorate version. That equivalence is not, by itself, a reason to reject a mirror. It does show that the proposed continuous weights add no new computational question to the result this paper proves: its central claim already quantifies over societies, while the paper’s contribution is the structural characterization.

A stronger salvage would ask for the complexity of recognizing maximal Condorcet domains, or of finding a cyclic distribution when one exists. Either could be worthwhile research. But each makes a new algorithmic problem from the paper’s subject matter; neither mirrors a named complexity result here. The same applies to deriving an algorithm from the necklace construction or testing circular single-peakedness: these are possible projects, not computational findings the paper reports.

So I would reject this as a strong ChoCo mirror on the available anchors. I would not claim that high multiplicity is implausible, or that an unchanged answer makes a question uninteresting. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers a substantial structural theorem and a natural population interpretation, but no computational result for continuization to extend. A separate complexity project could still be valuable; the paper alone does not establish that value.
