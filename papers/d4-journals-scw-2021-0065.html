What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are mechanism characterizations; the paper gives no named algorithmic, complexity, or computational-formulation result. The multi-unit proposal is an unproved extension.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that in a single-object auction, among perfect-information dynamic communication mechanisms satisfying weak tightness, a mechanism is both efficient and ex-post incentive compatible if and only if it is an English auction. The paper’s English-auction class consists of monotone-price mechanisms with three further properties: a unique active agent at the current highest price cannot be asked next; the next price is at most one unit above the current highest active price; and the auction ends when two active agents become one, who wins and pays their last offered price. There is a cap-value tie exception. This is a characterization result, not a computational-complexity result; the paper proves it in Appendix B.4 and cites the classic second-price-auction characterizations only as its static counterpart.

A plausible *extension* would model a large market for identical units. Let a unit mass of unit-demand bidders have values in a finite set \(V\), with rational type masses \(\mu_v\), and let supply be a fraction \(\kappa\) of the population. A threshold protocol asks bidders whether they accept successive prices; the outcome assigns supply to the highest-value mass, splitting the marginal type’s mass if needed, and charges winners their highest accepted price. The question would be: given \((\mu,\kappa)\), characterize or construct finite threshold protocols that implement this efficient allocation and make sincere answers ex-post optimal. A large cohort with few value types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and this extension is close to the paper’s auction setting. For the direct cutoff allocation, the likely route is tractable: sort the supported values and find the supply cutoff.

That is a useful research question, but it is not a valid anchor-based mirror under the requested standard. Theorem 1 concerns one indivisible object and finitely many named bidders. Scaling supply with population changes the allocation problem; keeping one object instead leaves the winning individual as a zero-mass identity that \(\mu\) does not record. In an atomless model, one bidder’s deviation also cannot change aggregate mass, so ex-post incentives need not behave as they do in the paper. Section 3.4 discusses multi-unit auctions, but does not prove this extension.

More broadly, none of the paper’s numbered results asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, W[1]-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. For example, Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes EPIC mechanisms for an indivisible public good as equivalent to unanimous-acceptance mechanisms; it too is a mechanism-design characterization, not a complexity result. So the continuous auction above is a speculative extension, not a mirror of a named computational result. The weakest point is decisive: the paper supplies no eligible computational anchor, and the closest extension changes the supply and incentive structure that its theorem characterizes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 classifies mechanisms that are efficient and EPIC; Theorem 2 classifies EPIC mechanisms for public-good provision. Neither gives an algorithmic problem or a complexity bound, and neither takes a society distribution as input: both make claims across all value profiles and communication protocols. One could add a task such as “given type masses, construct an efficient EPIC protocol,” but that would be a new mechanism-synthesis problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 1’s single-object auction makes the population mismatch concrete. A distribution over values records how many bidders have each value, but not which individual wins a tie; with one object, the winner’s normalized mass tends to zero as the population grows. Scaling supply to a positive fraction \(\kappa\) repairs that degeneracy, and is the strongest version of the proponent’s idea. But it changes the result into a multi-unit auction question, an extension the paper mentions without characterizing. In an atomless population, one person’s answer cannot change aggregate demand or the allocation cutoff, so the individual pivotality behind the finite auction’s EPIC characterization disappears. Making a whole type cohort’s answer pivotal restores strategic force by treating that cohort as a single actor, rather than as many indistinguishable individuals.

Theorem 2 faces a sharper limit problem. Its unanimity characterization turns on whether *any one* agent rejects. A mass distribution over responses cannot distinguish unanimous acceptance from a single dissenting agent of zero limiting mass. If provision depends on acceptance by almost everyone, that dissent no longer vetoes; if it depends on literal unanimity, the rule must track individual responses outside the population distribution. A positive-mass or approximate-unanimity threshold is a natural alternative, but it removes the individual-veto result that Theorem 2 establishes. Treating each mass as a reporting bloc again changes the strategic object.

These objections do not show that continuum auction or public-good mechanism design is uninteresting. A continuum multi-unit auction is the clearest weak point in the negative case: it may be worthwhile in its own right. The narrower case against is that the paper supplies no computational anchor for ChoCo, while its two named characterizations rely on individual-level strategic effects that aggregate mass either erases or requires one to rebuild through a different model.
