The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchored positive case under the stated criteria: this paper has no named computational result. Its numbered results are axiomatic. For example, Corollary 3.2, proved in the paper, says that every Pareto-optimal, strict strategy-proof rule on the finite-agent, sphere-valued, single-peaked domain is dictatorial; Corollary 3.4 proves the corresponding statement for coalitional strategy-proof rules. Neither asserts an algorithmic complexity result.

The most plausible setting for a population mirror would be repeated public-good siting, with each type a peak location on the sphere, \(\mu_t\) the fraction of residents with that peak, and the outcome a point on the sphere. One could ask about Pareto-optimal, strategy-proof rules on such distributions. But that would continuize the paper’s axiomatic question, and changing from finite agents to population mass also requires defining what manipulation means when an individual has negligible mass. It supplies no objective or computational problem anchored in a result of this paper.

So there is no qualifying continuous problem to state per anchor, and no grounded tractability or hardness prediction. The strongest positive point is only that the location setting admits a natural population interpretation; it does not make this paper a computational continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a ChoCo mirror is strongest at the level of the paper’s actual results. Corollaries 3.2 and 3.4 characterize which social choice rules can satisfy Pareto optimality alongside strict or coalitional strategy-proofness: they must be dictatorial. They give no computational problem, algorithm, or complexity claim to continuize. The paper’s open question about ordinary strategy-proofness is axiomatic too.

The proposed population model is sensible: residents could be grouped by peak location, with \(\mu\) recording the mass at each location. But asking which rules on those distributions are Pareto-optimal and strategy-proof remains an axiomatic question. There is also a real modeling choice to make: under an atomless population, one person’s report changes no mass, so individual strategy-proofness can become vacuous for rules based on \(\mu\). Requiring positive-mass coalitions to be unable to manipulate gives a meaningful variant, but it changes the strategic axiom and still does not supply a computational result.

One could instead invent algorithms for checking Pareto efficiency or finding a desirable location from a finite-support distribution. Those may be useful siting problems, but the paper’s corollaries do not pose or analyze them. Likewise, the sphere being a continuous outcome space does not make the paper a continuous-population study: its population remains finite.

So the strongest honest negative case is not that repeated-location populations are implausible; they are quite plausible. It is that the proponent’s only candidate mirror changes an axiomatic characterization into another axiomatic question. Any computational siting problem would be a new project rather than a continuization of a named result here. The universal claim that no related scenario could ever be worthwhile is too broad to establish, but there is no anchored computational mirror in this paper for ChoCo to pursue.
