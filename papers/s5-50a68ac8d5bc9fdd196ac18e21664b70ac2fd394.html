The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a qualifying anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational-complexity result. Definitions 4–6 define UCI, CI, and EMU, and the paper computes them by simulation. Its comparisons—such as Minimax giving more incentive to appeal to strongly opposed voters than IRV—are empirical findings, not complexity results. I would not present them as anchors.

The strongest positive case I can make is a proposed problem based on those definitions: Continuous CID Evaluation. It is a plausible mirror of what the paper measures, but it is not anchored by a named computational result in the paper.

An instance would specify \(m\) candidates and a finite set \(T\) of voter types. Each type records a complete rational utility vector and the voter strategy used in the model; the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). To retain the paper’s randomness, the input can also include an explicit list of rationally weighted society-and-poll scenarios. For each candidate, sort the population by the paper’s normalized utility measure, divide it into \(K\) equal-mass quantile buckets, and split a type’s mass when a bucket boundary passes through it. In each bucket, perturb that candidate’s utility by \(+\epsilon\) or \(-\epsilon\), holding the poll fixed as in the paper. Recompute ballots and tabulate the resulting fractional ballot masses, using the paper’s tie rule.

The task is to output the continuous UCI for each bucket, using the same four signed winner-change events as Definition 4; then normalize to the CI vector of Definition 5 and compute its EMU as in Definition 6. In the finite scenario model, these are exact rational outputs. This is an evaluation problem, not an optimization problem: the quantity being computed is how much each voter quantile affects candidates’ chances of winning.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large election with many voters in a small number of political blocs, where members of each bloc share normalized candidate evaluations and voting behavior. There could be millions of voters and only dozens of such types. The paper itself uses \(n=72\), but reports nearly identical results at \(n=720\); its definition also interprets CID, in the limit, as a density over voter attitudes. Those points make a population limit recognizable as an extension of the authors’ metric, rather than an unrelated outcome-space or axiomatic continuization.

For explicit finite scenario lists, I expect this evaluation problem to be tractable whenever the voting rule can tabulate weighted ballots in polynomial time: sort the types, split masses at bucket boundaries, and run the tabulation for each candidate, bucket, perturbation, and scenario. EMU is a polynomial-size transport problem. With a sampler for a population model, additive estimates can also be obtained by sampling, much as the paper already does. A more interesting complexity question would require a compact representation of a continuous spatial voter model and an exact or guaranteed-accuracy computation of its CID.

This would cover the paper’s comparisons of CID profiles and EMU across voting methods, including its finding that Minimax and STAR are more balanced than IRV in the reported settings. It would not cover the paper’s claims about actual political moderation or conflict outcomes.

The main weakness is that the finite-type version smooths away a defining feature of the paper’s clustered spatial model: voters have personalized, continuously varying utilities. Approximation by a finite utility grid might change winner flips at thresholds, so stability would need study. More fundamentally, the proposed computation may be routine under explicit finite inputs. The paper supplies a promising continuous *object* to compute, but no named hardness or algorithmic result that makes its computation a substantial complexity-theoretic mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Definitions 4–6 define UCI, CI, and EMU, and the reported comparisons are simulation findings. None is a numbered computational-complexity result. The proponent acknowledges this, so their proposed Continuous CID Evaluation is a new formalization of the paper’s metric, not a continuous mirror of a named result.

The finite-scenario version is coherent: split type masses at bucket boundaries, tabulate the baseline and perturbed elections, count the winner changes, then compute EMU. But with an explicit scenario list, this is exact evaluation of the definitions by repeated weighted tabulation. That does not make it ill-posed; it means the proposal has not identified a substantial computational question in the paper’s findings for continuization to illuminate. The complexity of a version with a compact spatial voter model would depend on how that model and its randomness are encoded, and on integrating discontinuous winner-change events. That could become a worthwhile new problem, but it is not an algorithmic result this paper gives us to mirror.

The strongest version of the mirror would define a limiting CID as a sensitivity of win probability to shifting an infinitesimal mass of voters in a utility quantile. That is more faithful than the finite-scenario proposal, but it needs randomness over societies or near-tie elections to avoid degeneration: at a fixed nonatomic society with a unique winner, a vanishing-mass perturbation leaves the winner unchanged. Keeping positive-mass buckets avoids that problem, but measures the effect of moving a bloc, with results depending on bucket size. A randomized boundary-sensitivity formulation could be coherent, yet it is a new asymptotic model whose limiting behavior must be specified; it does not supply the missing computational theorem.

I would not claim that every continuous CID formulation is nonsensical. The paper’s buckets and its limit interpretation give a credible starting point for a bloc-level or boundary-sensitivity study. That is where the negative case is weakest. But under the requested standard of mirroring this paper’s computational results, no anchor survives: the article reports empirical incentive distributions, not computational claims, and the proposed evaluation problem does not fill that gap by itself.
