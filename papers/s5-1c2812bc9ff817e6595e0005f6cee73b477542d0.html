The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is qualified: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. It proves existence, nonexistence, and fairness-implication theorems, but no theorem stating NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), FPT, or a comparable algorithmic classification. Thus it has no formally eligible anchor under the programme’s strict rule. I would not pretend otherwise.

The best substantive candidate is the proposition labelled \(\texttt{lem:n=6k}\) (the supplied source exposes the label rather than a displayed numeral). Proved in the paper, it states:

\[ \text{There is no fully-balanced permutation sequence of length }n=6k \text{ for every }k\ge 2. \]

The stronger theorem labelled \(\texttt{thm:finitely-many-balanced}\) proves that only finitely many \(n\) admit fully-balanced sequences, and none admits one when \(n>61\). These are feasibility results, not hardness results, but they give a meaningful boundary for a continuous mirror.

I would define the following problem.

Continuous Full-Balance Feasibility. Fix \(q\) item-ranks and \(q\) days. The population is atomless with total mass \(1\). A type is a complete worker profile—here, one common ordinal ranking over the \(q\) picking rights—and its mass is \(\mu_\theta\); the lead case has one type of mass \(1\). On every day \(d\), each right \(j\in[q]\) is assigned to mass \(1/q\) of the population, and every individual receives exactly one integral right.

Equivalently, let \(x_p\) be the mass assigned to the integral trajectory \(p=(p_1,\ldots,p_q)\in[q]^q\). Then

\[ x_p\ge 0,\qquad \sum_p x_p=1,\qquad \sum_{p:p_d=j}x_p=\frac1q \quad\text{for every }d,j. \]

Let \(p^{\uparrow}_{1:d}[r]\) denote the \(r\)-th smallest right in the first \(d\) entries of \(p\). The question is whether there is such an \(x\) whose positive-mass trajectories all satisfy

\[ p^{\uparrow}_{1:d}[r]\le \left\lceil\frac{rq}{d}\right\rceil \qquad \text{for every }d\in[q],\ r\in[d]. \]

A solution is the mass distribution \(x\), or equivalently a measurable partition of the population into integral trajectories. This is not fractionalizing an individual’s bundle: every individual receives one whole right per day. Only indistinguishable population mass is divisible.

The natural regime is a large recurring workforce: \(Hq\) workers, \(H\) interchangeable copies of each rank-class of daily duty, and \(q\) days, with \(H\gg q\) and only a small number of worker types. This is a sensible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ picking-sequence model. The paper’s discussion of picking rights and its heterogeneous-valuation appendix make this interpretation especially natural.

I would expect the homogeneous version to be a plausible Class A problem. The finite obstruction in \(\texttt{lem:n=6k}\) uses integral pigeonhole arguments among named players; mass splitting may remove precisely that obstruction. The formulation is a finite LP over trajectories, though its exponentially many columns mean that a real result would require a pricing or flow algorithm. Whether the pricing problem is polynomial is the main open computational question generated by the mirror.

The easier top-balance theorem, labelled \(\texttt{lem:top-slots}\), says that for every positive integer \(n\), an \(n\times n\) Latin square that is top-balanced exists; it is proved in the paper using Latin-rectangle completion. Its continuous version is immediately feasible by placing mass \(1/n\) on each row of that square. That gives a clean Class A construction, but it is weaker and less novel.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper does not itself supply a computational anchor. Moreover, the continuous model introduces per-capita copies of rank-classes and therefore needs the programme’s allowed co-modification of supplies. So this is a credible future continuization of the paper’s feasibility problem, but not a fully compliant positive case under the stated anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at the programme’s gate: this paper contains no qualifying computational result. Its named results concern existence, nonexistence, and implications between fairness notions. The proposition for \(n=6k\), the theorem ruling out fully balanced sequences for \(n>61\), and the top-balance theorem are feasibility statements, not complexity, approximation, parameterized, or algorithmic results. The backtracking experiments and the open question about \(c\)-balance do not change that. The hardness and polynomial-time claims in the related-work section concern other papers and other problems.

The proposed continuous full-balance problem is mathematically coherent, but it does not repair this defect. In

\[ x_p=\text{mass assigned to complete trajectory }p, \]

the index \(p\) is not a pre-existing voter or worker type. It is the entire assignment history that the algorithm is choosing. The paper has no meaningful exogenous population distribution: players have the same ordinal ranking, and their only relevant distinction is which history they receive. Thus \(\mu\) is either a single homogeneous type or is replaced by types defined by the output itself. The \(x_p\)'s describe a convexified schedule, not a continuous society.

The proposed large-workforce interpretation does not overcome this. Replicating each rank \(H\) times produces a sensible high-multiplicity scheduling problem, but it changes the source problem from finding \(q\) rows of one \(q\times q\) table to finding an arbitrary distribution over complete rows. That may be a worthwhile new combinatorial-design problem; it is not a continuous-population version of a computational theorem in this paper. Introducing genuine type-dependent restrictions, availability, costs, or objectives could make the population matter, but those would be new modelling ingredients rather than continuizations of the paper’s results.

The exponentially large LP therefore does not provide an anchor. Its pricing problem is an interesting question invented by the proposed mirror, not a computational question exposed by the paper. Under the programme’s rules, one cannot turn a pure existence theorem into an eligible computational mirror merely by placing its feasible schedules in a configuration LP.

The top-balance theorem fares worse. The paper already constructs a top-balanced Latin square for every \(q\). Putting mass \(1/q\) on its rows gives an immediate continuous witness. There is no input population distribution, type-dependent intervention, or computational problem left to continuize. Asking for optimization or type-specific feasibility would again mean designing a new problem around the theorem, not mirroring it.

The PROP1 implications do not offer a better rescue. They are pointwise ordinal statements proved from the same schedule; replacing the finite set of players by an atomless mass preserves the quantifiers but introduces no computational population object. Replacing them by average fairness would be a different fairness notion.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer happens to be easy, nor that high-multiplicity work is already known. The objection is prior: the paper supplies no computational anchor, and its proposed continuous formulation makes the assignment history—not the society—the distributed object. A high-multiplicity scheduling paper could certainly be written from these ideas, but this paper does not contain a worthwhile continuization for the ChoCo programme.
