The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of patient types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita baseline utilities \(\alpha_t\), treatment gains \(q_t\), costs \(c_t\), budget \(b\), and threshold \(\Delta\), choose \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\) to maximize \(W_\Delta(y)=\Delta+u_{\min}(y)+\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(u_t(y)-u_{\min}(y)-\Delta\bigr)^+\), where \(u_t(y)=\alpha_t+q_ty_t\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c_t y_t\le b\) and any specified policy constraints. Determine an optimal treatment vector and its welfare value.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population of homogeneous patient types with masses \(\mu_t\); cohort-wide binary treatment decisions \(y_t\); type utilities \(u_t=\alpha_t+q_ty_t\); a per-capita budget; and maximization of the mass-weighted equity-utilitarian welfare function \(W_\Delta\).

The objection that survived

The mass instances are polynomially equivalent to the paper’s integer-sized group instances, so the mirror adds no distinct computational regime; this limits novelty but does not remove the continuous-population analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous-population version of the paper’s multi-group health-resource allocation problem. The paper has no named theorem asserting that this problem is in P, NP-hard, or in a parameterized class. Its strongest relevant computational anchor is instead the formulation result the authors actually compute with.

My sole anchor is Theorem 4, proved in the paper’s appendix. It concerns \(g\) groups, with integer sizes \(n_i\), per-capita utilities \(u_i\), and \(u_{\min}=\min_i u_i\). The welfare objective is to maximize \(z\) subject to
\[ z\le \left(\sum_i n_i-1\right)\Delta +\left(\sum_i n_i\right)u_{\min} +\sum_i n_i\bigl(u_i-u_{\min}-\Delta\bigr)^+, \]
along with utility-spread, nonnegativity, resource, and policy constraints. Theorem 4 says the paper’s MILP formulation (19) for this multi-group welfare model is sharp: its LP relaxation projects onto the convex hull of the welfare model’s feasible set, before additional policy constraints are imposed. That formulation introduces variables \(v_i,w,\delta_i\) and enforces
\[ \begin{aligned} z&\le \left(\sum_i n_i-1\right)\Delta+\sum_i n_i v_i,\\ u_i-\Delta&\le v_i\le u_i-\Delta\delta_i,\\ w&\le v_i\le w+(M-\Delta)\delta_i,\\ u_i&\ge 0,\qquad \delta_i\in\{0,1\}. \end{aligned} \]
The preceding formulation result, Theorem 3, establishes that (19) correctly represents the welfare model. The paper then uses the formulation in its health-care application and reports computations on instances with thousands of groups. These are modeling and computational results, not a polynomial-time guarantee for the full allocation problem.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. Let \(T\) be a finite set of patient types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type records all attributes relevant to the allocation decision: baseline QALYs \(\alpha_t\), per-patient QALY gain \(q_t\), treatment cost \(c_t\), and policy eligibility. For a per-capita budget \(b\) and threshold \(\Delta\), choose \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\), where the policy either treats the entire cohort of type \(t\) or treats none of it. Its per-capita utility is \(u_t(y)=\alpha_t+q_ty_t\), and the budget constraint is
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c_t y_t\le b. \]
Writing \(u_{\min}(y)=\min_{t\in T}u_t(y)\), maximize
\[ W_\Delta(y)=\Delta+u_{\min}(y) +\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(u_t(y)-u_{\min}(y)-\Delta\bigr)^+. \]
A solution is a feasible treatment vector \(y\) attaining the maximum, with its welfare value. Further rational policy constraints can be included as in the paper.

The regime is a large health system choosing policy across many patients and a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of clinically and economically homogeneous cohorts. Patients of a type share prognosis, treatment gain and cost, and eligibility; mass is their share of the population. Cohort-wide treatment preserves the paper’s group-allocation assumption. The population may be very large relative to \(\tau\), as when a policy applies to many thousands of patients in each of a few prognosis and eligibility classes.

This is an especially direct high-multiplicity mirror. If the paper’s groups have total size \(N=\sum_i n_i\), set \(\mu_i=n_i/N\) and \(b=B/N\). Its finite-population welfare divided by \(N\) differs from \(W_\Delta\) by the constant \(\Delta/N\), so the maximizing treatment plans are identical. Every rational mass vector has a common-denominator integer representation of this form. That identity is useful evidence that the question preserves the authors’ problem; it also keeps the claim modest. The mirror covers Theorem 4’s multi-group welfare model and the health-care allocation application, not the paper’s broader philosophical discussion or its question of how to choose \(\Delta\).

I expect hardness to transfer, rather than a general tractability gain. At \(\Delta=0\), \(W_0\) is simply average utility, so choosing treatments under a budget contains 0–1 knapsack: type choices are the items, with \(\mu_t c_t\) as weight and \(\mu_t q_t\) as value. Duplicating every cohort by the same factor makes the patient population arbitrarily large without changing the masses or the choices. This is hardness in the treatment options across types, not in population size. It is a deduction about the mirror, not a hardness theorem proved by this paper. Natural follow-up questions are whether useful restrictions on costs, gains, or policy constraints give tractable cases, and how the complexity depends on \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

The soft spot is that the paper already represents groups with integer sizes and solves the resulting MILP. Normalizing those sizes into masses is therefore a close high-multiplicity reformulation, not a dramatic new source of tractability. Also, Theorem 4’s sharpness concerns the welfare formulation before extra constraints; it does not make the full treatment-allocation problem easy. I still consider the mirror sound: the paper treats group size as substantive in its health model, and it leaves open the computational question of the welfare allocation problem when those group sizes are given directly as rational population shares.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the proponent’s only anchor, but it proves a formulation property: the group welfare model has a sharp MILP formulation. The paper gives no numbered result about the complexity of solving the full allocation problem. That alone would not rule out a new complexity question; the difficulty is that the proposed mass version has the same instances as the paper’s group model after normalization.

Let \(N=\sum_i n_i\) and \(\mu_i=n_i/N\). The paper’s welfare bound is
\[ F(u)= (N-1)\Delta+Nu_{\min}+\sum_i n_i(u_i-u_{\min}-\Delta)^+. \]
Dividing by \(N\) gives
\[ \frac{F(u)}N=(1-1/N)\Delta+u_{\min}+\sum_i\mu_i(u_i-u_{\min}-\Delta)^+. \]
That differs from the proposed \(W_\Delta\) only by the allocation-independent constant \(\Delta/N\). With the budget also divided by \(N\), the feasible treatment plans and their ordering are unchanged. Conversely, any rational mass vector has a common-denominator integer representation. These conversions have polynomial-size binary encodings. Thus the mass formulation does not create a distinct computational problem: it is the paper’s weighted-group model in per-capita units. Positive scaling and shifting the welfare variable also preserve the sharpness claim, so Theorem 4 transfers directly.

The stronger version—allowing a continuous range of patient attributes or treating a fraction of each type—does not rescue this anchor. A continuum of attributes requires new assumptions about how costs, health gains and policy constraints vary across types; fractional treatment changes the paper’s cohort-wide treatment decision. Either could motivate a separate allocation problem, but neither continues Theorem 4’s finite-group sharpness result. The health-care example and the group formulation therefore support that large cohorts are a sensible regime, but they do not supply a new continuous result to prove.

The negative case is strongest as a novelty objection, not as a claim that patient populations cannot sensibly be modeled by mass. If ChoCo treats a per-capita restatement of an already scale-weighted group model as a worthwhile mirror, this case weakens.
