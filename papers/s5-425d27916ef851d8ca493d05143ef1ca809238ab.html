The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result: no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic classification. The authors explicitly say they offer “no general theorems.” Its numbered equations are equilibrium conditions, not computational results. So there is no eligible anchor under the rule in the prompt.

The strongest foothold for a positive case is the unnumbered large-committee finding in Section 3. The paper considers the agenda \(a\) versus \(b\), followed by the winner versus \(c\), and three voter types: \(T_1\) ranks \(a>b>c\), \(T_2\) ranks \(b>c>a\), and \(T_3\) ranks \(c>a>b\). With odd committee size \(n\), independent type probabilities \(q_1,q_2,q_3>0\), and middle-option utility \(v\), it derives that for sufficiently large \(n\), the only symmetric pure-strategy equilibrium has \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) vote for \(b\) first, while \(T_3\) votes for \(a\). If \(q_1+q_2>1/2\), \(b\) then wins with probability approaching one; yet \(a\) can be the Condorcet winner with probability approaching one as \(q_1\) approaches one. This is a real high-multiplicity phenomenon, but it is an analytic equilibrium result, not an anchor of the required kind.

A possible follow-up—not a compliant mirror of a named result—would be Asymptotic SVE Outcome Computation. An instance would give a finite set of alternatives, a finite support \(T\) of complete preference-and-utility types, a rational prior \(\mu\) over those types, and a fixed amendment agenda. For each odd \(n\), \(n\) voters draw types independently from \(\mu\), know their own type, and observe the open ballots. The question is to compute the set of limiting outcome distributions induced by symmetric pure-strategy strategic voting equilibria as \(n\to\infty\); a solution would give an exact description of that set and, for a specified alternative, whether its probability is zero in every limit distribution. A plausible regime is a large legislative body with many exchangeable members and few distinct preference types. The paper’s three-type example gives a tractable restricted case; it provides no basis for classifying the general problem.

That candidate is also where the positive case is weakest. In a deterministic atomless population, an individual vote has zero effect on the outcome. The paper’s incentives instead depend on conditioning on being pivotal, however unlikely that is, and on how ballot histories update beliefs. The asymptotic finite-committee formulation retains that mechanism, but leaves open whether the authors would regard it as their continuous-population problem. I would not present it as an anchor-based continuous mirror under the stated rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no eligible computational result to mirror. Its contributions are equilibrium claims about finite Bayesian voting games: incomplete information can change outcomes, extra agenda stages can matter, and straw polls can affect later votes. The authors explicitly offer “no general theorems”; none of their results classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithm. The large-\(n\) Condorcet example is a substantial analytical result, but it is unnumbered and not computational. Under the requested anchoring rules, it cannot carry a case for a continuous computational mirror.

The proposed “Asymptotic SVE Outcome Computation” is a possible new research question, but its best version exposes a basic difficulty. In a direct atomless population, one voter’s action changes neither the outcome nor the aggregate ballot signal. The incentive comparisons in the paper instead condition on being pivotal, using beliefs about the types of the other voters in that rare event. A model that keeps only the mass distribution \(\mu\) loses that individual influence. Preserving it requires a finite-\(n\) or rare-event scaling analysis, so the computational object is a sequence of Bayesian games, not simply a computation over a continuous society.

That objection is strongest against a direct mirror, and weaker against the asymptotic proposal. Large committees with a small set of preference types are a natural high-multiplicity regime, and computing limiting equilibrium outcomes could be worthwhile in its own right. So I would reject the proposal as an anchored mirror of this paper’s results, but I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population question can arise from it. The negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational result to mirror and on the loss of pivotal incentives in the direct continuum; it is vulnerable to a carefully specified asymptotic formulation.
