The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational arrival shares \(\alpha_t\) summing to \(1\), sojourn durations \(\ell_t \ge \epsilon\), degree parameter \(d \ge 2\), and tolerance \(\delta > 0\), consider arrivals at rate \(m\), with type \(t\) arriving at rate \(m\alpha_t\), and independent pairwise compatibility probability \(d/m\). In the continuous type-and-age mass model for GDY with uniform partner choice, compute the long-run fraction of arrival mass that expires unmatched to additive error at most \(\delta\).

The model it lives in

A type-and-age mass process with type-\(t\) arrival share \(\alpha_t\), deadline \(\ell_t\), and sparse contacts governed by \(d\). If total pool mass is \(Q\), the candidate dynamics send an arriving fraction \(e^{-dQ}\) into the pool and remove incumbent mass at per-capita hazard \(h=(1-e^{-dQ})/Q\), with its continuous extension at \(Q=0\); the objective is the long-run fraction of arrivals that expire unmatched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: mirror the paper’s analysis of greedy matching, not the whole dynamic matching problem.

There is no named theorem here classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The paper does, however, prove numbered algorithm-performance results. My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: for any \(\epsilon>0\), if every agent’s sojourn time is at least \(\epsilon\), then for \(d\ge 2\) the limiting loss of GDY satisfies \(L_{\mathrm{GDY}}\le \exp(-\epsilon d/(2\ln 2))\). The paper also says this holds when agents have different sojourn distributions, provided each agent has the same minimum-sojourn guarantee. This is a performance bound, not a complexity classification.

A plausible mirror is Continuous GDY Loss. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, rational arrival proportions \(\alpha_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational sojourn duration \(\ell_t\ge\epsilon\) for each type. Type \(t\) completely describes an agent’s role in the model; agents of that type share the same sojourn duration and compatibility behavior. In a market with arrival rate \(m\), type \(t\) arrives at rate \(m\alpha_t\), and every pair is independently compatible with probability \(d/m\). The regime is \(m\to\infty\) with \(|T|=\tau\) moderate, so many agents share each type while each agent still has only a bounded expected number of compatible peers.

Normalize pool counts by \(m\). Let \(q(t)\) be the resulting pool mass at time \(t\). A new arrival sees a Poisson limit with mean \(dq(t)\) for its number of compatible incumbents, so a fraction \(\exp(-dq(t))\) of arriving mass joins the pool; the rest matches an incumbent. Under GDY, the incumbent is uniform in the pool. Thus matching removes pool mass at total rate \(1-\exp(-dq(t))\), distributed across the existing type-and-age mass, while that mass ages and expires at its deadline. The action is this mass flow under GDY; the objective is the long-run fraction of arrival mass that expires unmatched.

The problem is: given \(T\), \((\alpha_t,\ell_t)_{t\in T}\), \(d\), and an additive tolerance \(\delta>0\), compute the continuous model’s long-run GDY loss to within \(\delta\). A solution is a certified approximation to that loss. Theorem 4.1 is the anchor because it predicts that, when all \(\ell_t\ge\epsilon\), the answer is at most \(\exp(-\epsilon d/(2\ln 2))\). It does not establish that the fluid equations below give the exact limit or provide an algorithm for computing the loss.

I would initially expect this fixed-policy evaluation problem to be Class A, at least for approximation: the sparse compatibility randomness becomes a Poisson contact rate, and the pool’s type-and-age profile appears to give a closed transport system. A key step is to prove that the finite markets converge to that system over the long horizon used in the loss definition, then show its long-run behavior can be computed effectively. This is a conjecture about the mirror, not a complexity result in the paper. Natural follow-up questions are whether the fluid system has a unique stable stationary profile, whether its loss can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(\log(1/\delta)\), and how far the formulation extends to optimal policies or patient matching.

The authors would likely recognize this as their problem: it preserves their sparse compatibility scaling, deadline-driven departures, GDY policy, and unmatched-agent objective. It covers Theorem 4.1 and the stated heterogeneous-sojourn extension, not the patient-policy or waiting-time results.

The weak point is that the paper already defines its loss through a large-market limit, so a fluid reformulation could look like a repackaging unless it yields a distinct, computable mass-dynamics model. Also, sparse compatibility means each agent has only finitely many neighbors on average; aggregate masses may not capture enough information for policies beyond GDY. That is why I would make only this narrow case: under GDY, uniform partner choice and the Poisson contact limit give a credible route to a type-and-age mass model, while the paper itself provides no complexity-class anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 4.1, including its heterogeneous-sojourn extension. It bounds GDY’s loss; it gives no algorithm for computing that loss and makes no complexity claim. The proposed mirror adds a tolerance \(\delta\) and asks for a numerical approximation to a fixed policy’s long-run performance. That is a coherent question, but it sits outside ChoCo’s stated gap: complexity and algorithms for computational problems over continuous societies. The paper already defines \(L_{\mathrm{GDY}}\) through the large-\(m,T\) limit, so the proposed mass dynamics would recast the quantity the paper studies rather than introduce a new population-level optimization problem.

The fluid formulation is plausible, which makes this a scope argument rather than a modeling objection. For finitely many duration classes with proportions \(\alpha_t\) and durations \(\ell_t\), let \(Q\) be stationary pool mass and \(h\) the removal hazard for an incumbent. Under the proposed closure, \(h=(1-e^{-dQ})/Q\), and the age profile for class \(t\) is \(f_t(a)=\alpha_t e^{-dQ}e^{-ha}\) up to age \(\ell_t\). Thus \(Q=\sum_t \alpha_t e^{-dQ}(1-e^{-h\ell_t})/h\), while the loss is \(e^{-dQ}\sum_t\alpha_t e^{-h\ell_t}\). Establishing convergence and stability could be worthwhile fluid-limit analysis. Its computational task, though, is to evaluate the asymptotic performance measure already defined by the paper; it does not expose the kind of complexity landscape the programme targets.

A stronger attempted mirror might start from Theorem 4.12 and ask for optimal continuous matching policies. The theorem’s lower bound has a direct mass-level reading, but optimizing over policies changes the question. GDY’s symmetric random tie-breaking supports an aggregate age-and-type description; policies that use compatibility information may depend on the evolving sparse graph, whose state is not captured by those masses. A richer model might track distributions of local graph states, but that would be a new dynamic-control problem, not a continuization of the theorem’s computational result.

The case against is weak at the modelling level: repeated cohorts and urgency classes are credible high-multiplicity scenarios, and a fluid limit could be a useful result in its own right. Its strongest point is narrower: this paper supplies a policy-performance theorem for an asymptotic stochastic market, not a complexity result for a computational problem. If ChoCo counts the computation of limiting performance as an adequate anchor, the proposed mirror survives; the objection is that doing so broadens the programme from computational complexity into mean-field analysis.
