The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and tertiary utility vectors \(u_t\in\{-1,0,1\}^m\), determine whether there is a finite-support allocation \(x_{t,k}\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\) satisfying sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\) and sum_{t,k} k_j x_{t,k}=q_j\) for every item category \(j\), and output one if so. Require EFX3: for each pair of positive-mass cohorts \((t,k)\), \((t',\ell)\), and each \(S\in\{[m],G,B\}\), apply the paper's EFX inequalities to the bundles restricted to \(S\), evaluated using \(u_t\), where \(G=\{j:\exists t,\ u_{tj}>0\}\) and \(B=\{j:\forall t,\ u_{tj}<0\}\). Require Pareto optimality under type-preserving couplings from current to new integral bundles that preserve each type's mass and all supplies: every positively coupled reassignment must weakly improve that type's utility, and a positive mass must improve strictly. Analyze the computation in \(m\), tau\), and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with type masses mu_t\), where each person receives an integral bundle of item categories and the aggregate per-capita supplies are q_j\). The allocation is a finite-support measure over type-bundle pairs; fairness compares every pair of represented cohorts, and efficiency rules out any type-preserving, supply-conserving Pareto improvement.

The objection that survived

Finite Pareto optimality need not imply Pareto optimality under a continuum coupling, so denominator expansion does not establish existence of the proposed continuum solution.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the tertiary-utility EFX and Pareto-optimal guarantees of Theorems 1 and 2, with Theorem 2 as the anchor. It leaves aside the general-utility impossibility examples, welfare guarantees, and the identical-utility results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can EFX3 and coupling-based Pareto optimality be decided and an allocation returned in time polynomial in \(m\), tau\), and \(L\), with polynomially bounded output support?
  • Does finite EFX3 and Pareto-optimal existence extend to the coupling-based continuum notion, or can splitting mass create a Pareto improvement unavailable in every finite expansion?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the fair allocation of a large population with a small number of complete utility types. Think of a regional service or food bank serving hundreds of thousands of people, where recipients fall into a few stable preference or need profiles and inventory grows with the population. Some item categories may be beneficial to some types, harmful to others, or neutral. The key is that an individual still receives a whole bundle of indivisible items; only the mass of people receiving each bundle becomes continuous.

Inventory must scale with population for this regime to be meaningful. Normalize population mass to \(1\). Let \(T\) be the \(\tau\) represented types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and let \(m\) item categories have rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\). Type \(t\) has a complete utility vector \(u_t\in\{-1,0,1\}^m\), normalizing the paper’s common tertiary utility scale. A bundle is an integral vector \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\), with utility \(u_t(k)=\sum_j k_j u_{tj}\). An allocation is a finite-support list of masses \(x_{t,k}\ge 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,k} k_jx_{t,k}=q_j\quad\text{for every }j. \]
Thus \(x_{t,k}\) assigns a fraction of type \(t\) to the whole bundle \(k\); it does not give anyone a fractional item. For rational inputs, choose a population size \(N\) making \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nq_j\) integral. The resulting finite instance has \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) copies of item \(j\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that with tertiary additive utilities, Nash-Max-Tertiary returns an EFX and Pareto-optimal allocation. The paper reports polynomial running time \(O(2m(n+1)\ln(nm))\) for the tertiary-utility algorithms in this section; its proof uses the existing Alg-Binary algorithm as a subroutine.

That yields the continuous problem Tertiary Continuous EFX-PO Allocation: given \((T,\mu)\), the utility vectors \(u_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q\), return a finite-support \(x\) satisfying the mass and supply equations above, EFX, and Pareto optimality, or certify that none exists. There is no scalar welfare objective: the output is an allocation with both properties. For EFX, compare every pair of positive-mass cohorts \((t,k)\) and \((t',\ell)\), evaluating both bundles using the evaluator’s type \(t\). For each \(j\) with \(u_{tj}<0\) and \(k_j>0\), require \(u_t(k-e_j)\ge u_t(\ell)\); for each \(j\) with \(u_{tj}>0\) and \(\ell_j>0\), require \(u_t(k)\ge u_t(\ell-e_j)\).

Here Pareto optimality means that no feasible reassignment of the same item supplies makes almost every person weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off. More formally, a reassignment can couple each type’s current bundles to its new bundles; every positive-mass pair in that coupling must be weakly improving, and a positive mass must improve strictly. This preserves the paper’s individual-bundle notion of fairness and efficiency while expressing the population as a measure.

My second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. With tertiary additive utilities, Nash-Max-Min-Tertiary returns an EFX3 and Pareto-optimal allocation. EFX3 requires EFX for all items, for the mixed goods, and for the pure bads, each considered as its own suballocation. It is a genuinely stronger guarantee than Theorem 1’s EFX.

That gives Tertiary Continuous EFX3-PO Allocation on the same inputs: return a finite-support allocation measure satisfying the mass and supply equations, EFX3 on those three item sets, and Pareto optimality. If \(G=\{j:\exists t,\ u_{tj}>0\}\) and \(B=\{j:\forall t,\ u_{tj}<0\}\), the additional EFX3 requirements are EFX when bundles are restricted to \(G\), and EFX when restricted to \(B\). This asks whether the stronger fairness guarantee survives when cohorts have continuous mass, while every recipient still gets an integral bundle.

I expect both compressed problems to be Class A candidates. Their mass and supply constraints are linear, the number of utility types is \(\tau\), and tertiary utilities give substantial structure for balancing goods and bads. Configuration LPs and column generation are natural tools to investigate. For rational inputs, existence is already inherited from the paper’s finite theorems through the denominator expansion above. That expansion is not an efficient compressed algorithm in general: its population size can be exponential in the binary input length. The open computational question is whether one can return a succinct allocation in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. EFX3 is the harder version because its simultaneous requirements on three item sets may constrain which bundle types can have positive mass.

The paper’s numbered results that fit the anchor requirement are algorithmic; it offers no comparable numbered hardness result of its own. Its remarks about hardness of exact Nash-welfare maximization cite earlier work, but that is a different underlying problem and not an anchor for these allocation questions. I would therefore lead with the positive algorithmic results, not stretch a welfare-hardness citation into one.

The weakest point is that the finite algorithms do not automatically give a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and \(L\). They process individual agents and items, whereas the continuous input compresses many copies into rational masses. Also, replacing integral bundles with average fractional bundles would make the constraints easier but would change the question: it would continuize outcomes rather than the population. The mirror stands only with the integral-bundle condition intact. The further questions are whether the inherited existence result has a polynomial-size rational witness, whether EFX and EFX3 admit compressed algorithms, and where those algorithms stop working as the utility domain expands beyond tertiary values.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proponent’s claim that the finite theorems give continuous existence by clearing denominators. They do give finite allocations whose mass distributions satisfy EFX and EFX3. But finite Pareto optimality need not survive the move to a continuum: a continuum reassignment can move an arbitrarily small positive mass of people between whole-bundle assignments, while any one finite expansion has a minimum population increment. So the proposed argument does not establish that the resulting mass allocation is Pareto optimal under the stated continuum definition. This gap affects both Theorem 1 and Theorem 2.

That is a real weakness in the case as stated, but it does not defeat the better mirror. One can ask directly for a mass allocation with continuum Pareto optimality, and whether it can be computed from the compressed rational input. Theorems 1 and 2 are genuine algorithmic anchors; calling them non-computational would be wrong. Nor do the usual objections work here: large populations with repeated complete utility types and supplies that scale with population are plausible, and EFX remains meaningful when recipients get whole bundles. EFX3 adds another well-defined constraint.

The negative case is therefore weak. The paper’s algorithms do not automatically solve the continuum problems, and the continuum Pareto condition may make them substantially different from the finite theorems. But that leaves a legitimate computational question; it does not show that either mirror is unworthy of the programme. I would challenge the claimed inheritance of existence and the suggested easy Class A path, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives.
