The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), an integer generalized scoring rule \(GS(f,g)\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types \(T=L(C)\), and a target \(c\), find the minimum \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\) such that \(x_{t,t'}\ge 0\), \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\), and \(g\!\left(\operatorname{Ord}\!\left(\sum_t\mu_t f(t)+\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}(f(t')-f(t))\right)\right)=c\), or report \(\infty\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete vote types, with mass-transfer variables \(x_{t,t'}\) converting type \(t\) to \(t'\); minimize total changed mass subject to the target winning under the resulting aggregate generalized-score vector.

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers minimum constructive operation mass, especially constructive opt-bribery, and can extend to destructive and change-winner variants; it leaves the paper's probabilistic fluctuation classification, Gaussian second-order behavior, and detailed control applications unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • What rounding and additive-error bounds relate the continuous optimum to finite high-multiplicity opt-bribery instances?
  • When the number of alternatives varies or the GSR is succinctly represented, does the mass-transfer problem remain tractable, or does its pricing problem become hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a continuous version of the paper’s constructive vote-operation problem, with constructive opt-bribery as its most natural concrete instantiation. The paper contains no NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result, so there is no hardness anchor to prefer. Its best anchor is the main quantitative computational result.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, for an integer generalized scoring rule \(GS(f,g)\), a vote-operation set \(\Delta\), a distribution \(\pi\) over linear orders, and a fixed number of alternatives, if \(n\) votes are drawn independently from \(\pi\), then for every alternative \(c\), every \(VO\in\{\mathrm{CVO},\mathrm{DVO},\mathrm{CWVO}\}\), and every \(\epsilon>0\), there is a constant \(\beta^\ast>1\) such that, with probability tending above \(1-\epsilon\) as \(n\to\infty\), the minimum number of operations is either \(0\), between \(\frac{1}{\beta^\ast}\sqrt n\) and \(\beta^\ast\sqrt n\), between \(\frac{1}{\beta^\ast}n\) and \(\beta^\ast n\), or \(\infty\). The proof combines the central limit theorem with sensitivity results for the integer programs encoding the possible generalized-score preorders.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\[ \mathrm{CVO}_\infty: \]

An instance consists of candidates \(C\), an integer GSR \(GS(f,g)\) of order \(K\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over voter types \(T=L(C)\), a target candidate \(c\), and operation vectors \(\Delta=\{\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_q\}\subseteq\mathbb Z^K\). The type distribution satisfies \(\mu_t\ge 0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Define

\[ F_\mu=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f(t). \]

A solution is a vector \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^q\), where \(x_j\) is the mass of agents applying operation \(\delta_j\). The objective is to minimize

\[ \|x\|_1=\sum_{j=1}^q x_j \]

subject to

\[ g\!\left(\operatorname{Ord}\!\left(F_\mu+\sum_{j=1}^q x_j\delta_j\right)\right)=c. \]

If no such \(x\) exists, the value is \(\infty\). The output is the minimum operation mass and a witnessing mass vector \(x\). For the paper’s opt-bribery specialization, \(x_{t,t'}\) is the mass transferred from ranking \(t\) to ranking \(t'\), with

\[ \sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le \mu_t, \]

and the objective is \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\).

This is recognisably the authors’ problem, not a softened substitute. A finite profile with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) has \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); an integral operation vector \(v\) becomes the normalized mass vector \(x=v/n\). The only change is replacing individual counts by real mass. The same candidates, rankings, generalized scoring vectors, target, and operations remain in place.

A plausible regime is a large election with, say, \(10^6\) voters and five or six candidates. There are at most \(m!\) ranking types—\(120\) when \(m=5\)—and perhaps only a few dozen occur in practice, with thousands or tens of thousands of voters per type. A type includes every feature relevant to the operation: ranking, eligibility, and bribe price if prices vary. Thus the model does not pretend that individually different voters have identical costs; it refines the type space until equal-treatment is true.

The paper’s i.i.d. distribution \(\pi\) is especially suggestive here: \(\mu\) is the deterministic population-level object around which its random finite profiles fluctuate. But this is not a novelty collision. The paper studies finite random profiles and the asymptotic scale of their integer operation counts; it does not give the deterministic continuous-population optimization problem above.

For fixed \(m\) and an explicitly represented type space, I expect \(\mathrm{CVO}_\infty\) to be Class A. Fixing a generalized-score preorder produces linear equalities and inequalities in \(x\), so the problem becomes a finite family of linear programs. For ordinary scoring rules, the winning constraints are directly linear. The more interesting computational question is whether one can solve the problem polynomially when \(m\) varies or when the operation set and GSR are represented succinctly. That is precisely where separation and pricing issues can create a genuine boundary.

The bridge to Theorem 1 is informative but not exact at every scale. If the finite optimum is \(k_n\), then the normalized operation mass is \(k_n/n\). The theorem’s \(0\) and \(\Theta(n)\) cases become, respectively, zero mass and positive macroscopic mass. Its \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\) case collapses to mass of order \(1/\sqrt n\), so it disappears at first order. That is not a defect in the mirror; it identifies a further question: characterize the boundary profiles where the continuous optimum is zero but the finite problem has a nontrivial Gaussian-scale cost. The covariance of \(f(t)\) under \(\mu\) should govern that second-order refinement.

My weakest point is that Theorem 1 is probabilistic and asymptotic rather than a hardness or algorithmic-complexity theorem, and its \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\) phenomenon is not retained by the bare continuum. Moreover, for fixed \(m\) the continuous problem may be comparatively easy. Still, the underlying optimization problem is exactly the one the authors formulate—only its electorate has entered the high-multiplicity regime—and the loss of the fluctuation scale is a clear research question rather than evidence that the mirror has ceased to be their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 1 is not really a population-continuum result waiting to be continuized. Its object is the random sequence of finite empirical societies, not the limiting society itself.

Let \(\widehat{\mu}_n\) be the empirical distribution of the \(n\) sampled votes. The paper studies the minimum operation count \(k(\widehat{\mu}_n)\), where \(f(P_n)=nF_{\widehat{\mu}_n}\). The central-limit argument is precisely that \(F_{\widehat{\mu}_n}-F_\pi\) is typically of order \(n^{-1/2}\). Together with the integer operation lattice, this produces the \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\) case. A deterministic continuum society supplies only \(F_\mu\). It has no sample size, empirical fluctuation, covariance, or integer lattice.

Consequently, the proposed \(\mathrm{CVO}_\infty\) does not retain the paper’s central phenomenon. At a limiting tie, an arbitrarily small real mass can usually cross the relevant score boundary, so the continuous optimum is \(0\) as an infimum, whereas the finite optimum is typically \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\). After normalization, the latter is merely \(\Theta(n^{-1/2})\), which disappears. Away from a boundary, the continuous problem is simply a finite family of linear programs; its positive optimum represents a macroscopic intervention, not the paper’s fluctuation-scale result. The \(0\), \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\), \(\Theta(n)\), and \(\infty\) classification is therefore not being translated into a new population model. Most of its content has been discarded.

The obvious repair does not help the mirror case. One could retain \(\mu\), a covariance matrix, and a parameter \(n\), then study a Gaussian perturbation \(\mu+n^{-1/2}Z\). But that is a second-order sampling or noise model over finite elections, not a computational problem whose society is itself continuous. Alternatively, one could encode the random seed or the identity of every sampled voter into the type. That preserves the fluctuations only by making the number of types grow with \(n\), destroying the high-multiplicity regime.

Constructive opt-bribery is a somewhat better rescue: real mass transfers \(x_{t,t'}\) with \(\sum_{t'}x_{t,t'}\leq\mu_t\) are a coherent high-multiplicity election problem. But this is no longer a mirror of Theorem 1’s result. It is the immediate continuous relaxation of the ILPs already written in Section 4. The paper proves neither a runtime classification nor a hardness result for that optimization problem. With fixed \(m\), it is just a finite collection of LPs; with variable \(m\), its complexity depends heavily on how the GSR, winner map, and exponentially many rankings are represented. That could motivate an independent high-multiplicity bribery paper, but the present paper supplies no distinctive computational phenomenon for it.

The same objection applies to DVO and CWVO: changing the target or taking the current winner away does not restore the missing empirical fluctuation. Their continuous versions are the same aggregate LP with a different winning region.

This negative case has an important limit. It would be wrong to claim that the mass-transfer problem is meaningless, or that existing high-multiplicity work counts against it. Large elections with many repeated ballot types are plainly a sensible regime. Thus I can make a strong case that Theorem 1 is a weak and partly mismatched anchor, but I cannot honestly establish the requested universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The proponent’s opt-bribery formulation survives as a legitimate new high-multiplicity problem, even though it does not preserve the paper’s main \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\) phenomenon.
