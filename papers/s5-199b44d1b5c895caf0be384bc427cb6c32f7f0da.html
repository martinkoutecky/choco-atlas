The continuous mirror question

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a single anchor: Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. The paper contains no numbered theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, W[1]-hardness, or FPT; its tables contain competitive-ratio results, but those entries are not themselves numbered theorems. I therefore do not pretend that this is a complexity anchor.

Theorem 3.1 states:

For online fair allocation of goods and chores with identical utility functions, under any fairness notion \(F\in\{\mathrm{EF},\mathrm{EFX},\mathrm{EF1}\}\), the competitive ratio for chores is the reciprocal of the competitive ratio for goods:
\[ > \mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm c}^{F}(H) > = > \frac{1}{\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm g}^{F}(H)}. > \]
This theorem is proved in the paper, not cited from elsewhere.

The continuous mirror I would put to the authors is Continuum-Batch Online Fair Division for Identical Utility Types.

Take a nonatomic population \(\Omega=[0,1]\) with Lebesgue measure. Every agent has the same complete utility type, so the type distribution is the degenerate distribution \(\mu=(1)\), with \(\tau=1\). This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime: there are arbitrarily many exchangeable agents but only one type.

Items arrive in \(m\) batches. Batch \(j\) contains \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) copies per unit population, and every copy has value \(w_j\ge0\) for goods or cost \(w_j\ge0\) for chores. The batch is revealed online and must be allocated irrevocably. To preserve indivisibility, an individual agent may receive only an integer number of copies of each batch. Formally, an allocation is a family of measurable functions
\[ z_j:\Omega\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0} \]
satisfying
\[ \int_\Omega z_j(a)\,da=q_j. \]
Thus \(q_j\) is a mass of item copies, not a fractional item given to one person. The item supply is scaled because otherwise a finite number of indivisible items would affect only a null set of agents in the continuum.

Agent \(a\)'s bundle utility is
\[ U(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_jz_j(a). \]
An online policy sees only the batches revealed so far and chooses each \(z_j\) without knowing later batches. In the normalized variant, the total per-capita utility \(\sum_jq_jw_j\) is announced in advance, exactly paralleling the paper’s known-total semi-online setting. Offline comparison allocations know the entire stream.

Fairness is imposed on individual bundle utilities, not merely on the average. For goods, EF requires
\[ U(a)\ge \alpha U(b) \]
for almost every pair \(a,b\). EFX replaces \(U(b)\) by \(U(b)-\min\{w_j:z_j(b)>0\}\), while EF1 replaces it by \(U(b)-\max\{w_j:z_j(b)>0\}\). For chores, EF requires
\[ U(a)\le \alpha U(b), \]
EFX uses \(U(a)-\min\{w_j:z_j(a)>0\}\le \alpha U(b)\), and EF1 uses \(U(a)-\max\{w_j:z_j(a)>0\}\le \alpha U(b)\). These conditions hold almost everywhere, with the same empty-bundle conventions as in the paper.

For an allocation \(z\), let \(F_{\mathrm g}^{z}(I)\) be the largest goods guarantee and \(F_{\mathrm c}^{z}(I)\) the smallest chores guarantee. Let \(F^*(I)\) be the optimal offline guarantee over all measurable allocations. The problem is:

Given a rational batch model, goods or chores, and \(F\in\{\mathrm{EF},\mathrm{EFX},\mathrm{EF1}\}\), design an irrevocable online policy \(H_\infty\) maximizing
\[ > \inf_I\frac{F_{\mathrm g}^{H_\infty}(I)}{F_{\mathrm g}^*(I)} > \]
for goods, or minimizing
\[ > \sup_I\frac{F_{\mathrm c}^{H_\infty}(I)}{F_{\mathrm c}^*(I)} > \]
for chores. Determine the optimal competitive ratio and whether the goods and chores values are reciprocal.

This is recognizably the authors’ problem. They explicitly identify identical-utility fair division with parallel-machine scheduling: agents are machines, items are jobs, and bundle utilities are machine loads. The continuum version is the high-multiplicity limit of that interpretation. A plausible setting is a very large food-bank or public-service programme in which many beneficiaries belong to one eligibility class and value each arriving parcel identically. The same formulation covers a large homogeneous workforce receiving assignments or absorbing identical chores. The regime has \(N\gg\tau\), indeed \(\tau=1\), while item copies and population both scale.

The paper’s reciprocal proof survives almost verbatim. For every fixed continuum allocation, the goods EF, EFX, and EF1 guarantees are reciprocals of the corresponding chores guarantees, with minima and maxima replaced by essential extrema where appropriate. Taking the offline optimum and then the worst online instance preserves the reciprocal relation. Thus Theorem 3.1 is not merely analogous in the continuum: its structural statement remains exactly true.

I would expect the structural part to be tractable. The offline problem can be written as a configuration LP over measurable distributions of integral bundle patterns, and the online problem becomes a high-multiplicity load-balancing problem. In a fixed or bounded item-value alphabet, column generation and convex scheduling methods should plausibly give Class A results. With unrestricted binary-encoded item values, however, the configuration-pricing problem may contain knapsack-like pattern selection. I would therefore treat the unrestricted version as a possible Class C problem: population multiplicity disappears, but indivisible bundle-pattern combinatorics may remain for a genuinely continuum-specific reason. This is a useful boundary rather than a defect.

The further questions are whether the configuration LP has an efficient separation oracle, whether normalized information improves the exact ratio, whether discrete finite-\(N\) instances converge to the continuum value with an additive error bound, and how the model changes for finitely many heterogeneous types \(\mu\) rather than the one-type case.

The weakest point is that the mirror must replace individual item arrivals by high-multiplicity batches. Without that co-modification, finitely many indivisible items have zero aggregate effect on a nonatomic population. Also, \(\tau=1\) makes this a deliberately narrow mirror, and Theorem 3.1 is a competitive-ratio identity rather than a hardness or polynomial-time theorem. I would not claim that it mirrors the paper’s general-utility results. But the paper itself supplies the justification: its identical-utility case is already a scheduling problem, and high-multiplicity parallel-machine scheduling is precisely a natural population-continuum regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is the wrong kind of result for this programme. Theorem 3.1 is a competitive-ratio identity, not a complexity theorem or an algorithmic optimization result. For identical utilities, the paper observes pointwise that, for the same allocation \(\mathcal A\),

\[ \mathrm F_{\mathrm g}^{\mathcal A} = \frac{1}{\mathrm F_{\mathrm c}^{\mathcal A}}, \]

and then takes the appropriate infimum or supremum. No algorithm is constructed, no computational problem is classified, and no population parameter is involved. The identity holds for every finite \(n\), so there is no theorem-level computational claim whose population can be continuized.

The proposed continuum model does not repair this. If the paper’s finite set of indivisible items is retained, a nonatomic population makes every item affect only a null set of agents. Almost every agent receives the empty bundle, and the fairness quantities become degenerate. The proponent correctly repairs this by introducing per-capita supplies of item copies, but that repair changes the primitive problem: it creates a continuum-supply online scheduling model rather than a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s item-arrival model.

More importantly, with identical utilities the society is always \(\mu=(1)\). The continuous population carries no instance data. The actual object being optimized is a distribution over integral bundle configurations,

\[ \nu(\text{bundle pattern}), \]

subject to average supply constraints. That is a configuration or load-balancing formulation over identical machines. The continuous mathematical object is therefore the allocation/configuration distribution, while the population distribution—the object ChoCo is supposed to continuize—is trivial. Calling the machines a continuum does not make Theorem 3.1 a population-continuum result.

The obvious stronger repair is to use finitely many heterogeneous utility types. But then the anchor disappears. The reciprocal argument depends on identical utilities: with heterogeneous agents, swapping the two bundles does not swap the evaluating agent’s valuation, so the goods and chores guarantees are no longer generally reciprocals. A multi-type model would be a new theorem, not a better continuous version of Theorem 3.1. Moreover, a complete utility type specifies values for the entire future item stream. Under the paper’s online information model, fixing a finite type catalogue requires additional assumptions about future items and valuations that the paper does not supply.

The proponent’s configuration-LP discussion is consequently speculative rather than an extension of the paper’s result. It may define an interesting online high-multiplicity scheduling problem, but it is not forced by the theorem, and its tractability or hardness would come from indivisible bundle patterns and arrival uncertainty—not from a continuous society. That is a legitimate research direction, but it is too far removed from the sole named anchor to count as a worthwhile mirror of this paper.

The negative case is not airtight. A one-type continuum-batch scheduling model is coherent, and the programme explicitly permits per-capita supply as a co-modification when population continuization demands it. Thus I cannot honestly claim that no sensible continuous problem can be invented here. The stronger and more defensible conclusion is narrower: this paper supplies no named computational result whose substance lies in population multiplicity, and the proposed mirror obtains its content by replacing the paper with a new continuum-supply scheduling model. I would therefore reject this anchor as sufficient for a green verdict.
