The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) resource classes, XOR types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_r\), and explicit rational bids \((B_{tj},p_{tj})\), choose feasible masses \(x_{t,S}\) assigned to whole bundles \(S\subseteq R\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:r\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_r\). For rational \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a feasible plan within \(\varepsilon\) of the maximum normalized log Nash welfare \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log u_t(S)\), when positive welfare is feasible; otherwise report that no positive-welfare plan exists.

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents described by complete XOR utility types, with mass \(\mu_t\) per type and per-capita supplies \(q_r\). Each unit of mass receives a whole bundle, while a type’s mass may be split across bundles. The objective is the normalized Nash product \(G(x)=\prod_{t,S}u_t(S)^{x_{t,S}}\); for positive welfare, maximizing it is equivalent to a finite LP maximizing \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log u_t(S)\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Nash winner determination for XOR utility types with integral bundles and per-capita resource supplies. It leaves the distributed negotiation results, positive-cube hardness result, heuristic search result, and experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What additive log-welfare guarantee can be obtained when rounding a continuous plan to a finite population with \(n_t\) agents of each type?
  • How do approximation and rounding guarantees change when the number of types \(\tau\) grows with the population or when some types cannot receive positive utility?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s centralized Nash winner determination problem, with the population represented by masses of agents who have identical utility functions.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: the decision problem \(\mathrm{Nash}\)—given a resource allocation scenario and \(K\in\mathbb Q\), is there an allocation \(A\) with \(\prod_i u_i(A(i))>K\)?—is claimed to be NP-complete for XOR utilities, even when each agent submits just one atomic bundle bid. The proof invokes the cited NP-hardness of utilitarian winner determination [18] and claims a reduction by changing bid prices.

Here is a continuous version of that underlying problem. There are \(m\) resource classes, a finite set \(T\) of \(\tau\) agent types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Type \(t\) is a complete XOR utility function: it has bids \((B_{tj},p_{tj})\), and values bundle \(S\) at
\[ u_t(S)=\max\bigl(\{p_{tj}:B_{tj}\subseteq S\}\cup\{0\}\bigr). \]
Each resource class \(r\) has rational per-capita supply \(q_r\), with \(0\leq q_r\leq1\). An allocation plan \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) says what mass of type \(t\) receives the whole, indivisible bundle \(S\subseteq R\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq R}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:\,r\in S}x_{t,S}\leq q_r. \]
The decision question, which I would name \(\mathrm{Nash\text{-}WDP}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{XOR}}\), is: given \(\kappa\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), is there a feasible plan with
\[ G_\infty(x)=\prod_{t,S}u_t(S)^{x_{t,S}}>\kappa? \]
A positive-mass assignment to a zero-utility bundle makes \(G_\infty(x)=0\). A solution is a feasible mass plan witnessing the inequality. This is the geometric mean form of Nash welfare: for a finite population with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\), \(x_{t,S}=n_{t,S}/n\), \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and resource supplies scaled by \(n\), it satisfies \(G_\infty(x)^n=\prod_i u_i(A(i))\). Each person still gets an integral bundle; only the mass of a type can be split across bundles.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime when a large allocation platform serves many copies of a small number of recipient profiles, such as recurring household need and preference classes, and stocks of each resource class grow with the population. The type records the entire utility schedule, including bids and prices. The model makes the society continuous; it does not make the goods or individual bundles fractional. Scaling supplies with the population is a co-modification of the allocation model, and fits the paper’s centralized winner determination setting, whose resource constraints already allow goods to go unsold.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, in the standard additive-\(\varepsilon\) sense for log-welfare. On allocations with positive welfare, maximizing \(G_\infty\) is equivalent to maximizing the linear objective \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log u_t(S)\). Under free disposal and monotone XOR utilities, a bundle can be reduced to a contained bid bundle with the same utility and lower resource use. Thus the useful positive-utility columns can be drawn from the explicitly listed bids, giving a finite LP over the type masses and resource capacities. Its input size depends on \(m\), \(\tau\), the number of bids, and their bit lengths. Logarithms of rational prices are computable to arbitrary precision, so additive approximation in log-welfare is the clean algorithmic target; exact threshold comparison at a knife edge is a separate precision question. This makes the mirror a plausible Class A case: the continuous population turns indivisible choices by individual agents into mass assignment among integral bundles.

The finite-to-continuous bridge still needs work. In particular: what rounding or additive guarantee follows when each type has many agents; how should zero utilities be handled in approximation guarantees; and what changes when \(\tau\) grows with \(n\)? The especially sharp single-bid restriction in Theorem 1 becomes simple in this mirror: each type has only one positive-utility bundle, so the continuous model has little optimization left beyond checking whether those masses fit the supplies. The substantive LP is for general XOR types.

My weakest point is the anchor’s proof as printed. With the paper’s stated utility semantics, if every agent has only one positive-valued bid, positive Nash welfare requires every agent to receive a bundle containing that bid; checking whether all such bundles are mutually compatible is polynomial. Replacing prices \(p\) by \(2p\) also does not, by itself, turn a sum of selected prices into a product of utilities. So although Theorem 1 is the right named result and the right underlying problem to mirror, its stated strengthening and the short reduction given here need clarification before I would rely on them as a sound discrete-hardness baseline. That caveat weakens the “hardness dissolves” comparison; it does not make the high-multiplicity Nash allocation question any less recognizable as the paper’s problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor as stated has a serious problem: Theorem 1’s “single bundle per agent” restriction does not appear to be NP-hard under the paper’s own XOR semantics. With one positive bid per agent, an agent’s utility is either that bid’s price or zero. For any positive threshold, the product is positive only if every agent receives their bid bundle. Whether all those bundles are mutually compatible can be checked directly, and if they are, the product is fixed. Replacing prices by \(2p\) does not turn the utilitarian sum over a chosen subset of bids into this all-or-nothing product. That weakens the claimed discrete hardness baseline.

But it does not defeat the better mirror. With multiple bids per type, the proposed population model is coherent: types contain complete utility schedules, agents still receive whole bundles, and mass can be divided among those outcomes. The aggregate objective \(\prod_{t,S}u_t(S)^{x_{t,S}}\) is the normalized Nash product, and for a finite population of size \(n\) it equals the original product raised to \(1/n\). Scaling resource supplies with the population is a sensible co-modification.

In fact, the XOR version reduces to a finite LP. For each type, the useful bundle choices can be drawn from its listed bids: if a bundle gives positive utility, some contained bid already gives at least that utility using no more resources. Assign mass to these choices, constrain each type’s total mass and each resource’s supply, and maximize \(\sum_{t,j}x_{tj}\log u_t(B_{tj})\). Thus the continuum can turn the indivisible allocation problem into a tractable mass assignment problem.

The strongest negative case is that this may be a fairly direct application of an LP, and the paper’s sharp single-bid hardness claim does not support the comparison the proponent initially suggested. That could lower the mirror’s priority. It cannot establish that no worthwhile mirror exists: the programme treats a clean tractable continuous formulation as a potential result, and the general-XOR version remains a well-posed population problem. On the evidence here, the honest negative case is weak against the improved mirror.
