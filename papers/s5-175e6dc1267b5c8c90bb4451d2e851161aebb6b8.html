The continuous mirror question

Given three types with masses \(\mu_i=1/3\), chore categories \(M\) each supplied at mass \(1/3\), and type cost functions \(c_1,c_2,c_3\), where \(c_1,c_2\) are additive and satisfy \(\max_{b\in M}c_i(b)/\min_{b\in M}c_i(b)\le 2\), and \(c_3\) is monotone, find masses \(x_{i,S}\ge 0\) assigned to integral bundles \(S\subseteq M\) such that \(\sum_S x_{i,S}=1/3\) for each type and \(\sum_{i,S:b\in S}x_{i,S}=1/3\) for each chore category. Require that for every occupied pair \(x_{i,S}>0\), \(x_{j,R}>0\), and every \(b\in S\), \(c_i(S\setminus\{b\})\le c_i(R\cup\{b\})\).

The model it lives in

A population of three cost types, each of mass \(1/3\), with chore-category supply \(1/3\) per category. Variables \(x_{i,S}\) assign mass of type \(i\) to whole bundles \(S\); the task is to find a supply-feasible tEFX allocation. The theorem supplies one by assigning its partition bundle to every member of the corresponding type.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 8.2's tEFX existence and construction for three equal-mass types, with two additive cost functions satisfying the ratio bound and a third monotone cost function. It leaves aside Theorem 7.3's EFX result and questions with other type masses or chore supplies.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does tEFX existence and efficient construction extend to arbitrary rational type masses and chore-category supplies?
  • Can the ratio bound be relaxed when each type's mass may be split across different bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a continuous tEFX problem for three large cohorts with shared cost profiles. A type is a complete chore-cost function: agents in the same type have the same costs for every bundle. Take a population of mass \(1\), split into types \(1,2,3\) with \(\mu_i=1/3\). Each chore category \(b\in M\) has aggregate supply \(1/3\) of identical copies. This is the limit of \(N=3q\) agents, \(q\) per type, with \(q\) copies of each chore. Thus the population can grow while the support stays at \(\tau=3\).

Here is the problem precisely. Costs \(c_1,c_2,c_3\) are normalized and monotone; \(c_1,c_2\) are additive and each has maximum single-chore cost at most twice its minimum. An allocation is a set of masses \(x_{i,S}\ge 0\), where \(x_{i,S}\) is the mass of type \(i\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\). Require
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{i,S}=\tfrac13 \quad\text{for each }i, \qquad \sum_{i,S:\,b\in S}x_{i,S}=\tfrac13 \quad\text{for each }b\in M. \]
The question is to find such an allocation satisfying tEFX: whenever \(x_{i,S}>0\), \(x_{j,R}>0\), and \(b\in S\), require
\[ c_i(S\setminus\{b\})\le c_i(R\cup\{b\}). \]
Each person receives a whole bundle of chores; the mass variables describe how many people receive each bundle.

The anchor is Theorem 8.2, proved in this paper. It says that for three agents and a finite chore set \(M\), if two agents have additive costs with maximum-to-minimum single-chore cost ratio at most \(2\), while the third has any monotone cost function, a tEFX allocation can be found in polynomial time \(O(m)\). The theorem’s algorithm produces a partition \(B_1,B_2,B_3\) of \(M\). Set \(x_{i,B_i}=1/3\) and all other \(x_{i,S}=0\). Each chore’s mass supply is then respected, and the theorem’s tEFX inequalities apply to every pair of occupied bundle classes. This is a tractable mirror. The stated \(O(m)\) is the paper’s operation bound; binary rational costs add bit complexity, and an unrestricted \(c_3\) needs an explicit-function or value-oracle representation.

This is a plausible population setting: a large organization can have three job families, each with many workers who share the same full cost profile, and recurring chore copies can scale with cohort size. The result’s tEFX comparisons carry over directly between cohort members. The mirror covers Theorem 8.2’s restricted tEFX allocation problem. It does not cover Theorem 7.3’s separate EFX existence result or claim anything about unrestricted additive costs. The paper offers no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer question.

The main weakness is that this is an exact clone lift: the three type masses are fixed at \(1/3\), and the theorem supplies a solution where everyone in each type receives the same bundle. The population size does not affect the algorithm. The next questions are whether tEFX can be found for arbitrary rational type masses and chore supplies, and whether the ratio bound can be relaxed when agents of a type may receive different bundles. This limitation makes the mirror narrow, but the underlying problem and its fairness test remain the paper’s own.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 8.2. The strongest case against it is that the paper’s computational structure lives in a fixed three-recipient allocation: three cost functions, three bundles, and transfers among those bundles. The cohort construction keeps exactly that structure by giving each of three groups mass \(1/3\), assigning each group one bundle, and scaling each chore’s supply to \(1/3\). Its mass variables therefore record a clone expansion of the original instance; cohort size has no role in the algorithm or the fairness test.

A richer version would let each type split across integral bundles and allow arbitrary rational type masses and chore supplies. That is a more substantive high-multiplicity allocation problem. But splitting a type across bundles removes the paper’s three-bundle invariant, while chore supplies and rules for assigning repeated chore categories need additional modeling choices. Once those choices are made, the result could be worthwhile—but it would be a new many-recipient tEFX problem, rather than a continuous version of the theorem’s specific algorithmic setting.

This is a reason to question the value of the proposed mirror, not a decisive objection. The three-cohort workplace scenario is plausible, and the broader mass-allocation question is well posed. Under the programme’s stated standard, I cannot honestly defeat this anchor: the negative case is weak, and the mirror likely survives.
