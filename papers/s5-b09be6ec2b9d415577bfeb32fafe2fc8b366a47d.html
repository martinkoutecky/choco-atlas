The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result in the required sense: it proves mathematical properties of priority procedures and inconsistency indices, but says nothing about whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. So there is no qualifying anchor for a continuous computational mirror.

The best positive modeling intuition is a very large panel of evaluators with a small number of recurring judgment types. A type could be an evaluator’s complete pairwise-comparison matrix, and its mass the fraction of evaluators of that type. One could aggregate those judgments into a reciprocal matrix and derive a normalized priority vector, measuring its worst pairwise discrepancy. The paper’s closest result is that the eigenvector procedure is regular and follows Koczkodaj’s inconsistency index. But that theorem concerns a single matrix; it does not analyze a population of evaluators, an aggregation step, or computational complexity.

That scenario is an extension, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s results. Its weakest point is that the paper treats the comparison matrix as its input and does not specify a population of judges or how their judgments should be aggregated. I therefore can’t honestly state a continuous problem per anchor or classify its expected complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case for a red verdict is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its theorem that \(P_{ev}\) is regular and follows \(\mathcal K\), and its bounds relating inconsistency indices to the eigenvalue and order preservation, are mathematical claims about a given reciprocal matrix. None states the complexity of a decision or optimization problem. That leaves no named computational anchor for a continuous question.

The best repaired version of the proponent’s panel idea is coherent: let each evaluator type be a complete reciprocal matrix \(M^t\), give each type mass \(\mu_t\), and pool judgments, for example by taking weighted geometric means of entries. Then compute a priority vector and its discrepancy, or ask how much mass must be reweighted to meet a discrepancy threshold. The first version reduces to constructing one pooled matrix and applying the paper’s results to it; the masses do not create a new computational structure in those results. The reweighting version could pose an interesting optimization problem, but it needs a pooling rule, intervention costs, and an objective that the paper never supplies. It would be a new group-judgment problem in the paper’s vicinity.

The other apparent continuizations fare worse. Treating concepts as the population shifts the continuum to the alternatives, while treating comparison pairs as mass gives judgments a population meaning the paper does not define. Either route needs a new model before its computational questions can be assessed.

I would reject this paper as a ChoCo mirror candidate. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could arise from it is less secure: mass-based sensitivity analysis for a large evaluator panel might be worth studying. Its weakness is that such a study would extend the paper’s setting rather than mirror one of its computational results.
