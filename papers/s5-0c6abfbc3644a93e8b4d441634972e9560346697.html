The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a symmetric binary affinity function \(w:T\times T\to\{0,1\}\), choose a welfare-maximizing assignment of type mass to a fixed finite menu \(\mathcal H\) of bounded-size seat components. The input includes rational component masses \(\nu_H\) with \(\sum_{H\in\mathcal H}\nu_H|V(H)|=1\). For each type assignment \(\phi:V(H)\to T\), choose rational configuration mass \(\lambda_{H,\phi}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_\phi\lambda_{H,\phi}=\nu_H\) and \(\sum_{H,\phi}\lambda_{H,\phi}|\phi^{-1}(t)|=\mu_t\). Maximize total per-capita B-utility welfare, where each unit of mass receives a whole seat and its neighbors in its component.

The model it lives in

A continuous assignment model with complete preference types \(T\), type masses \(\mu\), fixed bounded seat-component templates \(H\), and configuration masses \(\lambda_{H,\phi}\). The objective is aggregate per-capita welfare under the paper’s B-utility; mass balance assigns every type and fills the per-capita seat supply.

What the mirror covers

Covers the binary symmetric MWA case of Theorem 9 for a fixed menu of bounded-size components; leaves MUA, symmetric-strict preferences, and more general seat graphs aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the worst-case integrality or additive welfare gap when a finite cohort is rounded from the configuration LP optimum?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of Maximum Welfare Arrangement. The anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper: for each of B-, S-, and W-utility, both MWA and MUA are NP-hard when preferences are binary and symmetric, or symmetric and strict, even when the seat graph is planar and has bounded degree. I focus on its binary, symmetric MWA case. The theorem is proved here by reductions from Partition Into Triangles; it is not merely cited from earlier work.

Call the mirror Cohort Maximum Welfare Seat Arrangement. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete preference types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a symmetric binary affinity function \(w:T\times T\to\{0,1\}\). Two agents of type \(t\) have the same valuation \(w(t,t')\) toward every agent of type \(t'\). The seat supply is a fixed finite menu \(\mathcal H\) of planar component templates—triangles and stars with at most \(19\) leaves, for example—with rational component masses \(\nu_H\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{H\in\mathcal H}\nu_H|V(H)|=1. \]
Thus the population and the seats both have unit mass. A component mass represents a continuum of copies of that small seat graph.

For each template \(H\), a local configuration is a map \(\phi:V(H)\to T\), assigning a type to each seat. The solution is a nonnegative rational mass \(\lambda_{H,\phi}\) of components in each configuration, satisfying
\[ \sum_\phi\lambda_{H,\phi}=\nu_H \quad\text{for every }H, \qquad \sum_{H,\phi}\lambda_{H,\phi}\,|\phi^{-1}(t)|=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
With the paper’s B-, S-, or W-utility, let \(u^U_{H,\phi}(v)\) be the utility of the type assigned to seat \(v\), calculated from its neighbors in that configuration. The question is to choose \(\lambda\) maximizing per-capita welfare,
\[ \sum_{H,\phi}\lambda_{H,\phi}\sum_{v\in V(H)}u^U_{H,\phi}(v). \]
This is the same assignment and welfare question as MWA. A configuration still gives each unit of mass a whole seat and its usual set of neighbors; only the proportions of repeated component configurations can be fractional.

The regime is a large organization or conference that repeatedly seats people in small collaboration pods. A type could be a job role or team category, and \(w(t,t')=1\) could mean that those categories work well together. Many people share each complete type, while the number of types is much smaller than the population. Repeated breakout pods or roundtables make the component supply natural. This is an instance regime, not a claim that every seating problem has high multiplicity.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. For the fixed component menu, the number of configurations is at most \(\sum_{H\in\mathcal H}|T|^{|V(H)|}\), hence polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\), since every component has at most \(20\) seats. The constraints and welfare objective form a rational linear program. This gives a precise continuous question with an exact polynomial-time solution. It also explains how Theorem 9’s discrete hardness could disappear: the finite problem must choose integral numbers of each configuration, while the continuum can mix configurations in fractional proportions. The paper’s hardness reduction uses local components of precisely this bounded size, but the continuous LP need not preserve the reduction’s indivisibility.

The mirror’s scope is deliberately limited: it covers the binary symmetric MWA branch of Theorem 9 for repeated bounded-size components. It does not claim to mirror MUA, the symmetric-strict branch, or arbitrary planar bounded-degree seat graphs. An immediate next question is whether the corresponding continuous MUA problem is also tractable. Another is how large the rounding or additive-approximation gap can be when converting a continuous optimum into a finite seating.

The weakest point is that the repeated-component supply is an added regime assumption, and the theorem’s own hard instances do not establish that their agents fall into a small number of complete types. Still, this is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ underlying assignment problem: it keeps their agents’ utilities, seat graph, and welfare objective, while scaling the population and its per-capita seats together. The restriction is also visible in the paper’s own reduction, which uses only small repeated seat components.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 9’s hardness reduction is built around named-agent compatibility. Its agents encode vertices and candidate triangles of a Partition Into Triangles instance; their valuations distinguish particular other agents. In general, those agents do not collapse into a small set of complete preference types. The proposed LP’s polynomial running time is in \(\tau\), so if \(\tau\) grows with the population, it gives no high-multiplicity speedup. And the theorem’s planar, bounded-degree condition does not imply bounded component size: the fixed menu of components with at most \(20\) seats is an extra restriction. A continuous model allowing large connected seat graphs could have exponentially many local configurations.

That limits what the mirror can claim about the theorem’s hard instances. But it does not defeat the proposed question. Repeated small pods with a fixed vocabulary of roles and symmetric type-to-type affinities are a coherent high-multiplicity seating regime. Types are complete for this model: people of one type have the same valuation toward every other type. The seat supply scales with the population, and each unit of mass still receives a seat and a finite set of neighbors. Both are within the programme’s stated scope.

Nor does the LP become invalid because it is generic. With a fixed component-size bound, enumerating type assignments gives polynomially many configurations in \(\tau\), and mass balance gives a rational LP. That is a real tractability result for this version of MWA; rounding or additive-approximation guarantees could make the discrete-to-continuous bridge substantive too. The fact that the reduction’s gadgets depend on identities explains why the paper’s hardness result does not establish hardness for this cohort regime. It does not show the cohort regime is artificial.

So the negative case I can honestly make is narrower: this mirror does not explain the paper’s NP-hard instances, and its contribution may be modest if the configuration LP and its rounding consequences are immediate. I cannot turn that into a case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed MWA anchor survives: it is a plausible continuous-population question, and the objection that it changes the seat-graph regime is not enough to rule it out under the programme’s criteria.
