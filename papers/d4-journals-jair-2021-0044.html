What the mirror covers

The attempted mirror covers Theorems 6, 8, and 10, but leaves the remaining optimality, dominance, aggregation, axiomatic, and experimental results untouched; the covered results are already native to the paper's population-distribution model.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a valid but fairly close high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s multi-agent PCP-net reasoning. It is not a new bribery-style frontier: the paper already discusses frequencies of CP-net types and explicitly interprets PCP probabilities as population proportions. Still, it gives a legitimate continuous-population formulation in which outcomes remain integral and only the society becomes a mass distribution.

Take a fixed set of binary features \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\), with a shared acyclic dependency graph. A type \(t\) is a complete CP-net: its dependency information and every conditional preference-table row are part of the type. Two agents have the same type exactly when they are indistinguishable for every preference query in the paper. A society is a rational mass vector \(\mu=(\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users with type \(t\). A natural regime is a large population of customers, patients, or households repeatedly choosing configurations from the same finite catalogue: \(N\) may be in the millions while the number of recurring preference types is much smaller.

For the compact instances studied in the paper, \(\mu\) can be specified by rational local frequencies \(q_{i,u}\) for the ordering of feature \(X_i\) under parent assignment \(u\). Under the PCP independence assumption, the induced population distribution is

\[ \mu_t=\prod_{i,u}q_{i,u}\bigl(\pi_{t,i,u}\bigr), \]

where \(\pi_{t,i,u}\) is the ordering used by type \(t\) in that row. This is population mass, not epistemic uncertainty: it says what fraction of the society has each complete CP-net. Clearing denominators produces a finite clone population with exactly the same aggregate answers.

My lead mirror is Continuous Mass-Maximal Optimality. Its instance consists of such a factorized society \(\mu\), together with the induced CP-net type space. Each acyclic type \(t\) has a unique optimal outcome \(\operatorname{opt}(t)\). The task is to output

\[ o^\star\in\arg\max_o \sum_{t:\operatorname{opt}(t)=o}\mu_t, \]

with the paper’s fixed lexicographic tie-breaking rule. Thus the decision variable is an integral outcome \(o\), and the objective is the mass of agents for whom \(o\) is optimal. This is a direct continuous version of choosing the outcome preferred by the largest fraction of a high-multiplicity population.

The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It states, in full, that for a PCP-net \(Q\) and its Opt-net: (i) positive-probability assignments of the Opt-net correspond one-to-one with outcomes that are optimal in at least one induced CP-net; (ii) the probability that an outcome is optimal equals the joint probability of the corresponding Opt-net assignment, and is zero when there is no such assignment; and (iii) the most probable optimal outcome is obtained by finding a maximum-joint-probability assignment in the Opt-net. The paper then observes that this is polynomial for polytrees of bounded induced width, while unrestricted maximum assignment is NP-hard.

Under the population reading, Theorem 6 says that the mass-maximal outcome can be found by constructing the corresponding population Opt-net and solving its maximum-probability assignment problem. I would classify the bounded-width version as Class A: dynamic programming gives a polynomial algorithm, parameterized naturally by induced width. The unrestricted factorized version is plausibly continuum-specifically hard, because the difficulty comes from MAP-style aggregation over a compactly represented population law, not from the number of named voters. The mirror therefore produces a meaningful tractability boundary rather than merely replacing \(N\) by a real number.

A second, independently useful mirror is Continuous Population Dominance Certification. Given two integral outcomes \(o,o'\), ask for a certified lower bound on the fraction of the population whose CP-net entails \(o\succ o'\). The answer is not a fractional outcome or a probabilistic lottery: it is a guaranteed population fraction.

The anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. In the binary polytree setting, let \(\mathrm{Diff}\) contain the features on which \(o\) and \(o'\) differ. For each such feature \(X_i\), define \(\bar\rho_i\) as the product, over the relevant parent assignments \(u\), of the population frequency of the opposite local comparison \(o'|_{X_i}\succ o|_{X_i}\) conditional on \(u\); for a parentless feature it is simply that unconditional frequency. The theorem states that

\[ P_Q^L(o\succ o') = \prod_{i\in\mathrm{Diff}}(1-\bar\rho_i) \]

is a lower bound on the exact population fraction \(P_Q(o\succ o')\). The paper notes that it can be computed in \(\mathrm{O}(n2^k)\) time, where \(k\) is the maximum number of parents.

The continuous problem is therefore: given rational local population frequencies, a binary polytree, and \(o,o'\), output the rational certificate \(L_\mu(o,o')\) above, guaranteed to satisfy

\[ L_\mu(o,o')\le \sum_{t:t\models o\succ_t o'}\mu_t. \]

This is Class A for bounded \(k\). It is author-recognizable because the paper itself describes dominance in the multi-agent setting as determining whether one outcome is preferred by most agents. The continuous formulation simply makes “most” an exact mass rather than a finite headcount.

The exact boundary case is Separable Continuous Population Dominance. Here every feature is independent, and \(p_i\) is the mass fraction preferring \(x_i\) to \(\bar x_i\). Given \(o,o'\), define

\[ \mathrm{Diff}^+ = \{i:o|_{X_i}=x_i,\ o'|_{X_i}=\bar x_i\}, \]

and

\[ \mathrm{Diff}^- = \{i:o|_{X_i}=\bar x_i,\ o'|_{X_i}=x_i\}. \]

The task is to return the exact fraction of the population whose type entails \(o\succ o'\). The anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper: for a separable PCP-net,

\[ P_Q(o\succ o') = P_Q^L(o\succ o') = \left(\prod_{i\in\mathrm{Diff}^+}p_i\right) \left(\prod_{i\in\mathrm{Diff}^-}(1-p_i)\right). \]

This is a linear-time Class A mirror. It gives an exact high-multiplicity majority-dominance query while preserving the paper’s integral outcomes and CP-net semantics.

I would not use the paper’s unnumbered statement that PCP-net dominance is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hard as an anchor: it is attributed to Bigot et al. rather than proved as a numbered result here. Nor would I claim mirrors for the axiomatic results, experiments, or least-squares aggregation as computational anchors.

The weakest point is obvious: the paper already defines anonymous CP-net profiles with relative frequencies and already says that PCP probabilities can represent a large population. An opponent can therefore call the lead mirror a relabelling of the paper’s own semantics. I concede that it is near-collision and mathematically modest. The positive case survives because the paper does not formulate the society itself as a rational-mass high-multiplicity object, does not give the finite-clone dictionary, and does not chart the complexity of population queries as a function of dependency width and type representation. The natural next questions—minimum mass or cost of changing CP-net types to alter the collective optimum, exact dominance for correlated type distributions, and rounding guarantees from rational societies to finite populations—would make the population variable genuinely active.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that all three anchors are already population-distribution queries in the paper itself. The proposed mirror does not continuize a discrete society; it renames the paper’s existing probabilities and relative frequencies as masses.

This is not the objection that some unrelated high-multiplicity literature already exists. The collision is internal. Definition 4 introduces anonymous CP-net profiles with relative frequencies, and Section 6 explicitly interprets those frequencies as the proportions of agents holding each CP-net. Definition 8 gives the induced distribution over complete CP-net types. Thus, for rational masses, clearing denominators produces an ordinary finite clone population with exactly the same aggregate answers.

For Theorem 6, the proposed objective

\[ \max_o\sum_{t:\operatorname{opt}(t)=o}\mu_t \]

is exactly Definition 11’s “most probable optimal outcome.” Theorem 6 already proves that this quantity is computed by the Opt-net. Nothing changes when “probability that an induced CP-net has \(o\) as its optimum” is read as “fraction of agents whose CP-net has \(o\) as its optimum.” The proponent’s factorization

\[ \mu_t=\prod_{i,u}q_{i,u}(\pi_{t,i,u}) \]

is precisely the probability of an induced CP-net in Definition 8.

A more general mass vector does not rescue the mirror. If the support \(T\) is explicit, the population objective is just a weighted sum over types; the continuous variable contributes no new computational structure. If the distribution is given compactly through local factors, the problem is exactly the PCP-net/Opt-net problem already studied. If correlations are added, the resulting difficulty is difficulty of a new graphical-model representation, not a consequence of replacing a finite population by a continuum. A mass-transfer or bribery problem over CP-net types could be worthwhile, but it would introduce a new intervention objective and a new cost model absent from the theorem; it would not be a mirror of Theorem 6.

Theorem 8 is even more directly a collision. Its quantity

\[ P_Q^L(o\succ o') \]

is already defined as a probability mass over induced CP-net types, and the paper explicitly describes dominance in the multi-agent setting as determining whether one outcome is preferred by most agents. Replacing \(P_Q\) by

\[ \sum_{t:t\models o\succ_t o'}\mu_t \]

is only a change of interpretation. The proposed “population dominance certificate” is the paper’s own lower-bound theorem, with population language added afterward. The threshold version is also already present as LB-DD in Definition 20.

The better exact mirror fares no better. The exact fraction of types entailing \(o\succ o'\) is Definition 14 itself:

\[ P_Q(o\succ o') = \sum_{C:C\models o\succ o'}P(C). \]

For an explicit finite type population, this is a weighted version of the same query. For a compact PCP-net distribution, it is the same weighted inference problem. The polytree lower bound is already the paper’s algorithm, and any hardness of exact dominance would concern CP-net reasoning under a compact distribution, not a newly continuized society.

Theorem 10 is simply the clearest special case. Under separability, the paper already proves the exact product

\[ P_Q(o\succ o') = \left(\prod_{i\in\mathrm{Diff}^+}p_i\right) \left(\prod_{i\in\mathrm{Diff}^-}(1-p_i)\right). \]

Reading \(p_i\) as the population fraction preferring one local value gives exactly the proposed continuous population answer. There is no residual finite-count issue to relax: the theorem already computes the aggregate fraction exactly.

There is also a regime problem with the proposed story. A complete CP-net type contains every relevant conditional preference-table row. With \(r\) binary rows, a generic factorized PCP-net has up to \(2^r\) positive-probability complete types. That is not naturally a society with a moderate number of recurring types; it is a succinct distribution over exponentially many types. If one instead restricts to a genuinely small set of recurring CP-net types, the paper’s anonymous profile already represents the society by their frequencies. Thus the two useful regimes are either already covered explicitly or are precisely the paper’s compact probabilistic model.

The honest limitation is that one could design a new and potentially valuable ChoCo problem about continuously bribing, editing, or redistributing CP-net types. But that would be a new computational-social-choice problem, requiring a canonical notion of type-edit cost. None of the three anchors survives as a worthwhile continuous mirror: Theorem 6, Theorem 8, and Theorem 10 already perform the relevant population aggregation in the paper’s own formalism.
