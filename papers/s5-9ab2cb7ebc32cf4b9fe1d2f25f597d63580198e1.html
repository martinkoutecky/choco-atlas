The continuous mirror question

Given projects \(P\), costs \(c(p)\), a budget \(B\), \(\lambda\), a finite support \(T\) of complete utility vectors \(u_t:P\to\mathbb{N}\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational target \(q\), does there exist \(S\subseteq P\) with \(\sum_{p\in S}c(p)\leq B\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathrm{sat}^{\lambda}_t(S)\geq q\)? Here \(\mathrm{sat}^{\lambda}_t(S)=0\) when \(|S|<\lambda\), and otherwise it is the \(\lambda\)-th largest utility \(u_t(p)\) among \(p\in S\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete resident utility vectors, with rational type masses, choosing an indivisible project set under the original budget constraint to maximize mass-weighted \(\lambda\)-median satisfaction. The corresponding Best-UK version replaces satisfaction by the sum of each type's top \(\lambda\) selected utilities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2.1's hardness claims for Median-UK and Best-UK, including the stated single-crossing and unanimous restrictions. It does not cover the paper's parameterized algorithms, approximation scheme, or tractable special cases.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed λ, is Mass-UK solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • When λ is part of the input, how does the complexity change, and what parameterized dependence on \(\tau\) is achievable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a population version of the paper’s Median-Utilitarian Knapsack problem, in the paper’s own participatory-budgeting setting. Imagine a city with a large population—say, a million residents—rating a modest set of proposed projects. A type is a resident’s complete utility vector over those projects. The input lists the \(\tau\) types present and each type’s rational fraction \(\mu_t\) of the population. Residents of one type have identical utilities; the number of residents can be far larger than the number of types.

Call the resulting family Mass-UK. An instance gives projects \(P\), their costs \(c(p)\), a budget \(B\), a type support \(T\) with utility vectors \(u_t:P\to\mathbb N\), masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and \(\lambda\). Choose a project set \(S\subseteq P\) with \(\sum_{p\in S}c(p)\le B\) to maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathrm{sat}^{\lambda}_t(S)\). For the lead version, \(\mathrm{sat}^{\lambda}_t(S)\) is zero when \(|S|<\lambda\), and otherwise the \(\lambda\)-th largest utility \(u_t(p)\) among selected projects. A decision version adds a rational target \(q\) and asks whether some feasible \(S\) has value at least \(q\). A sister version replaces satisfaction with the sum of the type’s top \(\lambda\) selected utilities, matching Best-UK. Projects remain indivisible; the population’s composition is what becomes continuous.

My anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved in the paper. It states that both Median-UK and Best-UK are NP-hard for every \(\lambda\), even on single-crossing profiles; for \(\lambda>1\), both remain NP-hard on unanimous profiles. Here, unanimous means voters share a top item, while single-crossing means voter preferences can be ordered so that, for each pair of items, the voters preferring one to the other form a consecutive block. The authors prove the theorem by reducing from Diverse Knapsack, whose single-crossing hardness they cite from earlier work. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02766)

The population mirror preserves this decision question exactly. Given a discrete instance with \(n_t\) voters of each complete type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Then the mass objective is the paper’s total satisfaction divided by \(n\); set \(q=u_{\mathrm{tgt}}/n\). The selected project set and budget are unchanged, and a single-crossing ordering of voters induces an ordering of their types. Thus the paper’s NP-hardness transfers to Mass-UK when support size is part of the input. I expect that unrestricted version to be hardness-transferring (Class B), for both the median and best objectives. This is the mirror of the paper’s central project-selection problem, not a claim about encoding a particular integer program.

The weakness is support size. That reduction does not show hardness when \(\tau\) is fixed or small: its embedded discrete profile may have many distinct complete utility types. Also, common top choice is not the same as having one complete type. So the unanimous hardness does not settle the small-\(\tau\) case. Indeed, the paper’s polynomial result for strongly unanimous profiles marks a tractable boundary when preferences agree much more closely. The main follow-up question is whether Mass-UK is polynomial-time solvable or fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), with binary-encoded masses and costs; it is also worth separating fixed \(\lambda\) from \(\lambda\) supplied as input. The mirror covers the Median-UK and Best-UK hardness claims of Theorem 2.1, not the paper’s other algorithmic results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the paper’s hardness theorem does not establish hardness in a regime with small type support. Its types must include each resident’s *complete* utility vector: sharing a top project, or even a full ranking, is not enough to merge residents whose utility levels differ. With many projects, that can leave nearly as many types as residents. The theorem’s single-crossing restriction does not fix this, and its unanimous restriction is weaker than having one type. So the result supports hardness for unrestricted Mass-UK, but says little about whether continuity yields useful algorithms when \(\tau\) is moderate.

That is a real limit on what this anchor shows, but it does not defeat the mirror. A plausible better version uses a modest project slate and bounded ratings. Then many residents can share complete utility vectors, and a distribution over those vectors is a sensible high-multiplicity model. The decision question remains computational, with projects indivisible and population mass as the continuous object. The paper’s theorem is a named complexity result; identity is not needed for its objective; and normalizing total satisfaction and the target gives a nondegenerate mass formulation. Nothing in the supplied prior art is an existing continuous computational treatment. High-multiplicity work supports the regime rather than colliding with it.

So the negative case is weak. The proponent’s theorem only anchors the unrestricted, variable-support version, and the paper does not answer the more interesting small-\(\tau\) questions. But those are reasons to narrow the claimed result and identify follow-up questions, not reasons that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor on the grounds available.
