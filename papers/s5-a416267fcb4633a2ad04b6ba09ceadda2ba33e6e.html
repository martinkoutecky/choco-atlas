What the mirror covers

Theorem 1.1 and its supporting lemmas concern asymptotic probability for finite random electorates; none supplies a computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1.1, proved here, shows that when \(m\gg n^4\), \(Q_{m,n}=Q_n+O(n^2m^{-1/2})\), and that \(Q_n\) is super-polynomially small as \(n\to\infty\). That is a result about asymptotic probability, not about the complexity or algorithms for a computational problem. The lemmas and cited results discussed in the paper are probabilistic or asymptotic as well.

A tempting mirror would take rankings \(t\in S_n\) as types, set \(\mu_t=1/n!\) for impartial culture, and ask for the probability that \(m\) independent draws from \(\mu\) produce a Condorcet winner. But that retains a finite random election and uses \(\mu\) as a sampling law. It is a noise model over discrete profiles, rather than a computational question about a continuous society. In the deterministic society \(\mu_t=1/n!\), every pairwise contest is tied at exactly \(1/2\), so there is no strict Condorcet winner.

So the paper offers a possible probabilistic connection, but no named computational result to anchor the requested continuous mirror. I would not present that connection as a positive case under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1.1 and the surrounding lemmas concern the asymptotic probability of an event in a random finite election. They give no complexity classification, algorithm, or optimization problem. A new question about manipulating or changing the society could be interesting, but it would add a computational task the paper never studies.

The best charitable mirror is to represent rankings by a distribution \(\mu\) and ask whether the resulting population has a Condorcet winner. Given the supported rankings and their masses, each pairwise share is a direct sum over types, so checking for a winner is straightforward. For impartial culture, every pairwise share is exactly \(1/2\): the strict winner disappears in the aggregate. The paper’s likelihood result concerns the finite-sample fluctuations around those ties.

One could retain those fluctuations by asking for the probability that \(m\) independent draws from \(\mu\) produce a winner. That is coherent, but it keeps the finite random election and makes \(\mu\) a sampling law—the noise-model angle the programme excludes. Taking the aggregate population itself as the object instead removes the randomness that drives the paper’s result. Adding mass transfers, robustness, or another computational objective may yield a worthwhile project, but it would be a new problem rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

I can’t establish that no worthwhile continuous problem could ever be built from this setting. The narrower negative case is much stronger: none of the paper’s results supplies a computational anchor, and the closest natural reformulations either reduce to checking pairwise masses or retain the finite sampling problem.
