The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no named computational result: it proves no problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified by computational complexity. The nearest results are analytic, so they cannot honestly serve as anchors.

The closest is Proposition 1, “Majority-preferred means testing,” proved here: for any fixed tax rate \(\tau\in(0,1)\), the median-income household is decisive on the means-testing rate, and the majority-winning \(\hat\beta(\tau)\) satisfies \(y_m=\partial\tilde\alpha(\tau,\beta)/\partial\beta\). Theorem 1, “The size of the welfare program does not affect the fraction or the identity of the subsidy-receiving households,” also proved here, says exactly that the set and fraction of recipient households are invariant to the tax rate. Neither is a computational-complexity result.

The best positive case I can make is a computational question about calculating the paper’s second-stage policy. A plausible regime is a large jurisdiction administering an income-tested benefit: many households, with income the only difference between them, described by a compact distribution rather than a list of named people. A type is income \(y\); mass is the fraction of households in an income range. Given a tax rate, the action is the means-testing rate \(\beta\), with \(\alpha\) pinned down by the balanced budget.

Call the problem Majority Means-Testing Computation. An instance gives a rational tax rate \(\tau\in(0,1)\) and an income distribution \(F\), encoded as a finite piecewise-polynomial density with rational coefficients, satisfying the paper’s regularity and mean-above-median assumptions. The task is to output the majority-winning \((\alpha,\beta)\), with algebraic numbers represented exactly, or by certified approximations. A solution must satisfy the budget and be a majority winner among feasible means-testing policies. Writing \(M(z)=\int_0^z y\,dF(y)\) and \(z=\alpha/\beta\), Proposition 1 gives a direct characterization: find \(z\) such that \(M(z)=y_mF(z)\), then set
\[ \beta=\frac{\tau Y}{zF(z)-M(z)},\qquad \alpha=z\beta. \]
For this encoded family, I would expect a Class A result: the characterization reduces the computation to locating roots of polynomial pieces. That algorithmic prediction is mine, not a theorem of the paper. The proposed question covers the second-stage targeting decision; it does not mirror the first-stage tax vote or the full sequential equilibrium.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already makes the population continuous and studies its equilibrium analytically. This is therefore a new computational layer around an existing continuum model, not a discrete-to-continuous mirror anchored in discrete hardness. The compact distribution encoding is also an added computational specification, and the paper’s smooth income-distribution assumptions need not match every finite cohort model. The next questions would be the bit complexity of exact versus approximate computation, how to compute the full two-stage equilibrium, and whether a discrete high-multiplicity version has a useful approximation relationship.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper already starts with the continuous population. Its households are a continuum indexed by income, and the policy outcome is characterized through majority voting over that population. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 are analytic results about this model, not computational-complexity results. They therefore cannot serve as computational anchors.

The proposed “Majority Means-Testing Computation” asks for the policy pair that the paper already characterizes. Its equation for \(z\) follows from Proposition 1, and \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\) then follow from the budget constraint. Choosing piecewise-polynomial densities makes this characterization an exact computational input-output problem, but it adds an encoding to an existing continuous analysis; it does not produce a discrete-to-continuous mirror. A stronger finite version—with many households grouped by income and mass \(n_y/n\)—is plausible. But that would introduce a finite model absent from the paper. The continuous model would be its proposed limit, not the mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The weakness in this negative case is real: households sharing an income are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and studying the bit complexity of the full equilibrium could have independent value. I cannot honestly rule out that work being worthwhile. I can say that the proponent’s anchor is a computational reformulation of a result already derived for a continuous society, not a continuous mirror of a discrete computational-social-choice problem.
