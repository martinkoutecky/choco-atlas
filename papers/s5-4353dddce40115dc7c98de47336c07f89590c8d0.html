The continuous mirror question

Given a finite tournament \(G\) on entrant types \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a distinguished singleton favorite \(f\) with mass \(\mu_f=2^{-R}\), and \(R\) rounds, can the current type masses be paired in equal blocks each round so that the favorite's entire block advances every round and wins? A match between distinct types produces the winner specified by \(G\); a same-type match produces that type. Restrict to instances where the subset feedback arc number of \(G\) at \(f\) is \(1\).

The model it lives in

A type is a standardized entrant design with deterministic outcomes against every type. The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over those types. In each round, decision variables specify equal masses paired between types; each paired mass advances as the winning type, while the favorite remains one indivisible block of mass \(2^{-R}\). The objective is feasibility: whether some sequence of pairings makes \(f\) the winner.

The objection that survived

The favorite remains an indivisible entrant of mass \(2^{-R}\), so it vanishes as the field grows and the model retains a tagged-player component.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's hardness at sfas-v number \(1\); it leaves the sfas-in and sfas-out hardness results, the FPT results, and the sufficient winning conditions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mass-pairing feasibility problem be solved in time polynomial in \(|\mathcal T|\) and the input bit length when the subset feedback arc number at \(f\) is \(1\)?
  • For masses of the form \(\mu_t=n_t/2^R\), what exact relationship holds between the mass-pairing problem and discrete TFP?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Mass-TFP\(_\infty\): a large knockout tournament whose entrants belong to a small number of deterministic matchup types. Think of a competition with many entrants running a few standardized agent designs. A type specifies the entrant’s result against every other type; two entrants of the same type are interchangeable for the question. When same-type entrants meet, the winner’s identity does not matter to the type-level model: the same type advances.

Let \(G\) be the tournament on the finite type set \(\mathcal T\), with \(\tau=|\mathcal T|\), and let \(f\) be the distinguished favorite. The input is \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a round count \(R\) with \(\mu_f=2^{-R}\). Each of the \(R\) rounds pairs equal masses of current types; a paired mass advances as whichever type beats the other. Same-type pairings return the same type. The favorite’s mass must stay whole: in each round it faces one block of mass \(2^{-R}\), rather than being split across opponents. The question is whether there is a sequence of such pairings in which \(f\) survives all \(R\) rounds. A solution is the round-by-round pairing masses and the favorite’s opponent type in each round. Restrict the question to instances where the type tournament has subset feedback arc number \(1\) at \(f\), using the paper’s definition of cycles through the favorite.

The population regime is many entrants drawn from relatively few known matchup classes, with seeding chosen for the whole field. The masses describe fractions of entrants, not probabilities over outcomes; the rounds and the tournament rule remain discrete. The favorite is a distinguished singleton, represented by mass \(2^{-R}\), while the rest of the population is aggregated by type. This is a high-multiplicity formulation: if the field has \(n=2^R\) entrants, a mass of \(1/n\) corresponds to one entrant.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for an input tournament \(D\) and favorite \(v^\star\), TFP is NP-hard even when the sfas-v number equals \(1\). The theorem’s reduction constructs an equivalent tournament with that parameter value. It gives a clear reason to ask whether hardness survives when opponent multiplicities become rational masses: the discrete question is exactly about arranging the favorite’s sequence of knockout matches, and the proposed mirror retains that sequence while aggregating interchangeable opponents.

I would provisionally expect Mass-TFP\(_\infty\) to be Class C, hard for continuum-specific reasons. The discrete hardness does not establish that verdict: fractional pairing flows may remove constraints that matter in a finite bracket. But the favorite still has to meet a single type at each round, and those choices must fit the changing mass available across the whole tournament. If the type sequence is fixed, the remaining round-by-round pairing constraints are linear. The open algorithmic question is whether those linear feasibility problems can be combined without enumerating an exponential number of favorite paths. That question is directly in reach of continuous optimization, and a polynomial algorithm would instead put the mirror in Class A.

The weakest point is the singleton favorite. The model aggregates the opponents but preserves one named entrant as an indivisible atom; as the field grows, that atom’s mass shrinks. A referee could reasonably ask whether this is a continuous population problem or a continuous bulk surrounding a finite target. I think the mirror remains recognizable because the decision in TFP is inherently about a designated player, and the bulk—the many possible opponents—is what becomes continuous. Still, the model’s value depends on authors accepting fractional pairing of type mass as the right high-multiplicity analogue of assigning entrants to bracket slots.

The further questions are whether Mass-TFP\(_\infty\) has a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length when sfas-v is \(1\); whether rational instances with masses \(n_t/n\) admit an exact dictionary to discrete TFP; and whether analogous mass questions for the paper’s sfas-in and sfas-out hardness results behave differently.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational anchor, so I would not reject the mirror for lack of a result to continuize. The problem is the identity of its target. TFP asks whether one named player wins. In Mass-TFP\(_\infty\), that player has mass \(2^{-R}\). As the field grows and the number of rounds increases, this mass tends to zero. The limiting population distribution therefore loses the favorite, even though the question “does this particular player win?” remains sensitive to them. Keeping the favorite as a separate atom makes a coherent tagged-player problem, but it leaves the proposed mirror as a continuous bulk surrounding a finite target, rather than a continuous population whose distribution captures the question.

Giving the favorite positive mass avoids that vanishing atom only by changing the target to a type: “can some member of this class win?” Then a member can be eliminated by another member while the class still advances. That is a different outcome from the paper’s, and same-type matches cannot preserve the identity of the winner in a type-level description. It may be a worthwhile new tournament problem, but Theorem 1 does not establish it as a mirror of TFP. The opponents’ same-type matches are less troublesome because only their type needs to survive; the difficulty is specific to the named favorite the theorem is about.

A population of standardized entrants with shared deterministic results is imaginable, so I cannot rule out every useful tagged-player formulation. But its appeal depends on accepting that hybrid as a ChoCo continuization: the continuous masses describe who the target might face, while the target’s winning status remains an individual event invisible to the limiting distribution. That is the strongest reason to resist this anchor. It is a real concern, not a no-go theorem, and a reader could reasonably decide the tagged-player question is worth studying anyway.
