What the mirror covers

No computational mirror is anchored. Theorem 1, Lemma 4.3, and Theorem 2 state accuracy guarantees; Algorithm 1 proposes a threshold-based procedure without a stated complexity or optimization guarantee.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is federated learning with many clients in recurring cohorts: clients share data relevance, update behavior, transmission cost, and a role in a repeated delegation topology. A type’s mass is its fraction of clients. The decision is how much vote or update mass to delegate, and the practical objective is to trade communication cost against accuracy and robustness. That fits the paper’s motivation, especially its threshold-based FedVRD proposal.

But I cannot make the requested case rest on a qualifying named result: the paper states no named computational-complexity result. The closest is Theorem 2, proved here: under upward delegation, viscous-retained democracy satisfies do no harm if no guru has \(O(n)\) direct or indirect delegators and Assumption 4.2 holds (the stated edge-count condition and no equal competencies). Theorem 1, also proved here, says viscous democracy fails do no harm under the same assumptions used for liquid democracy. These are guarantees about voting accuracy, not claims that a problem is in P, hard, or has another computational classification. Lemma 4.3 is likewise a do-no-harm statement; Algorithm 1 gives no named complexity guarantee.

One could study a type-mass version of do no harm for viscous-retained democracy, but that would be an analytic extension of these theorems, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The scenario also has a real weakness: delegation topology may make clients’ neighborhood roles part of their complete types, inflating the number of types. And repeated types tend to have equal competencies, contrary to Theorem 2’s stated assumption. Its proof also invokes a positive minimum competence gap, which need not stay bounded away from zero as a high-multiplicity population grows.

So the paper offers a plausible continuous-population setting, but no anchor-compliant continuous problem whose computational status I can honestly classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 says when viscous-retained democracy satisfies do no harm; Theorem 1 says when viscous democracy can fail it; Lemma 4.3 gives a condition for liquid democracy. These are asymptotic accuracy claims, not complexity classifications or algorithmic results. Their statements specify neither a computational input and output nor a running-time question. A continuous-population version of them would be an analytic extension of the paper, outside ChoCo’s computational brief.

The proponent’s repeated-client-cohort scenario is plausible, so lack of multiplicity is not a convincing objection by itself. But a faithful type must include competence, update behavior, transmission cost, adversarial status, and network role. The theorems also assume distinct competencies; in a cohort model, repeated types naturally have equal competencies, and the proof’s positive minimum gap need not persist as the population grows. One could restate the results to allow ties, but that would require new accuracy theorems. More fundamentally, a distribution over individual types does not say which clients are connected: delegation paths, guru loads, and attack leverage depend on that arrangement. Preserving those facts calls for a continuum network model, not just a population distribution.

There is also a limit issue specific to the named results. Accuracy comes from random individual votes. In a continuum of agents, aggregate vote shares tend toward their expected masses, so the winner is typically determined by a deterministic expected margin. That can erase the finite-population fluctuations relevant to the do-no-harm claims. A model that retains those fluctuations would need extra, size-dependent information beyond type masses. That is not an impossible extension, but it is additional analytic modeling, not a computational mirror already posed by these theorems.

The strongest improved anchor is FedVRD’s choice of threshold \(\tau\) to trade communication cost against adversarial robustness. A mass version could ask for an optimal threshold on a continuum network. That sounds worthwhile as a new research question, but the paper does not state a computational problem or result for it. With repeated fixed network motifs, it may reduce to aggregating the motifs’ threshold choices; with arbitrary networks, one must first define a finite representation and an adversary model before complexity can even be assessed. Either way, it would be a new optimization problem built from the paper’s proposal, not a continuous mirror of a result it establishes.

So the negative case is strong for the requested kind of mirror, but not airtight against any adjacent research direction: a continuum-network optimization model could be worth studying in its own right. What the paper does not supply is a named computational finding for such a mirror to continue.
