The continuous mirror question

Given local electoral cohort types \(t\) with population masses \(\mu_t\), per-resident Republican-minus-Democratic margins \(a_t\), and a target population share \(q\), does there exist a selection \(x_t \in [0,\mu_t]\) such that \(\sum_t x_t \ge q\) and \(\sum_t a_t x_t > 0\)?

The model it lives in

Represent a large population by a distribution \(\mu\) over homogeneous local electoral profiles, with each profile recording the attributes relevant to the arithmetic question. Choose mass \(x_t\) from each profile to form a proposed group, and maximize its net vote margin subject to the target-mass and type-capacity constraints; fractional selection of a profile is allowed.

What the mirror covers

Covers a continuous analogue of Theorem 1's numerical majority-group bounds. It leaves whole-precinct feasibility, partitioning into nine districts, contiguity, spatial clustering, and the paper's empirical Massachusetts conclusions unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the continuous optimum compare with the whole-precinct feasibility bounds for finite instances?
  • Can the model be extended to select several disjoint district-sized groups while preserving a meaningful spatial constraint?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Numerical Representability. Its type is a homogeneous local electoral cohort: it specifies a local distribution of ballots and any other attributes relevant to this arithmetic question. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s share of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(a_t\) be its expected number of Republican votes minus Democratic votes per resident. The cohort profiles stay fixed when a portion of a type is assigned to a group. This captures local variation in vote share while allowing population mass to be divided continuously.

Given a target population share \(q\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), where \(x_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to a proposed group. The question is whether there is a choice with
\[ \sum_t x_t\ge q \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t a_t x_t>0. \]
A solution is such a vector \(x\). Equivalently, maximize \(\sum_t a_t x_t\) subject to the mass and type-capacity constraints, and ask whether the optimum is positive. This is a linear program; sorting types by \(a_t\) gives an exact algorithm in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). Allowing a fractional portion of the cutoff type removes the indivisibility gap.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper’s appendix. It is a named algorithmic feasibility result, though the paper does not state it as a formal “in P” classification. In the theorem’s notation, order units by decreasing \(\delta_i/p_i\), let \(D_k\) be the first \(k\) units, and let \(K\) be the first index with \(\delta(D_K)\le 0\). For a target size \(M\), the theorem says: if \(M\le p(D_{K-1})\), an R-majority grouping of size at least \(M\) exists; if \(p(D_{K-1})<M\le p(D_K)\), the test is inconclusive; and if \(p(D_K)<M\), no such grouping exists. The continuous problem mirrors that numerical feasibility question and its margin-per-capita ordering, while making the boundary exact by permitting a fractional cutoff.

A plausible regime is a large electorate represented by many residents but a much smaller number of recurring local electoral profiles—for example, millions of residents summarized by hundreds or thousands of precinct-scale cohorts. The population is the mass of residents; the action is assigning portions of those cohorts to a proposed district-sized group; the objective is its net Republican vote margin. For the paper’s nine-seat example, \(q\) could be \(1/9\). This is a population continuization, with local electoral profiles retained as types.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous counterpart to their arithmetic feasibility analysis. Theorem 1 deliberately ignores geography and asks whether a sufficiently large grouping can have a Republican majority. This mirror keeps that question narrow. It does not claim to construct a partition into equal districts, impose contiguity, or reproduce the paper’s geometric analysis. Its further questions include the continuous feasibility of several disjoint districts, adding spatial constraints, and comparing the continuous optimum with the whole-precinct answer.

The weak point is that the paper’s striking lockouts depend in part on whole towns or precincts being indivisible. Allowing portions of local cohorts can change which groups are feasible, so this mirror should be presented as a relaxation or limit model, not as a replacement for the empirical conclusion. It remains a credible mirror of Theorem 1’s specific numerical question; it does not establish a continuous mirror for the paper’s full districting problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed LP changes what can be assigned. Theorem 1 orders indivisible geographic units by \(\delta_i/p_i\). Its “grouping” is a union of whole towns or precincts. In the proposed mirror, \(x_t\) can select any fraction of a local profile. If each precinct is a type, that permits cutting precincts; if types are individual ballots, it permits selecting voters without respecting the precincts that create the observed variation. Neither choice preserves the paper’s building-block constraint.

That matters even in the numerical section, which drops contiguity but not unit integrity. A fractional group can have a positive margin and enough mass while no corresponding union of whole precincts does. It also need not split into the required number of equal-population districts; the paper explicitly treats its grouping bounds as insufficient to guarantee that. Theorem 1 itself is a certificate theorem with an inconclusive interval, not an exact complexity classification. The LP therefore answers a cleaner fractional relaxation, rather than the paper’s exact grouping question.

The best repair is to model many small, interchangeable local units with the same profile. Then the mass variable can represent fractions of a large cohort of units, while preserving local vote variation. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors could recognize it as a useful continuous bound. It does not settle their Massachusetts instance, where the permitted atoms are the observed precincts, but that limitation alone does not disqualify the mirror.

So this is a real objection to the mirror’s fidelity, not a decisive case that no worthwhile mirror exists. I cannot honestly defeat the anchor across all plausible scenarios: a repeated-cohort regime makes the continuous numerical question sensible, and the paper’s arithmetic result gives it a clear point of contact. The negative case is weak precisely where the prompt says it must be strongest.
