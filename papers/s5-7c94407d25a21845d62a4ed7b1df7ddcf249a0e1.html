The continuous mirror question

For each scale \(N=q^4\), fix an atomless society with speed masses \(\mu_1=\mu_\alpha=1/2\), speeds \(\{1,\alpha\}\), and \(\alpha=\sqrt{N}/4\). Per unit of population, provide \(s_{\mathrm{long}}=N^{-1/2}\) jobs of length \(\alpha\) and \(s_{\mathrm{short}}=1/2+N^{-1/4}\) jobs of length \(1\). A feasible randomized oracle takes the aggregate report measure and a tagged infinitesimal agent's report, and returns a distribution over measurable schedules; each agent receives at most one integral job, and every realized schedule respects the aggregate supplies. Given oracle access to any such algorithm \(A\) with expected makespan \(O(1)\), does a uniform transformation using \(\operatorname{poly}(L,\tau,|J|)\) queries exist that returns only queried schedule kernels and produces a BIC mechanism with expected makespan at most \(N^{o(1)}\phi(A)\)? The tagged agent's deviation leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, and BIC is defined by its expected payment-minus-processing-time utility.

The model it lives in

An atomless fleet described by speed-type masses \(\mu\), with per-capita supplies of integral jobs and a randomized kernel over feasible schedules. Each infinitesimal agent's report selects its lottery without changing the population measure; BIC constrains its expected utility, while makespan is evaluated over the full schedule distribution.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4.1 relies on finite empirical-profile fluctuations and \(1/N\) deviations, which disappear for a fixed atomless profile; a new kernel-based argument is needed to establish any lower bound there.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does any black-box query lower bound persist when the finite iid profile is replaced by a fixed atomless mass profile and the oracle exposes full schedule kernels?
  • Can type-level schedule kernels enforce BIC while preserving expected makespan within a subpolynomial factor?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Theorem 4.1’s Bayesian makespan result. The natural regime is a large pool of interchangeable machines: each machine has the same scheduling constraints and differs only in speed, so there are many machines but just two types. For example, a large compute fleet might contain many machines in each of two performance tiers, processing a large supply of short and long jobs. A type is a speed \(v\in\{1,\alpha\}\); its mass \(\mu_v\) is the fraction of machines in that tier.

The anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says there are constants \(c_1,c_2\) such that every polynomial-time black-box BIC transformation \(T\) has a makespan instance \((F,F)\) and an algorithm \(A\) with expected makespan \(\phi(A)\le c_1\), but \(\phi(T(A))\ge c_2 n^{1/4}\). The construction uses \(n\) machines with independently drawn speeds in \(\{1,\alpha\}\), where \(\alpha=\sqrt n/4\); \(\sqrt n\) jobs have length \(\alpha\), and \(n/2+n^{3/4}\) jobs have length \(1\). Each machine can receive at most one job. The theorem is about the difficulty of converting algorithms through a black box, not about the hardness of finding an optimal schedule.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: High-Multiplicity BIC Makespan Conversion. Take \(N=q^4\) machine-equivalents, with \(q\) even and large. Their society is the mass distribution \(\mu=(\mu_1,\mu_\alpha)\), where \(\mu_\alpha=j/N\) and \(j\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(N,1/2)\). The per-machine job supplies are \(1/q^2\) long jobs of length \(\alpha=q^2/4\) and \(1/2+1/q\) short jobs of length \(1\). An allocation is a randomized mass assignment \(y_{v,J}\): \(y_{v,J}\) is the mass of speed-\(v\) machines receiving a job of class \(J\). It must satisfy \(\sum_J y_{v,J}\le\mu_v\) and \(\sum_v y_{v,J}=s_J\), where \(s_J\) is that class’s supply. Each machine still receives at most one integral job; the aggregate mass assigned to each class may be fractional. The objective is expected makespan, the maximum \(\ell_J/v\) among machine/job pairs receiving positive mass.

Given black-box access to a constant-expected-makespan allocation algorithm \(A\), the task is to produce a BIC direct mechanism \(B\), using only allocations observed in polynomially many oracle queries, with minimum possible expected makespan. BIC is defined for a tagged machine of mass \(1/N\): changing its report changes the society masses by \(1/N\), and truthful reporting must maximize its expected utility over the prior on the other machines. Equivalently in this single-parameter scheduling setting, the expected workload assigned to the tagged machine must be nondecreasing with its reported speed. The computational question is whether a uniform transformation can achieve loss \(N^{o(1)}\), with running time polynomial in the binary input length \(L\), the support size \(\tau=2\), and the job-type count. Here \(N\) can be exponentially large in \(L\), as expected in a high-multiplicity model.

This is a close analogue of the paper’s problem: it keeps makespan, speed reporting, BIC, oracle access, and the requirement to return an observed feasible allocation. Normalizing the job supplies per machine and describing assignments by type-level masses are the co-modifications needed to represent a large fleet; they do not replace the population as the continuous object. I expect hardness to transfer on the embedded rational-mass instances: the anchor already gives a severe barrier in a two-type, symmetric machine environment, and binary-rational masses make exhaustive enumeration of population compositions especially costly. That expectation is a research question, though; Theorem 4.1 itself does not establish the continuous result.

My main reservation is that the paper’s gap uses randomness in finite population compositions. A single fixed continuum society would concentrate at \(\mu=(1/2,1/2)\), and the sublinear job supplies and composition fluctuations driving the construction could disappear. I have therefore kept the Bayesian prior over society masses and the \(1/N\) tagged-machine deviation in the mirror. If a referee requires one deterministic limiting mass profile with no such finite-multiplicity structure, this anchor becomes much weaker. The sharp follow-up is whether arbitrary rational-mass oracle queries or fractional aggregate assignments can bypass the lower bound, or whether the \(\Omega(N^{1/4})\) barrier survives this high-multiplicity formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is Theorem 4.1, and it is the strongest one to attack. Its difficulty lives in a finite Bayesian profile: each machine has a named allocation, BIC compares that machine’s allocation under different reports, and the oracle transformation must work across random empirical profiles. The proposed mirror keeps all three features by giving one machine mass \(1/N\) and drawing \(\mu_\alpha=j/N\) from a binomial law. That makes it a compressed representation of the finite model. It does not yet show what remains when the population itself becomes the continuous object.

There is a real modeling fork here. Fix an atomless society at \(\mu=(1/2,1/2)\), and the binomial fluctuations that the construction uses are gone. Keep the fluctuations, the \(1/N\) deviation, and the supplies \(N^{-1/2}\) and \(N^{-1/4}\), and the model retains the finite population’s lattice and sampling noise. The latter may be a sensible high multiplicity problem, but Theorem 4.1’s lower bound does not by itself establish a result about a fixed continuous society. That distinction matters especially because the programme asks for the population to be continuous, rather than merely encoded as rational proportions.

The proposed mass assignment also needs a more precise oracle interface. Aggregate masses \(y_{v,J}\) do not, by themselves, specify how a randomized schedule treats a tagged machine or the probability of a high-makespan outcome. In a makespan objective, any positive mass of long jobs on slow machines can make the makespan \(\alpha\); expected makespan depends on the distribution over schedules, not just the expected masses. A proper repair could give the oracle a randomized allocation kernel and define each machine’s interim workload from it. But that is additional structure, and it must preserve the tagged agent’s incentive constraints as well as the aggregate schedule.

The best repaired question would be an anonymous, high multiplicity BIC conversion problem with a specified kernel from reports and population masses to per-agent job lotteries, plus a clear representation of the prior and oracle outputs. That is a coherent question. The paper itself notes that, when symmetry can be used, sorting machines by speed and allocations by size repairs its particular algorithm while preserving makespan. So the stated lower bound is not evidence for a barrier in that more structured setting; a prover would need a new argument for the repaired model.

This is a substantial objection to the proposed transfer of Theorem 4.1, but it does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Once the kernel and prior are specified, a high multiplicity version remains plausible and computationally distinct. The honest negative case is therefore weak: the proposed mirror needs sharper modelling, but the anchor likely survives as a research question.
