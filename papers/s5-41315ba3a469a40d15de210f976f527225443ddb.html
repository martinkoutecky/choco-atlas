The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive prospect is a continuous version of the paper’s core-partition question. But the paper has no numbered computational result of the kind the brief requires as an anchor. Its algorithm for finding core partitions is presented without a complexity classification. The numbered results—including Theorem 1—are structural stability results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. So I cannot make a compliant anchor-based case.

A plausible regime would be a large population of small firms considering joint ventures, with finitely many firm types. A type fixes everything relevant to coalition payoffs; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of firms of type \(t\). A coalition is described by its composition \(x\), and \(p_t(x)\) is the profit per member of type \(t\). A society’s coalition structure is a partition of this mass. Its core question is whether some selected masses from existing coalitions could regroup and make every participant strictly better off.

That could be posed as a Core-Mass Partition problem: given rational type masses \(\mu\), per-type payoff functions \(p_t(x)\) with efficiency and PMAS monotonicity, and a candidate mass partition, decide whether any blocking regrouping exists. This is a plausible population mirror of the paper’s core-partition work. It is not, however, anchored by a numbered computational result, so it should be treated as a research question suggested by the paper—not as a result this paper establishes or studies computationally.

For reference, the closest numbered hook is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that for every discount-factor vector and every absorbing deterministic EPCF, the grand coalition is EPCF-stable; the grand coalition is the only EPCF-stable partition exactly when \(N\) has no extreme coalitions. It also says that if \(S\subset N\) is an extreme coalition, then \(\{S,N\setminus S\}\) is EPCF-stable, and if \(T\subset N\setminus S\) is extreme in \(N\setminus S\), then \(\{S,T,N\setminus(S\cup T)\}\), \(\{S\cup T,N\setminus(S\cup T)\}\), and \(\{N\setminus T,T\}\) are also EPCF-stable. Those claims may motivate continuous stability questions, but they do not supply the required computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own concession is the strongest point against a mirror: this paper states no numbered complexity result for a computational problem. The core-partition algorithm has no complexity classification, and Theorem 1 characterizes stability in terms of extreme coalitions; neither claims an algorithmic upper or lower bound. A continuous question inspired by either result could be new research, but it would not mirror a computational result established here.

The best repair is the proposed large-firm setting. Suppose firms fall into finitely many complete types, coalition payoffs depend only on coalition composition, and a coalition structure is represented by a distribution over compositions. One could ask whether a positive mass of firms can regroup so that every participant gains. This is coherent, and it gives the paper’s core-partition algorithm a continuous analogue. But it adds anonymity and composition-based payoffs that the paper does not assume. More importantly, it does not yet define a computational problem: the payoff functions and the distribution representing a partition need finite encodings. Choosing piecewise-linear functions, a finite basis, or a restricted family of partitions could make the problem computable, but each choice determines a different problem and potentially its complexity. The result would be a new algorithmic study motivated by the paper, rather than a continuization of a result in it.

Theorem 1 is the strongest apparent hook, but its core condition becomes awkward in this setting. For a positive-mass coalition \(x\), “extreme” could mean that each participating type earns the grand-coalition payoff at \(x\). Yet in the paper, EPCF moves named players among partitions, and the theorem quantifies over every absorbing deterministic process. With divisible masses, a type can be split among many coalitions, so a state must encode a distribution over compositions and a move may transfer arbitrary portions of several types. A finite representation of those states and transitions would need additional restrictions. The literal theorem supplies no canonical computational question over them.

These problems do not establish that no useful continuous model exists. The firm-type model is plausible, and its core question could be worth studying on its own. The negative case is strongest on programme fit: the paper offers structural stability results, while a computational mirror requires choices of type symmetry, function encoding, partition representation, and allowed deviations that the paper does not settle. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario; I can say the proposed mirror is a substantial new modelling and complexity project, not a computational result this paper already gives the programme a natural anchor to continue.
