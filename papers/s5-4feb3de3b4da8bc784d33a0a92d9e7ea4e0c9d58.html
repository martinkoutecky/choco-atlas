The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a two-period high-multiplicity mirror, with a natural extension to the paper’s full dynamic-stability notion. One important qualification is that this paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or other formal complexity result. Its relevant anchors are existence and algorithmic guarantees, so the proposed mirror asks whether those guarantees become computationally meaningful after continuization.

The natural regime is a large dynamic admissions or hiring market. In each of two or more rounds, many workers and positions arrive, but only finitely many complete types occur: side, cohort, exact utility over opposite-side types, discount factor, and any other payoff-relevant characteristic. A type therefore means genuine interchangeability, not merely a shared label. If there are \(N\) agents but only \(\tau\) types, the input records rational masses \(a_{t,\theta}\) and \(b_{t,\phi}\), rather than individual names. A mass \(x_{t,\theta,\phi}\) means that a set of measure \(x_{t,\theta,\phi}\) of individual agents receives one integral match with the opposite type; it is not a fractional assignment to an individual. Irreversibility requires matched mass to remain matched in every later history.

My lead anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It states: when \(T=2\), if \(\mada\) is the dynamic version of side-\(A\)-proposing deferred acceptance—every \(A\)-agent proposes using their intertemporal preferences and every \(B\)-agent evaluates proposals using their intertemporal preferences—then, for every \(a\in A_1\), there exists a conjectured matching \(\matchingb\in M_{\ds}(a,\mada,\economyr^1)\) such that \(U(a,\mada,\economyr^1)\geq U(a,\matchingb,\economyr^1)\). In full, the result says that every first-period \(A\)-agent has some dynamically admissible conjecture under which waiting to participate cannot improve their payoff. The proposition is proved here, using the matching obtained by running the same dynamic DA algorithm after moving \(a\) from period one to period two.

The corresponding continuous problem is Two-Period Continuous Dynamic-DA Participation. An instance contains finite type sets \(\Theta_A,\Theta_B\), rational two-period arrival masses, rational utilities \(u_\theta(\phi)\) and \(v_\phi(\theta)\), discount factors, and optionally an explicitly represented finite distribution over aggregate arrival histories. The decision variable is a contingent mass-matching plan \(x\), with \(x_{t,h}(\theta,\phi)\) bounded by the currently available unmatched mass of each type and fixed thereafter by irreversibility.

The plan \(\mada_\infty\) is obtained by running capacitated side-\(A\) deferred acceptance: all \(A\)-mass from both cohorts proposes to \(B\)-mass in the relevant intertemporal order, and each \(B\)-type retains its most preferred offers. For every \(A\)-mass unit present in period one, the output must also provide a conjecture \(y_i\) that leaves that unit unmatched in period one, has a statically stable period-one matching among agents who exit, and has a statically stable period-two continuation. The required certificate is \(U_i(\mada_\infty)\geq U_i(y_i)\) for every such unit. Equivalently, the problem asks either for the mass matching and all admissible no-waiting certificates, or for a type and conjecture witnessing profitable delay.

I expect this problem to be Class A for explicitly represented two-period instances. The continuous DA computation is a capacitated version of the same algorithm, and the conjecture used in the paper’s proof is another capacitated DA computation after moving an infinitesimal unit to the second cohort. With rational masses, the natural representation has polynomially many type-pair variables, and one can use flow or LP machinery to handle the mass constraints. This remains the authors’ question: agents still arrive over time, matching is one-to-one and irreversible, utilities are discounted, and the conjecture must satisfy the paper’s dynamic-stability conditions. Only redundant individual labels have disappeared.

A second, broader anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for every finite \(T\), the correspondence \(\ds_T\) is nonempty-valued. The proof recursively chooses payoff-minimizing admissible conjectures, truncates preferences using those continuation values, runs deferred acceptance, and constructs a dynamically stable matching by induction on the horizon. The theorem is not an efficient algorithmic result; indeed, the paper explicitly leaves open whether an algorithm exists for \(T\geq3\).

Its continuous mirror would be Continuous Dynamic-Stability Search. The input is a finite type universe, rational arrival masses or an explicit finite scenario tree, utilities and discount factors, and a horizon \(T\). A solution is a complete contingent mass-matching plan \(x\), together with a conjecture \(y_i\) for every currently available infinitesimal agent \(i\), such that:

  • \(x\) is feasible and irreversible;
  • at every history, no contemporaneously available pair prefers matching together to its prescribed outcome;
  • for every available agent \(i\), some admissible conjecture \(y_i\) leaves \(i\) unmatched now, has a dynamically stable continuation, and gives \(i\) no more than the prescribed plan.

The objective is feasibility: output any dynamically stable mass policy and its conjecture certificates. This is not a welfare optimization problem, because the paper’s stability notion has no welfare objective.

For \(T=2\), I would again expect Class A. For unrestricted \(T\), however, I would treat the problem as a candidate Class C boundary case. The paper’s Proposition 1 shows that dynamically stable matchings need not have the lattice structure of static stable matchings, while the proof of Theorem 1 repeatedly minimizes over sets of admissible continuation matchings. That recursive selection may remain difficult even after individual multiplicity has been compressed. If hardness appears there, it would be continuum-specific rather than transferred from a discrete population-size reduction: the combinatorics would live in the type-level continuation and conjecture structure.

The mirror is plausible because the paper’s own Appendix C explicitly allows agents with repeated characteristics and says that the main results do not depend on the assumption that characteristics are unique. A market with thousands of students and seats but finitely many preference, priority, and timing types is therefore a credible high-multiplicity version of exactly this model. The mirror covers Proposition 3 emphatically and, in its broader form, Theorem 1; it does not claim to cover every implementation or participation result in the paper.

The weakest point is the treatment of an individual deviation in a nonatomic population. Removing one infinitesimal agent does not change aggregate masses, so a careless continuum model could erase precisely the externality that makes the paper’s waiting problem interesting. The mirror must therefore retain tagged infinitesimal agents and their individual conjectures, while using mass flows only to represent the surrounding market. If instead one allows only positive-mass cohort deviations, that is a new coalition-level problem and should be labelled as such. This issue is serious, but it is a modelling refinement rather than a reason to reject the continuous mirror outright.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is prior to the proposed mirrors themselves: this paper contains no named computational result in the programme’s sense. Theorem 1 is a nonemptiness theorem, not a complexity or algorithm theorem. Proposition 3 is an incentive property of a specified deferred-acceptance procedure, not a runtime or optimization result. Theorem 2 is an implementation characterization. None specifies an input encoding, computational task, complexity bound, approximation guarantee, or parameterized algorithm. Thus the proposed “continuous problems” would largely be new problems invented around qualitative results, rather than continuous mirrors of computational results in the paper.

Proposition 3 is especially problematic because its substantive content is precisely an atomic deviation. For a named \(a\in A_1\), the proof removes \(a\), moves that individual to period \(2\), reruns deferred acceptance, and compares \(a\)’s payoff under the two outcomes. In a society represented only by masses, removing one member of a type changes its mass by \(1/N\), which disappears as \(N\to\infty\). At the continuum state \(\mu\), the deviation produces the same aggregate state and therefore does not determine a different continuation economy.

The proponent’s proposed “certificate for every infinitesimal unit” does not solve this. A mass plan \(x\) does not identify which infinitesimal unit receives which partner, nor which continuation matching that particular unit conjectures after becoming unavailable. One can impose anonymous tie-breaking and require a common type-level conjecture, but that is a new price-taking stability concept. It removes the individual residual-economy externality that Proposition 3 is about.

The suggested repair—one tagged agent against a continuous background—is coherent, but it is not a continuous-population mirror in the programme’s sense. The interesting deviator is then an atom with an identity, while the continuum is merely its environment. The result is a finite-agent DA argument with capacitated background markets. If a positive mass is moved to the next cohort instead, the problem is no longer Proposition 3: it becomes a coalition-deviation question, and the paper’s strategy-proofness argument does not establish it. Thus the anchor is either undefined in a pure mass model or changed into a different model when repaired.

Theorem 1 has the same, deeper problem. Its proof repeatedly chooses, for each named agent \(k\), a payoff-minimizing complete contingent matching \(\bar m^k\) from a finite set of matchings. Dynamic stability then permits different agents to use different conjectures, and explicitly does not require common beliefs. The conjecture depends on the residual named agents, the matching history, and the continuation matching after \(k\) alone becomes unavailable.

A mass vector does not contain that information. To recover it, one must add a measurable family of agent-specific conjectures, an allocation rule for splitting identical types across partners, and a rule determining how a tagged unit’s removal affects continuation matching. The proponent’s proposed output therefore contains an uncountable family \(y_i\), not a finite computational certificate. If all units of a type are forced to share one conjecture, that adds common-belief and anonymity assumptions absent from the paper. If they are allowed separate conjectures, the type-level representation has failed to compress the object that drives the theorem.

Appendix C does not establish otherwise. It permits repeated characteristics, but it still indexes agents individually by \(i\) and \(j\), keeps arrival sets \(I_t,J_t\), defines matchings on those individuals, and evaluates \(U(i,\cdot)\) and \(V(j,\cdot)\). It shows that finite replicated instances are legitimate; it does not show that dynamic stability is invariant under quotienting those instances into masses. In particular, it does not eliminate the one-agent residual economy used throughout the definition and proof.

One could certainly formulate a new anonymous, finite-type, price-taking dynamic matching model with mass flows and common type-level continuation policies. That might be worthwhile market-design theory. But it would require new primitives and a new stability notion; it would not be a computational mirror of either Proposition 3 or Theorem 1. The proponent’s further claim that the unrestricted version is a candidate Class C problem is also premature: the paper supplies no finite computational problem whose complexity could be classified.

This negative case is not based on whether continuity would help, whether the answer would be easy, or whether the high-multiplicity story is plausible. The school-admissions story is plausible. The obstruction is that the paper’s anchors derive their content from atomic, identity-specific deviations and agent-specific conjectures, while the proposed continuous representation removes or must artificially reinsert exactly those objects. The paper therefore offers no worthwhile continuous-population computational mirror on the terms proposed.
