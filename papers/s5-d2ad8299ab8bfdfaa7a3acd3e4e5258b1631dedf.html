The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of roommate types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and each type’s strict ranking of at most three acceptable types, choose a stable mass assignment \(x\) maximizing the mass \(p_1(x)\) of residents matched to their first-ranked type. Require \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}=\mu_t\); for mutually acceptable types \(t,u\), require \(d_{t,u}(x)=0\) or \(d_{u,t}(x)=0\), where \(d_{t,u}(x)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}y_{tv}\), \(y_{tu}=x_{tu}\) for \(t\ne u\), and \(y_{tt}=2x_{tt}\). Equivalently, decide whether a stable assignment exists with \(p_1(x)\ge q\) for a rational threshold \(q\).

The model it lives in

A population distribution over roommate preference types, with rational type masses and mass \(x_{tu}\) of pairs between types \(t\) and \(u\). Every unit of mass receives one roommate; stability rules out mutually blocking positive masses, and the objective maximizes the mass receiving a first-choice type.

The objection that survived

The paper gives no evidence that housing cohorts rank compatibility types and are indifferent among members, so the authors’ recognition of this changed preference domain remains the weakest link.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the short-list hardness results for first-choice-maximal, rank-maximal, and generous SRI. It leaves the egalitarian and minimum-regret results, the inapproximability and parameterized results, and the finite-agent solver experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of maximizing \(p_1(x)\) as a function of the number of types \(\tau\), the maximum list length, and the mass bit length?
  • When do optimal stable mass assignments require fractional splitting of a type across several roommate types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror here is a typed, high multiplicity roommates problem. Imagine a large university housing system: thousands of residents, but a much smaller set of recurring profiles, defined by the preferences and compatibility rules that matter for roommate assignment. Each type \(t\) has a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a strict ranking of at most three acceptable *types*. Residents of a type are interchangeable. The relevant regime has \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the number of residents and \(\tau\) the number of distinct types.

A solution is a symmetric mass assignment \(x\), where \(x_{tu}\) is the mass of pairs between types \(t\) and \(u\); \(x_{tt}\) is the mass of pairs within type \(t\). Require
\[ 2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}=\mu_t \]
for every \(t\), so everyone is matched. This is a reasonable housing regime and matches the hardness gadgets’ fully matched setting. Each resident still gets one roommate; it is the *aggregate* that can split, so a type may have fractions assigned to different roommate types.

For stability, let \(y_{tu}=x_{tu}\) when \(t\ne u\), and \(y_{tt}=2x_{tt}\). For mutually acceptable types \(t,u\), define
\[ d_{t,u}(x)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v} y_{tv}, \]
the mass of type \(t\) assigned to someone it likes less than \(u\). Require \(d_{t,u}(x)=0\) or \(d_{u,t}(x)=0\) for every such pair. If both quantities were positive, some positive mass on each side could form blocking pairs. This is weak stability for agents indifferent among interchangeable members of the same type. Define \(p_r(x)\) as the mass of residents assigned their \(r\)-th-ranked type. These are profile counts per capita.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: deciding whether an SRI instance with preference lists of length at most \(3\) has a stable matching with at least \(K\) first choices is NP-complete; consequently, maximizing first choices in 3-FC-SRI is NP-hard. Its typed continuous mirror is 3-FC-SRI\(_\infty\): given \(T\), the rational masses, and the type rankings, find a stable mass matching maximizing \(p_1(x)\), or decide whether one exists with \(p_1(x)\ge q\) for a rational threshold \(q\). The solution is the mass assignment \(x\), not a fractional roommate for any individual. This is the cleanest anchor because the objective becomes the familiar per-capita statement “what fraction of residents gets a first-choice roommate?”

Two further anchors concern other profile objectives. Theorem 2, proved here, says rank-maximal SRI is NP-hard even when every list has length at most \(3\). Its mirror asks for a stable \(x\) whose profile \((p_1(x),\ldots,p_\ell(x))\) is lexicographically maximum, where \(\ell\) is the longest type list. Theorem 3, also proved here, says generous SRI is NP-hard with lists of length at most \(3\). Its mirror asks for a stable \(x\) whose reverse profile \((p_\ell(x),\ldots,p_1(x))\) is lexicographically minimum. The paper’s generous proof adapts a construction from Cseh et al. and also gives a proof based on its own gadget. These are not independent modeling arguments: they use the same typed population and stability notion, but ask different, recognizable questions about which stable allocation to choose.

For all three, my prior is Class C, continuum-specific hardness, though it is a hypothesis to investigate rather than a consequence of the paper’s reductions. The blocking conditions leave a choice about which side of each type pair must have no residents who prefer to deviate; those choices can retain combinatorial structure as \(\tau\) grows, even when mass is divisible. The first question is whether that structure admits an efficient description or optimization method in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length.

This would cover the paper’s first-choice, rank-maximal and generous hardness results in a large-cohort regime. It does not continuize the outcome: a resident still receives one partner. Nor does the paper’s IP/CP work already answer these questions; its variables describe named agents and binary pairs, rather than a population distribution over types.

The weakest point is the type model. The paper assumes strict rankings over named agents, while this mirror assumes residents rank cohorts and are indifferent among members of a cohort. That is a real co-modification. It is plausible for a large housing system organized around recurring compatibility profiles, but it will need a convincing application for the authors to recognize it as their problem. There is also a substantive complexity risk: the vertex-cover gadgets encode all-or-nothing choices, and divisible cohorts may mix those choices, weakening the reduction. I would not claim hardness transfers. That is precisely why these questions are useful: they ask whether profile-based stable matching becomes tractable when the population, rather than the individuals or their roommates, is the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that they all depend on one consequential change to the problem: replacing rankings of named people with rankings of roommate types. The paper’s stability condition is about specific pairs of agents. A repeated type in the proposed model instead means its members are interchangeable and indifferent among people in the same category; stability then means that two positive masses cannot both prefer to re-pair. That is a coherent model, but it is a weakly stable, type-based roommates problem. It is not merely the paper’s strict, individual-level SRI with a larger population.

If types retain the paper’s full preference information, that distinction is hard to avoid. An agent’s type must record whom they find acceptable and how they rank those people. In a roommate instance where preferences are genuinely about particular people, those records can be nearly unique, so \(\tau\) grows with the population and little multiplicity is gained. Compressing people into compatibility categories restores repeated types, but changes the preference domain and the blocking relation. That co-modification is permitted in principle; the adverse case is that the paper itself gives little reason to think its authors would regard this particular version as their problem.

For Theorem 1, the first-choice objective does carry over cleanly as a per-capita quantity. But the vertex-cover reduction encodes its choices in the named agents’ local preference lists and their particular links to other agents. Preserving that information requires retaining a type for each relevant role, while allowing a type’s mass to split among roommate types removes the reduction’s all-or-nothing gadget choices. The theorem therefore supplies no evidence that the typed mass problem is the same problem at high multiplicity. This is not an argument that the new problem is easy or that its answer would be uninteresting; it is a reason to doubt that Theorem 1 motivates this mirror.

The same objection applies to Theorem 2 and Theorem 3. Rank-maximality and generosity are natural profile objectives for masses, but their hardness proofs still depend on the same gadget-level assignments and rank counts. In the proposed mirror, those counts become fractional, and the stable allocation may divide each type across several partner types. The reductions do not establish that their profile thresholds survive that change. A refined mirror could allow unmatched mass and define rank levels over partner categories, but those repairs leave the central issue untouched: the paper’s results concern choosing among stable matchings of individually ranked people, while the mirror chooses among mass allocations under category-level preferences.

A stronger version of the proponents’ scenario is plausible: a large housing system where residents rank a fixed set of compatibility profiles and are indifferent among residents in each profile. That gives a meaningful high-multiplicity question, and fractional profile counts are exactly the right quantities there. But it is also the point where my negative case is weakest. The programme explicitly allows population-driven co-modifications, and this scenario could pass its test that the authors recognize the resulting question as theirs. I can argue that the three theorems do not themselves secure that connection; I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile typed mirror or prove that no such scenario would interest the authors.
