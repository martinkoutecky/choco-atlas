The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of worker types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, a finite deadline set \(D\), and rational means \(a_{td}=\mathbb{E}[\min\{X_t,d\}]>0\) and \(q_{td}=\mathbb{E}[R_t(d)]\), choose a stationary lottery \(p_{td}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_{t,d}p_{td}=1\), to maximize \(F_\alpha(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\frac{(\rho_t(p)/\mu_t)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\), using the logarithmic limit at \(\alpha=1\), where \(\rho_t(p)=\frac{\sum_d p_{td}q_{td}}{\sum_{j,d}p_{jd}a_{jd}}\).

The model it lives in

Types are worker group-and-performance-law classes, each carrying population mass \(\mu_t\). The controller chooses a type and deadline for each task using lottery \(p\); the objective is population-average \(\alpha\)-fair utility of per-member reward rates \(\rho_t(p)/\mu_t\).

The objection that survived

The opponent's strongest point is that \(\mu_t\) changes only the welfare weights and leaves the feasible lotteries unchanged; the proponent acknowledges the per-member objective but does not answer that it is mathematically a reparameterization of Proposition 5.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow population-weighted version of the paper’s offline stationary allocation result.

My lead anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper. For each group \(k\), it says the optimal deadline \(t_k^*\) maximizes expected reward divided by expected processing time, \(r_k^*\) is that maximum rate, and \(\mu_k\) is the mean processing time at that deadline. For every \(\alpha>0\), it gives the optimal stationary randomized policy explicitly:
\[ P^\star(k,t)=\mathbb{I}\{t=t_k^*\} \frac{w_k^{1/\alpha}(r_k^*)^{1/\alpha-1}/\mu_k} {\sum_j w_j^{1/\alpha}(r_j^*)^{1/\alpha-1}/\mu_j}, \qquad \varphi_k= \frac{(r_k^*)^{1/\alpha-1}w_k^{1/\alpha}} {\sum_j(r_j^*)^{1/\alpha-1}w_j^{1/\alpha}}. \]
It also gives the corresponding optimal \(\alpha\)-fair utility as
\[ \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \left(\sum_k (r_k^*)^{1/\alpha-1}w_k^{1/\alpha}\right)^\alpha. \]
The paper’s Proposition 2 supplies an \(O(1/B)\) finite-budget correction to the stationary policy’s limiting rates. Proposition 5 is a closed-form algorithmic result, though it does not state a formal polynomial-time bound.

A concrete mirror is Population-Weighted Continuous-Time \(\alpha\)-Fair Allocation. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of worker types, with rational population shares \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one; a finite deadline set \(D\); and, for every type \(t\) and deadline \(d\), rational inputs
\[ a_{td}=\mathbb{E}[\min\{X_t,d\}]>0,\qquad q_{td}=\mathbb{E}[R_t(d)]. \]
A type completely specifies the worker’s group and reward/completion-time law. For each task the controller chooses a type and deadline by a stationary lottery \(p_{td}\), where \(p_{td}\geq 0\) and \(\sum_{t,d}p_{td}=1\). The resulting aggregate reward rate for type \(t\) is
\[ \rho_t(p)= \frac{\sum_d p_{td}q_{td}} {\sum_{j,d}p_{jd}a_{jd}}. \]
The population’s average per-member rate for that type is \(\rho_t(p)/\mu_t\). For fixed \(\alpha>0\), the question is to find a lottery maximizing
\[ F_\alpha(p)= \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \frac{(\rho_t(p)/\mu_t)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}, \]
with the logarithmic limit used at \(\alpha=1\). A solution is an optimal lottery and its objective value.

This is a plausible hiring-platform regime: a very large workforce falls into a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of profile-and-performance types, so the number of workers greatly exceeds the number of distinct types. A finite workforce with \(n_t\) workers of type \(t\) has \(\mu_t=n_t/\sum_j n_j\). The controller still chooses one worker type and deadline per task, as in the paper; mass gives the population basis for per-member fairness. The original framework permits arbitrary group utility functions, and this objective is precisely its \(\alpha\)-fair objective with weights \(w_t=\mu_t^\alpha\).

The expected direction is tractable. Set \(d_t^*=\arg\max_d q_{td}/a_{td}\), \(r_t^*=\max_d q_{td}/a_{td}\), and \(a_t^*=a_{t,d_t^*}\). Proposition 5’s formula then gives
\[ p^\star_{t,d}= \mathbb{I}\{d=d_t^*\} \frac{\mu_t(r_t^*)^{1/\alpha-1}/a_t^*} {\sum_j\mu_j(r_j^*)^{1/\alpha-1}/a_j^*}, \qquad \varphi_t= \frac{\mu_t(r_t^*)^{1/\alpha-1}} {\sum_j\mu_j(r_j^*)^{1/\alpha-1}}. \]
With rational means and fixed \(\alpha\), this requires scanning the deadlines for each type and evaluating the displayed formula. The computational claim is about the underlying allocation problem, not an encoding. It is a Class A style result for this stationary, known-statistics variant; the paper itself does not provide a bit-complexity analysis.

The mirror covers Proposition 5’s offline stationary \(\alpha\)-fair allocation, not the paper’s delayed-feedback regret guarantee. The weakest point is that population mass enters through per-member welfare, while the paper places no capacity constraint on repeatedly hiring a type. If a referee requires population composition to constrain which assignments are available, this version would need an additional supply model. It also changes the paper’s aggregate group-rate fairness into per-member fairness, albeit a natural interpretation for a large workforce.

There is no numbered theorem or proposition in the paper asserting a standard complexity classification such as PSPACE-hardness or membership in P. The PSPACE-hardness claim for the exact causal offline policy appears in unnumbered prose and cites prior work; it concerns a different, finite-horizon policy problem. I would not present it as a numbered anchor or imply that Proposition 5 resolves it. Further questions include whether the finite-budget population-weighted problem retains the cited hardness, and how delayed-feedback regret depends on the type support size \(\tau\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that population mass in the proposed mirror changes only the welfare weights. For \(\alpha\ne1\),
\[ F_\alpha(p)=\sum_t \mu_t^\alpha\,\frac{\rho_t(p)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}. \]
That is exactly the paper’s \(\alpha\)-fair objective with \(w_t=\mu_t^\alpha\). At \(\alpha=1\), the objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t\log\rho_t(p)\) plus a constant independent of \(p\), so Proposition 5 applies with \(w_t=\mu_t\). The masses do not constrain which type can be hired, change its reward or processing-time law, or otherwise change the feasible policies. Proposition 5 already gives the optimal deadlines and lottery for arbitrary weights; Proposition 2 supplies the stated finite-budget correction under its assumptions. The proposed anchor is therefore an immediate reparameterization of results in the paper, rather than a new computational question about a continuous population.

That objection applies to the proposed mirror, not to the idea of a large workforce with many interchangeable workers per type. The paper already abstracts workers within each group as independent draws from a common law, making type-level aggregation plausible. I would not argue that high multiplicity is nonsensical here. But in this particular formulation, the workforce shares act only as normative weights. The computation is unchanged if those numbers are supplied directly as weights, with no workforce interpretation.

A stronger mirror would make population share affect feasible allocations—for example, by limiting each type’s available worker-hours or number of contracts in proportion to its mass. The paper gives no such limit: it permits repeated selection of a group according to a stationary policy and models each task’s outcome by that group’s law. A population-based cap would need additional assumptions about worker availability, capacity, or task demand. Those assumptions could define a worthwhile adjacent problem, but they are not consequences of the paper’s model. A simple cap may still leave a standard concave allocation problem; a dynamic capacity model would change the source of the computational difficulty.

So the negative case is strongest against the anchor as stated: its population coordinate gets absorbed into parameters the paper already allows, and its claimed tractability is inherited directly from Proposition 5. The universal claim is much weaker. A high-multiplicity hiring regime is credible, and a capacity-sensitive version is plausible; the text alone does not rule out a worthwhile mirror built around one of those models.
