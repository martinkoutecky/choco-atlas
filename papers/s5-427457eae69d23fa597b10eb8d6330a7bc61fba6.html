The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, there are no qualifying anchors in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Tables 4–6 report computations, but they do not meet your anchor rule. So I can’t honestly make an anchor-backed case. The strongest positive case I see is a plausible continuous mirror of those central computations.

Call it Continuous Bloc-Decision Probability. Imagine a large federation with many local jurisdictions. A jurisdiction’s type records everything relevant to the voting model: its voting and population weights, its ideological bloc, and its affirmative-vote probability. Many jurisdictions share each type, so the society is described by rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and support size \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of jurisdictions. This regime fits the paper’s focus on blocs and correlated voting better than the Council’s 27 or 28 distinctive member states do.

An instance consists of those types and masses; their voting and population weights; affirmative-vote marginals \(p_t\); feasible pairwise covariances \(c_{tu}\); and a voting rule \(\mathcal R\). For a coalition pattern \(b\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}\), where \(b_t=1\) means type \(t\) votes yes, the rule tests the corresponding mass, weighted-vote, and population shares against its thresholds. Nice- or Lisbon-style clauses can be written as a predicate over those shares. Let \(\pi^0\) be the independent distribution with marginals \(p_t\). As in the paper’s regularization, define \(\pi^\star\) as the distribution on coalition patterns minimizing
\[ \sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}}(\pi_b-\pi_b^0)^2 \]
subject to the specified marginals and covariances. The problem is to compute the exact decision probability
\[ \sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}}\pi_b^\star\,\mathbf 1[\mathcal R(b)=1]. \]
The answer, rather than an intervention, is the paper’s object: it has no action variable to preserve. This is the lead candidate mirror, though it is not attached to a qualifying named result.

The paper’s tables show why this is a faithful candidate. For example, Table 6 computes decision probabilities for five blocs under independent and ideology-correlated votes. At \(p=0.5\), the reported values for Nice 14, Nice 18, Lisbon 15, and Lisbon 20 are \(0.19,0.19,0.31,0.16\) with independent votes, and \(0.33,0.33,0.40,0.31\) with ideological correlations. The proposed problem keeps the paper’s ingredients—coalition formation, vote correlations, institutional thresholds, and the probability of passage—while replacing a finite list of members with masses of jurisdiction types. Its scope is those decision-probability calculations, not the broader historical claims about the Council or the costs of bargaining.

I would expect exact computation to retain hardness from the discrete problem as \(\tau\) grows: the many-agent population does not remove the combinatorics of which types support a motion. The independent-vote case already leaves the problem of aggregating threshold-winning coalitions. Approximation or algorithms for moderate \(\tau\) are natural further questions. This expectation is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result established by the paper.

The weakest point is the regime shift. The paper’s actors are sovereign states with distinctive weights and political circumstances; a large federation of repeated local authorities is a plausible but different setting. And while the authors would likely recognize the probability being computed as their central question, they did not study its asymptotic complexity. That is why this is a credible candidate mirror, but not an anchor-backed continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no anchor results to defeat under the stated rule. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary asserting a computational complexity result. Tables 4–6 report decision-probability calculations, but they are not complexity results.

The proposed mirror also has a modeling gap. Its pattern \(b\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}\) gives each type one binary vote, so the entire mass of a type votes in unison. That is a finite random-bloc model with divisible weights. If instead the mass represents individual jurisdictions voting independently with type-specific probabilities \(p_t\), the continuum vote share of type \(t\) is \(\mu_t p_t\) almost surely. Passage then becomes the deterministic threshold test \(\mathcal R(\mu\odot p)\), and the decision probability is \(0\) or \(1\). A nontrivial limit needs persistent within-type dependence, such as a shared latent shock.

That repair is conceivable: model random yes-vote fractions \(q_t\in[0,1]\) and compute the probability that the mass-weighted thresholds pass. But the paper’s finite distribution over bloc vote patterns does not specify a law or regularizer for those random fractions. Defining one would create a new computational problem, rather than directly continuizing a result the paper proves.

So the strongest case against is that this paper is a weak target for the programme: it offers no complexity theorem to anchor a mirror, and its headline calculations either become deterministic under independent voting or require a new dependence model. But I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A large federation with repeated jurisdiction types and persistent shared shocks could make a coherent computational question. The negative case is strongest against this paper as an anchor, and weaker against that repaired model.
