What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern belief updates, concentration, and convergence. For example, Theorem 1.1 proves convergence to a point distribution under stated regularity and uniqueness assumptions; Proposition 7.3 gives the Gaussian consensus formula. Neither asserts a complexity result about a computational problem.

The paper’s continuum is also the state space \(\Theta\), while the society remains a finite set of \(N\) agents on a strongly connected graph. That is not population continuization. A plausible extension would replace the finite network with a continuum network and represent agents by their signal and network roles, but the paper neither defines nor analyzes that model. There is therefore no honest way to state one continuous problem per anchor, or to predict its complexity, under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1.1 proves convergence of beliefs under analytic assumptions; Theorem 6.9 extends that convergence result; Proposition 7.3 derives a Gaussian consensus formula. None states the complexity of a computational problem, or gives an algorithmic problem whose continuous version could be classified.

The apparent anchors do not change that. The continuous \(\Theta=\mathbb R^k\) in Theorem 1.1 is the space of possible states of the world. The society remains \(N\) named agents on a finite graph. In Proposition 7.3, the consensus is explicitly given by
\[ \theta_{\max}=\frac{\sum_i v_i\tau_i\mu_i}{\sum_i v_i\tau_i}. \]
That is a result about how beliefs aggregate, not a complexity result about a social choice computation.

There is a plausible population extension: represent a continuum of agents by their network roles and signal parameters, perhaps using a graphon and a distribution of \((\mu,\tau)\). The Gaussian formula would then become an integral against the network’s centrality profile. But to make this a computational problem, one would need to add an objective and feasible interventions—for example, costs and constraints for choosing which agents to inform or how much to improve their signal precision. The paper discusses seeding and precision as policy questions, so such a project might interest its authors. It would nevertheless introduce the computational problem that the paper never formulates; it is not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

This is a strong negative case under the programme’s mirror standard, but it does not prove that every computational extension of the paper would be uninteresting. The paper’s policy discussion leaves a credible route to a new intervention-design problem. What it does not supply is a computational anchor for a mirror.
