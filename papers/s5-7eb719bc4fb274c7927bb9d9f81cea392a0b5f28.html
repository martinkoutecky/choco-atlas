The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(\mathcal T\) with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), item supplies per capita \(\sigma_j\), a common budget \(B\), valuation vectors \(v_t\), and a matroid \(\mathcal M\), compute the optimal per-capita revenue and a truthful-in-expectation mechanism. The mechanism maps each reported type \(r\) to a lottery \(q_{r,S}\) over integral independent bundles \(S\in\mathcal I(\mathcal M)\) and a payment \(p_r\in[0,B]\); it must satisfy \(v_t\cdot x_t-p_t\ge v_t\cdot x_r-p_r\) for all \(t,r\), where \(x_{rj}=\sum_{S\ni j}q_{r,S}\), and per-capita feasibility \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le\sigma_j\) for every item \(j\). Maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with type shares \(\mu_t\); each agent receives an integral matroid-independent bundle through a type-contingent lottery and pays a type-contingent amount, with aggregate supply \(\sigma_j\) per capita. Individual incentive constraints and per-capita revenue are the continuous counterparts of the paper's single-buyer LP and large-market mechanism.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 8 and 15 for large markets with finite valuation support, budgets, and matroid constraints. It does not mirror the paper's other single-buyer applications or its prophet-inequality result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-market convergence rate implied by \(\gamma_{K_N}\) be sharpened?
  • Which compactly represented type distributions or broader feasibility systems retain an efficient continuous formulation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a large market of buyers whose private valuation types follow a common finite distribution. As the number of buyers grows, each valuation type occupies a predictable fraction of the population, while item supply grows with the market. The continuous object is that population mass: it does not make anyone’s bundle fractional. Each person still gets an integral bundle, and incentive constraints still apply to individuals.

The lead anchor is Theorem 8, proved in the paper. It says that if each buyer has an \(\alpha\)-approximate incentive-compatible single-buyer mechanism and a concave benchmark, and the post-rounding assumptions hold—computable exact ex-ante allocations, the specified cross-monotone cost-sharing condition on objectives, a mechanism class that allows the resulting BIC mechanism, and matroid weighted-rank valuations—then for any \(\gamma\le\gamma_k\), post-rounding gives a feasible BIC mechanism with a \(\gamma\alpha\) approximation to the optimal mechanism. Here \(\gamma_k\ge 1-1/\sqrt{k+3}\).

Its continuous mirror is the Matroid Market-Expansion Limit. The instance has \(m\) items, a matroid \(\mathcal M\), an explicit finite support \(\mathcal T\) of valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a common budget \(B\), and per-capita supplies \(\sigma_j>0\). Type \(t\) has an additive value vector \(v_t\); every individual allocation must be an independent set of \(\mathcal M\). The question is to compute the per-capita optimal revenue \(V(\mu,\sigma)\) and construct BIC mechanisms for the finite markets that converge to it. More precisely, the \(N\)-buyer market has independent private types drawn from \(\mu\) and \(k_j(N)=\lfloor N\sigma_j\rfloor\) units of item \(j\); feasibility must hold for every report profile.

The second anchor is Theorem 15, also proved here. For one buyer with an explicitly listed finite distribution over correlated additive valuation vectors, a public budget, and matroid constraints, the paper’s LP computes the optimal truthful-in-expectation revenue mechanism subject to an upper bound \(x\) on the buyer’s ex-ante allocation of each item. The theorem also establishes that this mechanism meets the post-rounding requirements.

That theorem gives a precise companion problem, the Continuous Matroid Quota Auction. A population of unit mass has type-\(t\) fraction \(\mu_t\). For each report \(r\), choose a lottery \(q_{r,S}\) over integral bundles \(S\in\mathcal I\) and a payment \(p_r\in[0,B]\). Write \(x_{rj}=\sum_{S\ni j}q_{r,S}\). Require truthful-in-expectation incentives, \(v_t\cdot x_t-p_t\ge v_t\cdot x_r-p_r\) for every \(t,r\), and per-capita feasibility, \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le\sigma_j\) for every item \(j\). Maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\). Each type’s mass is split among integral bundles according to its lottery; no individual receives a fractional bundle. This is the single-buyer LP with the type probabilities read as population masses and the allocation cap read as per-capita supply. It is Class A: matroid-polytope separation gives an LP solution in time polynomial in \(m\), the support size \(\tau\), the encoding length \(L\), and the matroid oracle calls.

For the link back to Theorem 8, in the symmetric market the finite ex-ante benchmark is \(N V(\mu,\sigma_N)\), where \(\sigma_N=(k_j(N)/N)_j\). Theorem 15 supplies the exact single-buyer mechanism, and Theorem 8 supplies post-rounding. Thus, under its assumptions, the paper’s results imply
\(\gamma_{K_N}V(\mu,\sigma_N)\le \mathrm{OPT}_N/N\le V(\mu,\sigma_N)\), where \(K_N=\min_j k_j(N)\). Since \(K_N\) grows with \(N\) and \(\gamma_{K_N}\to1\), the finite market’s per-buyer optimum approaches the continuous quota value, subject to the usual continuity at \(\sigma\). This is an inference from the two theorems, not a result stated in the paper as a population-limit theorem. It also gives follow-up questions: sharpen the convergence rate, extend beyond explicit finite supports and matroid constraints, and determine which other feasibility systems admit the same limit.

The authors should recognize this as their market-expansion problem: independent buyers, scarce items, per-agent integral bundles, incentive compatibility, and revenue maximization all remain. The co-modification is that supply scales with population, so the limiting capacity is per capita. Theorem 8’s dependence on the minimum supply \(k\), rather than on the number of buyers, makes that regime especially credible.

The weakest point is that \(\mu\) plays two roles: in the paper it is a prior over one buyer’s private type; in the mirror it is the actual population share of that type. The iid large-market limit connects them, but they are not identical finite models. The theorem-derived convergence bound is what makes the identification persuasive. Also, this paper has no named hardness result: both anchors are algorithmic, so the mirror does not test whether discrete hardness dissolves. It is a strong, narrowly scoped tractability mirror, not a hardness-transfer case or a new complexity boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a collision in the computational model, not that a large market is implausible or that its agents would get fractional bundles. A market with recurring valuation types and supply that scales with its size is sensible. But the proposed mirrors do not seem to leave a new computational question for ChoCo to chart.

For Theorem 15, the correspondence is exact: set the single buyer’s type probabilities to the population masses, \(f(t)=\mu_t\), and its ex-ante allocation caps to the per-capita supplies, \(x_j=\sigma_j\). The LP has the same variables \(x_t,p_t\), matroid constraints, incentive constraints, and weighted revenue objective as the proposed Continuous Matroid Quota Auction. Writing lotteries over integral bundles as \(q_{t,S}\) only gives an implementation of those same marginals. The paper already solves that problem in polynomial time. Calling the probabilities “population shares” supplies a new interpretation, but no new optimization problem or separation question.

Theorem 8 makes the stronger market-expansion version vulnerable for the same reason. For \(N\) identical buyers and supplies \(k_j(N)=\lfloor N\sigma_j\rfloor\), its post-rounding guarantee already compares the finite market with the per-buyer ex-ante benchmark, with loss at most \(1-\gamma_{K_N}\le 1/\sqrt{K_N+3}\), where \(K_N=\min_j k_j(N)\). Thus the claimed convergence, subject to continuity of the benchmark, follows by substituting the growing supplies into the paper’s result. That is a useful consequence to state, but it does not open a new complexity landscape: the paper calls this result “Market Expansion” and already provides the approximation mechanism and its rate.

A more literal deterministic-population model does not readily escape the collision. If masses of each type are split among lotteries over integral bundles and feasibility is measured in aggregate, its constraints reduce to the same weighted allocation constraints. If instead one insists on exact feasibility for every finite report profile, one is back in the finite market that Theorem 8 rounds. A distinct continuum mechanism model could be developed, but the paper does not identify a new computational question there; one would first need to specify what its population-level strategic constraints add beyond the LP and finite-market guarantee.

This negative case is narrow, and that is its weakness: the high-multiplicity regime is credible, and the authors would likely recognize the market-expansion interpretation. The case only holds if ChoCo requires a mirror to contribute a distinct computational problem, rather than treating a population interpretation and an explicit asymptotic corollary of the paper’s own theorems as worthwhile by themselves.
