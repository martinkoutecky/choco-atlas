The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(t\in[\tau]\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), entitlement coefficients \(a_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=1\), and bounded rational finite-support value distributions \(F_t\), consider \(N\) tenants with \(N\mu_t\) tenants of type \(t\), each with share \(a_t/N\). Under the Competitive Subsidy Mechanism with \(\bar b=8/3\), determine the worst-case limiting ratio of a type-\(t\) representative’s expected utility to their ideal utility when they use the top-\(a_t/N\)-quantile request policy and other tenants may use arbitrary strategies; in particular, does the ratio remain at least \(5/8\) as \(N,T\to\infty\)?

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over tenant types, where each type specifies a value distribution \(F_t\) and entitlement coefficient \(a_t\); each mass unit represents an infinitesimal tenant with an indivisible allocation in each round. A tagged representative has per-capita share \(a_t/N\), private values drawn from \(F_t\), and the original budget and request policy. The decision rule is whether to request; the mechanism allocates the single item uniformly among requesters and charges the winner according to the requester count. The objective is the representative’s worst-case utility ratio against arbitrary opponent strategies.

The objection that survived

The Poisson request limit follows when agents use the proposed aggressive policies, but arbitrary opponents in the robustness guarantee can coordinate, so their limiting request counts need not be Poisson.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s robust guarantee and the restricted payment-rule optimization in Theorem 4.1; it leaves the equilibrium results in Theorems 5.1 and 6.1 outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the numerical \(5/8\) upper bound suggested by Theorem 4.1 be certified exactly for the one-type limiting price optimization?
  • What limiting class of aggregate request processes captures coordinated adversarial strategies while preserving the paper’s robustness guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow but credible: this paper has a natural high-multiplicity version of its robust allocation problem. The qualification is that it has no named result classifying a problem as in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The results below are mechanism and optimization results, so they can anchor a continuous analogue in the broader computational sense, but they are not complexity-class anchors.

A plausible regime is a large cloud-computing service allocating one accelerator slot per round among many tenants. A type \(t\) consists of a tenant’s value distribution \(F_t\) for a slot and its entitlement coefficient \(a_t\); tenants of that type have the same relevant attributes. Let their population fractions be rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=1\). In an \(N\)-tenant approximation, there are \(N\mu_t\) tenants of type \(t\), each with share \(a_t/N\). Thus \(N\) can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of types. Values remain private and independently drawn each round, the item remains indivisible, and the objective remains each tenant’s utility relative to its ideal utility. This is population continuization, with the per-capita budget scaling as \(a_t/N\).

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that in the Competitive Subsidy Mechanism with \(\bar b=8/3\), an \(\alpha_i\)-aggressive strategy is \(\lambda_i\)-robust, where
\[ \lambda_i\ge \frac{5}{8}-O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{T}}\right). \]
Here each agent starts with \(\alpha_iT\) artificial credits, requests or abstains each round, and the item is allocated uniformly among requesters. If \(k\) agents request, the winner pays \(\bar b\,k/(k+1)\); an agent with no remaining budget cannot request. An \(\alpha_i\)-aggressive strategy requests when the realized value is in the top \(\alpha_i\)-quantile, randomizing at a cutoff if needed. Robustness means the agent gets the stated fraction of ideal utility regardless of the other agents’ strategies.

Its continuous mirror could be called Typed Robust Subsidy Synthesis. Given \((\mu_t,a_t,F_t)_{t\in[\tau]}\), ask for a type-based request policy family that, under the share-scaled budgets and the competitive subsidy price rule, guarantees every type at least a specified fraction of its ideal utility in the high-multiplicity limit, against arbitrary behavior by the other tenants. A solution is the policy family and a guarantee; a counter-solution is an adversarial sequence of behaviors showing the target fraction cannot be guaranteed. In the aggressive policy’s limit, type \(t\)’s request count per round has Poisson intensity \(\mu_t a_t\); this preserves the single-item competition while representing the society by type masses. Theorem 3.1 supplies the lead witness: the top-\(a_t/N\) policies achieve \(5/8\) in the long-horizon limit. For that specific guarantee, I expect a Class A result: the policy is explicit and the paper’s proof gives a uniform certificate. This does not establish tractability for optimizing over every possible policy.

A second, more optimization-focused anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here. It reduces an upper bound for General Cost Mechanisms to an optimization over \(0<p_1\le2e\), \(0\le p_2\le4e\), and \(0\le p_3\le12e\), with objective \(\min_{k\in\{1,2\}}\psi(p_1,p_2,p_3,k)\). The theorem says that if \(\lambda\) exceeds this optimization problem’s value, then for some number \(n\) of equally entitled agents, an \(1/n\)-aggressive strategy has robustness at most \(\lambda+O(\sqrt{\log T/T})\), regardless of the payment schedule. The function in the theorem is
\[ \psi(p_1,p_2,p_3,k)= \max_{\gamma} \left(\frac{\gamma}{1+k}+1-\gamma\right) \min\!\left\{1,\frac{1}{ \frac{p_{k+1}}{k+1}\gamma+p_1(1-\gamma)} \right\}, \]
where
\[ 1\ge\gamma\ge \min\!\left\{1,\max\!\left\{ \frac1{p_k}, \frac{p_1}{p_k-\frac{p_{k+1}}{k+1}+p_1} \right\}\right\} \]
and
\[ \gamma\in\left( \min\!\left\{1,\max\!\left\{ \frac1{p_k}, \frac{p_1}{p_k-\frac{p_{k+1}}{k+1}+p_1} \right\}\right\}, \frac{p_1}{\frac{p_{k+1}}{k+1}-p_1} \right). \]
The paper’s Numerical Result 4.2 reports a grid-search upper bound of \(5/8\); that is numerical evidence, not a proof of exact optimality.

The corresponding continuous question is Optimal Robust Pricing in a Homogeneous Limit: in the one-type society of mass one, find the payment schedule maximizing the aggressive focal agent’s limiting robust factor against the static attacks captured by the theorem, or give a certified upper bound on that optimum. The theorem reduces its certificate to three relevant prices and two attack sizes; the paper’s numerical calculation suggests the optimum is \(5/8\), while the \(8/3\) subsidy scale from Theorem 3.1 provides the matching lower-bound candidate. I expect the narrowly stated certificate problem to be Class A, given that finite-dimensional reduction. The exact \(5/8\) upper bound remains open in the paper, so I would not present it as an established tractability result.

These mirrors are plausible to the authors because the model keeps their central questions: robust per-agent utility, strategic requests, artificial budgets, and a single indivisible resource. The continuous object is the society of tenants grouped by complete valuation and entitlement types. The Poisson request limit and \(1/N\)-scaled shares are co-modifications forced by the rare requests and single-item supply; they preserve the allocation problem rather than replacing it with fractional goods or lotteries as the goal.

The scope is the robust guarantee of Theorem 3.1 and the static payment-rule bound of Theorem 4.1. I would leave the equilibrium results in Theorems 5.1 and 6.1 outside this case; mirroring them would require separately preserving their minimum-request constraints and, for asymmetric shares, their simulated-agent construction.

The weak point is that the paper’s proofs still use discrete bidder counts, and the Poisson limit retains discrete competition counts. The results are already uniform over finite populations, so the continuum mirror may yield a clean high-multiplicity formulation without uncovering a new complexity landscape. In particular, the paper supplies no hardness anchor whose difficulty might dissolve in the limit. The most useful follow-up is to prove—or refute—the exact \(5/8\) optimum in the continuous pricing problem and to establish that the typed limit preserves the paper’s adversarial robustness guarantees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a scope objection: neither anchor is a computational result in ChoCo’s stated sense. Theorem 3.1 proves a robustness guarantee for a specified mechanism and an explicit strategy. It does not give an algorithm for an input problem or classify its complexity in \(n\), \(\tau\), or input bit length. The LP in the proof is a device for bounding adversarial request patterns, not a pricing problem whose efficient solution makes the mechanism tractable. Asking for an optimal policy over continuous types would require choosing a mechanism class, an information model, and rules for how type populations coordinate. That could be a worthwhile new mechanism-design problem, but it is not a computational mirror supplied by this theorem.

The proposed rare-request limit is nevertheless coherent. With \(N\) agents, type-\(t\) mass \(\mu_t\), and each agent’s share \(a_t/N\), requests from type \(t\) can converge to a Poisson process with intensity \(\mu_t a_t\). Conditional on a focal agent requesting, its competitors and the winner lottery remain discrete; the focal agent’s budget and utility also remain individual quantities. The resulting object is a tagged-agent stochastic game in a Poisson environment. That is a plausible continuous-population model, but its natural questions concern limiting guarantees and strategic behavior, rather than algorithms over a society represented by type masses. Theorem 3.1’s guarantee is already uniform over finite populations and shares, so its \(5/8\) bound has a direct high-multiplicity limit. That fact supports the regime’s plausibility; it does not by itself create the missing computational question.

Theorem 4.1 is the closer computational anchor, but its proposed continuous question is one-type price tuning. The theorem’s analysis already considers equal shares \(1/n\) as \(n\) grows and reduces the relevant upper bound to three prices and two attack sizes. The unresolved numerical optimization may be worth solving, but its variables are payment parameters and adversarial patterns; a population distribution over types plays no role. Extending it to arbitrary type masses and the full payment schedule could be interesting, but that would broaden the mechanism-design problem rather than identify a complexity landscape produced by continuizing the population.

The equilibrium results do not repair this gap. A stronger limit could preserve a tagged agent’s budget and unilateral incentives, but then the subject is again an individual’s stochastic game against an aggregate request process. To make it a ChoCo computational project would require an additional computational task and a precise finite input representation.

So the negative case is strongest if “worthwhile” means worthwhile for ChoCo’s stated complexity-and-algorithms programme: the paper offers guarantee and mechanism-design results, not a surviving computational-complexity anchor. I cannot honestly make the stronger universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proponent’s Poisson limit is a sensible analytical mirror of Theorem 3.1; the case against is that it appears to belong to adjacent stochastic game theory unless a distinct computational problem is supplied.
