The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many complete household types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), housing categories \(M\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_x\), and rational piecewise-linear preferences satisfying money monotonicity and possibility of compensation, compute an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and type-to-category assignment masses \(y_{tx}\). Require \(\sum_{x\in M\cup\{0\}}y_{tx}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}y_{tx}\le q_x\), \(y_{tx}>0\Rightarrow x\in D_t(p)\), and \(p_x(q_x-\sum_t y_{tx})=0\); return the coordinatewise least equilibrium price vector.

The model it lives in

A continuum of households represented by finitely many complete preference-and-income types, each carrying mass \(\mu_t\), assigned across indivisible housing categories with per-capita supplies \(q_x\). The variables are category prices \(p_x\) and assignment masses \(y_{tx}\); each household receives at most one home, while a type’s mass may split across categories.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s MPE computation and draws on the housing-market application, including Theorem 4’s link to housing-equilibrium prices. It leaves the paper’s incentive-property characterizations and other structural results unclaimed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a mass-level algorithm compute the least equilibrium prices in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), without expanding rational masses into individual replicas?
  • Can the paper’s demand-connectedness and price-adjustment arguments be adapted to category capacities and type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that for every finite assignment market with agents whose preferences satisfy money monotonicity and the possibility of compensation, the Serial Vickrey process introduces objects sequentially and reaches a minimum price equilibrium in finitely many steps. The result covers general preferences, including income effects. It does not establish a polynomial running time or a standard complexity classification; the paper has no named NP-hardness or P result.

A plausible continuous mirror is Continuous Minimum Price Assignment for Housing Categories. Take a finite set \(T\) of complete household types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) summing to \(1\). A type includes the household’s preferences, income, and any other characteristic relevant to its choices. Let \(M\) be a finite set of standardized housing categories—such as location and size—with rational supplies \(q_x\) measured as fractions of the population. Each type has a preference \(R_t\) over a category and its payment, satisfying the paper’s preference assumptions. For an exact computational input, these preferences could be given by rational piecewise-linear utility functions.

The decision variables are prices \(p_x\geq0\) and masses \(y_{tx}\geq0\) assigned from each type to each category, with \(x=0\) denoting no house and \(p_0=0\). Require
\[ \sum_{x\in M\cup\{0\}} y_{tx}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}y_{tx}\leq q_x, \]
and require every positive assignment \(y_{tx}\) to go to a category in type \(t\)’s demand set at \(p\). Also require \(p_x(q_x-\sum_t y_{tx})=0\): any category left partly unsold has zero price. The problem is to return an equilibrium allocation and the coordinatewise least equilibrium price vector. Fractional \(y_{tx}\) means that a fraction of a type’s households get a category; each household still receives at most one indivisible home.

The regime is a large housing market with many households but relatively few complete preference-and-income types: for example, a metropolitan area with hundreds of thousands of households, standardized housing categories, and perhaps hundreds of recurring household types. Category supplies scale with population. This is a meaningful co-modification of the assignment model: individual homes are represented by categories with per-capita supply. The paper itself motivates the housing application and minimum-price computation, so its authors should recognize the question as concerning their equilibrium-computation problem.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be Class A, because finite types and category supplies make aggregate assignment natural, and the paper’s connectedness and price-adjustment ideas may extend to mass-weighted assignments. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 3. The mirror covers the paper’s exact MPE-computation result and its housing application; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s incentive properties.

The main follow-up questions are whether these continuous markets always have a least equilibrium price vector under the stated assumptions, and whether a mass-weighted version of the Serial Vickrey process can compute it in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s finite-step proof works with individual agents and unit-capacity objects. Replacing them with type masses and category supplies requires new arguments, and finite termination in the original theorem gives no efficiency bound. That makes this a credible problem for the authors’ framework, but not an already-established continuous result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3 proves finite termination, not a complexity bound. The paper gives no runtime in \(n\), \(m\), or the size of an input encoding, and its domain allows arbitrary preferences rather than specifying a finite representation of them. The process asks for indifference-price reports; in Stage 3 it may examine bijections between unconnected agents and objects. The theorem establishes that this procedure ends on each finite instance, not how its work scales.

That limits what the proposed mirror can claim to mirror. To ask for polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), one must add an encoding—such as rational piecewise-linear utilities—and replace individual objects with categories whose supplies scale with the population. Those are sensible choices, but they create a new computational problem: equilibrium computation in a capacitated, type-level assignment market. The original proof’s one-agent/one-object reassignment and its enumeration of bijections do not provide an algorithm for that model. A mass-based extension needs its own result.

That is the negative case’s real force: the paper does not establish a computational-complexity result whose population axis a continuum can directly relax. Its named algorithmic result is a finite-step existence claim, with no stated complexity landscape. The proponent’s mirror would need to supply both a computational input model and a new algorithmic analysis.

But this does not defeat the anchor. The proposed housing regime is plausible: many households share complete preference-and-income types, while category supplies scale with population. Fractional type assignments can represent subsets of households receiving indivisible homes. That is a sensible high-multiplicity object, and the paper’s housing application makes it easy to see why its authors would recognize the equilibrium-computation question. The fact that the continuous version needs new arguments is a research opportunity, not evidence that it is unworthy.

So the best case against narrows the proponent’s claim: Theorem 3 itself does not support a prediction of tractability, and the proposed polynomial-time question is not a direct complexity classification of the paper’s result. I cannot honestly make the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The housing mirror survives as a credible question; the negative case is weak precisely there.
