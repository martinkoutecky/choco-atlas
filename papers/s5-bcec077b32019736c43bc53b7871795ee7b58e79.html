The continuous mirror question

Given two centers with population mass \(1/2\) each, a finite set \(G\) of good categories, rational per-capita supplies \(b_g \geq 0\) with \(\sum_{g \in G} b_g = 1\), and finitely many agent types \(t\), each with center \(i(t)\), rational mass \(\mu_t\), and a nonnegative additive value \(u_t(g)\) for each category, does there exist a mass allocation \(x_{t,g} \geq 0\) such that \(\sum_g x_{t,g} = \mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,g} = b_g\), and \(W_i(\eta_i) \geq W_i(\eta_j)\) for both centers \(i \neq j\)? Here \(\eta_{j,g} = \sum_{t:i(t)=j} x_{t,g}\), and \(W_i(\eta)\) is the maximum total value obtainable by assigning category mass \(\eta_g\) to center \(i\)’s type masses, with each unit of mass receiving one whole good. Agent inter-EF1 is automatic for singleton bundles; center envy-freeness is the mass-scale counterpart of center EFX.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete valuation types, grouped by center, with rational type masses and rational category supplies. The decision variables \(x_{t,g}\) allocate each type’s mass to indivisible one-good bundles; each center evaluates a category profile by its maximum welfare over fractional type-to-category matchings, and the feasibility constraints require equal center value for their own and the other center’s profile.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10’s one-to-one regime with category supplies that can be split at mass scale; it does not mirror the paper’s other algorithmic results or establish a continuous counterpart for the distinct-valued goods used in the hardness reduction.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the feasibility question change when recipients may receive multiple goods and the type-level fairness constraints must capture EF1?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper: deciding whether a delegated fair-division instance has an allocation that is EFX for the centers and inter-EF1 for all agents is NP-hard, even when every agent has the same additive valuation. The proof reduces from Equal-Size Partition. In its hard instances, the numbers of agents and goods are equal, and the fairness constraints force every agent to receive exactly one good. The paper also singles out one-to-one allocation as a setting worth studying in its own right.

A plausible mirror is Mass One-to-One Delegated Fair Allocation. Consider two equal-sized centers, each with population mass \(1/2\). An agent type records its center and its complete additive, nonnegative valuation vector over a finite set \(G\) of good categories. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with the masses in each center summing to \(1/2\). Category \(g\) has rational per-capita supply \(b_g\), where \(\sum_{g\in G} b_g=1\). Each unit of mass receives one whole, indivisible good; only the mass of people receiving a category may be fractional. This fits, for example, large, similarly sized food-distribution centers with many recipients but a limited set of recurring package categories.

The decision variable is \(x_{t,g}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving category \(g\). A feasible allocation has \(\sum_g x_{t,g}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_t x_{t,g}=b_g\) for each category. Thus every recipient gets one integral item, and all supplied items are allocated.

Use the paper’s bundle-based potential valuation for centers. Since each recipient gets one item, center \(i\)’s value for an item-category profile \(\eta\), with total mass \(1/2\), is the maximum welfare from matching that profile to its own types:
\[ W_i(\eta)=\max_z\sum_{t\in T_i,g\in G}u_t(g)z_{t,g}, \quad \sum_g z_{t,g}=\mu_t,\quad \sum_{t\in T_i}z_{t,g}=\eta_g,\quad z_{t,g}\geq0. \]
Here \(\eta_{j,g}=\sum_{t\in T_j}x_{t,g}\) is the profile received by center \(j\). Ask whether there is a feasible \(x\) such that \(W_i(\eta_i)\geq W_i(\eta_j)\) for both centers. This is the limiting form of center EFX: deleting one item from one recipient’s bundle has vanishing effect on normalized center welfare as the population grows. Agent inter-EF1 remains exact and is automatic here: removing the other recipient’s sole item leaves the empty bundle, worth zero.

This question has a direct Class A answer. Give each center half of every category, so \(\eta_{1,g}=\eta_{2,g}=b_g/2\). For each center, a transportation allocation from its type masses \(\mu_t\) to these category masses exists because both sides total \(1/2\). The centers then have identical bundle profiles and equal potential values; agents satisfy inter-EF1. A transportation algorithm constructs \(x\) in polynomial time. Moreover, for any compatible finite replication in which the category counts can be split evenly, the flow can be made integral. Each center then has exactly the same multiset of one-item bundles, so center EFX holds exactly, including after any single-item deletion.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 10’s balanced, one-to-one regime, not every instance in the paper. The connection is credible because one-to-one allocation is forced in the theorem’s hard construction and is explicitly discussed in the paper. It also gives the question a concrete computational answer: does the hardness persist when repeated goods of the same category and a large population make category supplies available at mass scale?

The weakest point is the category aggregation. Theorem 10’s reduction encodes Equal-Size Partition in distinct-valued goods; in effect, each encoded item has its own category and cannot be split evenly between centers. Those instances do not lie in the fixed-category, high-multiplicity regime above. So this mirror is a good fit for settings with many recipients and recurring goods, but it does not show that Theorem 10’s hardness dissolves generally. Natural follow-up questions are how much hardness returns when category multiplicities stay small, and whether similar guarantees survive for multi-item bundles or realized center valuations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 10 is the strongest anchor, and its one-to-one version is where the continuous mirror most clearly degenerates. In the proposed model, split each good category equally between the two equal-sized centers: \(\eta_{1,g}=\eta_{2,g}=b_g/2\). For each center, the type masses and category masses both total \(1/2\), so a transportation flow assigns every type’s people whole items. The centers see identical category profiles, so their bundle-based potential values are equal. In a compatible finite replication, deleting an item from the other center can only lower its value, giving center EFX. Agent inter-EF1 is automatic because every bundle is a singleton. This construction works regardless of the valuation vectors.

That construction erases the feature driving the Equal-Size Partition reduction. Its distinct-valued goods must be assigned as indivisible units to one center or the other. A mass supply of recurring categories lets each category be split across centers in equal proportions, so the reduction’s partition choices disappear. Keeping each encoded good as a single indivisible object does not yield a one-to-one continuum population: finitely many goods cannot supply a positive mass of recipients. Scaling the goods into repeated categories makes the balanced split possible. The result is a universal feasibility witness, not a complexity landscape for the NP-hard question in Theorem 10.

A more elaborate mirror could assign distributions of multi-item bundles to valuation types and ask for agent EF1 alongside center envy-freeness. That may lead to a substantive computational problem, but it extends beyond the one-to-one hardness anchor; adding welfare objectives or further constraints would likewise create a different question. The negative case is therefore strong against this anchor and the proposed mirror, but it cannot honestly rule out every continuous question suggested by the paper. Heterogeneous types and multi-item bundles may still support worthwhile mirrors.
