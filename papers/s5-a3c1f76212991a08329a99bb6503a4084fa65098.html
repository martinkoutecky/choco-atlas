The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Its theorems characterize mechanisms and allocations; none classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Theorem 5 constructs an auction, and Theorem 7 characterizes a second-best allocation, but neither gives a computational complexity result for the underlying problem.

The closest lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: an ex post efficient mechanism satisfying interim incentive compatibility, interim individual rationality, ex post budget balance, and ex post cash constraints exists if and only if
\[ \sum_{i\in N}\min\{C(r_i),l_i\}\ge (n-1)G. \]
That is an important feasibility characterization, but the paper does not state a computational problem with an input encoding or running-time bound. I therefore would not present it as the requested computational anchor.

The strongest plausible population regime would be a large consortium of firms dissolving a joint venture, with many partners sharing a small number \(\tau\) of public classes—such as ownership share and cash cap—so \(n\gg\tau\). A candidate extension would put mass \(\mu\) on those classes and ask whether a measurable direct mechanism can allocate the jointly owned asset and transfers while satisfying truthful reporting, participation, budget balance, and each class’s cash limit, maximizing mass-averaged surplus. But the paper’s first-best rule gives the whole asset to the highest-valuation partner. In an atomless population with continuously distributed valuations, there is generally no agent attaining the essential supremum. Preserving the paper’s exact efficiency question would therefore require additional structure, such as an atom at the top or an approximate-efficiency criterion.

So there is a conceivable research extension, but no precise, paper-anchored continuous computational question I can honestly offer. The paper’s continuous valuation uncertainty is not itself a continuous population, and its central results concern a finite set of strategic partners.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper does not establish a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes when a mechanism with specified incentive, participation, budget, and cash properties exists:
\[ \sum_{i\in N}\min\{C(r_i),l_i\}\ge (n-1)G. \]
It gives no computational problem with an input representation, algorithm, or complexity bound. Theorem 5 constructs an auction, and Theorem 7 characterizes a second-best allocation; neither supplies that missing complexity result. A mirror could be invented by asking how to compute one of these objects, but that would be a new computational agenda attached to the paper, not a computational question the paper itself puts on the table.

The best population setting is perhaps a large consortium of similar firms or investors. But the paper’s mechanism turns on each partner’s private valuation, initial ownership share, and cash limit. These enter individual truth-telling, participation, and payment constraints. Grouping partners by public characteristics such as cash limits does not remove the valuation differences that determine who wins. Grouping them by realized valuation instead yields no positive-mass cohorts under the paper’s continuous valuation distributions. And if a continuum model treats each partner as having zero mass, that partner cannot be pivotal in allocating the asset; individual ownership and participation constraints lose the role they have in the finite partnership.

One could improve the mirror by making the asset divisible across a continuum of partners or by allocating it to a positive-mass top valuation class. But the first changes the paper’s efficient allocation problem, which awards the whole asset to the highest-valued partner. The second needs a special atom at the top of the valuation distribution, or another device to identify a winning group. Those may define sensible new models, but they do not establish a robust high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s central result. A continuum limit also turns each partner’s initial ownership claim into zero mass, changing the outside option that drives the dissolution condition.

This objection is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo’s computational programme, not as proof that no related continuous model could be worthwhile. A large, deliberately constructed investor consortium could make population multiplicity plausible, and Theorems 1 or 7 could inspire new computational questions once the inputs and outputs are specified. But the paper itself offers no named computational result to mirror, and its individual strategic constraints make the natural population limit alter the central problem.
