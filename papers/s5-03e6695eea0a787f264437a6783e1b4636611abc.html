The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is the paper’s discussion of the All Affected Principle: it says that influence should reflect how much people’s interests are at stake, and discusses potential, probable, and actual influence. One could imagine a large population facing a recurring policy decision, represented by a distribution over types with different stakes and preferences. But turning that into a computational problem would require choosing a currency of affectedness and a rule mapping stakes to influence. The paper explicitly leaves those questions open.

There are no named computational results in the paper: no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting complexity or an algorithmic classification. So there is no result to anchor a continuous problem to, and no honest basis for predicting whether such a problem is tractable or hard. The imagined population model would formalize a normative question; it would not mirror a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The All Affected Principle is the paper’s strongest possible anchor, but it is a normative principle, not a computational result. The paper gives no algorithmic problem, complexity claim, or formal voting model whose complexity could be mirrored. Its central proposal is that influence should track relevantly affected interests; it leaves the currency of those interests and the form of influence to future work.

The best version of a mirror would use a recurring policy decision with a large constituency, and types that capture each person’s preferences, stakes, legal exposure, and other relevant attributes. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; grouping genuinely identical people would not itself distort the model. One could then ask how to allocate voting power across those types. But the answer depends on choices the paper deliberately leaves open: what counts as being affected, how different stakes compare, and whether influence means a vote, a veto, agenda control, or something else. A computation would test those added choices, not the paper’s claim.

The paper’s discussion of potential, probable, and actual influence offers a narrower possibility: compute a power index for a specified voting rule and type distribution. That could be a worthwhile problem in its own right, but it still needs an externally chosen index and voting model. And in a nonatomic society, an individual has zero mass, so individual pivotality can disappear; treating an entire type as a powerful bloc instead changes the object from individual influence to collective influence.

So the negative case is strongest at the threshold: there is no named computational result here to continuize. The paper may motivate later algorithmic work once its normative choices are supplied, but that would be a new formalization of its open agenda, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.
