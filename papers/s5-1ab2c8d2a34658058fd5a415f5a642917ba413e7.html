The continuous mirror question

Given item classes \(J\) with rational capacities \(q_j\) summing to \(1\), and rational masses \(\mu_r\) of types \(r=(\succ_r,b_r)\), where \(\succ_r\) is a complete ranking and \(b_r\) is the benchmark item with \(\sum_{r:b_r=j}\mu_r=q_j\), run fluid probabilistic serial and compute its marginal allocation \(p_{rj}\). Decide whether the expected ordinal welfare \(\sum_r\mu_r\sum_{j:\,j\succcurlyeq_r b_r}p_{rj}\) is at least a given rational threshold \(\alpha\).

The model it lives in

A finite set of preference-and-benchmark types with masses \(\mu_r\), per-capita item capacities \(q_j\), fluid PS allocation variables \(p_{rj}\), and expected ordinal welfare as the objective; each unit of population still receives one integral item, while \(p\) records aggregate lottery mass.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper does admit a credible continuous mirror, although it offers no NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity anchor. Its named results are welfare guarantees, so the positive case is for a Class A continuum analogue: compute the mechanism’s mass allocation and determine its limiting welfare factor.

The natural regime is a large course-allocation, housing, or dormitory market. There is a population of total mass \(1\), partitioned into finitely many types \(T\), with type \(t\) having mass \(\mu_t\). Agents of one type have the same complete ranking, eligibility, and utility parameters. The number of agents is enormous compared with \(|T|\): for example, many thousands of students may fall into a few hundred preference-and-eligibility profiles. Item supplies scale with the population: item \(j\) has capacity \(q_j\), with \(\sum_j q_j=1\).

A feasible assignment is a mass transport \(x_{t,j}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,j}=q_j\). This is not merely continuizing the outcome: the population is the continuous object, while each unit of population still receives one item. The matrix \(x\) records the aggregate of those integral assignments. Rational masses can be expanded into a finite market by clearing denominators.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that, with linear utilities and on an efficient instance, PS has welfare factor at least \(2/3-o(1)\); moreover, there are efficient instances on which PS has welfare factor at most \(2/3+o(1)\). Here “efficient” means that an optimal matching gives every agent one of their top \(o(n)\) choices, and the linear utility of the \(i\)-th-ranked item is \(1-(i-1)/n\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

*Efficient Fluid-PS Linear Welfare.*

An instance consists of rational type masses \(\mu\), item capacities \(q\), complete rankings for each type, and the induced rank-based utilities. The instance is \(\eta\)-efficient if there is an optimal mass assignment in which almost every unit of type-\(t\) mass receives an item from the top \(\eta\)-fraction of that type’s ranking. The PS fluid process runs for time \(s\in[0,1]\): every unassigned unit of type mass consumes its most-preferred currently available item at unit rate. The decision variable is the resulting mass assignment \(x^{\mathrm{PS}}\). The objective is its total linear welfare, compared with the optimum transport value:

\[ \rho(\mu,q)= \frac{\sum_{t,j}x^{\mathrm{PS}}_{t,j}u_t(j)} {\max_x\sum_{t,j}x_{t,j}u_t(j)}. \]

The problem asks the prover to compute \(x^{\mathrm{PS}}\), the optimal transport value, and the ratio \(\rho\), and then determine the worst-case limit of \(\rho\) over \(\eta\)-efficient instances as \(\eta\to0\). A solution is therefore a mass allocation together with an optimal-transport certificate and a welfare-ratio certificate.

I would expect this to be tractable for a fixed finite type/item description. The PS process is event-driven: each event exhausts at least one item capacity, and the resulting phase durations are rational functions of the \(\mu_t\) and \(q_j\). The optimum is a transportation LP. The paper’s \(2/3\) analysis is already based on phase durations and rank integrals, so its proof has a direct mass-limit form. The natural conjecture is that the continuous worst-case factor is exactly \(2/3\).

The second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It says that for every instance and every benchmark matching \(M^\ast\), the expected fraction of agents receiving, under PS, an item at least as good as their item in \(M^\ast\) is at least \(1/2\).

Its continuous problem is:

*Fluid-PS Ordinal Welfare.*

The input consists of \((T,\mu,q,\succ)\) and a measurable benchmark assignment \(B\) of the population mass to items. The same fluid PS process produces an assignment \(X^{\mathrm{PS}}\). The objective is the ordinal welfare

\[ W_{\mathrm{ord}}(X^{\mathrm{PS}},B) = \lambda\{a:X^{\mathrm{PS}}(a)\succeq_{\tau(a)}B(a)\}. \]

Given a rational threshold \(\alpha\), the problem asks whether the PS mass allocation satisfies \(W_{\mathrm{ord}}\ge\alpha\), and a solution consists of the event-by-event fluid allocation and the resulting welfare value.

This should be Class A. For finitely many types and item classes, the process can be simulated by a polynomial number of exhaustion events. The expected finite-market welfare becomes ordinary population mass in the continuum, so the theorem predicts \(W_{\mathrm{ord}}\ge1/2\) without sampling noise.

The paper’s Theorem 7 supplies the natural boundary for this mirror. It proves that if every agent has the same preference list \((1,\ldots,n)\), then the ordinal welfare factor of any mechanism is at most \(1/2+1/(2n)\). In the atomless limit with a common ranking over item mass, a randomly chosen benchmark gives the corresponding ceiling \(1/2\). Thus the continuous PS question is not merely asking whether a fractional allocation can be written down: it asks whether the fluid mechanism reaches the exact universal welfare boundary. The expected answer is \(1/2\).

There is also a natural RSD version. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for every instance and benchmark matching, RSD gives an expected ordinal welfare of at least \(1/2-o(1)\). In the high-multiplicity regime, a uniformly random arrival order has a deterministic type composition in the limit. If type \(t\) arrives at rate \(\mu_t\) and always chooses its best available item, the limiting RSD process satisfies the same fluid equations as PS. That coincidence is itself a useful “turns out” result: discrete random priority and simultaneous probabilistic eating become the same population-level process, while arising from genuinely different finite mechanisms.

The authors should recognise these as their problems. The mirror retains unit demand, complete preference rankings, per-capita item supply, PS/RSD’s actual allocation rule, and exactly the paper’s ordinal and linear welfare objectives. The only new object is the population measure. PS’s fractional output is not being offered as the novelty; it is already in the paper. The continuization is the replacement of a named electorate by a distribution of repeated preference types, followed by a complexity analysis of the resulting fluid process.

The weakest point is the \(2/3\) claim under a genuinely small type space. The paper’s tight examples use detailed preference structures and may involve nearly as many distinct preference profiles as agents before replication. Their exact extremal behaviour therefore needs to be checked under the intended regime \(|A|\gg |T|\), rather than assumed from the finite theorem. The ordinal \(1/2\) mirror is safer, because its statement applies to every profile and its common-ranking upper bound already survives the limit. The broader programme would then ask for finite-to-fluid convergence rates, parameterized algorithms in \(|T|\), continuum analogues of Theorems 5 and 6, and whether any mechanism-specific separation between RSD and PS reappears once type-level heterogeneity or arrival priorities are introduced.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has found a sensible large-market assignment model, but not a qualifying mirror of the paper’s results. The strongest objection is that the proposed anchors cannot simultaneously preserve the paper’s welfare objectives, its finite-type high-multiplicity regime, and its asymptotic examples.

First, none of Theorems 1, 2, 4, or 7 is a complexity theorem. They are approximation guarantees and impossibility bounds for already-defined mechanisms. “Compute fluid PS and compare it with a transport optimum” is a new algorithmic study, not a computational result established by this paper. That matters under ChoCo’s anchor rule.

Theorem 4 also collapses in the proposed finite-type regime. Suppose type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t>0\) and all its agents have the same strict ranking over \(n\) items. The first \(o(n)\) positions in that ranking contain only \(o(n)\) items, so at most \(o(n)\) of the \(\mu_t n\) agents can receive one of them. Thus a positive-mass type cannot have almost all its agents assigned within the top \(o(n)\) choices. Globally, with \(\tau\) fixed types, the union of all top-\(\eta n\) sets contains at most \(\tau\eta n\) slots, which cannot serve a unit population as \(\eta\to0\).

This is not merely a change in the eventual \(2/3\) value: the proposed “\(\eta\)-efficient” domain is empty in the intended fixed-type limit. The paper’s tight examples avoid this precisely by giving agents identity-specific special items; the number of effective preference profiles grows with \(n\). Reintroducing those profiles defeats the high-multiplicity compression. Replacing named items by tied item classes or a rank-quantile utility creates a different welfare model. The same obstruction appears numerically: for a type of mass \(\mu_t\), even its best possible assignment has average normalized rank loss at least about \(\mu_t/2\), so with fixed \(\tau\) the optimum cannot approach unit welfare.

Theorem 2 has a separate identity problem. An aggregate PS assignment does not determine ordinal welfare relative to an aggregate benchmark. Take one preference type and two equally supplied items \(a\succ b\). Suppose both PS and the benchmark assign half the population to each item. If the two assignments are coupled on the same halves, everyone is at least as well off; if they are oppositely coupled, only half the population is. The same type masses and the same two transport matrices produce different ordinal welfare.

The proponent’s measurable benchmark \(B\) repairs this only by reintroducing an agent-level coupling. To encode arbitrary \(B\) finitely, benchmark allocation must become part of the type description; for an arbitrary matching this can require as many strata as there are item labels or agents. If \(B\) is instead treated as an arbitrary continuum coupling, the input is no longer a finite distribution over types, and the claimed polynomial event simulation computes PS but not the welfare objective. The \(1/2\) theorem therefore does not yield the advertised finite-type computational problem.

Theorem 7 does not rescue that construction. Its random benchmark is exactly an identity-level random bijection. An atomless analogue requires an additional random measure-preserving coupling, not merely a population distribution. Once supplied, the \(1/2\) ceiling is a rearrangement fact about that coupling, not a new computational question about the society.

The RSD proposal is the most plausible one, but it has the same limitation. With repeated types and scaled item supplies, a law-of-large-numbers limit of random arrival orders may indeed produce the same aggregate consumption process as PS. That is a legitimate fluid-limit theorem. It does not, however, preserve the paper’s expected ordinal comparison without the benchmark coupling, and the aggregate process itself is already computable by a finite exhaustion sweep. Thus it supplies useful new analysis, but no population-specific complexity bottleneck or named computational anchor from this paper.

The honest qualification is that a fixed-type course-allocation or housing model could support a worthwhile new ChoCo project. What it cannot presently support is the proponent’s stronger claim: Theorem 4 loses its efficient regime, Theorems 2 and 7 lose their identity-sensitive benchmark, and Theorem 1 reduces to a routine fluid limit of PS. Making any of these survive requires either growing type spaces, identity-level benchmark data, or a genuinely new optimization objective. That is new research rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
