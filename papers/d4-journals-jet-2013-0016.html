The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under your anchor rule: none of the paper’s numbered results asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. The results are about equilibrium existence, characterization, and comparative statics.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that, with finite-support worker and firm degree distributions, scarcity of high-productivity workers, and a matching procedure parameterized by \(\phi_M\in[0,1]\), at least one large-market \(0\)-equilibrium exists. If firms use label-free strategies, a hiring fraction \(\Omega^*\) is a \(0\)-equilibrium exactly when it is a fixed point of the paper’s explicit map, \(\Omega^*=\tilde{\theta}_{\theta_W,\theta_F,M,\alpha}(\Omega^*)\). That is a useful equilibrium characterization, but it does not classify the complexity of computing an equilibrium.

The strongest plausible computational continuation would be a Mass Early-Hiring Equilibrium problem. A repeated-cohort regime could have many workers and firms but relatively few complete types: workers grouped by productivity and signal profile, degree, preference class, and network role; firms grouped by early-hiring cost, degree, and network role. The instance would give rational masses for these types and their network connections, along with the paper’s payoffs and post-graduation matching rule. The question would be to find type-level offer and acceptance policies, and the resulting mass of early hires, such that each type’s policy is a best response and the aggregate hiring mass is consistent with those policies. A solution is the policies, their induced mass flows, and the equilibrium conditions they satisfy; there is no welfare-maximization objective in the paper. In the restricted regime of Theorem 1, this reduces to finding a fixed point of its scalar map. I would expect approximate equilibrium computation there to be tractable; the more general type-level problem could behave differently.

This is a plausible mirror of the paper’s early-hiring game: annual cohorts in fellowship or clerkship markets can be large relative to stable groups of training profiles and employer roles, and the authors would likely recognize mass-level equilibrium as their underlying question. But it is not an eligible anchor-based case. The paper already studies a large-network limit and derives a deterministic aggregate equilibrium equation, so the proposed computation problem sits close to its existing analysis rather than opening a clear computational gap.

That is also the weakest point: fitting the model to a finite set of complete high-multiplicity types may require grouping continuous preferences, costs, and realized network neighborhoods. Those distinctions affect the original agents’ decisions. Preserving them exactly strains the finite-type mirror; aggregating them risks changing the problem. So the paper suggests a credible continuous-population *setting*, but supplies no named computational result on which to base the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is also the one the proponent concedes: the paper has no numbered result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 proves existence of a large-market equilibrium and, under label-free strategies, characterizes it by a fixed point. Propositions 1–5 compare how model parameters affect unraveling. These are analytic results, not computational results to mirror.

The best candidate for a mirror is the proposed Mass Early-Hiring Equilibrium problem: represent repeated cohorts and employer groups by exact types, then compute equilibrium policies and the resulting mass of early hires. That setting is plausible, so I would not reject it on the grounds that labor markets lack multiplicity. But under Theorem 1’s assumptions, the proposed computation reduces to finding a fixed point of the paper’s explicit scalar map. To get a richer complexity question, one would need to add a general type space and strategic structure beyond the paper’s label-free, large-network analysis. That could be worthwhile work, but it is a new computational equilibrium project, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The paper’s mean-field limit is relevant context, but it does not itself settle a computational question; I would not call it a continuous collision. The honest limitation of this negative case is that a mass-level equilibrium problem could still be valuable on its own. The case against is strongest when the decision is whether this paper supplies an anchor for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme.
