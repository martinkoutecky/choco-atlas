The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has named combinatorial counting results, but no named result about the complexity of an algorithmic problem—such as whether one is in P, NP-hard, or FPT. Theorem 1, proved here, says that with two agents and arbitrary monotonic valuations there are at least two EFX allocations, and that this bound is tight for every number of items. Theorem 2, also proved here, says the number of EF1 allocations is at least \(f_{\mathrm{EF1}}(m)\), where \(f_{\mathrm{EF1}}(m)=\binom{m}{m/2}\) for even \(m\), and \(f_{\mathrm{EF1}}(m)=2\binom{m-1}{(m-1)/2}\) for odd \(m\); this bound is tight for every \(m\). Neither theorem classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithmic formulation the paper computes with, so neither qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard.

A natural population model might give each valuation type \(t\) mass \(\mu_t\) and use \(x_{t,S}\) for the mass receiving bundle \(S\). But the paper counts partitions of one fixed set of indivisible items between two agents. A population allocation plan creates a different, generally continuous space of outcomes, with no canonical way to count its EF1 or EFX allocations. Asking whether a population-wide fair allocation exists would be a new question, not a continuization of either theorem’s stated result. So I can’t make a grounded positive case with the required named anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no positive anchor: they explicitly declined to propose a continuous question for either theorem. The strongest case against a mirror is that neither Theorem 1 nor Theorem 2 states the complexity of a computational problem. They prove extremal bounds on the number of fair allocations in a finite instance. The query-complexity claims in the introduction are background results cited from other papers, not results this paper establishes.

The nearest rescue is to read Theorem 2’s EF1-versus-EFX count as a robustness result and ask for its population analogue. But the theorem’s growing parameter \(m\) counts indivisible items; there are always exactly two agents. Increasing \(m\) never creates a large population. A society distribution over valuation types changes the agents in the model, while the theorem counts partitions of one fixed set of goods between Alice and Bob. Those are different objects.

A stronger proposed mirror could use many agents, type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies, and variables \(x_{t,S}\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity fair-division model. It still does not carry over these theorems: the count of allocations becomes a count over mass assignments, generally an uncountable set. Replacing count with volume requires choosing a measure, and that choice is not supplied by the paper. Asking about existence or computing such an allocation would pose a new many-agent problem, rather than continue either two-agent counting result.

Replicating independent two-agent instances across a large cohort leaves the theorem operating separately in each pair. Coupling the pairs changes the allocation problem. Taking a limit in the number or proportions of item types follows the goods axis, not the population axis.

So the negative case is strong for the requested standard: there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous mirror, and the obvious type-based extension does not preserve the paper’s counting question. The broader claim that no worthwhile population-based fair-division project could arise from this topic is weaker; such a project may be worthwhile on its own, but these results give it no direct mirror.
