The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant anchor-based case for a continuous mirror from this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition establishing a complexity result or a formal optimization characterization. Algorithm 1, SETMARGIN, is a named computational procedure: it repeatedly solves the MILP to choose diverse configurations and weight vectors, asks the user pairwise comparisons, adds the answers to \(D\), and finally solves the \(k=1\) recommendation problem. But it makes no complexity claim. The numbered MILP constraints in Section 3 and the scaling evidence in Section 4 do not supply the required theorem anchor.

The best positive direction I can see is a future version for a large product marketplace: many customers in a small number of segments, with each segment sharing an additive utility model, and the segment masses determining how query effort or recommendation quality is weighted. That is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. But SETMARGIN models one user’s unknown utility; its \(k\) weight vectors are competing hypotheses about that user, not agent types, and its configurations are candidate products, not bundles assigned to population mass. Turning it into segment-level elicitation changes the problem and its objective. I therefore can’t honestly state a precise continuous problem as the mirror of a named result here, or predict its complexity as a continuation of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that there is no formal result here to anchor a continuous mirror. SETMARGIN is a named algorithm, and the paper gives an MILP formulation, but it proves no complexity or algorithmic guarantee about that formulation. Its performance claims come from experiments on a single user’s unknown utility vector. The \(k\) weight vectors are competing hypotheses about that user, and the \(x_i\) are configurations used to query them.

A stronger version of the proposed marketplace scenario could define a distribution over customer segments and ask for queries that reduce aggregate recommendation regret. That is coherent, but it changes the problem: the segment masses become part of a new objective, and the system must decide how to learn multiple segment utilities or make a shared recommendation. If all customers have exactly the same type, repeated noisy responses may improve estimates, but that still turns the paper’s sequential elicitation task into a population survey problem. Neither formulation mirrors a result established by this paper.

That supports rejecting a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. It does not establish the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous-population problem can be built from its methods. Population-weighted elicitation could be worth studying; the case against it is that it would be a new computational problem, not a continuization of a named result here. The honest negative case is therefore strong on the absence of a paper-faithful anchor, but weak as a universal claim about every possible scenario.
