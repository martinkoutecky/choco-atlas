The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest population mirror is easy to state, but this paper gives no named computational result to anchor it. Theorems 1–6 are proved here; they characterize when a collective relation is guaranteed to be triple-acyclic, rather than classifying an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard.

A natural continuous version would take a complete voter type to be a reciprocal preference matrix \(R\) satisfying one of the paper’s rationality conditions. A society is a probability distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with mass giving the fraction of voters of each type. The collective relation at threshold \(\alpha\) has \(x_i \succ_\alpha x_j\) exactly when \(\int r_{ij}\,d\mu > \alpha\). This is the population analogue of the paper’s rule, with \(\alpha=(m+k)/(2m)\). A plausible regime is a large electorate choosing from a standardized menu of preference-intensity profiles: many voters, relatively few distinct matrices.

The nearest mirror question is: for a given rationality condition and threshold \(\alpha\), does every such population distribution produce a triple-acyclic collective relation? Theorem 1 says that for finite electorates with 0.5-transitive preferences, no \(k<\lfloor 2m/3\rfloor\) guarantees triple-acyclicity; Theorem 2 says every \(k\ge\lfloor 2m/3\rfloor\) does. Their normalized boundary tends to \(\alpha=5/6\). For min-transitive preferences, Theorem 4 gives failure below \(k=m/3\), while Theorem 3 gives the guarantee at and above it, corresponding to \(\alpha=2/3\). These results make the continuous-population question particularly natural—but they are structural threshold results, not computational anchors.

That distinction is decisive for ChoCo. Checking triple-acyclicity from an explicit finite support is direct aggregation; the paper does not study the complexity of that task, or give an algorithmic result for a harder version. Its contribution is about collective rationality and support thresholds, not computational social choice. So the honest positive case is that its *mathematical model* admits a clean population continuum; the case does not meet the requested anchor requirement. The weakest point is also the fatal one: treating these threshold theorems as complexity results would overstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with the missing anchor: this paper proves no named computational result. Theorems 1–6 characterize when a voting rule guarantees triple-acyclicity for every profile in a class. That is a question about collective rationality, not the complexity of deciding or solving a computational problem. Since ChoCo’s scope is computational, this is a substantial mismatch.

The proposed population model is coherent. Voters with the same reciprocal preference matrix can form a type, and a large electorate with a standardized menu of intensity profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But for a given society, aggregation is direct: compute \(\bar r_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_t r^t_{ij}\), then check the triples. That takes polynomial time in the support size \(\tau\) and the number of alternatives \(n\). Even a stronger per-society question—what is the lowest threshold that removes every triple cycle?—is answered directly by \(\max_{i,j,\ell}\min\{\bar r_{ij},\bar r_{j\ell},\bar r_{\ell i}\}\). The proposed mirror therefore supplies an elegant population interpretation of the paper’s axiom, but no difficult computational task.

The proponents’ threshold anchors do not change that. Theorems 1–2 give the finite-population boundary for 0.5-transitive preferences; Theorems 3–4 give the min-transitive boundary. Their normalized continuous versions ask whether *all distributions* over the relevant preference types avoid cycles at a given threshold. That is a robust characterization over a fixed type domain, not an algorithmic problem on an input society. With a finite menu of types, testing whether some distribution creates a cycle reduces to a linear feasibility problem for each triple. With the paper’s three specified rationality conditions, the proofs already use local structural bounds on triples. Either way, there is no pricing problem or comparable complexity question for the paper’s results to expose.

The best extra candidate is the max-transitive case: its finite bounds leave small intervals unresolved, and a population formulation could sharpen the limiting threshold. That may be worthwhile as a social-choice or geometric result. It still would be an analytic characterization, outside the programme’s computational remit. A continuous problem about bribing voters to remove cycles could be computationally richer, but it would require adding transition costs and an intervention objective that the paper does not study.

So I would argue against a ChoCo mirror of this paper, while conceding the limit of that argument: the paper has a sensible population model, and a researcher could build a new computational problem around it. The case against is strongest for the actual results and their natural population versions; it cannot rule out every useful future problem inspired by the paper.
