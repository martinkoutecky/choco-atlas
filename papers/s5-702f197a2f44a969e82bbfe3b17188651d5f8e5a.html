The continuous mirror question

Given hospitals \(H\), possibly overlapping regions \(\mathcal E\) of size at most \(2\), and a finite support \(T\) of resident types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), determine whether there is a feasible allocation with no strong blocking pair. Each type has a strict ranking over at most \(2\) acceptable hospitals, and each hospital strictly ranks at most \(2\) acceptable types. An allocation assigns mass \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) and unmatched mass \(x_{t,\bot}\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}+x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t\); hospital capacities \(q_h\) and regional caps \(c_E\) are rational per-capita limits. A pair \((t,h)\) blocks if positive mass of \(t\) is unmatched or assigned below \(h\), and either some positive amount of that mass can move to \(h\) while preserving feasibility, or \(h\) ranks \(t\) above a positive mass assigned there.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity hospitals-and-residents market: each complete type \(t\) carries mass \(\mu_t\), preferences, and its priority position at each hospital. The decision variables \(x_{t,h}\) allocate type mass among hospitals or leave it unmatched; rational per-capita hospital capacities and regional caps constrain the allocation.

The objection that survived

The paper’s strict rankings of named residents do not themselves provide repeated types; the proposed priority classes and positive-mass blocking define a revised stability notion whose relation to the paper’s model is not established.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror addresses Theorem 4’s NP-complete existence question. It leaves the other tractable and hard cases in Theorems 1–3 and 5–8 unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact or parameterized complexity of the existence problem as a function of \(|H|\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • How does this type-priority, mass-blocking model relate to finite HRRC instances with normalized population counts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that deciding whether an HRRC instance has a strongly stable matching is NP-complete even when every resident and every hospital has a preference list of length at most two and every region contains at most two hospitals; regions may overlap. The reduction is from One-In-Three Positive 3-SAT: variable gadgets encode truth choices, while clause gadgets and regional caps enforce the one-true-literal condition. This is a direct hardness result for the paper’s central existence question.

A plausible mirror is Mass Strong-\((2,2,2)\)-HRRC Existence. Its instance has hospitals \(H\), possibly overlapping regions \(\mathcal E\), and a finite set \(T\) of complete resident types. Each type specifies an acceptable hospital list and its strict preferences; it also specifies the priority class the resident belongs to at each hospital. Residents of a type share all these attributes. The input gives each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita hospital capacities \(q_h\) and regional caps \(c_E\). For the restricted version, each type lists at most two hospitals, each hospital ranks at most two acceptable types, and \(|E|\le2\).

A solution is an allocation \(x_{t,h}\ge0\), together with unmatched mass \(x_{t,\bot}\), satisfying \(\sum_h x_{t,h}+x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le q_h\), and \(\sum_{h\in E,t}x_{t,h}\le c_E\). Each individual still attends one hospital or remains unmatched; fractional \(x_{t,h}\) records what fraction of a type goes where.

Call \((t,h)\) a strong blocking pair if positive mass of type \(t\) is unmatched or assigned to a less-preferred hospital, and either a positive amount of that mass can move to \(h\) while preserving feasibility, or \(h\) ranks \(t\) above a positive mass already assigned there. The problem asks whether a feasible allocation with no strong blocking pair exists. It is a decision problem, so it has no separate welfare objective.

The regime is a large residency or medical-school placement market: many applicants share a comparatively small number \(\tau\) of preference-and-priority profiles, and hospital seats and regional ceilings are planned as shares of the cohort. Thus \(N\gg\tau\), with each type representing a substantial group. This is the paper’s own application area, and strong stability under regional caps remains the object being computed. The continuous question does require the high-multiplicity interpretation of hospital priorities: a hospital ranks types or priority classes, and aggregate mass may split across placements.

I would expect this problem to be continuum-specifically hard, though with low confidence. Strong stability makes the choice of which hospital or regional cap blocks a move part of the search; with overlapping caps, those choices can interact combinatorially. Theorem 4 makes that interaction a credible place to look for hardness. It does not establish hardness of the mass problem: fractional allocations may dissolve the reduction’s Boolean choices. A useful follow-up is to determine whether normalized discrete instances and mass allocations satisfy an equivalence, rounding, or approximation theorem, and how complexity depends on \(\tau\).

The soft spot is the preference-list restriction. In Theorem 4, a hospital’s list length counts individual residents; in the mirror, it counts acceptable types. That change is natural when identical residents are grouped, but it changes the meaning of the \(\beta=2\) restriction. The SAT gadgets may also stop working when type mass can split. So the honest claim is that Theorem 4 motivates a precise, recognizable high-multiplicity version of the same existence problem—not that its NP-completeness already transfers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real anchor: it proves NP-completeness for the paper’s central existence question, even under severe list and region-size bounds. My case against the mirror is that its proposed population types cannot preserve the paper’s preference model without changing the stability question.

Hospitals in this paper strictly rank individual residents. A type that fully records how hospitals rank its members therefore cannot contain two residents with different positions in those rankings. In an instance where hospitals list most residents, the number of such types grows with the population; the intended regime \(N\gg\tau\) disappears. Grouping residents by a shared priority class restores multiplicity, but it replaces strict individual rankings with ties. That affects strong blocking directly: the paper tests whether a hospital prefers a resident to at least one person it holds. Under class priorities, members of a type may be indistinguishable to that hospital, so this test changes.

The proposed mass definition makes a second consequential choice. A single resident moving in an atomless population has measure zero and cannot change any capacity or regional cap. If that is the deviation, the paper’s feasibility test loses its force. Requiring a *positive amount* of a type to move avoids that degeneracy, but turns a resident–hospital blocking pair into a feasible mass deviation. That is a coherent model; it is also a new stability notion, whose relationship to the paper’s strong stability needs to be established before Theorem 4 can motivate claims about its complexity. Theorem 4’s gadgets rely on specific comparisons between named residents, so its hardness does not itself settle the version with priority classes and mass splitting.

This is the strongest objection I can make, and it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large assignment market with shared priority classes and quotas scaled to cohort size is plausible, and authors might well recognize its fractional strong-stability question as a natural extension. The negative case is therefore weak at the universal claim: it challenges fidelity to the paper’s strict-ranking model, while the programme permits recognizable co-modifications.
