What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity *setting* here: a large LLM-evaluation market with many evaluators. A type could include an evaluator’s belief over the candidate models, evidence packet and its correctness profile, trading constraints, and risk preferences; \(\mu_t\) would be the share of the evaluator population of type \(t\). The action would be what mass of each type submits as evidence and how it reports or trades, and the market would aggregate evidence and positions. That could be a sensible continuous-population model, provided evidence counts and resolution are scaled consistently.

But I cannot make that a qualifying case under your anchor rule. The paper’s named results concern belief sensitivity, incentive compatibility, AMM equivalence, and refund correctness. The closest computational-looking result is Theorem 5, proved here: its two execution algorithms give each trader the same final net payment as synchronous sequential execution, charge them once and then issue only nonnegative refunds, and allow the platform to quote a deterministic lower bound on the refund at execution. That is an algorithmic correctness guarantee, but it does not classify the complexity of a problem or analyze the algorithms’ running time. The paper contains no named computational-complexity result to mirror.

One could formulate a continuous extension of the paper’s evidence-submission or execution questions, but presenting it as a continuous analogue of a qualifying computational anchor would overstate the connection. The weak point is decisive: the population mirror may be natural, but the paper studies mechanism design rather than the computational questions this programme is meant to chart.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed large evaluator population is plausible as a high-multiplicity setting. But it does not give this paper a computational result to mirror. Theorem 5 is the strongest candidate: it proves that the asynchronous protocol preserves each trader’s payment and refund guarantees. That is an accounting-correctness result, not a complexity result. It gives no running-time analysis or computational problem with a defined input size. Theorem 1 and Theorems 2–4 concern belief sensitivity, incentives, and equivalence between market representations; none supplies the missing complexity anchor.

The strongest repair would model evaluator mass arriving in cohorts and ask how to compute or guarantee their settlements. That would be a new computational question around the mechanism, rather than a continuous mirror of Theorem 5. The paper also leaves the quality function \(r\) largely unrestricted and relies in practice on an LLM judge and disputes, without specifying a finite computational input for verification. Conversely, treating each actual evidence packet as part of a type makes informative evidence highly individualized; identical submissions may be rejected as duplicates. Either way, the paper does not provide the model needed to chart a complexity landscape.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as written: its named results do not anchor a ChoCo-style computational mirror. It cannot rule out a worthwhile future computational study of high-volume evidence markets, but that would require new questions and modeling choices beyond the results here.
