Theorem 3

The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict rankings over acceptable types on the opposite side, find a rational flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with row sums \(\mu_i\) and column sums \(\nu_j\). For each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(P_{ij}\) be the mass of type \(i\) assigned to types it ranks below \(j\), and \(Q_{ij}\) the mass of type \(j\) assigned to types it ranks below \(i\); require \(P_{ij}Q_{ij}=0\). Among such stable flows, minimize \(d_A(x)+d_B(x)\), where \(d_A(x)=\max\{\operatorname{rank}_i(j):x_{ij}>0\}\) and \(d_B(x)\) is defined symmetrically.

The model it lives in

A balanced continuum placement market with finite applicant and post types, rational mass for each type, and strict preferences over counterpart types. The decision variables are the matching masses \(x_{ij}\); stability excludes any pair of types with positive mass on both sides preferring one another, and the objective minimizes the sum of the worst supported rank on each side.

The objection that survived

The objective counts the worst positive-mass type, so a uniquely worst-ranked individual whose type vanishes in a population limit disappears; this limits the bridge from arbitrary SMI instances.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the min-regret-sum objective in Theorem 3. It leaves the regret-equal algorithm, the minimum-cost refinement, other fairness criteria, and the experiments outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one compute a stable flow minimizing the sum of worst supported ranks in time polynomial in the number of supported types and the input bit length?
  • Can stability and the existence of a flow under given rank bounds be tested efficiently when each type’s mass may split among several counterpart types?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Min-Regret-Sum Stable Matching. It keeps the paper’s central question intact: among stable outcomes, how small can the sum of the worst rank on each side be?

A credible regime is a large, centralized placement market—say, a yearly cohort of applicants matched one-to-one with individual apprenticeship posts. Applicants can share a type when they have the same eligibility and the same strict ranking of post types; posts can share a type when they rank applicant types alike. Hundreds of thousands of people might then be represented by a few dozen types. This is a regime claim, not a claim about every hiring market: it fits only where class-level preferences are a genuine description of how the matching is made.

Here is the continuous problem. The input is finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and each type’s strict ranking of the types on the other side. A solution is a nonnegative flow \(x_{ij}\) with row sums \(\mu_i\) and column sums \(\nu_j\). It represents matching mass between types \(i\) and \(j\); since agents form a continuum, each unit of mass can still receive one whole partner.

For each acceptable type pair \((i,j)\), let \(P_{ij}\) be the mass of type \(i\) assigned to types it ranks below \(j\), and \(Q_{ij}\) the mass of type \(j\) assigned to types it ranks below \(i\). The flow is stable exactly when \(P_{ij}Q_{ij}=0\) for every pair: there cannot be positive mass on both sides who would prefer one another. Define \(d_A(x)=\max\{\operatorname{rank}_i(j):x_{ij}>0\}\), and define \(d_B(x)\) symmetrically. The problem asks for a stable flow minimizing \(d_A(x)+d_B(x)\), with the output flow given exactly as rational numbers.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in Appendix B of the supplied paper: for every SMI instance \(I\), Algorithm MRS produces a min-regret-sum stable matching in \(O(d_s m)\) time, where \(d_s=d_U(M_z)-d_U(M_0)\), \(m\) is the total length of the preference lists, and \(M_0,M_z\) are the man-optimal and woman-optimal stable matchings. The theorem number and statement appear in the [published paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol160-sea2020/LIPIcs.SEA.2020.20/LIPIcs.SEA.2020.20.pdf). This is an algorithmic anchor; the paper’s discussion of NP-hard balanced and sex-equal matchings reports prior results, rather than supplying a numbered hardness theorem here.

I expect the continuous problem to be tractable (Class A), with running time polynomial in the number of supported types and the input bit length. The paper’s MRS method scans bounds on one side’s worst rank and uses an optimal stable matching after truncation; a weighted deferred-acceptance procedure on type masses is a plausible analogue. Theorem 3 does not establish that extension: its stated running time depends on the individual-level preference-list length, and the type-level algorithm and its correctness would need to be proved.

The weakest point is that this mirror ranks counterpart *types*, while the paper’s SMI ranks named individuals with strict preferences. The mirror is plausible where a real placement system genuinely uses class-level preferences and treats people within each class as interchangeable. It is much less convincing where agents rank particular individuals. The scope I am claiming is just Theorem 3’s min-regret-sum objective in that type-based matching regime; it does not cover the paper’s other fairness criteria or its experiments.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only new computational anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 3: MRS finds a min-regret-sum stable matching in \(O(d_s m)\) time. That is a real algorithmic result, so there is no basis for dismissing the mirror on the ground that the paper has nothing computational to continuize.

The difficulty is that SMI’s types are harder to compress than “people with the same preferences” suggests. A preference list ranks *named individuals*. To replace many people with a finite set of types and give each type a rank for each opposite-side type, the model must treat members of an opposite-side type as interchangeable. But strict SMI rankings distinguish those individuals. If we keep the distinctions, a type-to-type flow does not record which named partners are matched, and the stability constraints cannot in general be recovered from it. The paper’s random complete-preference experiments underline the gap: there, rankings are over individuals, not a small set of interchangeable classes.

The proponent’s category-level flow is the strongest repair. With ranks over categories, fractional mass flows, and stability defined by the absence of positive masses on both sides who prefer each other, it is a coherent stable-allocation model. But it changes the anchor’s fairness question: the paper minimizes the sum of the worst *individual ranks in lists of named partners*; the flow model minimizes worst *category ranks*. Treating category members as tied, or replacing individual blocking pairs with positive-mass blocking, may be a sensible model change, but it is doing substantive work. It is not simply representing the same SMI instances at high multiplicity. Whether the authors would recognize that as their problem is the hinge, and the paper’s motivation does not establish it.

The worst-rank objective makes the bridge especially fragile. A single poorly matched person can determine \(d_U+d_W\), whatever fraction of the market they represent. In a sequence of larger societies, that person’s mass can shrink to zero: an essential-worst-rank objective then loses them, while a support-based maximum still counts any positive mass, however small. The latter preserves the discrete sensitivity but is discontinuous when that mass vanishes. Restricting attention to broad classes with non-negligible mass makes the category model more stable, but further shifts the fairness measure from the worst person toward the worst class.

So the strongest case against this anchor is not that its continuous answer would be easy or uninteresting. It is that preserving the MRS question seems to require preserving named identities, while the proposed tractable population model obtains its finite type space by replacing individual ranks with category ranks. That makes this a plausible research question about continuous stable allocation, but a questionable mirror of Theorem 3. The negative case remains weak at the boundary: a placement system whose genuine preferences are over interchangeable categories could make the flow model worthwhile, and a matching theorist might reasonably accept it as a natural variant of the paper’s problem.
