Why no mirror

Proposition 2 is computational, but its instance has exactly two agents, and the proposed mass variables count repeated cases rather than agents in a shared allocation society. Pooling goods among a continuum of individual agents would change the problem, so no continuous-population question for this result survives the scope test.

What the mirror covers

The proposed lift covers only Proposition 2’s lottery computation across repeated two-agent cases; it does not mirror the paper’s three-agent incompatibility result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. For two agents with nonnegative integral additive valuations over \(m\) indivisible goods, it gives a pseudopolynomial algorithm, running in \(O(m(v_1(M)+v_2(M)))\), to compute the uniform lottery over two lexicographically chosen allocations \(A_1,A_2\). For \(A_i\), agent \(i\) must be envy-free; subject to that, the other agent’s utility is maximized; subject to both, total utility is maximized. The lottery has support size at most two. The paper’s Theorem 4, also proved here, establishes the guarantee that makes this construction interesting: the lottery is ex-ante envy-free, and each allocation in its support is both EFX and Pareto optimal.

My lead mirror is Continuous Pair-Type EFX–PO Lottery. Consider a large service handling many isolated two-party allocation cases, each with its own copy of \(m\) indivisible goods. For example, the cases might be standardized asset splits between branches with recurring valuation schedules. A case type \(t\) specifies the complete valuation profile \((v^t_1,v^t_2)\) and the two roles. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of cases of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Equivalently, in the normalized population of agents, each role-type \((t,i)\) has mass \(\mu_t/2\). Here \(\tau\) counts the supported case templates; the number of cases can be much larger.

For each type, the question is to find a distribution over complete allocations \(A=(A_1,A_2)\) of that type’s goods. In mass form, output \(x_{t,A}\geq0\), where \(x_{t,A}\) is the mass of cases of type \(t\) receiving allocation \(A\), and \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\). For each type \(t\) and role \(i\), require
\[ \sum_A x_{t,A}v_i^t(A_i)\ \geq\ \sum_A x_{t,A}v_i^t(A_{3-i}). \]
Every allocation with \(x_{t,A}>0\) must be EFX and Pareto optimal for that case’s two valuations. The requested solution is a finite mass distribution over integral per-case outcomes; the average bundle for a type may be fractional. The construction in Proposition 2 gives support of size at most two per type.

This is a credible, if narrow, high-multiplicity mirror: the parties remain the agents, their item values and fairness tests are unchanged, and repeated identical valuation profiles are grouped by type. Mass records how many cases receive each outcome. Running the paper’s construction once per supported type gives time \(O\!\left(m\sum_t(v^t_1(M)+v^t_2(M))\right)\), plus the work to represent the rational masses. So I expect this question to be pseudopolynomially tractable, with complexity depending on \(m,\tau\), the encoded masses, and the valuation totals. Proposition 2 does not establish polynomial time in binary input length.

The paper’s unnumbered Partition observation says that computing its particular lexicographic allocation \(A_2\) is weakly NP-hard; the authors explicitly caution that this does not show hardness of finding *some* lottery with the desired guarantees. It is not a numbered hardness anchor, so I would not use it as one. Nor would I use Theorem 5’s three-agent incompatibility result as a computational-hardness anchor: it is an existence impossibility, not a complexity classification.

The mirror covers the paper’s two-agent EFX-and-PO lottery computation, not its three-agent impossibility result or a general many-agent fair-allocation problem. Its weakest point is the scenario: it is a population of isolated two-agent cases, rather than one large society sharing a common pool of goods. The many-agent scale comes from repeated cases, so the result’s local combinatorics do not dissolve with multiplicity; the continuous formulation batches the computation by type. If a mirror must be a single pooled allocation problem, this anchor may not qualify. If a continuum of repeated allocation cases is admissible as the population, this is an exact, recognizable mirror of the paper’s computed problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has identified a real algorithmic result: Proposition 2 computes the lottery guaranteed by Theorem 4 for a single two-agent instance. The strongest objection is to what gets made continuous. The proposition’s agents are the two participants in one allocation problem; its input has no population parameter to scale. Its mass variables instead count repeated *instances*.

In the proposed model, each case has its own copy of \(M\), its own pair, and its own fairness and efficiency tests. The constraints are conditional on the case type, so cases do not compete for goods or otherwise interact. The algorithm is therefore just Proposition 2 run once per supported case type. That may be useful for batching a service’s workload, but it makes the distribution over cases continuous; it does not make the society within the paper’s fair-division problem continuous.

Aggregating the actual people by valuation type does not by itself repair this. Envy is tested between the two people in the same case. Individual type masses do not specify which people are paired, so preserving the paper’s constraints requires retaining the pairing—effectively, treating each complete two-person case as the type. That returns to a population of instances. If instead the proposed mirror pools goods among a growing population of individual agents, it becomes a many-agent allocation problem. That could be worthwhile, but neither Theorem 4 nor Proposition 2 addresses it: both are specifically two-agent results.

So I would not count this as a ChoCo population mirror under the programme’s stated scope. The negative case is weakest at the boundary: a population of recurring two-person cases is plausible, and if ChoCo accepts cases as population units, the proponent’s lift is well-posed and Proposition 2 gives it a direct algorithm. The objection is that this is an instance population, not the continuous society the programme is set up to study.
