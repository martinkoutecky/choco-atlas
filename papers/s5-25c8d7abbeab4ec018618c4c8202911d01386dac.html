The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of complete agent types \(\theta\), with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to one, task sets \(K_\theta\), admissible slices \(S_{\theta k}\), and values \(V_{\theta ks}\), compute a feasible mass assignment \(y_{\theta ks}\) maximizing \(\sum_{\theta,k,s} V_{\theta ks}y_{\theta ks}\). Require \(\sum_{s\in S_{\theta k}}y_{\theta ks}\leq\mu_\theta\) for each task and \(x_s=\sum_{\theta,k}y_{\theta ks}\) to satisfy the service-node and governance capacity constraints, with capacities normalized per capita. Each assigned unit of mass receives one indivisible slice. Determine the problem’s complexity in the support size, task and slice counts, DAG size, and input bit length.

The model it lives in

A single epoch with a continuum of agents represented by a finite distribution over complete task-portfolio types; per-capita laminar service capacities and per-slice governance caps; mass assignment variables \(y_{\theta ks}\); and an aggregate welfare-maximization objective. Fractional assignment variables represent fractions of identical agents receiving individual indivisible slices.

What the mirror covers

Covers the exact welfare-allocation computation of Proposition 2(ii) over a continuous population. It leaves price-existence and individual-incentive claims, the empirical evaluation, encapsulation results, and inter-epoch dynamics outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the continuous optimum approximate finite markets as population and capacities grow?
  • Can positive-mass type groups have a meaningful incentive guarantee in this model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow one: Proposition 2(ii) is the paper’s named algorithmic anchor. There is no numbered hardness result to anchor on. Proposition 2(ii) assumes a polymatroidal feasible region, the paper’s additively separable gross-substitutes agent valuations, quasilinear utility, and integral capacities for discrete slices. It says the exact welfare-maximizing allocation can be computed in time polynomial in the number of agents \(n\), the number of slice types \(K\), and \(\log V_{\max}\), given the per-agent value oracles; the aggregate oracle is obtained by successive convolution of the agents’ oracles. The paper states this result as its own, while Appendix 10.3 gives a proof sketch reducing the computation to cited discrete-convex optimization machinery. Proposition 2(i) adds existence of prices supporting a welfare-maximizing allocation, and (iii) says that exact allocation with full-agent Clarke-pivot payments is DSIC, individually rational, and weakly budget balanced under the stated assumptions. I rely on the computational core, (ii); I do not claim that the DSIC result survives continuization.

A concrete mirror is Continuous Polymatroidal Service Allocation. Fix one mechanism epoch and a finite set \(S\) of slice types. Let the service DAG induce a laminar family of leaf blocks \(L_v\), with per-capita capacities \(\bar C_v\); include governance as per-leaf caps \(\bar u_s\). The feasible aggregate slice mass is
\[ \mathcal Q=\{x\ge 0 : x(L_v)\le \bar C_v\ \text{for every service node }v,\quad x_s\le \bar u_s\ \text{for every }s\in S\}. \]
A type \(\theta\) is a complete agent description for this epoch: its task portfolio, location and capabilities, admissible slices, and each task’s latency-adjusted value for each slice. Latency and quality are fixed within the epoch, as in the proposition’s assumptions. The instance gives the support of types, with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to one; \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of agents of type \(\theta\).

For each task \(k\) in type \(\theta\)’s portfolio, let \(y_{\theta ks}\) be the mass of those agents whose task \(k\) receives slice \(s\). Require \(\sum_s y_{\theta ks}\le\mu_\theta\), and let \(x_s=\sum_{\theta,k}y_{\theta ks}\) lie in \(\mathcal Q\). The question is to compute a feasible \(y\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{\theta,k,s} V_{\theta ks}\,y_{\theta ks}, \]
and, if price support is requested, a per-slice price vector under which each allocated task chooses a utility-maximizing slice and the aggregate allocation maximizes revenue over \(\mathcal Q\). An individual task still receives at most one indivisible slice: fractional \(y_{\theta ks}\) means that a fraction of the otherwise identical agents receive it. The mirror continues the population, rather than making services divisible or introducing lotteries as the object of study.

A plausible regime is a large deadline-bound service marketplace running repeated standardized agent workflows. Many agent instances may share the same complete task portfolio, latency-adjusted value table, location, and governance status, while the underlying platform provisions compute and service capacity roughly in proportion to the client population. There could be millions of agents but only dozens or hundreds of such complete types, so \(\tau\ll n\). Grouping by a broad label such as “inference request” would be too coarse; agents with different values, tasks, or admissibility rules belong to different types. This makes the finite-support assumption a property of the workload regime, not of agentic computing in general.

This is a computational population mirror, not a mean-field equilibrium-dynamics question. With explicit type support and an explicit DAG, the allocation problem has variables for type-task-slice assignments, constraints for task mass and service-node capacities, and a linear welfare objective. A linear program therefore gives a Class A expectation, polynomial in the support size \(\tau\), task count, number of slices, DAG size, and input bit length. Its natural output is an aggregate allocation; dual prices provide a route to the price-support question. That is recognizably the paper’s welfare-clearing problem, with its type support replacing the list of named agents.

The weakest point is also a real limit on the claim: a nonatomic agent has no pivotal effect on aggregate allocation, so Proposition 2’s individual VCG incentive guarantee does not transfer as stated. The mirror covers the exact allocation computation and its price-support interpretation, not the paper’s entire mechanism package. Its compression also depends on \(\tau\) staying moderate; if private values make almost every agent a distinct type, the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. Further questions are how the continuous optimum approximates finite populations as capacities and agent counts grow, and whether strategic reports by positive-mass type groups admit a meaningful incentive guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Proposition 2(ii) only yields a convincing population mirror under a demanding clone regime. A type must fix an agent’s whole task portfolio, value table, location, and governance status. In a marketplace with changing tasks and individualized private values, those complete types may be nearly all distinct, so \(\tau\) can approach \(n\). The paper’s evaluation does not establish that its workloads have a small support of complete agent types. And if the market is made more realistic by adding shared budgets, task complementarities, or congestion effects within the epoch, the separability and fixed-value assumptions that support the result no longer apply.

There is also a gap between this mirror and the paper’s incentive story. In a nonatomic population, one agent has zero mass and cannot affect aggregate allocation; Proposition 2(iii)’s individual VCG guarantee therefore does not carry over as stated. The proposed question studies efficient welfare allocation and perhaps price support, but it does not continuize the paper’s central concern with strategic agents as individuals.

Those objections limit the mirror’s reach, but they do not defeat its best version. The programme explicitly treats type support as a property of the instance regime, and capacity may scale per capita. For a large marketplace running standardized workflows, many agents could share the same complete task and value profile while supplies scale with population. Under the paper’s fixed-epoch and additive-separability assumptions, the proposed variables \(y_{\theta ks}\) then give a coherent continuous population allocation: each task still receives an indivisible slice, while the mass of identical agents can be divided across assignments. With explicit rational type data, that is a computational problem over the population distribution, not a continuization of outcomes or time.

So the negative case rests on a real but narrower point: the paper gives little evidence that low support of *complete* types is common in its target markets, and the continuous mirror leaves individual incentives behind. I cannot honestly turn that into the universal claim that no scenario makes the mirror worthwhile. The stated standardized-workflow regime is plausible, and Proposition 2(ii) supplies a named computational result for it. The against case is therefore weak at its decisive point: it narrows the mirror’s applicability but does not eliminate the anchor.
