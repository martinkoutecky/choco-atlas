The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a qualifying computational anchor: this paper states no result that an underlying problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. Its numbered results concern stochastic dynamics and equilibria. For example, Theorem 4.1 proves that a pure equilibrium in fixed threshold strategies may fail to exist, even with two symmetric agents and a suitable two-value distribution; Theorem 5.8 characterizes long-run win rates. Both are proved in the paper, but neither is a computational-complexity result. So under your anchor rule, there are no valid anchors and no per-anchor computational mirror to propose. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14916)

The strongest *nearby* positive case is Theorem 5.8. For any finite agent set with fair shares \(\alpha_i\) and fixed request probabilities \(p_i\), it proves there is a unique ordered splitting partition \((C_1,\ldots,C_m)\). For each \(i\in C_u\), the number of wins by time \(t\) is \(\frac{\alpha_i}{\sum_{k\in C_u}\alpha_k}\left(1-\prod_{k\in C_u}(1-p_k)\right)\prod_{v<u}\prod_{k\in C_v}(1-p_k)\,t+o(t)\). This is an analytic characterization, not an algorithmic result. [Theorem 5.8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14916)

If the programme accepts an analytic result as a foothold, a plausible mirror is Fluid DMMF Win-Rate Computation. Take many users of a shared computing cluster, grouped into a fixed number \(\tau\) of types by common value distribution and fair-share treatment; let type \(j\) have rational population mass \(\mu_j\). Each user keeps an integral win history, requests using a type-specific threshold with probability \(\lambda_j/N\), and DMMF awards each round’s indivisible resource to the requesting user with the fewest wins. Given the type masses, value distributions, and threshold intensities, ask whether the large-\(N\) limit of each type’s rescaled long-run win and utility rates exists, and compute those rates. Mass represents population fraction; the threshold intensities are the decision variables. For fixed \(\tau\), I would expect a structural, tractable characterization, perhaps by taking a limit of Theorem 5.8’s partition formula. That is a conjecture for a new problem, not a result of this paper.

This candidate covers only fixed-threshold long-run behavior. It does not mirror the paper’s equilibrium results: in a continuum, an individual deviation may have negligible effect on the population, unlike the two-agent strategic interaction in Theorem 4.1. Its weakest point is that DMMF depends on individual win histories, so a type’s mass and average wins alone may not capture the limiting dynamics. The limit and its computation would need to be established before this could count as a strong computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its nonexistence result for fixed-threshold equilibria, its win-rate characterization, and its Win-Rate Matching guarantees concern stochastic dynamics and equilibrium behavior. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. I agree with the proponent on that point; the win-rate theorem is an analytic foothold, not a computational result to mirror.

The proposed cohort mirror also needs more than type masses to represent DMMF faithfully. Identical users can be grouped by value distribution and fair share, but the mechanism gives priority according to each person’s cumulative wins. Its state therefore includes the distribution of users across win counts, not just each cohort’s mass or average wins. Retaining that state yields a measure-valued fluid-limit problem with a growing set of win-count classes. Scaling time to keep allocations visible may make that a sensible mean-field analysis, but it does not turn the paper’s result into a complexity question. For fixed thresholds, the paper already gives finite-population win-rate formulas; taking their large-population limit is an asymptotic analysis of those formulas.

The equilibrium results have a related problem. A single deviator has zero mass in an ordinary continuum, so cannot change the aggregate request-rate statistic that drives Win-Rate Matching. A tagged-agent or finite-atom extension could preserve that interaction, but it would be a hybrid mean-field game requiring additional modeling choices, rather than a direct continuous-population version of the paper’s result. The proponent is right to flag this issue, and a more elaborate formulation does not supply the missing computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as submitted: its named results do not support a computational mirror under ChoCo’s remit, and the closest proposed mirror leads naturally to fluid analysis. It is weaker as a universal claim. A type-by-win-count fluid model, possibly with a tagged deviator, is coherent and might interest these authors; I cannot rule out its value. But that would be a new dynamics project, not a complexity mirror of a computational result in this paper.
