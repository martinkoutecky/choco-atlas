The case FOR (proponent)

I do not find an eligible computational anchor in this paper. The numbered theorems concern asymptotic probabilities and moments for finite random instances: for example, Theorem 4.11 bounds the second moment of the number of two-sided e-stable matchings, and Theorem 5.4 does so for one-sided matchings. Neither asserts an algorithmic or complexity result. The NP-completeness claim in the introduction is attributed to Cechlárová and Manlove; it is unnumbered and not proved here. The paper’s \([0,1]\) variables model random preferences, not a continuous population.

There is a plausible *prospective* mirror of the cited one-sided decision problem, but it cannot serve as the required anchor. Let \(T\) be a finite set of roommate types, each with a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of partner types, and let \(\mu_t\) be the rational mass of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A matching is a symmetric nonnegative array \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) with \(\sum_u x_{tu}=\mu_t\). It is exchange-stable if, whenever \(x_{ac}>0\) and \(x_{bd}>0\), it is not the case that both \(d\succ_a c\) and \(c\succ_b d\). The decision question is whether such an array exists; a solution is the array itself.

A conceivable regime is a large roommate or placement market where many people share a finite set of preference profiles over partner categories, so the number of people is large relative to the number of types. The paper’s cited NP-completeness result motivates asking this question, but it does not establish whether hardness transfers, dissolves, or becomes continuum-specific. The main weakness is that the paper ranks named individuals, while this mirror ranks partner categories; adding identity-level preferences to the types could erase the small-support regime. So this is a candidate research question, not a continuous analogue anchored in a named result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject a continuous *computational mirror of this paper*. Its numbered results are about probabilities and moments for finite random preference lists: Theorems 4.11 and 5.4 bound the second moment of the number of e-stable matchings; Theorems 4.10 and 5.2 bound the probability that a doubly stable matching exists. None states an algorithmic or complexity result. The NP-completeness claim belongs to the earlier Cechlárová–Manlove paper, which Pittel cites as motivation.

The proposed mass model is a coherent high-multiplicity matching problem. Given types with rankings over partner types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a symmetric matching array \(x\) with row sums \(\mu_t\), one can ask whether the support of \(x\) is exchange-stable. That may be worth studying on its own. But it does not mirror Pittel’s results: those concern random permutations ranking individually named partners, with the population and partner universe growing together. Keeping those identities makes the type space grow with \(n\); replacing them with partner categories changes the preference model and the random-instance distribution. Either way, the paper’s moment and probability results do not become computational questions about a continuous society.

The same gap applies to a proposed continuous version of the cited NP-complete decision problem. It would mirror that prior problem, not a computational result of this paper. And replacing the paper’s count of finite matchings with a count of mass allocations would need a new statistic: fractional allocations can form a continuum, so that count has no direct counterpart.

The honest limit of this negative case is that it cannot establish that every high-multiplicity e-stability question is unworthy. The type-based decision problem is plausible and could merit a separate project. Under the requested standard—anchoring a mirror on a named computational result of this paper—it does not survive.
