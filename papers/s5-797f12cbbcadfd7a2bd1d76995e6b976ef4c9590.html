The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the required anchor rule: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Its numbered results concern incentives, equilibria, and welfare. For example, “efficient” in the paper refers to allocative efficiency, not runtime; none of the results establishes that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or similarly classified.

The closest candidate is Proposition 3, proved in the paper: among the specified reward-distribution mechanisms, \(r(n)=\frac{n}{2n-1}R\) is Sybil-proof for every \(c\ge 0\) and maximizes social welfare. That is a mechanism-design result, not a computational one, so it cannot serve as an anchor here.

A continuum model could be posed for a distribution of participants by valuation and Sybil-creation cost, with masses choosing how many identities to report. But if each participant has negligible mass, an individual’s Sybil deviation disappears; if a positive-mass group can deviate together, the question becomes about coalition deviations rather than the paper’s individual Sybil-proofness. Either would be a new equilibrium question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Its numbered results concern equilibrium, incentive compatibility, mechanism characterization, and welfare. “Optimal” and “efficient” refer to allocations or mechanisms, not running time. No result defines a computational problem or establishes its complexity. A continuous population could change these analyses, but it cannot turn them into mirrors of computational results the paper does not contain.

Proposition 3 is the closest candidate, but its \(r(n)=\frac{n}{2n-1}R\) result optimizes a reward schedule over reported identity counts; it is not an algorithmic result. A plausible population model could give principals types containing their valuations, costs, and preferences, while letting each choose an integer number of identities. Yet in an atomless population, one principal’s finite Sybil deviation has zero effect on the aggregate. Letting a positive-mass group deviate together restores an effect but changes individual Sybil-proofness into a coalition question. Keeping principals individually consequential retains atoms rather than a continuous population. Scaling rewards or costs could produce a nondegenerate mean-field equilibrium, but that would be a new equilibrium-analysis problem, not a mirror of Proposition 3.

The same gap applies to the paper’s other plausible candidates: its cake-cutting bounds and bidding-ring results ask about fairness, welfare, and incentives. One could devise computational problems around implementing these mechanisms or finding optimal ones from a chosen representation, but the paper does not pose or solve those problems. That would be a new research direction, not a continuization of a result in this paper.

This negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated computational programme, but weak as a universal claim that no continuous study could be worthwhile. Permissionless systems can have large populations with repeated types, so a continuous game-theoretic model may be sensible and interesting. The narrower conclusion is that this paper offers no computational anchor for such a mirror.
