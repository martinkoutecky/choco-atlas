The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no admissible numbered computational anchor. Its numbered Propositions 4.1–4.3 make claims about how the auctioneer and bidders behave; none asserts that a computational problem is hard or tractable. The closest candidate is the Introduction’s unnumbered statement that revenue-maximizing Winner Determination is NP-complete. It is presented as background with citations, not as a numbered result proved here. So the required anchor set is empty.

If that unnumbered claim were allowed as an anchor, the strongest positive mirror I see would be the high-multiplicity version of Winner Determination. Consider a wholesale auction with many small buyers, where buyers fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of types because each type shares an identical OR bid menu and fairness-value vector. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s fraction of buyers, and let \(q_j\) be the per-capita supply of resource \(j\), with total supply scaling with the number of buyers. A configuration \(A\) for type \(t\) is any compatible set of that type’s atomic bids. Let \(x_{t,A}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned configuration \(A\). The problem, Type-Continuum Winner Determination, is to choose rational \(x_{t,A}\ge 0\) maximizing aggregate bid revenue, subject to \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,A}d_j(A)x_{t,A}\le q_j\) for each resource \(j\), where \(d_j(A)\) is the amount of \(j\) consumed by \(A\). A solution is an optimal mass assignment and its revenue. Each infinitesimal buyer gets a whole configuration; fractional quantities arise only when configurations are aggregated across a type.

The regime is plausible for repeated auctions of standardized inventory or service lots: the buyer population can be large while bid menus and fairness valuations repeat across buyer classes. The authors should recognize this as the allocation problem their Sandholm procedure solves. The mirror covers that allocation stage, not the paper’s proposed payment rules or its fairness and incentive claims.

I would expect the unrestricted version to remain hard. Its configuration pricing problem selects a maximum-value compatible set of OR bids, a weighted set-packing problem that can be hard even for \(\tau=1\). That makes this a candidate for continuum-specific hardness; it does not show that discrete hardness transfers. The natural follow-up is to identify bid-menu restrictions that make pricing tractable and determine the integrality gap against finite auctions.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s central contribution is its fairness payment scheme, while the complexity claim concerns the underlying WDP and is not a numbered result of the paper. Also, the continuous formulation permits type mass to split among configurations, so it is a relaxation of finite-buyer allocation. These limits leave a concrete mirror candidate, but they prevent me from presenting it as a case anchored in a named computational result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper establishes no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 4.1–4.3 make behavioral claims; the NP-completeness of Winner Determination appears as cited background, and the paper applies Sandholm’s algorithm rather than analyzing its complexity. On a strict reading of the requested anchors, there is no result here for a continuous mirror to take up.

That point does not defeat the proposed Type-Continuum Winner Determination problem. Its wholesale-auction scenario is plausible: buyers share complete bid-menu and valuation types, supply scales with the population, and each buyer can receive an integral configuration while the type-level assignment is fractional. That is a valid population continuization, and it tracks the allocation stage this paper discusses. Calling it merely fractional outcomes or objecting that it changes the supply model would miss the programme’s stated scope. The paper’s central payment contribution does not appear in this mirror, but the brief allows a mirror of just one result.

The payment contribution does face a more fundamental continuum problem. A VCG discount based on removing one bidder can vanish when an individual has zero mass. Removing an entire type preserves a nonzero effect, but asks about withdrawing a positive population share rather than the contribution of one bidder. A type-level derivative could define another payment rule, though it would need a new interpretation. This weakens the case for continuizing the fairness mechanism itself; it does not dispose of the Winner Determination mirror.

So the negative case is strong on the paper’s lack of an eligible named computational result, and weak against the best alternative scenario. If the cited Winner Determination claim is accepted as an anchor, the proposed mirror survives; I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
