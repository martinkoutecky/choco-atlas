The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is the paper’s one-sided allocation result. One caveat: the paper has no named theorem claiming a standard complexity bound such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. The closest algorithmic anchor is Theorem 14, proved in the appendix. It gives a conditional guarantee, not a runtime bound or an unconditional algorithm for finding an equilibrium.

Theorem 14 considers \(n\) players and \(n\) items, with utilities \(u_{ij}\in[0,1]\) normalized so each player’s minimum is \(0\) and maximum is \(1\). It uses the regularizer \(f_0(x_{ij})=-\beta/x_{ij}\), with \(\beta=\bar\lambda^{-3}n^{-4}\), restricts bids to \(\lambda_{i,t}\in[0,\bar\lambda]\) for \(\bar\lambda>10\), and assumes every player has strong regret below \(\varepsilon T\), where \(\varepsilon=o(\bar\lambda^{-12}n^{-22})\). It concludes that the resulting allocation is a \(\delta\)-competitive equilibrium at unit budgets, for \(\delta=10/\bar\lambda<1\), supported by prices \(C'_j=(1-\delta/3)C_j\); \(C_j\) are VCG prices for the utilities scaled by each player’s best-response weight \(\lambda_i\).

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: Type-Compressed Approximate HZ Allocation. An instance has agent types \(a\in A\) and item types \(g\in G\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_g\) summing to \(1\), and rational utilities \(u_{ag}\in[0,1]\), normalized per type. The question is to find type weights \(\lambda_a\), item-type prices \(p_g\), and an allocation flow \(x_{ag}\ge0\). For each type with positive mass, its per-agent lottery is \(q_{ag}=x_{ag}/\mu_a\). A solution must clear supply, \(\sum_g x_{ag}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ag}=\nu_g\); keep each type’s bundle within its unit budget; and make that bundle \(\delta\)-optimal among all unit-demand lotteries affordable at \(p\). Prices should support the allocation as an approximate optimum of the type-weighted assignment problem, whose welfare objective is \(\sum_{a,g}\mu_a\lambda_a u_{ag}q_{ag}\). Each person still receives a lottery over integral items; the fractional object is the flow of population mass. Item-type supplies scale with cohort size.

A credible regime is a large annual school or course-placement cohort: many students, but relatively few complete utility profiles over course categories and relatively few course types with capacities proportional to the cohort. A type includes the full utility vector and the same budget and other parameters. The paper’s Section 3.2 is already about unit-demand allocation with lotteries, equal token endowments, VCG, and HZ equilibria, so this is recognizably the authors’ problem. Aggregating repeated utility profiles and co-scaling supplies changes the instance representation and market size; it does not replace population continuity with fractional individual outcomes.

My tentative expectation is Class A for approximate computation when the type support is moderate: the assignment variables compress to \(|A||G|\), and the paper’s regularized allocation step is a concave optimization problem. The conjectured target would be time polynomial in \(|A|,|G|\), input bit length, and \(1/\delta\), without dependence on the cohort size. That is a proposed research direction, not a consequence of Theorem 14. The paper does not prove that the low-regret execution exists or can be found efficiently; it explicitly leaves strong-regret algorithms and convergence open.

The weakest point is the passage from individual bid weights \(\lambda_i\) in Theorem 14 to type-level weights \(\lambda_a\). The theorem does not show that identical agents use identical weights, or establish the corresponding continuum equilibrium. A useful next question is whether type symmetry permits a type-level construction—or whether within-type mixtures of weights must be part of the continuous model. This mirror covers the paper’s one-sided allocation results, especially regularized VCG and approximate HZ equilibrium; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s generic framework, voting, or two-sided matching.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result to continuize. Theorem 5 is an existence result proved by a fixed-point argument; Theorem 14 is conditional on a low-regret execution and gives an equilibrium guarantee, not an algorithm or a running-time bound. The paper itself leaves the relevant computation and convergence questions open. So a type-compressed solver would be a new result prompted by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its computational results. On the programme’s usual benchmark, that makes this a weak anchor.

The proposed school or course-placement setting is nevertheless a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Grouping students with identical utilities and scaling supplies with the cohort is a recognizable population model, and the type-level flow still gives each person a lottery over integral items. Calling that a scope violation would be wrong.

The sharper objection is that the proposed problem does not preserve Theorem 14’s central object: a repeated mechanism in which each individual chooses bid weights, faces a token budget, and is assumed to have low strong regret. It asks instead for a type-level HZ equilibrium supported by weighted assignment. That is a coherent market problem, and it is close to the paper’s HZ results, but it removes the strategic dynamics that Theorem 14 analyzes. Restoring them would require tracking possibly different bid histories among agents of the same preference type; a single weight \(\lambda_a\) and allocation flow do not establish that the individual regret argument survives.

Nor does Theorem 14 immediately supply the missing bridge. Its regularizer and regret requirements depend on the number of individual agents, with the stated error condition shrinking as \(n^{-22}\). Replacing \(n\) by the number of types is not justified by the theorem. One would need a new analysis showing that type aggregation preserves prices, incentives, and the regret-to-equilibrium implication, or a richer continuum model that tracks within-type bid variation.

That is the best negative case: the paper is a poor direct source for ChoCo’s computational-complexity landscape, and the proponent’s formulation is a proposed research question rather than a continuization of a complexity theorem. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The type-compressed HZ computation problem remains plausible; the honest weakness is that this paper’s actual theorem does not yet give it an algorithmic foothold.
