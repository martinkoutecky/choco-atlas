What the mirror covers

No named result yields a computational mirror; the paper’s results concern structural characterizations of tight guarantees and sharing rules.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large community sharing a cleanup cost: a type is a resident’s pollution level from a finite set of levels, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents at level \(t\). That makes a continuous-population extension of the fairness question conceivable.

But I can’t honestly anchor it in this paper’s computational results: the paper has no named computational complexity result. Its results—including Proposition 4.1 on the Unanimity guarantee, Theorem 4.1 on the Stand Alone guarantees, Proposition 5.1 on tight guarantees for substitute inputs, and Theorems 6.1 and 7.1—are structural characterizations of fairness guarantees. They do not establish that an underlying computational problem is tractable or hard, and the paper gives no algorithmic formulation that it computes with.

The paper also already allows each agent’s type to vary continuously over an interval, while keeping the population finite. That is continuous type space, not continuous population. A mass-distribution version could be an interesting new problem, but it would not be a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. So there are no eligible anchors, and no per-anchor prover questions to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no eligible anchor. Proposition 4.1, Theorem 4.1, Proposition 5.1, and Theorems 6.1 and 7.1 are mathematical characterizations of tight guarantees. None states the complexity of a computational problem, gives an algorithm for an encoded input, or asks whether a computational task is tractable. Calling them anchors would mistake a theorem about fairness for a computational result. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, that is the strongest case against including this paper.

The pollution example is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s continuous type interval is not itself a continuous population. So neither fact rules out a population mirror. The strongest version would take residents’ pollution levels from a finite type set, represent their shares by a distribution \(\mu\), normalize the aggregate cleanup cost per capita, and ask for tight lower and upper transfer rates \(g^-(t)\) and \(g^+(t)\) satisfying integral bounds over population distributions. That could be worthwhile fair-division research. But it is a new functional characterization problem: the paper does not specify an encoded input, an algorithmic task, or a criterion for selecting among the many tight pairs. Its existing characterizations do not become computational results just by replacing sums with integrals.

The best apparent hook is Theorem 6.1, because it characterizes all tight guarantees for rank-separable functions. Yet computing a selected guarantee would require adding a selection objective or other rule the paper deliberately leaves to further axioms. Theorem 7.1 is even less direct: it characterizes the two-person case, which has no large-population regime as stated. Generalizing either theorem could generate new questions, but neither supplies a computational problem from this paper to continuize.

So the negative case is strong if the question is whether this paper offers a ChoCo mirror of its results: it does not. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population study could arise in any scenario. The pollution example makes such a study plausible; the honest objection is that it would be a new fair-division project, not a computational mirror of this paper.
