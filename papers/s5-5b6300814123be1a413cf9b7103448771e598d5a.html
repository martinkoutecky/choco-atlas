The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case has a firm limit: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Its Katz path formula defines the tally, and it reports simulations, but neither is a named result that a continuous mirror can anchor to. The closest hook is the unnumbered claim in “Importance of the Social Network and Abstentionism” that, on a connected network, anyone could win with the right delegation graph. So the number of eligible anchors is zero. I can propose a plausible computational question, but cannot present it as a mirror of a qualifying result in this paper.

A natural setting would be a very large online community with many users in a small number of recurring roles—for example, cohorts sharing issue expertise, tenure, candidacy status, and the same kinds of contacts. Let each complete type \(t\) have rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(E\) specify which types can delegate to which other types, including self-delegation. The regime is convincing only when the population is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of such types, and the shared contact structure is a credible feature of the community.

A possible follow-on question is Type-Level Viscous Winner Feasibility\(_\infty\). Given \(\mu\), \(E\), a rational damping factor \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), a set \(K\) of eligible candidate types, and a target \(t^\star\in K\), ask whether there is an aggregate delegation ballot under which \(t^\star\) has strictly higher per-person Katz score than every other eligible type. A ballot is a row-stochastic matrix \(P\) supported on \(E\): \(P_{tu}\) is the fraction of type \(t\)’s mass whose members choose a proxy of type \(u\). Each individual still chooses one proxy; the fraction records the choices across a large type. If \(r_t\) is type \(t\)’s aggregate score mass, its per-person score is \(r_t/\mu_t\). A solution is such a delegation policy \(P\), together with a certificate that the target’s score is strictly greatest.

This proposed problem appears tractable. Set \(f_{tu}=r_tP_{tu}\). Then the tally is captured by the linear constraints
\[ r_t=\mu_t+\alpha\sum_s f_{st},\qquad \sum_u f_{tu}=r_t,\qquad f_{tu}\ge 0, \]
with \(f_{tu}=0\) outside \(E\). The target wins strictly when \(\mu_jr_{t^\star}>\mu_{t^\star}r_j\) for every \(j\in K\setminus\{t^\star\}\). Maximizing the smallest score gap is an LP; if its optimum is positive, \(P_{tu}=f_{tu}/r_t\) gives a ballot. This is a candidate Class A result derived from the paper’s tally, not a result the authors establish. Further questions include how well this model approximates finite delegation graphs, and whether type-level versions of their abstention analysis or committee rules remain tractable.

The paper’s subject matter makes the proposal plausible: it preserves proxy delegation along social ties and the exponentially damped path tally, while making the population the continuous object. Its unnumbered claim about who can win is the nearest substantive connection. The mirror covers that winnability question and the tally only; it does not cover the paper’s abstention discussion, committee proportionality, or simulations.

My weakest point is also decisive for the requested format: the paper offers no named hardness result whose hardness might dissolve, and the proposed LP asks about candidate types under an exchangeable contact model, whereas the paper’s candidates are individual network nodes. Thus this is a credible research question suggested by the paper, but not an anchored positive case under the stipulated standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent concedes the strongest point: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its path-sum formula defines the tally; its claim that anyone could win on a connected network is qualitative; and its simulations report outcomes, not complexity. The paper even says that how scores determine a motion’s outcome is outside the voting system it studies. So there is no computational theorem here from which to build the programme’s continuous complexity landscape. That says nothing about what the continuous answer would be; it means the paper supplies no qualifying result to ask it about.

The nearest hook is the claim that, given the right delegation graph, any node could win. But the proposed Type-Level Viscous Winner Feasibility problem changes that question. In the paper, each person chooses one fixed proxy among their actual neighbors, and all influence arriving at that person follows that same choice. In the proposed LP, \(r_tP_{tu}\) distributes type \(t\)’s arriving score according to the type’s overall proxy proportions. If score reaches only the members who chose one particular proxy, that distribution is not possible. An edge relation \(E\) between types also omits which individuals are connected. Restoring those details can require splitting types by proxy choice and neighborhood, potentially leaving support size close to the population. The LP is valid for some stronger symmetry or fractional-delegation model, but then it asks whether a *type’s average score* can win, rather than whether a named node can win.

The best repair is a finite-type, equitable network model with fractional delegation and a type-level winner question. That could be a sensible new computational problem, and the paper mentions fractional delegation as a possible variant. It still would be inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result it established. The negative case is therefore strong on the requested mirror verdict, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile research could grow from this voting model. A symmetric type-level follow-on may be worth studying; this paper does not anchor it.
