The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result in the sense required for an anchor: it proves no problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified by computational complexity.

The closest results are Proposition 1, proved here, which says that under rotating agenda setting, the stated tax-protection rule and a balanced budget, vote-buying with sufficiently low moral costs prevents society from being educated in finite time; and Proposition 2, also proved here, which says that adding enough repeated votes lets the society become educated in finite time. Corollary 1, whose proof is omitted, extends Proposition 1’s impossibility to societies with some initially educated members. These are equilibrium and economic-feasibility claims, not computational results.

A continuous-population model of households and vote-buying could be a sensible new research question. But without a qualifying computational anchor from this paper, presenting it as the requested continuous *computational* mirror would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the decisive gap, but “this could be a sensible new research question” does not fill it. Proposition 1 establishes an equilibrium outcome in which vote-buying blocks education; Proposition 2 establishes that a constitution with repeated voting can permit education; Corollary 1 extends the first outcome. None defines a computational problem or gives a complexity or algorithmic result. The continuous analogue of each, on its own, would be a question about equilibrium and economic dynamics—the kind of analytic work the programme distinguishes from computational complexity.

The strongest rescue would define a mass vote-buying problem: given a distribution of household types and a redistribution proposal, find the least costly mass of voters to bribe to block it, or the repetitions needed to deter bribery. That could be an interesting problem in its own right. But the paper’s results do not pose or solve that optimization problem. They analyze strategic equilibrium under a common moral cost per vote, then derive whether education occurs. Adding type-dependent prices, a computational objective, and an input encoding would supply the core of a new problem rather than mirror a result the authors proved. The rotating agenda rule also needs substantive redesign: in an atomless population, random selection cannot give each person the turn that drives the finite society’s education result. A type-level rotation is plausible, but it changes the institution and its guarantee.

So the negative case is strong for this paper under ChoCo’s computational scope: its numbered results do not provide an anchor, and the best computational salvage requires building one. The universal claim is weaker. A high-multiplicity model of households facing vote-buying could still be worthwhile research; I cannot honestly rule that out across every scenario. It would be a new computational political-economy project, rather than a continuous computational mirror of these propositions.
