The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and each type's strict preferences over feasible coalition compositions \(x\in[0,\mu]^{|T|}\) with \(x_t>0\), encoded by rational affine lexicographic keys and a singleton option, does every instance admit a measurable partition that is internally stable against positive-mass within-coalition deviations and Pareto optimal, and can one construct such a partition from the finite input?

The model it lives in

An atomless hedonic society with finitely many interchangeable types and masses \(\mu_t\); each coalition is described by its type-mass vector \(x\), and preferences are encoded over those vectors. A measurable allocation partitions the type mass among coalitions and singleton options; internal stability excludes blocking subgroups of positive mass, and Pareto optimality excludes alternatives that weakly improve almost every agent and strictly improve a positive mass.

The objection that survived

The paper provides no finite output representation for an atomless partition, and the proponent's composition-vector description does not establish that a solution can be finitely represented.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's guarantee of a partition that is internally stable and Pareto optimal; it does not cover the conditional core- and Nash-stability claims in Propositions 1 and 2 or identity-dependent preferences in arbitrary hedonic games.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational finite-type instance admit an internally stable, Pareto-optimal measurable partition, and can one be given an effective finite representation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every finite hedonic game \(G=(N,\succ)\) and every ordering \(\sigma\) of its agents, the authors’ algorithm terminates with a partition whose coalitions are all internally stable and which is Pareto optimal. Thus the set of internally stable, Pareto optimal partitions is nonempty. The algorithm repeatedly searches agents’ ranked coalitions for an internally stable one, removes it, and continues on the remaining agents.

That is an algorithmic existence result, not a complexity classification: the paper gives no polynomial-time bound and has no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer question. I would use Theorem 1 alone. Propositions 1 and 2 add conditional stability properties when the output is all singletons or the grand coalition, but they are weaker anchors for the main mirror.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large community forming clubs or project groups. Residents may differ by a small number of relevant profiles—skills, roles, and preferences over group composition—while residents with the same profile are interchangeable for the problem. For example, a member may care about the group’s size and the mix of roles in it, but not the names of its members. The society is then a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite type set \(T\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of residents of type \(t\) and \(|T|=\tau\ll n\). A type must include every attribute affecting preferences; idiosyncratic differences that matter belong in different types.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Finite-type continuous IntS–PO partition. An instance has a finite set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and an atomless population of each type with that mass. A coalition is described by its composition vector \(x\), where \(x_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) in it. Each type \(t\) has a strict preference order over the singleton option \(\bot_t\) and all feasible coalition vectors \(x\in[0,\mu]^\tau\) with \(x_t>0\). To make the input finite and explicit, encode the order by lexicographic keys: coalition \(x\) has key \((a_{t,1}(x),\ldots,a_{t,d_t}(x),x_1,\ldots,x_\tau)\), where the \(a_{t,k}\) are rational affine forms; the singleton has its own rational key with trailing zero coordinates. The final coordinates break ties between distinct coalition profiles.

The question is whether one can construct a measurable partition of this population that is internally stable and Pareto optimal. A solution can be represented by the composition vectors of its positive-mass coalitions, plus the residual mass of each type assigned to singleton coalitions; any such feasible vectors can be realized because each type population is atomless. Internal stability means that no positive-mass subgroup of a coalition can leave and form a group whose participating types all strictly prefer its composition, and no positive mass of any type in a coalition prefers to become singletons. Pareto optimality is tested against all feasible measurable partitions: there must be no alternative that makes almost every resident weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off. The question is thus whether every encoded instance admits such a partition and, if so, to construct one.

This keeps the paper’s central object and its two criteria: a partition of a hedonic society, internal deviations, and Pareto efficiency. It continuizes the population; representing a group by a vector of type masses is the needed co-modification. It does not replace the question with fractional outcomes or continuous time. The authors could reasonably recognize it as their problem in an anonymous, high-multiplicity regime, though not as a mirror of every hedonic game with identity-dependent preferences.

My provisional expectation is continuum-specific difficulty (Class C), or possibly failure of universal existence, rather than transferred hardness. There is no discrete hardness result here to transfer. The finite algorithm searches ranked coalitions in a finite list; the mirror has a continuum of possible group compositions and fractional subgroups, so that search argument does not carry over directly. If existence does hold, the next question is whether it has a finite, efficiently constructible description—perhaps through optimization over the type-mass profiles.

The weakest point is exactly that change in domain. The paper permits preferences over named members, while this mirror requires that type composition fully determines preference; it also replaces individual deviations with positive-mass deviations in an atomless society. Those assumptions are defensible for large anonymous clubs, but they narrow the claim. What remains is still the paper’s central existence question—whether internal stability and Pareto optimality can be achieved together—rather than a different continuous-outcome problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only serious anchor. It gives a terminating procedure for finding an internally stable, Pareto-optimal partition, but no running-time bound, hardness result, approximation guarantee, or parameterized analysis. Its input is a complete ranking over every coalition containing each agent, and the algorithm searches those rankings and checks coalitions for internal stability. So the paper establishes constructive existence; it leaves the computational landscape largely uncharted.

That leaves a representation problem for the proposed mirror. In a finite-type continuum, each type needs preferences over a continuum of coalition compositions. The paper supplies no finite language for those preferences. The affine lexicographic keys in the proponent’s model provide one, but they add structure absent from the paper; other concise preference languages could define materially different problems. A computable mirror therefore needs choices about preference encoding, measurable partitions, and which positive-mass deviations count. The paper does not make those choices for us.

The strongest red-side claim is that this is a poor fit for a programme aimed at complexity questions: Theorem 1 has no complexity classification to continue, and a well-posed continuous algorithmic problem would require a new preference model whose computational properties could depend on the chosen encoding.

That case has a clear limit. Large clubs or project teams with a small number of interchangeable roles are plausible high-multiplicity settings, and preferences over group composition can preserve the paper’s internal-stability and Pareto questions. The programme permits that kind of co-modification. I therefore cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The objection is that this paper does not provide a canonical computational mirror—not that a credible one cannot be built.
