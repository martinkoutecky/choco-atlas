A plausible continuous follow-up would study a large supply-chain cluster with a small number of complete firm types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of firms of type \(t\), where a type fixes the economic attributes used by the model. Given a rational polynomial \(W(x)\) for the worth of a coalition with type masses \(x\), form finite games with \(q\mu_t\) replica firms of each type and coalition worth \(qW(k/q)\), where \(k_t\) is the coalition’s count of type \(t\). The problem is to compute the limiting Shapley allocation by type:
\[ A_t=\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}\phi_i =\mu_t\int_0^1 \partial_t W(u\mu)\,du. \]
For explicitly represented bounded-degree \(W\), this is tractable by exact polynomial integration. It remains recognizable as the paper’s Shapley-allocation problem, but it is an illustrative follow-up, not a problem anchored in a named result of this paper.

The weak point is substantial: the paper studies six heterogeneous companies and coalition values on a finite player set. It does not supply the continuous worth function or establish that large groups of firms sharing complete types are a useful regime. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no result from the paper as an anchor: there is no theorem giving a complexity classification, and the proposed algorithm has neither a correctness proof nor a running-time bound. The six-company calculation is an example, not a computational result. That leaves no anchor to mirror.

The proposed replica game is coherent, but it supplies assumptions the paper does not: a sequence of economies with worth \(qW(k/q)\), complete firm types, and coalition worth determined solely by type masses. The paper instead starts with named companies, their individual profits and price tolerances, and a supply-chain coalition structure. It never defines a worth function on mass vectors or explains how feasible coalitions scale as the cluster grows. One could build those ingredients from the paper’s economics, even allowing a piecewise-smooth worth function to accommodate minimum price tolerances. But that would be a new model. In a large chain, eligibility may depend on partners or network position; including those distinctions in complete types could make the support grow with the number of firms. Assuming away that dependence is precisely what makes the proposed integral available.

So the integral does not establish a continuous mirror of the paper’s algorithm. It answers a different, more structured question: how to allocate value in a replicated game whose worth function is already succinctly specified. That may be a worthwhile study of continuous cooperative games, but the paper supplies neither the computational result nor the scaling model that would connect it to this programme.

The negative case is not airtight. A cluster of many interchangeable firms across a few echelons is plausible, and its type-level Shapley allocation could be recognizable to the authors as a useful extension. The case against rests on the gap between that extension and what this paper actually formalizes—not on a claim that such a continuous question could never be worth asking.
