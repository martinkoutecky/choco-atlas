The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), two rational quota vectors \(b,\hat b\), and type weight vectors \(a_t\in\{0,1\}^k\) and \(\hat a_t\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}\), decide whether the two games accept exactly the same mass coalitions \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). A coalition is accepted by the first game when \(\sum_t x_ta_t\ge b\), and by the second when \(\sum_t x_t\hat a_t\ge\hat b\), with inequalities interpreted coordinatewise; return a distinguishing \(x\) if one exists.

The model it lives in

Use types defined by each member’s pair of complete weight vectors in the two games, with each type carrying mass \(\mu_t\). A mass coalition chooses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\); the per-capita quotas and weighted sums determine acceptance. The decision objective is whether the two acceptance regions in this box are equal.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers equivalence in Theorems 4 and 5 and row relevance and minimality in Theorem 9. It leaves the dimension bounds, parameterized equivalence algorithms, and the minimum-dimension question in Theorem 11 unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite multiplicities \(n_t\), what margin conditions let a fractional distinguishing coalition be rounded to a discrete coalition \(z_t/n\) that still distinguishes the games?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is continuous voting-game equivalence, anchored by Theorem 4 (proved in this paper). The theorem says that deciding whether two given vector weighted voting games have the same winning coalitions is co-NP-complete, even when every weight is \(0\) or \(1\), so both games are monotone.

A plausible regime is a very large federation or membership organization whose members fall into a small number of voting classes. Many members may share the same voting role under both rules being compared. For this comparison, a type is the pair of their complete weight vectors in the two games. If there are \(n\) members but only \(\tau\) such pairs, with \(\tau\ll n\), the society is represented by rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\).

Theorem 4’s mirror: Continuous Vector-Game Equivalence. An instance consists of those masses, two rational quota vectors \(b,\hat b\), and type weights \(a_t\in\{0,1\}^k\) and \(\hat a_t\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}\). A coalition is represented by \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass selected from each type. The games accept that coalition respectively when
\[ \sum_t x_t a_t\ge b \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t x_t\hat a_t\ge \hat b, \]
with inequalities interpreted coordinatewise. Decide whether they accept exactly the same mass coalitions; a NO solution can give a distinguishing vector \(x\).

This is in P, with running time polynomial in \(\tau,k,\ell\), and the input bit length \(L\). To test whether the first game accepts a coalition the second rejects, try each row \(j\) of the second game and test feasibility of \(Ax\ge b\), \(\hat A_jx<\hat b_j\), and \(0\le x\le\mu\); then reverse the games. Each strict inequality can be checked by maximizing a slack variable and testing whether its optimum is positive. Thus the Boolean coalition search becomes a polynomial number of rational LP feasibility checks. I would expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). It preserves the paper’s underlying question—whether two voting rules define the same winners—and changes the coalition domain to mass selections in an atomless population.

A second anchor tests whether that tractability is only an effect of removing variable dimension. Theorem 5 (proved here) says that equivalence of two ordinary weighted voting games is co-NP-complete even when both games are nonnegative and hence monotone. Its reduction compares games with the same weights \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), but quotas \(T\) and \(T+1\); they differ exactly when some subset of weights sums to \(T\).

Theorem 5’s mirror: Continuous Scalar-Game Equivalence. Types now have a pair of nonnegative scalar weights \(a_t,\hat a_t\), one for each game, and the two games have rational per-capita quotas \(Q,\hat Q\). For every \(x\in[0,\mu]\), they accept according to whether \(\sum_t a_tx_t\ge Q\) and \(\sum_t\hat a_tx_t\ge\hat Q\). Decide whether these rules agree for all \(x\), returning a distinguishing \(x\) if not. Two LP feasibility checks decide this in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\). In the reduction’s same-weight special case, partial selection fills the whole interval of attainable total weights from \(0\) to \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t\). The discrete exact-subset-sum witness is no longer needed. This is also Class A, and it shows the continuum removes hardness even in the paper’s one-dimensional setting.

A third useful anchor is Theorem 9 (proved here): both RELEVANCE and MINIMALITY are NP-complete even when all weights are \(0\) or \(1\). Here RELEVANCE asks whether deleting a specified row changes the game, and MINIMALITY asks whether every row is relevant; the paper proves hardness by reduction from VERTEX COVER.

Theorem 9’s mirror: Continuous Row Relevance and Minimality. Given one vector game \(A x\ge b\) on the same type-mass box, ask whether deleting row \(i\) changes the winning mass coalitions; continuous minimality asks whether every row is relevant. Deleting a row can only add winners, so row \(i\) is relevant exactly when there is an \(x\) satisfying all other rows but violating row \(i\): \(A_{-i}x\ge b_{-i}\), \(A_ix<b_i\), and \(0\le x\le\mu\). One strict-feasibility LP per row decides relevance, and checking all rows decides minimality, in time polynomial in \(k,\tau,L\). This is another Class A mirror of a named hardness result.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is explicit: a finite society with \(n_t\) members of type \(t\) has \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and each discrete coalition gives a mass vector \(x_t=z_t/n\) for integer \(z_t\). The continuous mirror admits every point in the box, including fractional selections unavailable to that finite coalition. That makes it a genuine population continuization, not continuity of the outcome space. The scope here is deliberately limited to the paper’s equivalence and row-deletion results; it does not cover its dimension bounds or Theorem 11’s question about whether a two-vector game has any equivalent one-vector representation.

My weakest point is that a continuous coalition may split a type fractionally, while a finite voter cannot be split. The LP results therefore do not solve the paper’s finite problems. They establish the complexity of the high-multiplicity limit, and leave a concrete bridge question: when does a fractional distinguishing coalition have enough margin to round to a discrete one? For relevance, the corresponding question is when a row’s continuous witness survives finite-population rounding. That limitation is also what makes the comparison informative: it separates hardness caused by indivisible coalitions from hardness that would persist over mass coalitions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that all three reduce to the same elementary geometry. Let \(P=\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\) be the set of mass coalitions. Non-equivalence asks whether \(P\) intersects a winning region of one game and a losing region of the other; row relevance asks whether \(P\) intersects the region satisfying the other rows while violating the deleted row. Each is a polynomial number of rational LP checks. The scalar case is simpler still: attainable weighted totals fill an interval. These are three versions of polyhedral containment, not three independent computational findings.

That makes Theorem 5’s mirror the weakest research prospect. Its subset-sum reduction uses potentially distinct weights, so it offers no high-multiplicity compression in the hard instances: the number of types can be \(n\). With a sensible mass model and per-capita quota, the exact-subset-sum obstruction disappears because a type can be split. That is a clear effect of continuization, but the resulting interval calculation is so direct that it may not justify much research effort by itself.

Theorem 4 and Theorem 9 fare better as examples, but the proposed mirrors still look like routine consequences of writing coalition selection as \(x\in P\). They do identify precisely what the discrete versions add: indivisibility. Yet the programme’s standards make that a possible result, not a disqualifier. The authors could reasonably care whether that is where the hardness goes, and whether finite witnesses can be recovered from fractional ones. Calling the LP routine does not show those questions are unworthy.

Nor can I make the stronger modeling objections stick. A large membership organization with many interchangeable voting classes gives a plausible population, with each type recording its weights in both games and quotas scaled per capita. Aumann and Shapley’s work on voting games with a continuum of individually insignificant voters confirms that this is a familiar regime, though it studies values rather than computational equivalence; under the programme’s rule, that is supporting context, not a novelty collision. [Milnor and Shapley, “Values of Large Games II: Oceanic Games”](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.3.4.290). The mass coalition also preserves the information these three questions use: voters with identical weight vectors in both games are indistinguishable for equivalence and row relevance.

So the best negative verdict is limited: the three anchors largely repeat one simple LP observation, and the scalar anchor is especially thin. But that does not defeat the better mirrors. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario; the negative case is weak precisely because the proposed mass-coalition questions are coherent, tractable, and plausibly useful for separating integrality-driven hardness from its continuous counterpart.
