The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), schools \(J\) with rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_j\), and positive rational utilities \(u_{tj}\) that are distinct across type-school pairs, compute the feasible assignment \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{tj}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tj}\le\kappa_j\), that is stable. Stability means there is no pair \((t,j)\) for which positive mass of type \(t\) is unassigned or assigned to a school \(k\) with \(u_{tk}<u_{tj}\), while school \(j\) has spare capacity or holds positive mass of a type \(s\) with \(u_{sj}<u_{tj}\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity matching market whose types are complete utility vectors, with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_j\), and assignment variables \(x_{tj}\). Schools value assigned mass additively, by \(\sum_t u_{tj}x_{tj}\); stability uses positive-mass blocking pairs as specified in the mirror question. The max-max-lex procedure repeatedly assigns the largest possible mass to the currently highest-utility available type-school pair.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2.1's max-max-lex characterization and its stable, unique allocation result. It leaves the max-min-lex algorithm and the paper's inequality discussion aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the stable aggregate assignment behave when distinct type-school pairs can have tied utilities?
  • Can the characterization extend to an atomless spatial population with an explicit, tractable representation of its utility distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is Aligned Stable Mass Assignment, in a large placement market where applicants fall into a modest number of standardized fit profiles. For example, a city might assign a very large annual cohort to training programs, with each applicant’s type given by their full vector of utility scores for the programs. Programs value a cohort by the sum of its members’ scores, as in the paper’s aligned-preference assumption. The regime is \(N\) applicants and \(m\) programs, with \(\tau\ll N\) distinct profiles; each profile has the same preferences, and programs’ evaluations depend on those same scores.

Here is the continuous problem. An instance gives a finite set \(T\) of types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational capacities \(\kappa_j\ge 0\) for each program \(j\in J\), and positive rational utilities \(u_{tj}\). Assume distinct type–program pairs have distinct utilities, so comparisons between types are strict. A feasible solution is a mass assignment \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_jx_{tj}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{tj}\le\kappa_j\). Each unit of mass gets one actual program; \(x_{tj}\) records how much of type \(t\) goes to \(j\). Thus the variables are fractional only at the cohort level. Program \(j\)’s utility is \(\sum_tu_{tj}x_{tj}\), preserving the paper’s alignment.

Call \(x\) stable if there is no type–program pair \((t,j)\) that can block with positive mass. More explicitly, define
\[ A_{tj}=\mu_t-\sum_kx_{tk}+\sum_{k:u_{tk}<u_{tj}}x_{tk}, \qquad B_{tj}=\kappa_j-\sum_sx_{sj}+\sum_{s:u_{sj}<u_{tj}}x_{sj}. \]
Here \(A_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) that is unassigned or would prefer \(j\); \(B_{tj}\) is \(j\)’s spare capacity plus the mass it would prefer to replace with type \(t\). Stability requires that \(A_{tj}\) and \(B_{tj}\) are never both positive. The problem is to compute the stable mass assignment. In the generic case above, ask for the unique \(x\); equivalently, lexicographically maximize the mass assigned at each distinct utility level, ordered from highest to lowest, with unassigned mass at level \(-\infty\).

The anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved in this paper. Under positive utilities, no indifferences, and strictly aligned preferences, it establishes three things: (a) max-max-lex maximizes the vector of student–school utilities ranked from highest to lowest among feasible allocations; (b) its allocation is stable; and (c) the stable allocation is unique. The theorem does not state a complexity class, and the paper gives no named hardness result. But its stated algorithm repeatedly selects the highest-utility pair among remaining students and schools with capacity, so it directly yields a polynomial-time algorithm in the finite model.

I expect the mass version to be tractable (Class A) as well. Repeatedly choose the active type–program pair with highest \(u_{tj}\), assign \(\min\{\text{remaining mass of }t,\text{remaining capacity of }j\}\), and update the residuals. Each step exhausts at least one type or one program, so there are at most \(\tau+|J|\) steps, each requiring a maximum over at most \(\tau|J|\) pairs. With binary rational input, the expected running time is polynomial in \(|J|,\tau,L\). This is the paper’s max-max-lex procedure expressed in population units. The bridge is exact for rational data: scaling the masses and capacities by a common denominator gives a finite market of indistinguishable copies, and a finite market of that form gives the rational mass instance.

This mirror covers Theorem 2.1’s stable-allocation result and its max-max-lex characterization. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s separate max-min-lex algorithm or its inequality discussion. It also generates questions about ties and non-unique mass assignments, extension from finitely many profiles to the spatial continuum illustrated in the paper, and how rounding affects finite cohorts whose masses are not exact multiples of the profile shares.

The weak point is that the paper’s no-indifferences assumption cannot hold literally between repeated copies of one type. The mirror therefore asks for uniqueness of the aggregate mass assignment, not of named individuals’ identities, and assumes strict comparisons between distinct type–program pairs. That is a real adjustment, though it preserves the decisions and aligned utilities the theorem studies. The other limitation is modest computational novelty: the source already has a simple algorithm, so the mirror mainly makes its dependence on support size explicit. The paper’s uniform-square illustration also gestures toward a continuous population, but its theorem and algorithm are stated for a finite set of students; the mass problem above makes that population the input to a computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is about fit with ChoCo’s computational agenda. Theorem 2.1 proves a structural characterization and the correctness of an algorithm; it does not state a complexity result. So the mass-assignment question is supplied by the analyst, not singled out by the paper as an unresolved computational problem. A more ambitious mirror could use an atomless spatial population, like the paper’s illustration, but then the highest-utility pairs may occur only at locations of measure zero. The paper’s step-by-step algorithm no longer directly assigns positive mass, and a complexity question would need extra choices about how the population distribution is represented and queried.

That is a limited objection. The proposed finite-support mirror is coherent: exact repeated profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and assigning the mass of the best available type–school pair preserves the paper’s aligned preferences and stability idea. Rational masses and capacities also give a clean bridge to finite cohorts. Neither the repeated-type indifferences nor the change to mass blocking defeats it.

There is continuum stable-matching work with unique outcomes and Deferred-Acceptance analogues, such as [Arnosti’s model with finite capacities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881). That is not a collision: it does not answer this computational-complexity question. It does, however, underline that the continuum matching model itself is not the new contribution.

So I cannot honestly make the universal negative case. The paper supplies no complexity theorem to mirror, and the richer spatial version needs a computational input model. But the finite-profile version survives those objections; it is a legitimate high-multiplicity question, even if its connection to the paper’s algorithm looks direct.
