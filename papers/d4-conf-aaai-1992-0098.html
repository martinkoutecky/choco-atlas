What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a qualifying named anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition stating a computational complexity result. Its closest claims are unnumbered: the paper describes WALRAS as deriving competitive equilibria, reports a transportation example reaching specified numerical tolerances, and gives equilibrium outcomes in Table 1. Those are not numbered complexity results. The general computability discussion points to earlier work rather than proving a named result here. So there are zero qualifying anchors; that is a limit on the positive case, not a verdict that no mirror exists.

The strongest candidate I would put to a prover is Continuous System-Equilibrium Routing, as a mirror of the paper’s reported transportation system-equilibrium result. Its instance is a directed network \(G=(V,E)\), a finite set \(T\) of shipper types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a per-agent cargo requirement \(q_t\) and origin–destination pair for each type. Each link \(e\) has a rational convex cost function \(f_e\), such as the quadratic link costs in the example. The question is to find nonnegative type-specific flows \(x_{t,e}\) that route demand \(\mu_t q_t\) from each type’s origin to its destination, minimizing
\[ \sum_{e\in E} f_e\!\left(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,e}\right). \]
A solution is an optimal set of flows, with link loads and, where useful, their marginal-cost prices. This makes the *population* continuous: many similar shippers become mass in a small number of origin–destination types. Cargo is divisible in the paper already; that feature supports the formulation but is not the continuization being proposed here.

A plausible regime is a large freight operation with many similar shipment accounts on recurring lanes: \(n\) shippers, \(n\gg\tau=|T|\), with agents of each type sharing the same relevant preferences, requirements, costs, and budgets. The paper’s shippers already represent movement requirements, so grouping repeated requirements by type seems recognizable to its authors. For the fixed-network convex-cost version, I would expect tractability through convex optimization. That is a hypothesis for this proposed question, not a result established by the paper. It leads naturally to questions about exact versus approximate equilibrium computation, the number of rounds needed by WALRAS’s decentralized bidding process, and how system-equilibrium routing compares with user-equilibrium routing for a continuous population.

The weak point is substantial: this candidate is not backed by a qualifying anchor, and it formalizes a population-scaled optimization problem rather than a named complexity result of the paper. The paper’s experiment uses a finite collection of shipper and carrier modules; a critic could say that the proposed question shifts attention from WALRAS’s decentralized coordination procedure to aggregate routing. I still think the repeated-shipment regime gives the mirror a credible connection to the paper’s transportation problem, but I would not present it as an anchor-compliant case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against an anchor-backed mirror is that the paper contains no named computational result of the kind the programme asked analysts to mirror. The transportation table and the reported bidding-cycle tolerances are experimental results, not complexity theorems. The paper says competitive equilibria are computable by methods developed elsewhere, and explicitly says the convergence behavior of WALRAS itself has not been characterized. The proposed “Continuous System-Equilibrium Routing” is therefore a new question inspired by the paper, not a continuous version of one of its named complexity results.

That proposal also misses the paper’s distinctive computational object. It centrally minimizes aggregate transportation cost. The paper’s contribution is a distributed market architecture: agents submit bids through separate auctions, maintain individual agendas, and react to changing prices. Its flow variables are already divisible, so the proposed convex program makes the cargo continuous without showing that the *population* is doing meaningful work. For repeated shippers on the same routes, the type flows collapse to aggregate origin–destination demands. That may be a useful optimization problem, but it does not analyze the computation performed by WALRAS.

The best repair would use masses of small shippers grouped by complete type—route, preferences, endowments, and budgets—and ask about computing equilibrium or about the bidding procedure’s convergence. Repeated shipments on common lanes make that population regime plausible, so I cannot dismiss it as an unnatural story. But there is a real modeling fork: aggregating each type into one bidder changes the paper’s individual price-taking agents and their separate bid agendas; preserving those agents leaves the asynchronous process to be defined for a continuum. A model that tracks a distribution of evolving bid states could be built, but it would be a new mean-field algorithm problem, not a result the paper already poses or solves.

So the negative case is strong on the narrow claim that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and on the objection that the proposed routing program is not a faithful mirror of WALRAS’s computation. It is weak on the assigned universal claim. A continuous market-equilibrium or bidding-dynamics question for repeated shipper types remains recognizable and potentially worthwhile; the paper’s lack of a named theorem does not make that question disappear. I would argue against grading the proposed routing program as an anchor-backed mirror, but I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
