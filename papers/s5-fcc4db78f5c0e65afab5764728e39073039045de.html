The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one narrow mirror, anchored on Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It is a mechanism-design approximation lower bound, not an NP-hardness or polynomial-time result. The theorem says that for every strategyproof-in-expectation mechanism \(M:(\mathbb{R}^d)^n\to\Delta(\mathbb{R}^d)\), with \(d\ge2\),
\[ \alpha(M)\ge 2-e^{-\Theta(\sqrt d)}-O\!\left(n^{-2/(d-1)}\right). \]
In particular, when \(d=\Theta(\log^{2/3}n)\), it gives \(\alpha(M)\ge 2-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{1/3}n))\), so no such mechanism guarantees a \((2-\varepsilon)\)-approximation uniformly over all \(n,d\), for any universal \(\varepsilon>0\).

The continuous question I think is worth asking is Mass-Strategic Euclidean Minimax Facility Location. Fix \(d\ge2\). A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{p_j}\) over locations \(p_j\in\mathbb{R}^d\), with positive rational masses summing to one. A type is a location: it fully determines an agent’s Euclidean-distance preferences. Mass is the fraction of the population at that location. A mechanism \(M\) maps each society to a lottery over facility locations. Its expected maximum cost and approximation ratio are
\[ C(M,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{F\sim M(\mu)} \left[\max_{p\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\|F-p\|\right], \qquad \alpha(M)=\sup_{\mu:\,R(\mu)>0}\frac{C(M,\mu)}{R(\mu)}, \]
where \(R(\mu)=\min_f\max_{p\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\|f-p\|\).

The incentive condition needs care. For an atom at \(p\), let its entire mass report any finite distribution \(\rho\) with the same total mass, producing \(\mu'=\mu-\mu(\{p\})\delta_p+\rho\). Require
\[ \mathbb{E}_{F\sim M(\mu)}\|F-p\| \le \mathbb{E}_{F\sim M(\mu')}\|F-p\|. \]
That is, the cohort whose true location is \(p\) cannot lower its expected individual cost by jointly changing its reports. The problem is to determine the best worst-case ratio
\[ \alpha_d^{\mathrm{cohort}} =\inf_{M\ \mathrm{cohort\text{-}strategyproof}}\ \sup_{\mu}\frac{C(M,\mu)}{R(\mu)}. \]
A solution would give a matching mechanism and bound, or prove a universal lower bound for every such rule. The continuous analogue of Theorem 1 would be the lower-bound half: \(\alpha_d^{\mathrm{cohort}}\ge 2-e^{-\Theta(\sqrt d)}\).

The regime I have in mind is a large city or campus with many residents sharing exact origins, such as building entrances, and a single public facility placed to minimize the worst travel distance. Then the number of people \(n\) can be much larger than the number of distinct location types \(\tau\). Masses affect the available cohort deviations, while the maximum-cost objective still counts every occupied location, however small its mass. The facility remains a point and the lottery remains the paper’s existing randomization; the population distribution is what becomes continuous.

This mirror tracks the proof’s operative incentive constraint unusually closely. The paper’s Fact 1, citing Lemma 3.3 of Gomes et al. (2026), says that a collocated block cannot improve its expected distance from its true location by jointly changing its reports. The lower-bound construction repeatedly splits and relocates such blocks along orthogonal directions. In the mass model, the same construction can split a positive atom into arbitrary rational shares. The finite-\(n\) term comes from having enough individual agents at a location to distribute them over a sufficiently fine net; an atom can be divided among that finite net without an agent-count constraint. So I expect the geometric lower-bound phenomenon to persist, with that discretization penalty removed. This is an inference from the paper’s proof, not a theorem stated in it.

If forced into the programme’s trichotomy, I expect persistence, a B-like verdict: the obstruction is driven by dimension and Euclidean geometry, not by population multiplicity. But this is not computational hardness in the usual P-versus-NP sense; the paper’s anchor is an approximation impossibility, so the trichotomy does not literally classify it. The natural next questions are whether the dimension-dependent bound is sharp, and how it relates to the paper’s open question about fixed \(d\).

The weakest point is the incentive condition. Individual strategyproofness for a nonatomic agent is vacuous if one person has zero mass. My mirror instead carries over the paper’s collocated-block incentive consequence as a mass-cohort condition. That is motivated by the proof and by high multiplicity, but it is not automatically equivalent to a limit of ordinary strategyproof mechanisms. The paper’s finite model also permits mechanisms that use identities; the mirror treats agents with the same location as one type. Those are real qualifications, but they leave a focused question that preserves the paper’s minimax objective, location domain, randomized outcome, and central block-deviation argument.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1, and the strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that it does not assert a computational result. It lower-bounds the approximation ratio of every strategyproof-in-expectation mechanism, regardless of whether the mechanism can be computed. Runtime, input encoding, and support size play no role. The proposed \(\alpha_d^{\mathrm{cohort}}\) asks the same kind of rule-quality question over mass societies. It may be a worthwhile mechanism-design question, but it does not chart the computational landscape ChoCo is set up to study. Adding a question about the complexity of finding an optimal mechanism would be a new problem, not a mirror of this theorem.

The proposed mass model itself is credible. Treating location as type, giving each type’s cohort a joint deviation, and retaining maximum cost all fit the paper’s setup. In particular, the paper’s collocated-block incentive fact gives a real bridge to cohort deviations; arbitrary division of mass could remove the finite-\(n\) net penalty. I would not reject the mirror on grounds of a vacuous continuum, continuous locations, or an already-completed continuous-population analysis. Those objections would miss the model the proponent actually proposed.

That leaves a narrow negative case: even the strongest mirror preserves the theorem’s central object—how well a strategyproof rule can perform—rather than converting it into a question about the cost of computing a solution. The ordinary minimax center is efficiently computable, but that says nothing about the theorem; its bound applies even to mechanisms with unlimited computation.

This case is weak if ChoCo is willing to count approximation impossibilities for strategyproof mechanisms as computational questions. Under the programme’s stated emphasis on complexity and algorithms, though, Theorem 1 supplies no computational result to continuize. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous-population question exists in a broader mechanism-design programme; I can argue that this paper offers no strong anchor for ChoCo’s particular one.
