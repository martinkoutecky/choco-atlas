The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This paper has no qualifying computational result to mirror. CIBL’s advantage over 1ARC and its varying performance against ID3 are empirical findings, not complexity results. The paper gives a shortest path procedure, but does not analyze the computational complexity of a formally specified learning problem. So there is no named result here whose continuous-population version would extend the programme’s complexity landscape.

The strongest plausible mirror would take a population of astronomers and group them by their complete preference predicate. For personalized recommendations, the mass of each group does not affect what CIBL must learn: it still needs comparison examples and a model for each distinct predicate. The population weights would at most reweight evaluation scores; the training arcs and the algorithm’s central task remain. If instead the goal is to pool preferences into a collective ranking, the aggregate relation can be nontransitive even when every astronomer’s preferences are transitive, so CIBL’s central composition principle no longer applies. Keeping preferences separate preserves that principle but leaves the population measure computationally incidental.

One could define a worthwhile continuous problem about estimating population-wide prediction accuracy or aggregating users’ preferences. But that changes the target from learning an individual user’s predicate to statistical evaluation or social choice over users. It is an adjacent research question, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

The limit of this negative case is that a setting with many users sharing a small number of stable predicates could be plausible and useful. I cannot rule out a new project built around that setting. But it would need to supply the population-level computational problem that this paper does not formulate.
