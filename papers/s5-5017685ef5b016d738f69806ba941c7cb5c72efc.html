The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) divisible goods and a finite support \(\mathcal{T}\) of CES buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mu_t=1\), per-capita budgets \(e_t\), and utility parameters \((a_{t1},\ldots,a_{tn},\rho_t)\), let supply be one unit per unit of population mass and define aggregate excess demand by \(z_j(p)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mu_t x_{tj}(p)-1\). For positive initial prices, does asynchronous tatonnement with each good updated at intervals of at most one time unit, using an observed excess demand between its minimum and maximum since its previous update and the paper's update rule with \(0<\lambda\le 1/(26E)\), converge linearly to a market equilibrium whenever \(\max_t\rho_t<1\), \(\min_t\rho_t>-\infty\), and \(E=\max\{1/(1-\max_t\rho_t),1\}\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a buyer-population version of Theorem 2, which is proved in this paper. The paper has no numbered hardness result or complexity-class theorem to anchor on; its strongest relevant result is algorithmic convergence. Theorem 2 says: for a Fisher market whose buyers have CES utilities, if \(\rho=\max_i\rho_i<1\) and \(\min_i\rho_i>-\infty\), and \(E=\max\{1/(1-\rho),1\}\), then asynchronous tatonnement with update rule (2) converges linearly toward market equilibrium whenever \(0<\lambda\le 1/(26E)\). The rate can depend on the starting prices. This covers heterogeneous finite-\(\rho\) CES buyers; it does not cover the paper’s separate Leontief result.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous-Type Asynchronous Fisher Equilibrium Approximation. An instance has \(n\) divisible goods and a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) buyer types. Type \(t\) specifies the buyer’s complete CES utility parameters \((a_{t1},\ldots,a_{tn},\rho_t)\) and budget \(e_t\); \(\mu_t>0\) is the fraction of the population of that type, with \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t=1\). The goods have supply one per unit of population mass, and the initial price vector is positive. At prices \(p\), let \(x_t(p)\) be the demand of a representative type-\(t\) buyer and set \(z_j(p)=\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}(p)-1\).

Sellers update prices asynchronously. Each good is updated at least once per unit of time; at an update, its observed excess demand \(\tilde z_j\) may be any value between the minimum and maximum excess demand during the interval since its previous update. With elapsed interval \(\Delta t\), the update is \(p_j\leftarrow p_j[1+\lambda\min\{\tilde z_j,1\}\Delta t]\). Given such a market and a tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), the task is to produce, along an admissible run, a price vector within \(\varepsilon\) of the market’s equilibrium set. Equilibrium minimizes the corresponding convex potential \(\phi_\mu(p)=\sum_j p_j+\sum_t\mu_t e_t\log\hat u_t(p)\), where \(\hat u_t(p)\) is type \(t\)’s utility per unit of spending. Thus the objective and the price dynamics remain the paper’s; only the buyer sum becomes a mass-weighted sum.

A plausible regime is a large recurring market for cloud or energy capacity, with millions of buyers in stable procurement cohorts. Buyers in a cohort share preferences and budgets, while the number of distinct cohorts \(\tau\) is small relative to the population. The type definition preserves every trait the market uses. Normalizing supply per unit of mass is the natural scale adjustment: a finite market with \(N\mu_t\) buyers of each type and supply \(N\) per good has the same per-capita excess demand and prices. The goods were already divisible in the paper; the proposed continuization is of the buyers.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the convergence sense of Class A. Theorem 2 gives a strong reason: its dynamics already handle arbitrary asynchrony within the stated conditions and converge geometrically, while the aggregate demand and potential become finite sums over types. The proof’s demand and smoothness calculations are already aggregate calculations, so replacing buyer counts by type masses looks natural. The follow-up computational question is whether one can bound the convergence and arithmetic costs polynomially in \(n,\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), with explicit dependence on market conditioning and the starting prices. The paper’s linear-convergence statement alone does not establish that uniform bit-complexity bound. One could also ask whether the amortized proof extends to a distribution with infinite support, or what rate—if any—holds for the Leontief boundary case.

The weak point is exactly that gap: this is a dynamics theorem, not a complexity classification of a finite-precision search problem, and its rate constants are not shown to be polynomially controlled by the encoded instance. Still, the mirror retains the paper’s market, buyers, equilibrium objective, and asynchronous seller updates. It asks for the same result about the same process after aggregating indistinguishable buyers, so I think the authors would recognize it as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s sole anchor is Theorem 2. Its limitation as a ChoCo anchor is that it proves convergence of an assumed asynchronous process; it gives no bound on update count, arithmetic work, or precision for an encoded input. The proposed task—output an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate equilibrium in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\)—is a new bit-complexity question, not a continuous version of a computational-complexity result stated in the paper.

There is also an exact compression behind the proposed mirror. For each type \(t\), replace mass \(\mu_t\) by one representative buyer with budget \(\mu_t e_t\) and the same CES utility. Since CES demand scales with budget, this buyer demands \(\mu_t x_t(p)\). The compressed market therefore has the same aggregate excess demand, potential, equilibrium set, and price updates as the mass model. Theorem 2 already gives the same idealized convergence guarantee for that market. The extension to infinitely many types needs an input or oracle model for integrating demand; the Leontief variant likewise inherits the paper’s convergence result, while a finite-precision complexity bound would still need proof. Those could be worthwhile algorithmic questions, but their novelty would be about encoding and computation of CES equilibrium, not a new effect of making the buyer population continuous.

That is the strongest negative case, and it is not decisive. A large market with a small number of stable buyer cohorts is a convincing high-multiplicity regime; identity is not essential, and per-capita scaling preserves a nontrivial equilibrium problem. Nor does an existing convergence guarantee rule out a useful complexity result. The mirror may survive on that basis, so the honest negative case against *every* worthwhile scenario is weak.
