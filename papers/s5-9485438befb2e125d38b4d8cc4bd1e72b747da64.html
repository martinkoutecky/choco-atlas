The continuous mirror question

Given a finite college set \(C\), per-capita capacities \(q_c\), and a population measure over strict rankings and exam scores \(x\in[0,1]\), represented by rational piecewise-constant score densities for each supported ranking, define atomless TCDM by having each college hold the highest-score applicant mass up to capacity each round. Applicants who remain held retain their applications; rejected applicants apply to their most-preferred attainable option using the prior round’s cutoffs. Compute the exact allocation measure after \(T\) rounds and determine whether the unbounded process reaches a stable allocation in finitely many rounds, with a bound in terms of the college count, supported rankings, density intervals, and input bit length.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass admissions cohort with a distribution over ranking-score types, rational per-capita college capacities, and cutoff-based tentative holds. The decision process is each type’s mass application in each round; the output is the allocation measure and cutoff path, with finite stabilization as the convergence question.

The objection that survived

Exact exam score determines priority, so applicants within a score interval are not identical in every respect the process uses; representing them as a mass distribution over a continuous score coordinate extends the finite-type formulation.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result in the usual sense: it proves no P/NP-hardness, FPT, or similar classification. The strongest algorithmic anchor is Proposition 1, proved in Appendix C. It establishes that, under straightforward strategies, if the process continues until no student revises, it reaches the unique stable matching after at most \(|I|\) rounds; consequently, when \(T\ge |I|\), TCDM ends at that matching. This is a result about an iterative allocation process, not an input-size complexity theorem.

My lead mirror is the Atomless TCDM Allocation Problem. Its instance is a finite set of colleges \(C\), strict rankings over \(C\cup\{\emptyset\}\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_c\), a round limit \(T\), and a population measure \(\mu\) over ranking and exam score \(x\in[0,1]\). Higher \(x\) means higher priority. For each ranking, the score distribution has a rational, piecewise-constant density, with no positive mass tied at a score. The total population mass is \(1\); capacities scale with the cohort. The compact input records \(\tau\) supported rankings and \(H\) density intervals, rather than naming each applicant.

In round one, each agent applies to their most-preferred option. A college tentatively holds the highest-priority applicant mass up to its capacity and reveals its cutoff. In later rounds, tentatively assigned agents retain their applications; unassigned agents apply to their most-preferred attainable option, using the preceding round’s cutoffs, as in the paper. The requested solution is the exact round-\(T\) allocation measure: for each ranking and score interval, the mass assigned to each college or left unassigned, together with the cutoff path. Each unit of mass still receives one college or the outside option; only the aggregate output is fractional. There is no welfare-maximization objective—the task is to compute the mechanism’s outcome.

I expect this finite-horizon computation to be Class A. With piecewise-constant score densities, each round can be computed by sweeping score intervals and finding the capacity cutoffs. The natural algorithmic question is whether this can be done in \(f(T)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,H,L)\) time, where \(L\) is the rational input bit length. A fixed \(T\) is a direct computational version of the mechanism the authors study.

The further question closest to Proposition 1 is whether the unbounded continuum process reaches a stationary allocation after a finite number \(K\) of rounds, and whether that allocation is stable almost everywhere. If so, can \(K\) be bounded in \(m,\tau,H,L\), without reference to a finite population size? That is the real continuation question for the mirror. In the finite proof, the bound \(|I|\) comes from advancing through a discrete priority order. An atomless score distribution has no highest-priority individual, so that proof’s termination argument does not carry over. I would tentatively expect tractability when the number of ranking patterns and density intervals is controlled, but Proposition 1 alone does not establish finite convergence.

The regime is a large centralized admissions cohort: many applicants, a relatively small set of recurring preference rankings, exam priority represented by a score distribution, and college capacities that grow with cohort size. Applicants sharing a ranking and score range behave alike under the paper’s straightforward-strategy benchmark. That makes mass the natural unit for reporting how much of each group applies, is held, or is rejected. The score coordinate preserves the paper’s common priority order; scaling capacity per capita is a necessary co-modification of the model.

This is a plausible problem for the authors to recognize. It preserves TCDM’s colleges, cutoffs, tentative assignments, application revisions, deadline, and behavioral rule, while making the applicant population the continuous object. The mirror covers Proposition 1’s process and convergence claim only. I would not stretch it to cover Proposition 2’s equilibrium result or the paper’s lottery comparisons.

The weakest point is that the paper’s result is about economic properties of a finite mechanism, not computational complexity. Moreover, its finite-round guarantee depends on \(|I|\), and the continuum may replace that bound with a genuine limit problem. So the positive case is for a well-motivated, potentially tractable computational extension—not for a complexity landscape already latent in the paper, nor for any conclusion about hardness dissolving in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with the gap between the paper’s results and ChoCo’s computational questions. Proposition 1 bounds the number of rounds in a finite process; the paper does not pose an optimization, search, or decision problem whose complexity it classifies. Under the proposed atomless model, computing the round-\(T\) allocation looks like direct bookkeeping: split each ranking’s score density at the current cutoffs, aggregate applicants by college, and find each college’s cutoff as a weighted quantile. Repeating this for \(T\) rounds gives the allocation and cutoff path. That may be useful, but it appears to add little computational difficulty or structure for a complexity programme to chart.

The atomless cohort is plausible, and I would not dismiss it because high-multiplicity admissions work already exists; that literature supports the regime. But the proposed score coordinate makes the case less squarely high-multiplicity than it first appears. Since exact score determines priority and the paper assumes distinct scores, a student’s complete type includes a score with zero mass at each exact value. Grouping students into score bands would restore repeated types but lose the strict priority distinctions that drive the process. Keeping a continuous score distribution preserves the mechanism, but makes the mirror a fluid model of an ordered population. Even granting that as a worthwhile continuization, the finite-horizon computation still looks like a sequence of cutoff calculations.

The closer analogue to Proposition 1 is whether the unbounded atomless process reaches a stationary allocation in finitely many rounds. That is a legitimate question, but it is a new limit-dynamics question: the finite proof advances through a discrete priority order, while the atomless population has no highest-priority individual. Asking for an exact finite stopping bound may therefore demand a separate convergence theory. Asking instead for the limiting allocation to a specified precision would give a computational task, but it would require a new approximation target and representation that the paper does not supply. The finite-round mirror is easy to compute; the potentially difficult continuation is no longer its computational counterpart.

The paper’s other named results do not readily improve the case. Its equilibrium results concern unilateral deviations by identified students. In an atomless population, one student has zero mass and cannot change a cutoff, so individual deviations become payoff-irrelevant unless the model adds positive-mass coalitions or a different equilibrium concept. Those changes could be studied, but they would introduce a new strategic question. The assignment-probability comparisons could be averaged over a population distribution, yet the paper already proves them profile by profile; aggregation does not itself create a hard computational problem.

So I think the negative case is strongest on the mismatch: this paper offers substantive finite-mechanism results, while its most faithful continuous mirror appears to be a straightforward aggregate simulation. The unbounded continuation might be mathematically interesting, but it is the part least anchored to a computational problem in the paper. I cannot make the universal claim airtight: the large-cohort setting is natural, and a richer question about computing or approximating atomless dynamics could prove worthwhile. The paper itself, however, gives little reason to expect a substantial complexity landscape there.
