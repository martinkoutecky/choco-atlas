What the mirror covers

The corridor addresses full-participation supportability and the path's asymptotic capacity; it leaves entry, individual payment bounds, payer thresholds, other network extremizers, and the welfare gap aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a mathematical continuum limit of the paper’s path result. But the paper has no named computational-complexity result: it gives no theorem that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 1 characterizes Nash supportability, Theorem 2 bounds aggregate withdrawal responsibility, and Proposition 1 characterizes the welfare gap; each is proved in the paper, but none is a complexity result. So there are zero qualifying anchors, and I cannot present a continuous computational problem as anchored in the sense your brief requires.

The closest positive lead is Theorem 2 together with Proposition 1. Theorem 2 proves that for a connected active set of \(m\ge3\) vertices, \(m\le R(A)\le\lfloor(m+1)^2/4\rfloor\); for \(m\ge5\), paths are the only graphs attaining the upper bound. Proposition 1 proves that full participation uniquely maximizes welfare but cannot be sustained exactly when \(bR(V)<C<bN^2\). Both results are proved here, and together they give the path’s limiting financeable share of gross benefit as \(1/4\).

A precise *exploratory mirror* of that limit is a continuum relay corridor. Let positions \(x\in[0,1]\) be uniformly distributed, with mass \(dx\), and suppose everyone participates. Public output is the measure of the largest connected active component. Each person receives benefit \(b\) times that output. The institution commits to a nonnegative, measurable payment rate \(p(x)\), with total cost \(K=\int_0^1p(x)\,dx\); ask for the largest \(K\) for which some such schedule makes full participation a Nash equilibrium.

If the person at \(x\) withdraws, output falls from \(1\) to \(\max\{x,1-x\}\). Thus that person’s payment must satisfy \(p(x)\le b\min\{x,1-x\}\). The maximum supportable cost in this model is therefore \(b\int_0^1\min\{x,1-x\}\,dx=b/4\), attained when the payment rate equals the withdrawal limit almost everywhere. This is the normalized limit of the path’s finite capacity: \(R(P_N)/N^2\to1/4\).

The scenario would be many relay operators along a standardized corridor. A type is a normalized position \(x\), its mass is the share of operators at that position, its action is participation, and the objective is the greatest operating cost supportable by committed payments. The authors would likely recognize the connection: the paper singles out paths and derives their asymptotic capacity. For this narrow problem, the answer is tractable by integration.

The weak point is substantial. In finite paths, the positions are distinct; in this mirror, the type space is continuous, and a single zero-mass person’s withdrawal still splits the corridor. Finite-grid versions have as many types as positions, so they do not offer the usual high-multiplicity advantage of many agents sharing relatively few types. The continuum also depends on preserving the path’s topology: a representation that ignores measure-zero connections can lose exactly the separator effect that drives the result. This is a plausible continuous population limit of a structural theorem, but it does not supply the paper’s missing computational-complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors are real results, but neither is a computational-complexity result. Theorem 2 is an extremal graph theorem about aggregate withdrawal responsibility; Proposition 1 compares the supportability and welfare thresholds. Neither asserts an algorithm, a complexity class, or a hardness boundary. The proponent is right that there are no qualifying anchors here.

The corridor is the strongest possible continuous reading of those results, and it exposes the central mismatch. Its capacity calculation treats every position \(x\) as a distinct, pivotal type: withdrawing at \(x\) splits the corridor and removes positive measure of output, even though that type has zero mass. This gives a coherent continuum geometry, but not the paper’s high-multiplicity relaxation. There are uncountably many position types, and the structural fact doing the work is that each position is a potential cutpoint.

Trying to restore high multiplicity changes that mechanism. If many indistinguishable participants share a position, one person’s withdrawal does not remove the position or split the network. If the whole type’s mass withdraws together, the model has replaced unilateral participation with a type-level coalition decision. A finite path with one type per position preserves the original incentives, but its support grows with the path; the continuum limit has not compressed the population into a moderate number of types.

Theorem 2’s path result and Proposition 1’s financing gap therefore do not become computational questions merely by replacing sums with integrals. In the corridor, the input is a fixed geometric space and a payment function, not a finitely encoded instance with a complexity landscape to chart. To ask a computational question about richer continuous networks would require choosing a representation of the network and its topology. That would be a new problem: the paper’s graph is fixed, and its results do not supply an algorithmic question over such representations.

This does not rule out a continuous theory extension, and the absolute claim that no imaginable scenario could support a useful mirror is too strong to prove. But the best proposed mirror is analytic and relies on a continuum of individually pivotal positions; making the population genuinely high multiplicity removes that leverage. On the paper’s stated results, there is no worthwhile computational mirror for ChoCo to pursue.
