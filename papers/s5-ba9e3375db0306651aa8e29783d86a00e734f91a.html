The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with a gap the proponent already identifies: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its headline numbers—99.62% consistency, 91.90% bottom-two consistency, and the rule-swap reversal rates—are empirical findings. Equation (3) sketches an inverse optimization objective, but does not specify its losses or establish an algorithmic or complexity result. Turning those findings into a new computational problem would create an anchor; it would not mirror one the paper proves.

The best rescue is to treat viewers as a high-multiplicity population. Let each type describe a viewer’s voting behavior, and let \(\mu_t\) be its population mass. The weekly vote shares are then \(v=A\mu\), for a map \(A\) from type masses to contestant vote shares. But the paper’s rule constraints and regularizers depend on \(v\), not on \(\mu\). With unrestricted one-week voting types, every vote-share vector is already attainable, so optimizing over audience masses merely lifts the paper’s existing share-space problem into a redundant representation. That would make the population the formal object, but add no computational question about it.

A richer version could give viewers persistent voting patterns across weeks. That is a more credible mirror, but the paper supplies no type catalogue, preference-to-vote behavior, or type-level dynamics to define it. Elimination outcomes constrain aggregate weekly shares; they do not identify which kinds of viewers produced them. Adding restrictions or a regularizer on type masses could make inference identifiable and yield an interesting optimization problem, perhaps one with a nontrivial pricing problem. Yet those additions determine the problem’s substance and complexity. They are a new behavioral model prompted by the paper, not a continuous restatement of its results.

The same issue limits mirrors of the rule-comparison findings. The rule swaps are evaluated on contestant-level shares and scores; their reported reversal rates are not computational guarantees. Recasting the shares as masses does not turn those empirical comparisons into a complexity question.

So the negative case is strong against calling this paper a source of continuous mirrors: its plausible audience-mass interpretation either collapses to the aggregate vote shares it already uses or requires new assumptions that define a different problem. The universal claim that no scenario could make that new problem worthwhile is weaker. A well-specified model of repeated audience types could be a valuable ChoCo direction, but the paper does not supply that model or an algorithmic result to anchor it.
