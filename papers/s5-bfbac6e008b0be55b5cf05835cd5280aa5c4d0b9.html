The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational population distribution \(\mu\), finite category set \(Q\), additive values \(u_\theta(q)\), per-capita supplies \(\lambda_q\), an exact integer scaling sequence \(N_k\) with \(N_k\mu_\theta,N_k\lambda_q\in\mathbb Z\), and a target \(r_\theta\) obtained from the paper’s share \(s\) along that sequence, compute the supremal \(\alpha\) for the limit of the original one-good online process on \(N_k\lambda_q\) indivisible copies, against every adversarial arrival order, such that every positive-mass type-\(\theta\) state \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{Q}\) ends with \(u_\theta\cdot b\ge\alpha r_\theta\). Represent the limiting policy by mass transitions \(\eta_{\theta,b}\to\eta_{\theta,b+e_q}\), while every underlying agent receives an integral bundle.

The model it lives in

A continuum of repeated additive agent types with distribution \(\mu\), indivisible per-agent bundles, and per-capita category supply; the state is \(\eta_{\theta,b}\), decisions are mass flows between integral bundle states, and the objective is a worst-case online share guarantee under adversarial category order.

The objection that survived

The stated mirror does not specify an exact scaling and arrival-limit convention, so \(s_\infty\), the effect of individual arrivals, and the continuum version of the ex-post quantifier are not yet canonical.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.1 and share-based consequences such as Corollary 5.2 under frequency predictions; it leaves the no-information, normalization, noisy-advice, pairwise EF1/EFX, identical-valuation, and randomization results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which exact scaling sequences does \(s_\infty(\theta)\) exist, and when is it determined by per-capita data rather than integrality conventions?
  • Can a policy for fixed \(|T|\) and \(|Q|\) be computed by a finite flow or configuration LP against all admissible category orders?
  • Does the finite picking-sequence construction converge to a policy satisfying the guarantee on every occupied integral bundle state, rather than only in type-average value?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a frequency-prediction mirror, in a repeated-allocation setting such as advertising exposure, cloud-computing slots, or moderation tickets. The paper itself names exactly this kind of application: many stakeholders share a small number of valuation profiles, while item categories arrive in an adversarial order.

The paper has no named NP-hardness, P-membership, FPT, or W[1]-hardness theorem. Its strongest computationally usable anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper. The theorem says:

If \(s\) is any feasible share in the offline single-shot fair-division problem, then, with frequency predictions, there exists an online algorithm guaranteeing every agent \(i\) a final bundle of value at least \(s(V_i,n)\).

The result is constructive: it computes an offline allocation for the ordered valuation instance induced by the predicted frequency multisets, converts that allocation into a picking sequence, and dynamically simulates the sequence as goods arrive in an arbitrary order. The paper also states that the online procedure is polynomial whenever the offline share-achieving allocation is polynomially computable. Its concrete consequence, Corollary 5.2, is the \(\left(\frac34+\frac3{3836}\right)\)-MMS guarantee for every number of agents.

Here is a genuine continuous-population version.

Let \(T\) be a fixed finite set of agent types, with population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). A type \(\theta\) is a complete additive valuation profile: every agent of type \(\theta\) values an arriving good of category \(q\) at \(u_\theta(q)\). Let \(Q\) be a fixed finite set of good categories, and let \(\lambda_q\) be the number of goods of category \(q\) per unit population. Thus a finite \(N\)-agent realization has \(N\mu_\theta\) agents of type \(\theta\) and \(N\lambda_q\) copies of category \(q\). Goods remain indivisible.

The frequency prediction for type \(\theta\) is the per-capita value measure \(\nu_\theta=\sum_{q\in Q}\lambda_q\delta_{u_\theta(q)}\). The arrival process reveals one category at a time, in an arbitrary order, but preserves these aggregate frequencies. This is precisely the paper’s frequency-prediction model after grouping identical agents and scaling the supply with population size.

The population-level state is a mass distribution over integral bundles. If \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{Q}\) is a bundle, let \(\eta_{\theta,b}\) be the mass of type-\(\theta\) agents currently holding \(b\). When a category-\(q\) good arrives, the decision variable is a measurable mass of agents in state \(b\) that receive it and move to state \(b+e_q\). Thus the type-level allocation may be fractional, but every individual represented by the mass receives an integral bundle.

Call the resulting problem Continuous Frequency-Prediction Fair Allocation. Given \((T,\mu,Q,u,\lambda)\), a feasible share rule \(s\), and the induced continuum target \(s_\infty(\nu_\theta)\), compute an online transition policy \(P\) maximizing the worst-case factor \(\alpha\) such that, for every admissible arrival order, the final state satisfies \(u_\theta\cdot b\ge\alpha s_\infty(\nu_\theta)\) for all bundles \(b\) carrying positive type-\(\theta\) mass. Exact feasibility is \(\alpha=1\). For the paper’s MMS instantiation, \(s_\infty\) is the high-multiplicity limit of the \(N\)-agent MMS benchmark.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A, at least for fixed \(|T|\), \(|Q|\), and a polynomially computable offline share. The paper’s picking sequence becomes a type-level mass schedule; its online simulation becomes a flow over residual frequency counts and integral bundle states. A configuration LP or column-generation formulation is the natural computational representation. Any hardness should come from the offline share problem and transfer as Class B; the population continuum itself is not the apparent source of difficulty.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. The question remains about irrevocable online allocation, indivisible goods, future-information models, and ex post fairness. The only co-modification is the one forced by continuization: the number of repeated goods scales with population, while mass records how many indistinguishable agents occupy each integral-bundle state.

The weak point is that Theorem 5.1 already gives a strong finite-agent algorithm, so the continuous result may look like its compressed high-multiplicity implementation rather than a wholly new phenomenon. Also, EF1 and EFX are pairwise notions and do not pass cleanly to an atomless population. I would therefore scope the claim narrowly: this paper supports a serious continuous mirror for its frequency-prediction, share-based result—especially the MMS corollary—not for the whole paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 5.1, but it does not furnish the continuous computational problem the proponent claims.

The theorem is a finite-agent lifting lemma. Given the frequency multiset \(V_i\) for each named agent, it constructs a finite picking sequence of length \(m\), then deletes one turn whenever a good arrives. If we duplicate every type \(N\) times and introduce \(N\lambda_q\) copies of each category \(q\), the theorem applies verbatim. Dividing the resulting occupancy counts by \(N\) produces a mass trajectory, but that is a rescaling of the finite execution, not a new theorem about a continuous society. The population distribution \(\mu\) contributes no new structure to the theorem beyond saying how many copies of each named agent exist.

This is not the objection that high multiplicity is illegitimate. High multiplicity is a sensible regime, and existing high-multiplicity work supports that. The narrower objection is that, for this particular result, the proposed mirror contains no population-dependent computational question. The theorem already treats the number of agents as an arbitrary finite parameter and leaves the substantive computation inside the offline share algorithm.

More seriously, the proposed limit cannot preserve all of the paper’s features at once. At finite \(N\), one arriving good changes the type-level state by mass \(1/N\). In the limit, a single indivisible good changes no positive mass. If one instead lets a good move a positive mass of agents from bundle \(b\) to \(b+e_q\), then one has made the arriving object divisible at the population level. Saying that every individual receives an integral bundle does not repair this: the transition itself is no longer the arrival of one indivisible good.

A genuine limit therefore needs an additional continuum-time arrival trajectory describing how category mass is released. That is a continuization of the arrival process, not merely of the population. If one refuses that modification, one retains a sequence of finite problems indexed by \(N\), and the alleged continuous problem is only shorthand for that family. If one accepts it, the result is a new fluid scheduling or divisible-resource model, not the online indivisible-goods problem studied in Theorem 5.1.

The mass state also loses the paper’s ex post quantifier. A distribution over bundle states cannot distinguish an allocation in which every agent is fairly treated from one in which a null set of agents is treated arbitrarily. Requiring fairness almost everywhere gives a legitimate measure-theoretic objective, but it is weaker than the paper’s guarantee for every named agent. Requiring fairness for every point of the continuum requires precisely the identity-level information that continuization was supposed to remove.

Corollary 5.2 has an additional problem. The proposed \(s_\infty\) is not determined by the limiting frequency measure alone. Consider \(N\) identical agents valuing every good at \(1\). If there are \(m_N=N\) goods, the MMS is \(1\). If there are \(m_N=N-1\) goods, the MMS is \(0\), since every partition into \(N\) bundles has an empty bundle. Yet both sequences have the same per-capita supply limit \(m_N/N\to1\) and the same limiting frequency measure \(\delta_1\). Thus the continuous data \((\mu,\nu)\) do not determine the benchmark.

One can fix this by prescribing exact integer scaling, such as \(m_N=N\lambda\), together with a particular sequence of finite realizations. But then the discrete skeleton and its integrality conventions are part of the input. Alternatively, one can define a fluid MMS benchmark, but that is no longer the paper’s MMS benchmark for indivisible goods.

The proponent’s optimization problem—maximizing the worst-case \(\alpha\) of a continuum policy—is therefore either a rescaled family of the paper’s finite problems, a fluid fair-division problem with different indivisibility semantics, or a genuinely new problem not supplied by the paper’s theorem. The theorem itself gives no complexity classification, separation problem, or population-level optimization result to mirror.

There is a real residual weakness in this negative case: a carefully specified fluid limit with exact scaling could become an interesting new model. I cannot honestly prove that no such model would be worthwhile. But Theorem 5.1 and its MMS corollary do not by themselves justify it. Their apparent continuous mirror is either finite high-multiplicity replication or a change of model at exactly the online-indivisibility boundary that makes the paper interesting.
