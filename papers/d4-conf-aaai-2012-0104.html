Why no mirror

The continuum random-sampling version is a plausible extension of RBTS, and its truthfulness question is recognizable as a mechanism question from the paper. But Theorem 9 establishes an incentive property; it gives no algorithm, complexity bound, hardness result, or computational formulation used to compute, so the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns Theorem 9’s incentive-compatibility claim. It leaves the paper’s BTS comparisons, failure examples, and other RBTS properties aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does truthful reporting remain a strict Bayes–Nash equilibrium in this unit-mass random-sampling mechanism for every admissible prior?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is Theorem 9, proved in this paper: RBTS is strictly Bayes–Nash incentive compatible for every \(n\ge 3\) and every admissible common prior. In full, when agents share such a prior and each reports a binary signal and a prediction of the high-signal frequency, truthful signal and prediction reports are a strict equilibrium of the RBTS mechanism; the mechanism does not need to know the prior. This is the closest result to an anchor, though it is a mechanism-design theorem, not a computational-complexity result.

A plausible continuous counterpart is Continuum RBTS Incentive Verification. Its instance is a finite latent-state set \(\Theta\), with rational prior probabilities \(\pi_\theta>0\) and rational signal probabilities \(q_\theta=\Pr(S=1\mid\theta)\in(0,1)\), distinct across states. The society has unit mass, and in state \(\theta\), fractions \(q_\theta\) and \(1-q_\theta\) have signal types \(1\) and \(0\). All agents share the paper’s common prior and signal model, and have the same risk-neutral preferences. Thus the type set is \(\{0,1\}\), with support size \(\tau=2\), even as the population becomes arbitrarily large.

Each agent reports \((x,y)\in\{0,1\}\times[0,1]\). To score an agent, the mechanism independently samples a reference and a peer from the population. Let \(y_r\) be the reference’s prediction and \(x_p\) the peer’s signal report. Set \(\delta_r=\min(y_r,1-y_r)\), and let \(y'=y_r+\delta_r\) if \(x=1\), or \(y'=y_r-\delta_r\) if \(x=0\). The agent receives \(R_q(y',x_p)+R_q(y,x_p)\), using the paper’s quadratic scoring rule. The question is whether truthful reporting—\(x=s\) and \(y=\Pr(S'=1\mid S=s)\)—is a strict Bayes–Nash equilibrium of this continuum mechanism. A solution is a proof that every signal type strictly prefers its truthful reports to every alternative, or a counterexample giving a prior and a profitable deviation.

The scenario is a large, homogeneous pool of workers doing the same binary annotation task. Their private evidence differs, but they share a signal model and common prior. The mass of each signal type represents its share of the pool; the number of workers is far larger than the two types. That scenario is close to the paper’s crowdsourcing motivation. The continuum mechanism is a natural random-sampling version of RBTS’s reference and peer roles, though it does replace the paper’s cyclic selection of agents.

I would expect this verification question to be tractable: the proof should carry over the paper’s ordering of first- and second-order posteriors and the quadratic rule’s preference for the closest shadow posterior. Natural follow-up questions include whether strict truthfulness and bounded payments extend to more than two signal types, or to populations with several signal-accuracy types.

The weak point is decisive for the programme’s strict anchor test. The paper has no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 9 asserts incentive compatibility, while the other nearby named results concern BTS’s small-population failure, scoring-rule properties, and payment bounds. They are not computational-complexity results. So this is the best positive candidate only if a mechanism implementation theorem is accepted as a computational anchor. Even then, its continuum form adds little complexity theory: Theorem 9 already covers every finite \(n\ge3\), and the proposed extension changes the sampling model while preserving the incentive argument. Under the programme’s stated computational scope, the honest verdict is that this is an interesting population-continuum extension, but not a qualifying continuization result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 9 is the only serious anchor. It proves a Bayesian equilibrium property: truthful reports are a strict equilibrium for every \(n\ge3\) and every admissible prior. The paper poses no algorithmic problem and establishes no complexity result. Under the proposed continuum model, there are only two signal types, and computing the posteriors requires sums over the \(m\) latent states. Scoring an agent uses two sampled reports and a quadratic formula. Turning “is truth-telling an equilibrium?” into a computational problem would add a verification task that the paper does not pose; optimizing over new classes of mechanisms would add a design problem.

The annotation-pool scenario is credible, and the continuum version can preserve the relevant Bayesian reasoning. With hidden reports and random sampling, it may even be a clean limit of the finite mechanism. That makes it a legitimate mathematical extension, but the population size adds no computational structure to Theorem 9: each agent’s incentive depends on a fixed-size sample, not on solving an optimization over the society’s mass distribution.

A richer question could introduce heterogeneous signal models, worker costs, or a budget-constrained search over mechanisms. Such work might be worthwhile, but it adds assumptions and decision variables beyond this paper’s common-prior RBTS result. The negative case is therefore narrow but firm: the population can be continuized plausibly, yet no mirror of the paper’s named result fits ChoCo’s computational target. I would not claim that no interesting continuous peer-prediction research is possible; I would claim that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for it.
