What the mirror covers

Theorem 18 and Corollary 9 remain finite-player structural characterizations; the finite-\(|N|\) facet computations are also left without a continuous-population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant case for a continuous mirror: this paper has no named result about a computational problem’s complexity or an algorithmic formulation that it computes with.

The closest result is Theorem 18, proved in the paper. It says that, for \(|N|\ge 3\), a normalized inequality \(0\le\langle\theta,m\rangle\) is facet-defining for the cone of exact games if and only if \(\theta=\theta_{\mathcal S}\) for an indecomposable min-semi-balanced system \(\mathcal S\). That is a structural characterization of facets, not a complexity or algorithmic result. Corollary 9, also proved here, characterizes exact games by the inequalities from min-semi-balanced systems; it likewise gives no computational claim. The paper reports facet counts for small \(|N|\), but those counts are not a named complexity result.

There is a conceivable extension: treat players as an atomless population in finitely many productivity types, represent coalitions by their type masses, and ask for the facet structure of the cone of exact games over those mass vectors. But the paper supplies neither that model nor a finite representation for its games. Moving from coalitions in a finite power set to measurable coalitions would require substantial new choices, and restricting games to depend only on type masses could change the object being characterized. I would not present that as a continuous mirror of one of this paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 18 characterizes which inequalities define facets of the exact-game cone; Corollary 9 characterizes exact games by inequalities from min-semi-balanced systems. Neither states a complexity result, gives an algorithm, or even sets up an input-size question. The facet counts for \(|N|\le 6\) are computational evidence for the structural work, not computational results of the kind ChoCo is charting.

The best repaired mirror would put players into finitely many complete types, represent a coalition by its type-mass vector \(x\), and study exactness for games \(f(x)\). That is plausible in settings such as risk sharing. But the paper’s cone contains all characteristic functions on \(2^N\); the continuum version would contain functions on a whole box of mass vectors. Its facet question therefore has no finite polyhedral object to refer to unless one first chooses a representation or restricts the game class—for example, to piecewise-linear functions. Those choices define a new problem and determine its computational content. Theorem 18 does not supply the missing representation or an algorithmic question.

Keeping finite multiplicities while grouping identical players is also sensible as a high-multiplicity model. But it restricts the game to depend only on type counts, changing the cone whose facets the paper characterizes. An algorithm for that restricted cone could be worthwhile, yet it would be a new computational project rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The six-player counterexample to the earlier conjecture does not provide a better anchor: it is a finite structural witness, not an algorithmic claim.

So I would reject this paper as a ChoCo mirror candidate: it offers a geometric characterization to extend, not a computational result to continuize. The universal version of the claim is weaker. Homogeneous continuum cooperative games may be interesting in their own right; I can’t honestly rule out every worthwhile question in every scenario. But pursuing one would mean starting a different project, not continuing the computational programme through this paper.
