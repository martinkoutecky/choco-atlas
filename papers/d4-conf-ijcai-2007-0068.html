The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a configuration LP for coalition structure generation. There is a limit on the case, though: the supplied paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, so it has no admissible anchor under your rules. It describes Algorithms 1–3, sketches Algorithm 3’s correctness, and reports empirical performance. The reported result—99% efficiency in 0.0043% of the dynamic programming time for 20 agents—is experimental, not a numbered complexity result. I can’t honestly turn it into an anchor.

The paper-level mirror I would propose is High-Multiplicity Configuration Coalition Structure Generation. Take finitely many agent types \(T=\{1,\ldots,d\}\), where type captures everything relevant to coalition value, and a rational population distribution \(\mu\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For example, imagine many interchangeable sensors drawn from a few capability and location classes, forming teams of bounded size. Let \(Q\) be the explicitly listed set of allowed team compositions, where each \(q\in Q\) is a nonzero vector in \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^d\). Give each composition \(q\) a rational characteristic-function value \(v(q)\).

Choose \(\lambda_q\ge 0\), the number of teams of composition \(q\) per unit of population, to solve

\[ \max \sum_{q\in Q}\lambda_q v(q) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{q\in Q}\lambda_q q_t=\mu_t \quad (t\in T). \]

The solution is the optimal coalition-frequency vector \(\lambda\) and its per-agent-scale value. Each team still has an integral composition \(q\); only the frequency of each team composition is continuous. In a finite population of size \(n\), \(\lambda_q\) corresponds to \(n\lambda_q\) teams. Thus the continuum describes the population, rather than making coalition membership itself fractional.

This preserves the paper’s central question: partition agents into disjoint, exhaustive coalitions to maximize the sum of characteristic-function values. The authors already motivate coalition formation with sensor teams, so they could plausibly recognize this as their problem in a symmetric, high-multiplicity regime. It does assume that agents of a given type are interchangeable for coalition value, and that the relevant team compositions are explicitly listed. With that input, the problem is an LP, solvable in time polynomial in \(d\), \(|Q|\), and the input bit length. If \(Q\) is specified implicitly and is large, a natural next question is whether the pricing problem—finding a composition maximizing \(v(q)-p\cdot q\)—can be solved efficiently.

This is a tractable continuous problem, but it does not establish that the paper’s anytime algorithm becomes faster or inherits its reported bound. The paper contains no named hardness anchor to test whether discrete hardness dissolves, and its arbitrary characteristic function can distinguish agents in ways this type-symmetric model deliberately rules out.

That is the weakest point: the mirror captures the underlying optimization problem only for a restricted, symmetric instance regime, and there is no numbered result in the paper for it to mirror as requested. The positive case is that this regime is plausible, keeps the coalition-formation objective intact, and makes the population genuinely continuous. The proposed mirror covers coalition structure generation only; it does not cover coalition-value calculation or payment and stability questions.
