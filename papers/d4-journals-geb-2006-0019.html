The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive case under the anchor rule: this paper contains no named computational result. Its three numbered theorems, proved in the paper, are axiomatic characterizations:

  • Theorem 1 says a bargaining solution over the paper’s domain is the Nash solution exactly when it satisfies efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, and contraction independence.
  • Theorem 2 says it is the egalitarian solution exactly when it satisfies weak efficiency, strong symmetry, and contraction independence.
  • Theorem 3 says it is the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution exactly when it satisfies weak efficiency, strong symmetry, scale invariance, and weak contraction independence.

None asserts that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified; the paper gives no algorithmic formulation or complexity result to anchor a mirror.

The domain’s real-valued utility vectors and nonconvex feasible sets do not make its population continuous: the paper studies bargaining among \(n\) players, with \(n \ge 2\). Treating those utility coordinates as a continuum would continuize the outcome space, while replacing the players with a continuum would create a different bargaining problem. Either could motivate new work, but neither supplies a continuous-population version of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no anchor for ChoCo’s computational mirror: Theorems 1–3 characterize bargaining solutions axiomatically. Theorem 1 characterizes the Nash solution; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize the egalitarian and Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem, or gives an algorithm to mirror.

A tempting substitute is to ask how hard it is to compute those solutions. But the paper specifies a domain of compact, comprehensive feasible sets in \(\mathbb{R}^n_+\), not an input encoding for those sets. With a chosen encoding, one could study optimization over feasible utility outcomes; that would add a computational problem and a representation the paper never provides. The paper’s real-valued utility coordinates already describe possible outcomes. Making them continuous again would continuize the outcome space, not the population.

The harder proposed rescue is to treat each bargainer as a cohort of many interchangeable people. That leaves the paper’s \(n\) coordinates attached to \(n\) bargaining principals; replicating the people represented by one principal adds no population mass to the model. Turning those cohorts into populations whose members receive allocations requires a new feasible-set model and new solution concepts for aggregated or distributed utility. Treating \(n\) itself as a continuum also changes the finite-player domain and its symmetry, efficiency, and contraction axioms. Either route could motivate new bargaining theory, but it supplies no continuous-population mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case is strongest at the programme boundary: nearby continuous bargaining models may be worthwhile, but this paper gives no named computational result to continuize, and its player identities are part of the object being characterized. A broader project could build a computational theory of bargaining populations from scratch; calling that a mirror of this paper would stretch the term past ChoCo’s intended scope.
