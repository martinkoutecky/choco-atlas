The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper has no named computational result. Theorem 2.2, proved here, says that reinforcement is equivalent to having a balance-rule representation. Theorem 2.3, also proved here, says that neutrality plus reinforcement is equivalent to having a neutral scoring-rule representation. Proposition 2.5 characterizes formally utilitarian voting by expressiveness. These are representation and axiomatic results; none asserts an algorithm, a complexity classification, or hardness.

There is a natural continuous-population object nearby. For finite \(X\), take a finite support of signal types \(V\), a rational mass \(\mu_v\) for each type, and an explicit rational score table \(s_v^x\). Ask for all winners
\[ \operatorname*{argmax}_{x\in X}\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v s_v^x. \]
A plausible regime is a large electorate with many voters sharing a relatively small number \(\tau\) of complete signal types. The paper’s variable-population model and its normalization of profiles onto a rational simplex make this a plausible extension the authors could recognize. For this restricted input, winner determination is straightforward, taking polynomial time in the explicit input size.

But that candidate is not anchored to a computational result of the paper. It also narrows the paper’s arbitrary signal sets and ordered score groups, and turns its scoring-rule definition into an evaluation task. The positive case is therefore limited: the population-share model fits the paper’s setup naturally, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational question whose continuous mirror could be stated.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2.2 characterizes reinforcement by balance rules; Theorem 2.3 characterizes neutral reinforced rules by scoring rules. Propositions 2.4–2.6 characterize representations and expressiveness. None studies an algorithmic problem or establishes its complexity. That is decisive against a *paper-anchored* continuous mirror.

The paper does have a credible high-multiplicity regime: its inputs are anonymous counts of signals, and many voters can share a signal. I would not object that mass is unnatural here. Nor does the rational-simplex discussion make the paper an existing continuous computational treatment: it is geometric and axiomatic. The obstacle is that the paper gives no computational task for the continuous population to make tractable or hard.

The proponent’s winner-determination question is a natural extension, but with explicit rational scores and support of size \(\tau\), it just sums the \(\tau\) contributions for each of \(m\) alternatives and compares them, in \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations. The paper permits arbitrary signal sets and abstract ordered score groups, but provides no finite encoding for them. Restricting to explicit tables makes computation routine; using a succinct or implicit representation makes complexity depend on extra choices about that representation.

A stronger adjacent proposal would ask how little mass must change to alter the winner, perhaps with costs for moving mass between signal types. That could be a worthwhile continuous computational problem. But the paper defines no intervention, cost model, or robustness objective. Adding them supplies the computational question; the paper’s representation theorems do not. With explicit finite types and costs, a natural version is a linear program; harder variants would turn on additional structure such as implicit type spaces or a difficult pricing problem. Those may merit study in ChoCo, but they are new mirrors built around the paper, not mirrors of results it proves.

So the negative case is strong on the narrower, paper-anchored criterion: there is no named computational result here to carry over, and the obvious winner calculation is slight. The universal claim is weaker. If “mirror” includes a newly introduced intervention or robustness question that the authors would recognize, this paper’s variable-population setup offers plausible starting points. I cannot honestly rule out such a question being worthwhile; I can only say its value would have to come from the new computational model, not from a computational contribution of this paper.
