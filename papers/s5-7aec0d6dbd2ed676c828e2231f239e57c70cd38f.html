The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over installation types with entitlements \(\ell_t\) and a common cap \(M>\max_t\ell_t\), determine a dominant claim for each type and the resulting per-installation payoffs in the atomless slack-allocation game.

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) has entitlement \(\ell_t\), cap \(M\), and costless claims. A measurable claim profile \(q(t)\in[0,M]\) generates average overage \(x=\int(q-\ell)_+\,d\mu\) and slack \(i=\int(\ell-q)_+\,d\mu\). An individual takes \(x,i\) as fixed when deviating; cooperators receive \(q\), while defectors receive \(\ell_t+(q-\ell_t)_+\) if \(x\le i\), and \(\ell_t+(i/x)(q-\ell_t)_+\) if \(x>i\). Compute a dominant claim per type and the induced payoffs.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers dominant-claim selection in Proposition 3 and could extend to the TU robustness in Theorem 5; it leaves the boundary-continuity characterization and forward-market calibration results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-player dominant-strategy result be formally related to this atomless type-mass game?
  • Does the TU coalition bound extend to every measurable coalition in the same model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow. This paper contains no named computational-complexity result: it establishes no NP-hardness, polynomial-time algorithm, or parameterized classification. The best available anchors are its strategic results, read as answers to computational questions about finding a dominant outcome and testing for coalition improvements. That reading is plausible, but it is an extension of the paper’s framing.

A credible population regime is a large cap-and-share system with many regulated installations assigned from a relatively small schedule of allowance entitlements. A type \(t\) consists of an installation’s entitlement \(\ell_t\), its common claim cap \(M\), and the paper’s costless-claim payoff assumptions. The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of installations of type \(t\); the number of installations is large while the number of entitlement classes \(\tau\) is modest.

For a measurable claim profile \(q\), define average overage and slack by \(x=\int(q-\ell)_+\,d\mu\) and \(i=\int(\ell-q)_+\,d\mu\). Apply the paper’s same clearing rule to these mass aggregates: cooperators receive their claims, while a defector receives their entitlement plus the covered share of their overage. An installation chooses its claim to maximize its settlement payoff; a coalition with transferable utility evaluates deviations by total settlement payoff. The normalization changes totals to per-installation averages, but leaves the \(i/x\) rationing ratio intact.

My lead is Proposition 3, proved in the paper’s Appendix C. It states that, under the linear rule and bounded claims \(C_j\in[0,M]\), with \(M>\max_j L_j\), each player’s payoff is nondecreasing in their own claim for every fixed profile of the other players’ claims. Thus claiming \(M\) is a best reply regardless of what the others do.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Dominant-Claim Outcome. Its instance is the finite list of rational masses \(\mu_t\), entitlements \(\ell_t\), and a common rational cap \(M>\max_t\ell_t\). It asks for a type-symmetric dominant-claim strategy and the resulting payoffs. A solution is \(q_t=M\) and payoff \(\ell_t\) for each type; the induced aggregates have \(i=0\), so every installation receives its entitlement. In the atomless version, an individual deviation does not change the mass aggregates, and the individual’s payoff is nondecreasing in its claim at any fixed aggregate rationing factor. The expected classification is Class A, with a direct \(O(\tau)\) computation. This is the strongest mirror because it preserves the paper’s central act—making a claim—and its central result—efficient payoffs from dominant defection.

A secondary anchor is Theorem 5, proved here, with the implication from Strong Nash to coalition-proofness cited from Bernheim, Peleg, and Whinston. It states that at the all-\(M\) dominant-strategy profile, no coalition can deviate and use transferable utility to make every member weakly better off and at least one strictly better off. Therefore that profile is Strong Nash under TU, and hence coalition-proof.

Its mirror is Continuous TU Coalition-Improvement. In addition to the type masses, entitlements, and cap, the instance specifies the mass of each type in a proposed coalition. The question is whether a measurable reassignment of claims by that coalition can raise its total payoff above its all-\(M\) baseline, thereby permitting a TU Pareto improvement. The continuous extension predicts Class A: the answer is no. With the complement still claiming \(M\), it creates no slack; the coalition’s covered overage cannot exceed its own slack. This extends the paper’s coalition accounting to measurable groups. It is useful as a separate robustness question, though less strong than Proposition 3 as a computational anchor.

The scenario is plausible if entitlements really come from a small set of standard cohorts and claim costs and caps are shared within cohorts. Millions of installations over a modest number of such classes would be a high-multiplicity regime. It becomes much less persuasive if entitlements, caps, or costs are substantially idiosyncratic: those differences must be represented in the types, and \(\tau\) may then grow with the population.

The scope here is just the dominant-claim outcome and its TU coalition robustness. I would not use Theorem 4 or the repeated Appendix Theorem 8 as another anchor: their continuity is payoff continuity at the \(X=I\) boundary as \(\alpha\) varies, not continuity of the population, and they are not computational results. The forward-market proposition likewise concerns expected marginal costs, not a computational population problem.

The main weakness is decisive: neither anchor is a computational-complexity result in the paper. The continuous questions above are tractable extensions of strategic claims the authors actually make; they do not reveal whether discrete hardness dissolves in a continuum. Also, the paper proves its results for finitely many players, whereas the atomless mirror changes unilateral deviations and requires the coalition statement to cover measurable groups. The payoff formulas support that extension, but the paper does not prove it. So there is a credible continuous-population research question here, especially around Proposition 3, but under a strict reading of the requested anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The disqualifying issue is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 3 proves a monotonicity property; Theorem 5 proves a coalition-payoff bound. Neither asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a problem. Turning them into “compute the dominant claim” and “check for a coalition improvement” creates new computational questions, but the stated results already settle those questions by direct formulas.

The carbon-installation setting is plausible: many installations can share a modest number of entitlement classes. I would accept it as a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But for Proposition 3, the resulting computation is just to return \(q_t=M\) and payoff \(\ell_t\) for each type. In an atomless version, an individual does not change the aggregates. Given the aggregate coverage rate, their payoff rises with their claim up to \(M\), or becomes flat above entitlement when coverage is zero. Either way, \(M\) is a best response. The masses do not enter the answer; listing the type-level outputs is \(O(\tau)\) because there are \(\tau\) outputs. That is a valid formal mirror, but it offers no meaningful complexity landscape or algorithmic question beyond the proposition’s closed-form conclusion.

Theorem 5 has the same limitation. For a measurable coalition \(K\), with the complement still defecting, its total payoff is its entitlement total minus its slack plus the overage covered for coalition members. In the slack region, that covered overage cannot exceed the coalition’s slack; in the scarcity region, proportional coverage gives the same bound. So the proposed continuous test has a fixed answer. Supplying the coalition’s type masses does not create a search problem, and allowing arbitrary measurable claim deviations does not defeat the accounting bound. Optimizing coalition excess or checking a proposed deviation is therefore settled directly by the theorem’s argument.

A richer study of the full equilibrium correspondence or of policy design under additional costs and constraints might be worthwhile. But it would need new questions and, in the latter case, a changed payoff model; neither is a computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 4 and the repeated appendix result concern continuity of the rule as \(\alpha\) varies, not continuity of the population, so they do not provide another anchor.

The negative case is strongest on eligibility and substance, not on whether a continuous population is a sensible model: it is sensible here. Its weakness is that a simple Class A result can still be worth recording. But under this programme’s aim of charting computational complexity, neither proposed mirror reveals a problem whose algorithmic landscape the paper has left open.
