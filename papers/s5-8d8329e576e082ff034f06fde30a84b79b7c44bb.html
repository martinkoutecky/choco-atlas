The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(T_M,T_W,T_D\) with rational masses summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict cyclic rankings of the next side’s types, decide whether there is a nonnegative mass assignment \(x_{ijk}\) to triples whose three marginals equal the given type masses and has no blocking type triple. A triple \((i,j,k)\) blocks when positive mass of type \(i\) is assigned to women-types it ranks below \(j\), positive mass of type \(j\) is assigned to dog-types it ranks below \(k\), and positive mass of type \(k\) is assigned to men-types it ranks below \(i\).

The model it lives in

Three populations described by finite type sets and rational masses; each type ranks types on the next side in the cycle. The decision variable \(x_{ijk}\) is mass assigned to triples, with its marginals fixed to the input masses; stability excludes any triple for which each type has positive mass assigned to less-preferred partners.

The objection that survived

The \(8n\) gadget is proved for integral matchings of named agents, so it gives no evidence about stability when type mass can split across triples.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The question extends the stability-existence problem underlying Theorem 1; it does not transfer the \(8n\) gadget guarantee or Theorem 2’s \(n=20\) bound, and leaves the concluding symmetric variant and its quadratic-time construction untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding stable mass-matching existence as a function of the total support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Do discrete no-stable instances remain without stable allocations when each preference cohort can split its mass across triples?

The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying anchor: no numbered result classifies a computational problem as hard, tractable, or parameterized-tractable. Theorem 1, proved here, turns an unsolvable incomplete instance of size \(n\) into an unsolvable complete instance of size \(8n\). Theorem 2, also proved here, gives a complete instance of size \(20\) with no stable matching. Both are meaningful combinatorial results, but neither establishes a complexity classification. The NP-completeness claims in the introduction are attributed to earlier work, not proved in a numbered result here.

The strongest positive lead is therefore only a *prospective* mirror, not a compliant anchor: ask whether a large three-sided market with a fixed number of preference cohorts has a stable fractional matching. Give each side a finite set of types, rational masses summing to \(1\), and each type a strict ranking of types on the next side. A solution is nonnegative mass \(x_{ijk}\) assigned to triples of types, with its marginals equal to the given masses. For a proposed triple \((i,j,k)\), call it blocking if positive mass of type \(i\) is assigned to women-types ranked below \(j\), positive mass of type \(j\) is assigned to dog-types ranked below \(k\), and positive mass of type \(k\) is assigned to men-types ranked below \(i\). The question is whether a marginal-feasible \(x\) with no blocking triple exists. Large cohorts of clones give a plausible regime: many agents per side, but only a fixed or moderate number of types.

Theorem 2 is the closest paper result to this question: its \(20\)-agent counterexample could seed a market with \(20\) preference cohorts, each replicated to large mass. But the theorem does not say whether a stable fractional allocation exists. The mirror also coarsens preferences: clones in one cohort are indifferent among members of another cohort, whereas the paper uses strict rankings of named agents. That type-level preference model is a defensible high-multiplicity extension, but it is the case’s weakest point. I would not infer a tractability or hardness verdict from this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper proves no computational result of the kind ChoCo is set up to continue. Theorem 1 lifts an unsolvable incomplete instance to a complete one of size \(8n\); Theorem 2 constructs a complete instance of size \(20\) with no stable matching. The NP-completeness claim in the introduction is credited to Lam and Plaxton, whose paper proves that result; it is not a result of this paper. [Lam and Plaxton, *On the Existence of Three-Dimensional Stable Matchings with Cyclic Preferences*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.02844). The paper’s contribution is a smaller finite counterexample, not an algorithm, complexity classification, or approximation result.

The proposed fractional model is the strongest possible rescue: types rank cohorts, masses sum to one on each side, and a triple blocks when positive mass from each of its three types would prefer the proposed partner type. That is a coherent continuous-population question, and large cohorts are a plausible regime. But it does not continue Theorem 2’s result. The theorem’s construction and lemmas reason about named vertices, exact partners, and blocking triples in an integral partition. Its size-\(20\) contribution is a finite bound; after replacing agents by type masses, minimum instance size is no longer the question. The theorem supplies no result about stability of fractional allocations.

The rescue therefore looks like a new computational project motivated by the same matching problem. I can make a strong case that this article provides no qualifying computational anchor for ChoCo. I cannot honestly establish the broader claim that no continuous mirror of the underlying three-sided matching problem could be worthwhile: the type-level existence question is well-defined, and the programme explicitly permits the needed co-modification of preferences and outcomes. Calling it impossible to motivate in any scenario would overstate the evidence.
