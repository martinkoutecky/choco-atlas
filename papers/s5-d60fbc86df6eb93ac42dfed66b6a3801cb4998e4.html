The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), target \(c\), and rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings, compute an optimal allocation \(x_{t,k}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,k}\) is mass of type \(t\) moving \(c\) upward by \(k\) adjacent swaps and \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\). Require, for every \(d\ne c\), that its resulting pairwise margin satisfy \(\mathrm{mar}_\mu(c,d)+2\sum_{t,k:\,d\text{ is crossed}}x_{t,k}\ge 0\); minimize the per-capita swap cost \(\sum_{t,k}kx_{t,k}\), and report the optimal allocation and edited society.

The model it lives in

Each complete ranking is a type carrying mass \(\mu_t\); allocate that mass among actions that move \(c\) upward by an integral number of adjacent swaps, require \(c\) to be a Condorcet winner in the resulting society, and minimize total swap cost per capita.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover the underlying Dodgson and deletion-based Young scores targeted by Theorems 3 and 5. They leave aside the greedy algorithms' own approximation guarantees and running-time results, the generic framework, and the auction interpretation.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the continuous optimum compare with the optimum for a fixed finite electorate with specified type counts?
  • Does extending the paper's greedy process to divisible mass retain a logarithmic approximation guarantee against the continuous optimum?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the population version of Dodgson score. Theorem 3, proved in this paper, says that Marginal-Cost-Greedy-Dodgson is an \((\ln m+1)\)-approximation of Dodgson score for an election with \(m\) candidates. The algorithm greedily applies adjacent swaps that move the target candidate upward in one voter’s ranking, choosing a sequence with minimum swaps per deficit reduction; its score is the number of swaps it applies. The paper establishes the algorithm’s polynomial running time separately in Theorem 1. The introduction also reports discrete hardness results from prior work, but they are not numbered results proved here, so I treat Theorem 3—not those citations—as the anchor.

Call the mirror Continuous Dodgson Mass-Edit Score. An instance is a candidate set \(C\), a target \(c\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings of \(C\). For each ranking type \(t\), let \(B_t(c)\) be the candidates ranked above \(c\). Choose \(x_{t,k}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(t\) that moves \(c\) upward across the first \(k\) candidates in \(B_t(c)\), for \(0\leq k\leq |B_t(c)|\). Require \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\). If \(M_d\) is \(c\)’s initial pairwise margin over opponent \(d\), also require \(M_d+2\sum_{t,k:\,d\text{ is among the first }k\text{ in }B_t(c)}x_{t,k}\geq 0\) for every \(d\ne c\). The task is to minimize \(\sum_{t,k} kx_{t,k}\). A solution is an optimal rational allocation \(x\), its induced edited society, and its per-capita swap cost. Each unit of mass still receives an integral number of swaps; only the share of a type taking each action can be fractional.

This is the exact high-multiplicity relaxation of the underlying edit-distance problem, normalized by population size. It preserves the candidates, rankings, target, adjacent-swap metric, pairwise Condorcet condition, and objective. The reason the model simplifies is structural: swaps that do not move \(c\) cannot help it win, and an upward move by \(k\) positions gives a specified \(2\)-unit margin improvement against each of the \(k\) crossed candidates. The resulting LP has \(O(\tau m)\) variables and a number of constraints polynomial in \(m\) and \(\tau\), so it is tractable in \(m,\tau,L\). For any rational profile, a suitable replication makes an optimal rational mass allocation implementable by whole voters, linking the answer to homogeneous elections. This is a Class A expectation.

A second, credible anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here: Marginal-Cost-Greedy-Young is an \(O(\log m)\)-approximation of the paper’s deletion-based Young score. Its underlying question has a direct mirror, Continuous Young Mass-Deletion Score. Give the instance the same \(C,c,\mu\); choose \(0\leq y_t\leq\mu_t\), where \(y_t\) is the mass of ranking type \(t\) removed; minimize \(\sum_t y_t\), subject to \(M_d-\sum_t y_t\sigma_t(c,d)\geq 0\) for every opponent \(d\), where \(\sigma_t(c,d)=1\) if \(t\) ranks \(c\) above \(d\) and \(-1\) otherwise. The output is the removed mass by type and the remaining society. This is an LP with \(\tau\) variables and \(m-1\) margin constraints, hence tractable in \(m,\tau,L\). It is a weaker scenario than the Dodgson mirror because removing a share of an electorate needs a setting such as an eligible membership subset or ballot recusal; still, it is exactly the deletion operation the paper defines.

For both mirrors, a plausible regime is a large association or mass consultation with millions of participants but relatively few recurring complete rankings—say, hundreds or thousands of types. In the Dodgson case, the paper’s own auction interpretation supplies a natural action: some share of each ranking cohort accepts adjacent swaps to make the target a Condorcet winner. Ranking is a complete type for these scored problems because the paper assigns edits their uniform count cost; if the model adds voter-specific prices, those prices must become part of the type.

I would lead with Continuous Dodgson Mass-Edit Score. The case’s weakest point is that Theorem 3 proves a discrete greedy approximation, whereas the proposed question computes the exact fractional score; it does not inherit that algorithm or its approximation factor. The model also approaches the homogeneous Dodgson versions that the paper says were already solved in polynomial time by prior work. That is a reason to expect the answer to be known, not a reason to reject the mirror: the programme treats existing high-multiplicity work as evidence that the regime is sensible. The mirrors cover the underlying Dodgson and Young scoring problems computed approximately in Theorems 3 and 5; they do not claim to continuize the greedy rules themselves or every result in the paper. Useful follow-up questions are the finite-population rounding gap for fixed counts, the smallest replication needed to realize an optimal mass solution, and how the answer changes when edit prices vary across types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest attack is that the proposed LPs compute the exact continuous Dodgson and Young scores, while Theorems 3 and 5 analyze the approximation guarantees of particular greedy rules. The proponent has mirrored the scores those rules approximate, not the rules’ performance results. A closer mirror would define what the greedy edit process does to divisible mass and ask for its approximation guarantee. That is a different question, and the case does not show why studying that particular continuous heuristic would matter once the exact score is computable.

But this does not defeat the mirrors. The theorems are explicitly about approximating those scores, so computing their continuous versions is a natural computational question. Large elections with recurring rankings give a credible high-multiplicity regime; voter identity is irrelevant to the scores; and fractional mass can represent shares of each ranking cohort taking actions that remain integral for each voter. Young’s deletions also have plausible recusal or abstention interpretations. Neither mirror degenerates in the limit.

The fact that continuous Dodgson and Young scores already have LP treatments is the strongest novelty concern, but under the stated rules, a known answer to a well-posed mirror changes what needs proving; it does not make the question invalid. Existing homogeneous results likewise support the regime rather than undermine it. So the case against can question whether these mirrors add new work to the programme, but it cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The Dodgson mirror in particular survives the objections available here.
