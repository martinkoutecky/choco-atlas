The continuous mirror question

Given two finite type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) with each side’s total mass normalized to \(1\), integer capacities, and incomplete rankings with ties over acceptable opposite-side types, can one find an exact rational assignment in polynomial time? Represent the assignment by masses of agents receiving integral bundles of opposite-side types, with equal type-pair incidence on both sides. Require pairwise stability under the paper’s blocking conditions and two-sided Pareto efficiency: no feasible reassignment weakly improves almost every agent and strictly improves a positive mass. Measure running time and output size in the number of types \(\tau\), the preference description, the binary capacity encodings, and the mass bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided atomless population with finite sets of complete agent types and rational mass per type. Decision variables \(p_{i,B}\) and \(q_{j,D}\) give the mass of each type receiving each integral bundle; bundle sizes respect individual capacities, and type-pair incidence balances across the two sides. The task is a feasibility and search problem: produce a polynomial-size exact rational assignment that is pairwise stable and two-sided Pareto-efficient.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s general arbitrary-preference Pareto-stable assignment result. It leaves Theorems 2 and 3’s NTU mechanisms, the strategyproofness claim, and the simulation results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact rational distributions over integral bundles satisfying the paper’s stability and Pareto-efficiency conditions be found in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), with capacities given in binary?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Continuous Pareto-Stable Many-to-Many Matching.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper’s Appendix D4: the authors’ algorithm \(\mathrm{Alg\text{-}Pareto\text{-}Stable}\) computes a Pareto-stable assignment in polynomial time. Here, “Pareto-stable” means both pairwise stable and two-sided Pareto-efficient. In the paper’s model, assignments respect capacities and mutual acceptability; stability rules out blocking pairs, and Pareto efficiency rules out a feasible reassignment that weakly improves everyone and strictly improves someone. This is the paper’s own result, not a result merely cited from elsewhere. The paper offers no comparable hardness anchor, so this algorithmic result is the right one to mirror.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large online labor market that repeatedly matches workers to hiring teams or projects. There could be millions of participants but only hundreds or thousands of recurring profiles. A worker’s type records the skills and preferences that determine which kinds of projects they accept, their capacity for concurrent engagements, and their acceptable project types. A hiring team’s type records its hiring capacity, requirements, priorities, and acceptable worker types. The scenario is credible only where those features actually determine preferences: anyone with an identity-specific preference or capacity difference belongs to a different type. The paper itself names online labor markets as a possible application of its general many-to-many model.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. An instance gives finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\) for the two sides, rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\geq 0\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i+\sum_j\nu_j=1\), and integer capacities \(c_i,d_j\). Each type ranks the acceptable opposite-side types, allowing ties and incomplete lists. The ranking induces responsive preferences over bundles: each individual receives an integral multiset of partners, with size at most that individual’s capacity.

A candidate assignment can be represented by \(z_{i,B}\), the mass of type-\(i\) individuals receiving bundle \(B\), and \(y_{j,D}\), the corresponding mass of type-\(j\) individuals receiving bundle \(D\). For every type, these masses sum to its population mass. For every pair \(i,j\), the number of \(i\)-to-\(j\) incidences must equal the number of \(j\)-to-\(i\) incidences:
\[ \sum_B z_{i,B}b_j(B)=\sum_D y_{j,D}a_i(D), \]
where \(b_j(B)\) counts type-\(j\) partners in \(B\), and \(a_i(D)\) counts type-\(i\) partners in \(D\). This consistency condition lets the atomless populations be paired while preserving each person’s integral bundle.

The question is whether one can produce an exact rational assignment of this kind that is pairwise stable—no positive-mass subpopulations can form a blocking pair under the paper’s blocking conditions—and Pareto-efficient—there is no feasible reassignment making almost everyone weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off. The computational version asks whether such an assignment can be found with polynomial running time and polynomial-size representation in the number of types, preference description, and binary input lengths, rather than by expanding the population into individual agents.

I would expect this problem to be tractable, though that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 4. The paper’s algorithm uses capacity increments and augmenting-path and cycle operations. With a small number of complete types, there is a plausible route to batching those operations over masses instead of processing each clone separately. The anchor is encouraging because it already handles arbitrary preferences and capacities; the mirror retains the paper’s actual stability and welfare criteria rather than reducing the task to maximum matching or a scalar objective.

There is also a useful, limited existence argument. For rational input masses, scaling by a common denominator realizes the instance as a finite market with duplicate agents. Theorem 4 then supplies a Pareto-stable assignment, which can be expressed as bundle masses. But that expansion may have size exponential in the binary encoding of the masses. Thus the theorem establishes existence and a denominator-sized algorithm; it does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for the compressed continuous input. The open computational question is whether the algorithm can be batched by type, or whether the compressed problem has its own complexity barrier.

The authors would likely recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime: it preserves many-to-many assignments, capacities, ties, incomplete acceptability, responsive preferences, pairwise stability, and bilateral Pareto efficiency. The population is the continuous object; fractional type averages arise because some agents of a type receive one integral bundle and others receive another. This mirror covers Theorem 4’s general matching result only. It does not claim continuous versions of the draft or dictatorship mechanisms, strategyproofness, or the simulation findings.

The weakest point is the type assumption. The paper ranks named agents, and in real labor markets people and projects may care about idiosyncratic attributes that prevent useful grouping. If the number of distinct complete types grows nearly as fast as the population, the proposed mirror loses its high-multiplicity motivation. That limits the regime, but does not rule it out: recurring markets with standardized profiles and preferences are a plausible setting, and the programme asks whether the compressed problem is computationally different precisely in such a regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is that Theorem 4’s polynomial time guarantee is tied to an explicitly listed market. Its algorithm raises one agent’s virtual capacity by one, follows augmenting paths through named agents, and bounds progress by how many times an individual assignment can improve. It gives no bound in the number of types \(\tau\) or the bit length \(L\) of population masses.

That matters for the proposed mirror. Scaling rational masses by a common denominator \(D\) gives a finite market, so Theorem 4 supplies an assignment. But \(D\) can be exponential in \(L\), and the algorithm’s path operations may involve all \(D\) agents. Bundling agents by type is not an immediate fix: the mirror needs distributions over integral bundles to preserve individual capacities and preferences, while stability and Pareto efficiency depend on which bundles particular agents receive. Theorem 4 supplies no way to batch its operations over those distributions. The proposed mirror is therefore a new compressed computational problem, not a consequence of the paper’s polynomial algorithm.

The paper’s NTU data also provide weak evidence for a useful compressed regime. A student’s complete type includes their preference list over particular courses, capacity, and relevant priority attributes. The paper does not show that many students share such a type; if most lists differ, then \(\tau\) may be close to the number of students. The online labor market example faces the same test: workers must share preferences over project types, and projects must share priorities over worker types. A recurring cohort could meet it, but the paper’s general model does not establish that this regime is common.

Those points limit the claimed payoff; they do not defeat the strongest mirror. A recurring market with a fixed set of worker and project profiles could have many agents per complete type. A mass-based version of pairwise stability and Pareto efficiency is well defined, and the paper’s discussion of large markets and online labor makes it plausible the authors would recognize the question. Whether its exact solution can be found in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), rather than in \(D\), is precisely the unresolved computational question.

So the honest case against is weak. Theorem 4 is a genuine computational anchor, and the better continuous mirror survives. The strongest objection is that its high-multiplicity regime needs a convincing application and a new algorithmic analysis; neither is grounds to dismiss the question as unworthy.
