The continuous mirror question

Fix \(k\) divisible by \(8\), let \(V_k=\{j/k:j=1,\ldots,k\}\), and let \(T_k=\{S_v,B_v:v\in V_k\}\). An input is a finite adversarial role path \(\sigma\in\{S,B\}^N\), whose terminal length is unknown to the online intermediary; each position is a unit-mass cohort with mass \(1/k\) at each value tier of that role. At each cohort the intermediary posts one price: a seller price \(q\) receives mass \(F_k(q)=k^{-1}|\{j:j/k\le q\}|\), and a buyer price \(p\) receives demand mass \(1-F_k(p^-)\), subject to available stock. Starting from zero stock with nonnegative real inventory, what is the optimal worst-case competitive ratio \(\rho(N)\) for expected profit against a clairvoyant posted-price benchmark with the same cohort and inventory rules, allowing an additive \(O(1)\) term and requiring one online policy that does not know \(N\)?

The model it lives in

A fluid online market with fixed types \(T_k\) recording side and value tier. Each ordered role position releases a unit cohort with equal mass across its value tiers; posted prices yield exact acceptance masses, inventory is nonnegative real mass, and the objective is profit against a clairvoyant benchmark on the same role path.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 6’s profit competitive-ratio lower bound for seller-first streams. It leaves the welfare results, general-case upper bounds, distributional necessity results, limited-stock results, and balanced-stream mechanisms unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(k\), does the optimal competitive ratio on arbitrary role paths have a matching \(O(\sqrt N)\) upper bound, or does the unknown horizon impose an additional factor?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s profit lower bound for a long seller-first stream. Theorem 6, proved in this paper in §4.2, says that when buyers’ and sellers’ values are drawn independently from \(U[0,1]\), no online posted-price mechanism can achieve an approximation ratio better than \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\). The bad stream is \(S^{n/2}B^{n/2}\): the intermediary must build stock before any buyers arrive. The proof bounds how much it can spend on sellers, then shows that an offline intermediary can earn \(\Omega(n)\). The paper has no named NP-hardness, P, or FPT result; this competitive-ratio lower bound is the computational result I am anchoring on.

Here is a finite-type population mirror. Fix \(k\) divisible by \(8\), let \(V_k=\{j/k:j=1,\ldots,k\}\), and take types \(T_k=\{S_v,B_v:v\in V_k\}\), so \(\tau=2k\). A unit-mass arrival cohort consists of a continuum of traders whose value mass is \(1/k\) at each \(v\in V_k\); its role is either seller or buyer. The stream is an ordered sequence of these cohorts, and the intermediary learns each cohort’s role as it arrives but not the terminal mass \(N\). The society’s normalized type masses on the bad stream are \(\mu_{S_v}=\mu_{B_v}=1/(2k)\), with \(N\gg\tau\). The type records both side and value, so traders of the same type are interchangeable in every respect used by the model.

The problem, which I would call Fluid Online Market Intermediation on Seller-First Streams, is: for this type population, find the best online posted-price policy and determine its worst-case competitive ratio as a function of \(N\). The policy may set prices along the arriving mass; stock is nonnegative real mass. At seller price \(q\), a mass \(F_k(q)\) accepts, where \(F_k(q)=\Pr[V\le q]\), and the intermediary pays \(q\) per unit. At buyer price \(p\), a mass \(1-F_k(p^-)\) accepts, and the intermediary sells up to its available stock. The offline benchmark sees the entire role sequence and faces the same prices, mass, and stock rules. A single online policy must work without knowing \(N\); the guarantee is \(R^*(\sigma)\le \rho(N)R_A(\sigma)+C\) for every finite stream \(\sigma\), with \(C\) independent of \(N\). A solution gives such a policy and bound, or a lower bound ruling out a better factor.

I expect Theorem 6’s \(\Omega(\sqrt N)\) obstruction to survive. On \(S^{N/2}B^{N/2}\), \(F_k(q)\le q\), so buying mass \(F_k(q)\) costs \(qF_k(q)\ge F_k(q)^2\). If the policy must remain competitive even when the stream stops after a seller prefix, its total seller spending is \(O(1)\). Thus, by Cauchy–Schwarz, total stock acquired is at most \(O(\sqrt N)\), and so is the policy’s profit. The offline policy can buy at \(q=1/8\) and sell at \(p=1/2\): it buys mass \(N/16\), sells it all, and makes \(3N/128\) profit. The same argument works for exact \(U[0,1]\) type mass, where \(F(q)=q\). This is a transfer-style competitive impossibility, not a claim of NP-hardness. A natural next question is whether the optimal fluid ratio is \(\Theta(\sqrt N)\), or whether not knowing the stream length imposes an additional logarithmic factor.

The regime is a large market in standardized goods: many small orders arrive in waves, but buyers and sellers fall into a fixed set of value tiers. For example, a commodity intermediary might face repeated purchasing and selling cohorts with similar per-unit valuations. The number of traders grows while the number of distinct role-value types stays small. The order of arrival remains part of the problem; only the population within each cohort becomes mass. Each infinitesimal trader still trades one indivisible item, while stock is fractional only in aggregate. That is a population continuization with the inventory co-modification it requires.

The paper’s balanced-stream section provides useful modeling support: its fractional mechanisms already track exact quantities bought and sold. I would not use those results as a second anchor; the more useful point is that the authors themselves work with mass-like transaction quantities when analyzing this market. My mirror covers only Theorem 6’s profit obstruction for an unrestricted-stock, seller-first stream. It does not claim to mirror the welfare bounds, the stock-cap result, or the balanced-stream mechanisms.

The soft spot is the replacement of independent individual values by exact mass proportions. That removes sampling noise and gives the intermediary deterministic acceptance fractions. It is a real model change. But it is the natural high-multiplicity limit when many traders share known value distributions, and it does not erase the lower bound: the spending-versus-stock constraint survives even after the randomness disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is a named algorithmic result, so I can’t dismiss this anchor for lack of a computational question. Nor does the fluid limit obviously collapse it. With \(U[0,1]\) values, a seller price \(q\) buys mass \(q\) and costs \(q^2\) per unit of arriving seller mass. The seller-only prefixes constrain how much the online intermediary can spend, while fixed offline prices earn profit proportional to \(N\). That tradeoff survives when acceptance fractions are exact. The mirror has a coherent economic interpretation, and the population mass is not just an afterthought.

The strongest objection is about what drives the problem. The intermediary’s guarantee is over an ordered, adversarial arrival stream with an unknown stopping point. A static mass distribution over seller and buyer value types does not encode that order. To preserve the paper’s input, the mirror needs an ordered path of mass cohorts. If arrival position is part of the type, the support grows with the stream; if it is kept outside the type, the central object is a fluid online-control problem with a filtration and stopping horizon, rather than the programme’s usual type-mass optimization. The theorem’s main difficulty is managing inventory under that information structure, not choosing a mass transfer among types.

That makes this a plausible neighboring project in fluid online market design, but a weaker fit for ChoCo’s complexity programme. The paper optimizes an intermediary’s posted-price policy; it does not study a collective choice rule or computational manipulation of a social outcome. The paper’s fractional mechanisms in the balanced-stream section do not already answer this mirror, but they underline the distinction: exact transaction quantities can be useful analysis without making the society itself the computational input.

This case against the mirror is ultimately a scope argument, not a modeling impossibility. The seller-first example is especially hard to dismiss: it has only a few value types, a credible large-market interpretation, and a nondegenerate mass-based online question. I can argue that ChoCo should prioritize problems whose input is a distribution over types rather than an adversarial arrival path, but I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proponent’s anchor survives the strongest version of that objection.
