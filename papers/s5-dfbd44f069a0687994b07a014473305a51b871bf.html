Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 5 — survived: The type-distribution model induces finite negotiation MDPs whose optimal offering policies can be studied while each local negotiation retains the paper's strategic setting.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite outcome set, τ complete agent types with rational masses μ, DSM parameters, a fixed negotiation group size g, a finite disagreement penalty K, and a rational group-formation kernel q_μ that specifies responder-type groups conditional on the initiator type, compute each positive-mass type's optimal DSM offering policy in its induced finite MDP and the aggregate expected per-capita utility ∑_t μ_t E[U_t]. Classify the running time in the number of outcomes, τ, g, offer bounds, and input bit length.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution μ over τ complete agent types, and q_μ specifies how finite groups are formed from that population. Each encounter keeps the paper's individual agents, incentives, and DSM rules; only group frequencies and per-capita utility are aggregated over μ. Thus the limit does not replace the finite strategic interaction with a nonatomic or Wardrop solution concept.

The objection that survived

Theorem 5 guarantees only asymptotic convergence and supplies no sample or runtime bound, so the proposed efficient-computation question extends beyond its guarantee; the proponent acknowledges this limitation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5 and the initiator's optimal offering strategy. It leaves the faithfulness and budget-balance results, privacy metric, and empirical comparisons unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of computing the type-specific optimal policies and aggregate utility as a function of the outcome count, τ, g, offer bounds, and bit length?
  • Can the induced transition and reward data be computed efficiently when the group size g is part of the input?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Type-Distribution Optimal DSM Offering. Picture a large employer or city service that runs many recurring scheduling negotiations. Each negotiation still involves a small group, as in the paper, while the wider society consists of many agents who take part in repeated negotiations. Standardized schedules and interaction roles can give that society relatively few complete types: each type records the agent’s outcome values and feasibility constraints, bargaining cost, privacy sensitivities and ideal beliefs, convenience-point budget, belief about future negotiations, and network role. If there are \(N\) agents and \(\tau\) such types, the regime of interest has \(N\) large and \(\tau\) modest. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), the fraction of the society with that complete description. A rational type mass can represent a finite population whose size is a multiple of its denominator.

The continuous instance consists of a finite outcome set, a finite type support \(T\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu\), DSM parameters, and a rational model of the fixed-size negotiation groups formed from those types. For each initiator type, its type record and group model determine the MDP from the paper: state \(s\) is the number of feasible, previously undiscussed agreements; action \(L\) is the number of agreements to offer, subject to the mechanism’s bounds and the initiator’s budget. Transitions follow the paper’s strictness model. Rewards include the initiator’s agreement value, communication and privacy costs, and the rewards paid under DSM. Use a finite disagreement penalty \(-K\), as in the paper’s MDP formulation, so rewards are bounded.

The question is: compute a best offering policy for each positive-mass type and its aggregate expected per-capita utility. The solution is a policy table \(\pi_t(s)\), with each \(\pi_t\) maximizing that type’s expected utility against the type distribution in its local negotiations. The aggregate score is
\[ \bar U(\pi;\mu)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbb{E}[U_t\mid\pi_t]. \]
Each agent still makes an individual agreement and uses individual points; only population proportions and average utility are fractional. That co-modification fits the paper’s setting: it already makes a type the bundle of private parameters relevant to an agent’s decisions.

The anchor is Theorem 5, the paper’s numbered Q-learning convergence result. It says that when rewards are bounded, learning rates satisfy \(0\leq\alpha_m\leq1\), and, for every state–action pair \((s,L)\), the learning rates along its visits satisfy
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{m_i(s,L)}=\infty \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\bigl[\alpha_{m_i(s,L)}\bigr]^2<\infty, \]
then Q-learning converges to the optimal solution with probability \(1\). The paper uses this result for the initiator’s offering MDP; it cites Watkins and Dayan [94] for the theorem rather than proving it. A natural mirror asks whether the type-distribution version admits an efficient optimal-policy algorithm, or a learning method with guarantees on its sample requirements. A finite-state, finite-horizon instance with explicitly computable transition and reward entries looks tractable by dynamic programming, and Q-learning supplies a learning route when those entries are unknown. I would expect Class A for bounded-size negotiation groups and finite-support societies. The interesting boundary questions are how the running time scales with \(\tau\), the number of outcomes, and the offer bound, and whether computing the success rewards stays manageable when group sizes grow.

This is a narrow mirror of the paper’s initiator-strategy result. It does not claim to continuize the faithfulness proofs, individual budget balance, voluntary participation, or the privacy metric. The local interactions remain the places where those claims must hold; the continuous object is the population distribution over the agent types that participate in repeated interactions.

The main plausibility point is unusually direct: Assumption 2 explicitly calls the agents’ private parameter bundles their “types,” and the paper derives an offering strategy for an initiator facing uncertainty about responders. Agents sharing a complete type can share a type-level policy while retaining the same local negotiation and incentives. The authors could recognize that as their optimal-offering question evaluated over a high-multiplicity society.

The weakest point is that Theorem 5 is a general, cited convergence theorem, not a polynomial-time classification, and the article gives no named NP-hardness, P, or parameterized-complexity result. The population distribution affects the policy problem through the types and mix of local interaction groups, while the anchor itself concerns a finite MDP. Also, preserving small local groups is important: the paper’s strategic incentives rely on individual responders affecting a negotiation. Treating one negotiation as an interaction with an atomless continuum would require a different incentive analysis. So the positive case is credible for a specific high-multiplicity regime, but it does not by itself establish a broad complexity landscape for the paper’s mechanism.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the only plausible computational anchor here, but it does not establish a complexity result about a society. It gives a convergence condition for Q-learning on the initiator’s finite MDP: if rewards are bounded, the relevant state–action pairs are visited, and the learning rates satisfy the stated sums, Q-learning converges. The proponent’s proposed mirror then changes the target twice: from convergence to computing optimal policies or sample requirements, and from one initiator’s MDP to a planner’s policy table and population-average utility. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they are not continuous versions of the result Theorem 5 proves. Sample requirements, in particular, are not supplied by its convergence statement.

The strongest version would posit recurring negotiations in a large, standardized institution and give each negotiation a specified rule for drawing initiators and responders from complete types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But the type masses alone do not determine the encounters: the paper permits an arbitrary communication graph and chooses responder groups from an initiator’s neighborhood. The mirror therefore needs an additional law for neighborhoods, groups, and roles. Once that law is supplied and groups remain bounded in size, the relevant object is a collection of finite negotiation MDPs, one for each initiator type and local group configuration. The masses weight how often those MDPs occur and their average utility; they do not make the population distribution the object whose complexity Theorem 5 studies. This is a useful deployment or performance-analysis model, but a thin anchor for ChoCo’s computational landscape.

Making the population materially affect each negotiation requires going further. If group size grows with the population, each agent’s report, privacy loss, and incentive to score an offer sits inside a larger individual strategic interaction. The paper’s faithfulness claims concern those individual actions. A population-level limit that changes how an individual affects the outcome would need a new strategic model and new incentive analysis. That could be a good project, but it is no longer the paper’s Q-learning result carried over to type masses. Conversely, retaining the paper’s individual incentives and fixed-size groups preserves the local finite problem and leaves population mass chiefly as a way to aggregate encounters.

The article’s other numbered results do not repair the gap: Theorems 1–4 concern reward-function existence, faithfulness, and budget balance. They are mechanism properties, not computational complexity classifications. Nor does the paper state a complexity result for its heuristic or exact offering strategy. Theorem 5 is formally computational, but its population mirror lacks the direct complexity target the proposed anchor suggests.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight. Repeated scheduling or allocation among many agents with a small set of genuinely complete types is conceivable, and studying how group formation shapes average welfare could interest these authors. My case is strongest against treating that study as a worthwhile continuous *computational mirror* of this paper: the clean version adds population bookkeeping around finite MDPs, while versions in which the continuum changes the strategic problem require a different model.
