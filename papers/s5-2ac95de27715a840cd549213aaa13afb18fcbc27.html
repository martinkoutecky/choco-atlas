The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of strict rankings over room categories \(R\), rational type masses \(\mu_t \ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_r \ge 0\) summing to \(1\), characterize exactly all Pareto-efficient feasible matrices \(x \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T \times R}\) satisfying \(\sum_r x_{tr}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tr}=q_r\). For a ranking \(r_1 \succ_t \cdots \succ_t r_m\), type \(t\) weakly improves when every prefix sum \(\sum_{j=1}^k x_{t r_j}\) does not decrease; efficiency excludes a feasible reassignment that weakly improves every type and strictly improves some positive mass. Give an exact finite description of the efficient set, for example as rational polyhedral cells; each person still receives one indivisible room, while \(x_{tr}\) records type-level mass.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic cohort of students partitioned into complete ranking types, with room categories whose supplies scale with cohort size. The decision object is the type-to-category mass matrix \(x\); the output is the full set of Pareto-efficient feasible matrices, not fractional rooms for individuals.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 proves completeness only for finite inverse-TTC preimages; the paper gives no bridge from that construction to the proposed aggregate frontier or its polyhedral representation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper’s complete Pareto-frontier objective and the inverse-enumeration correctness motivation of Theorem 1. It does not transfer a runtime guarantee, and it leaves aside the empirical comparison and later welfare or fairness selection questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the proposed type-level dominance condition exactly capture individual Pareto improvements under measurable assignments?
  • Can the efficient frontier be represented or enumerated with useful complexity bounds in the number of categories, ranking types, and input bit length?
  • Does the discrete frontier problem reduce to this aggregate problem strongly enough to support a Class B prediction?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous type-compressed house-allocation frontier. The paper’s central object is the full set of Pareto-optimal matchings, and its introduction explicitly allows that frontier to be “succinctly represented.” In a large annual housing cohort, a type could be a complete ranking over a fixed set of interchangeable room categories. Mass is the fraction of students with each ranking; each student still receives one indivisible room, while the type-level totals are fractional. Room-category supplies scale with the cohort.

There is an important limit on the anchor. The only numbered result in the paper is Theorem 1 (Completeness of invTTC), proved here: for any allocation \(A\) such that TTC on \(A\) yields \(\sigma\), the inverse procedure \(\operatorname{invTTC}(\sigma)\) generates \(A\). It establishes completeness of the paper’s inverse enumeration, not that a problem is in P or belongs to a hardness class. The paper’s NP-hardness statement about selecting a welfare-maximizing PO allocation is unnumbered and attributed to earlier work, so it cannot serve as one of the required anchors. Theorem 1 is therefore the best available algorithmic-correctness anchor, but it does not fully meet the requested complexity-result criterion.

My lead mirror is the following underlying problem:

Continuous PO House-Allocation Frontier. An instance gives room categories \(R=\{r_1,\ldots,r_m\}\), a finite set \(T\) of strict rankings over \(R\), type masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), and category supplies \(q_r\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_r q_r=1\). A feasible allocation is a mass-transfer matrix \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{T\times R}\) satisfying \(\sum_r x_{tr}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tr}=q_r\). It represents a measurable assignment in which every person gets one room; fractional entries are the mass of a type assigned to a category, not fractional rooms for an individual.

The task is to give an exact finite description of all Pareto-efficient feasible matrices. For a type ranking \(r_1\succ_t\cdots\succ_t r_m\), \(y\) weakly improves every member of type \(t\) over \(x\) when \(\sum_{j=1}^k y_{t r_j}\ge\sum_{j=1}^k x_{t r_j}\) for every \(k<m\). Thus \(x\) is efficient if there is no feasible \(y\) satisfying all those inequalities with at least one strict. A solution is a finite union of rational polyhedral cells whose union is exactly this efficient set. This is a change from listing finitely many named-agent matchings to describing an aggregate frontier; the continuum makes that change necessary.

I would provisionally expect the general problem to have a Class B boundary. The combinatorics over rankings and room categories remain, and discrete PO matchings embed as integral vertices in the corresponding transportation model: a matching that is PO in the discrete instance cannot be improved by a fractional reassignment either. That gives a plausible route for hardness to persist as the number of categories grows, though this paper proves no such reduction. Fixed-category or fixed-type slices may be tractable by polyhedral methods. Further questions include whether the frontier has a compact exact description, whether a continuous TTC inverse can characterize its mass-level outcomes, and what the precise algorithmic relationship is to finite instances.

The mirror covers the paper’s frontier-enumeration objective and the role of Theorem 1’s inverse characterization. It does not claim a continuous analogue of the paper’s runtime analysis or its discussion of welfare and fairness selection.

The weak point is that room categories collapse the identity of individual rooms, and the output is now a continuum of aggregate allocations rather than the paper’s finite set of matchings. The authors might regard that as changing the problem. I think the case remains plausible because the paper’s central question—characterizing every PO assignment—is preserved at the level that matters in a large cohort, and the change in output is a direct consequence of making the population continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent has not found a computational theorem in this paper for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 1 proves that the inverse procedure recovers the finite set of named initial endowments that map to a given TTC outcome. The complexity discussion is separate: ITEA still processes all \(n!\) allocations, and its stated advantage is fewer forward TTC calls. That gives ChoCo little complexity result to carry into a continuous setting.

The sharper objection is to the proposed continuous frontier. The paper’s object is the set of matchings among individually ranked rooms. To represent a large cohort with fixed room categories, one must make copies within a category interchangeable, so that students rank categories rather than the distinct rooms in the paper. That is a sensible capacitated assignment problem, but it changes the object being enumerated. Its fractional type-by-category matrices form a multiobjective transportation polytope; describing their efficient frontier by polyhedral cells would be a new problem, not a continuization of Theorem 1’s inverse-TTC result. The theorem offers no bridge to that representation or reason to expect its inverse construction to extend.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A large housing cohort with a fixed set of room categories and a small set of complete ranking types is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Fractional entries can represent mass assigned to each category while every student still gets one room. Asking for the exact Pareto-efficient aggregate frontier is well-posed and preserves the paper’s main objective. The paper’s theorem may be a weak anchor for it, but the frontier itself is a strong one.

So I cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case is strongest against treating this paper as a direct source of continuous complexity results; it is weak against the cohort-based frontier question. The proponent’s assertion that discrete PO matchings embed as integral vertices also needs a proof, but that gap does not erase the mirror.
