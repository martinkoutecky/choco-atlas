The continuous mirror question

Given population size \(N\), rational type shares \(\mu_t\), qualities \(q_t>2/3\), nonnegative per-task costs \(c_t\), and a rational signal threshold \(M\) (the paper uses \(M=6\ln(1/\alpha)\)), find a minimum-cost continuous selection \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le N\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t(2q_t-1)x_t\ge M\); characterize the cost guarantee after rounding each positive type selection up to an integral number of workers.

The model it lives in

Types are worker cohorts with shared quality \(q_t\) and cost \(c_t\), available mass \(N\mu_t\); choose selected mass \(x_t\), minimizing \(\sum_t c_tx_t\) subject to the paper’s signal threshold and cohort capacities. The resulting linear program is solvable exactly by sorting types by cost per unit signal.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the Fractional Assured-Accuracy Knapsack over Worker Types. The paper has no numbered theorem classifying an underlying problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or similar; its minimum-knapsack hardness claim appears in the prose of Section 6. The closest numbered computational anchor is Lemma 6.1, which is an algorithmic result: the paper cites Csirik et al. [CSIRIK90] for the deterministic greedy algorithm \(GA\), and states that on the minimum-knapsack instance it returns a feasible set \(S^{GA}\) with \(C(S^{GA})\le 2C(S^*)\). The paper imports this guarantee; it does not prove it there. That is the one anchor I would use.

The scenario is a large platform running recurring, homogeneous binary-labeling tasks. Its workers fall into a small number \(\tau\) of stable cohorts: within a cohort, people have the same accuracy probability and per-task cost, while different cohorts may differ. Standardized contracts and training pipelines make this a plausible regime. The population might have millions of workers but only tens or hundreds of such profiles. A type is \(t=(q_t,c_t)\), and \(\mu_t\) is its share of the population. I focus on the paper’s known-quality, non-strategic selection subproblem; the learning and truthful-payment claims need separate mirrors.

In Section 6.2, under the stated high-quality condition, the paper computes with a minimum-knapsack formulation: workers have signal \(a_i=2q_i-1\), and the selected set must supply at least \(M=6\ln(1/\alpha)\). The continuous problem is: given population scale \(N\), rational type shares \(\mu_t\), qualities \(q_t>2/3\), nonnegative per-task costs \(c_t\), and a required signal threshold \(M\), choose selected mass \(z_t\) from each type to minimize total per-task cost,

\[ \min_{z}\quad N\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}c_tz_t \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad N\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}(2q_t-1)z_t\ge M,\quad 0\le z_t\le\mu_t. \]

Here \(M\) is the paper’s \(6\ln(1/\alpha)\); for a bit-complexity question, it can be supplied as a rational threshold at the desired precision. The instance is feasible when selecting the whole population meets the threshold, as the paper assumes. A solution is the selected mass vector \(z\), or equivalently the fraction \(z_t/\mu_t\) of each cohort commissioned. Individuals still receive an integral assignment; the mass records what fraction of an exchangeable cohort receives it.

This is a linear program with \(\tau\) variables. Sorting types by \(c_t/(2q_t-1)\) gives an optimum by filling the cheapest types first, with at most one type partially selected. So I expect this mirror to be Class A: exact solution in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. In a finite cohort instance, rounding up the partially selected type to the next whole worker preserves the signal constraint and adds at most that worker’s cost. This gives a concrete bridge from the mass solution back to an integral selection.

The mirror covers the minimum-knapsack optimization used in Section 6.2 and, more narrowly, the selection step that the paper’s algorithms call as a subproblem. It does not cover the full unknown-quality bandit problem, the regret bounds, or the ex-post incentive-compatibility results. Those suggest further questions: can the CCB learning analysis be expressed in terms of \(\tau\) and cohort-level observations; can the continuous solution preserve the paper’s accuracy guarantee beyond its stated knapsack surrogate; and what monotonicity or payment conditions would make type-level strategic costs truthful?

The weak point is that Lemma 6.1 is a cited approximation guarantee, not a new complexity theorem, and the paper’s NP-hardness statement is not a numbered anchor. Also, fractional selection is a relaxation of individual procurement, so the accuracy interpretation must retain the population scale and the paper’s signal constraint. The case survives within those limits: the authors explicitly formulate and compute with this minimum-knapsack subproblem, and the mirror changes its unit of choice from individual workers to shares of genuinely homogeneous cohorts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a scale mismatch in the proponent’s anchor, Lemma 6.1. The paper’s central results concern learning from worker-task observations and implementing truthful allocations. It gives no named classification of the AAB problem’s running-time complexity; its theorems bound regret or exploration and establish accuracy and incentive properties. Lemma 6.1 is the closest computational anchor, but it imports a greedy guarantee for a special minimum-knapsack problem.

The proposed cohort scenario is plausible. Its difficulty is that, with fixed target accuracy and fixed type qualities, the amount of signal needed per task stays fixed as the workforce grows. Write \(x_t=N z_t\) for the number of selected workers of type \(t\). The surrogate in Section 6.2 requires \(\sum_t (2q_t-1)x_t\ge M\), where \(M=6\ln(1/\alpha)\). Since \(q_t>2/3\), a feasible solution needs only a bounded number of workers, independent of \(N\). Thus the selected share \(z_t\) tends to zero. Keeping the normalized society measure gives a zero selected mass in the limit; retaining \(x_t\) gives a finite-count optimization over worker cohorts.

One can instead require a positive fraction of workers per task. Then the signal grows with \(N\), and the fixed accuracy constraint eventually stops determining the choice. Maintaining a nontrivial constraint would require the required signal to grow with \(N\), which changes the paper’s fixed per-task accuracy requirement. The other repair—using fractional worker counts—does yield the proposed linear program, but a fractional count cannot be assigned on an individual task while guaranteeing its accuracy constraint. Rounding up preserves feasibility, yet its cost gap need not shrink as the workforce grows, because the selected headcount remains bounded. This is a useful finite-population relaxation; it is weaker evidence for a population-continuum limit.

The paper’s other promising results have the same scale tension. A mirror of Theorem 4.2 could pool observations within \(\tau\) quality-cost cohorts, but labels are still discrete Bernoulli observations and the per-task selected set remains a finite set of workers. Sampling a positive fraction to obtain continuum averages changes the exploration cost and information available each round. For the strategic result, Theorem 5.2’s monotonicity and Corollary 5.3’s incentive guarantee concern each worker’s bid and allocation. A type-mass description records cohort totals; a single worker’s deviation has mass \(1/N\) and disappears in the limit. Preserving pointwise truthfulness would require extending the continuum model with a tagged deviating worker.

This is an honest negative case against the population-limit interpretation, not a decisive refutation of the mirror. A finite-\(N\) type LP is well-posed, tractable, and could give an additive approximation after rounding; the programme explicitly allows that kind of bridge. So I cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. The strongest defensible conclusion is that this paper is a poor fit for ChoCo’s population-continuum complexity agenda, while its most concrete proposed mirror remains plausible under the programme’s finite-instance, type-mass reading.
