The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no qualifying result to mirror. That is more than a numbering problem: the paper presents a statistical model, GLM estimation, hypothesis testing, and an application. It defines no computational decision problem and states no complexity or algorithmic result. Its claim that standard software can estimate the parameters does not supply an anchor for a computational-social-choice mirror.

The proposed foothold—estimating worth and dependence parameters from masses of judge types—has a natural best version. Let a type be a complete response pattern \(y\in\{-1,1\}^{p\binom{J}{2}}\), and let \(\mu_y\) be its population share. One could fit the model from \(\mu\), identify the highest-worth object, or test restrictions on the dependence parameters. But the paper already models the response patterns as multinomial cells with counts \(N_y\), and its parameters describe their joint distribution. Replacing counts by normalized masses makes the same statistical object population-sized; it does not reveal a computational problem the paper studies.

A stronger proposal would ask for the least mass transfer between response-pattern types that changes the highest-worth object after refitting the model. That is a plausible robustness question for a large evaluation panel. It also requires choices the paper does not make: a cost for changing reports, which changes are allowed, and how to define the result when the fitted model is uncertain. Those choices create a new intervention problem around the paper’s estimator; they do not mirror a named result. Defining types by latent judge dispositions would require a further extension, since the paper models dependence among responses and does not give judges type-specific parameters.

So the negative case is strongest for the paper as written: its statistical distribution is already central to its method, and its possible continuous variants lack a computational result to anchor. The universal claim remains less secure. A separate paper on robustness of model-based rankings for a large panel could be worthwhile, but that would be a new computational-social-choice application rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
