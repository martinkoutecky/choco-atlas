The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of \(\tau\) preference types with rational masses \(\rho_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\rho_t=1\), and rational piecewise-constant preference densities over \(q\) divisible cake cells \(E_k\), compute the maximum \(z\) and an allocation attaining it, where \(z=\max_{(B_t)}\min_{t\in T}\nu_t(B_t)/\rho_t\) and \((B_t)_{t\in T}\) partitions the cake.

The model it lives in

Represent each type by its mass \(\rho_t\) and normalized preference measure \(\nu_t\). With \(a_{tk}=\nu_t(E_k)\), choose fractions \(x_{tk}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t x_{tk}=1\) to maximize \(z\), subject to \(\sum_k a_{tk}x_{tk}\ge \rho_t z\) for every type. Each \(x_{tk}\) describes the type-level average allocation; the cake remains divisible and individual bundles can be actual pieces.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the cooperative maxmin value and Proposition 4.6's convergence assertion for finitely many preference types. It leaves aside the Shapley-value calculations, coalition-structure analysis, and computational bounds for general measurable preference measures.

Open questions for a prover

  • For general measurable preference measures supplied through an oracle, what oracle model and complexity bounds allow computation of the maximizing partition and the subgradient PVVs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is continuous type-cohort maxmin cake division. It is a real population continuization: residents are represented by masses of preference types, and fairness is measured per unit of mass. The cake is already divisible in the paper; that is not the continuization claim.

The anchor is Proposition 4.6, proved in this paper. Under complete divisibility (CD) and mutual absolute continuity (MAC), it considers minimizing the convex function \(g\) over the simplex \(\Delta_{m-1}\). Starting from any \(\alpha^0\), it constructs a suitably reduced diminishing step-size sequence \(s'_t\) and updates
\[ u^t=\operatorname{PVV}(\alpha^t),\qquad \alpha^{t+1}=\alpha^t-s'_t(u^t-\bar u^t). \]
The proposition says that this sequence either reaches a minimizer \(\alpha^{t^*}\) of \(g\) at some step, or converges to a minimizer \(\alpha^*\), with \(g(\alpha^t)\to g(\alpha^*)=v(\Gamma,w)\). The proposition is proved here, using standard subgradient results cited from elsewhere.

Here is the mirror problem. Let \(C\) be a nonatomic cake and let \(T\) be a finite set of \(\tau\) preference types. Type \(t\) has population mass \(\rho_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\rho_t=1\), and a common normalized preference measure \(\nu_t\) over the cake. All members of a type are identical in the respects the allocation uses. Choose a partition \((B_t)_{t\in T}\) of the cake to maximize
\[ z_\infty=\max_{(B_t)}\min_{t\in T}\frac{\nu_t(B_t)}{\rho_t}. \]
A solution is the optimal value and a partition attaining it. The ratio is the type cohort’s total value divided by its mass: it records maxmin utility per unit of population mass.

This is a direct high-multiplicity bridge to the paper’s cooperative model. In a finite population of \(n\) people, let type \(t\) contain \(n_t\) people, so \(\rho_t=n_t/n\). The cohort acts as a coalition. Because its members have identical preferences, its joint utility for \(B_t\) is \(\nu_t(B_t)\); under the paper’s cardinality weights, its weight is \(n_t\). Thus the paper’s value is \(\max_{(B_t)}\min_t \nu_t(B_t)/n_t\), and multiplying by \(n\) gives exactly \(z_\infty\). Complete divisibility also lets each finite cohort split its share into equal-value individual bundles. The allocation variable describes the cohort average; individual bundles in finite realizations remain actual cake pieces.

A plausible regime is a city or agency dividing a divisible resource among many residents whose preferences over locations or portions fall into a modest number of recurring, genuinely identical profiles: \(n\) is in the thousands while \(\tau\) is, say, dozens. A type must include the complete preference measure and any other relevant parameters. If most residents have idiosyncratic preferences, then \(\tau\) approaches \(n\), and the high-multiplicity case loses its point.

For a finite computational version, encode each type’s density as rational and piecewise constant over \(q\) cake cells \(E_k\). Let \(x_{tk}\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of cell \(k\) assigned to type \(t\), with \(\sum_t x_{tk}=1\). Writing \(a_{tk}=\nu_t(E_k)\), the mirror is the LP
\[ \max z \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_k a_{tk}x_{tk}\ge \rho_t z\ \ \forall t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tk}=1\ \ \forall k,\qquad x_{tk}\ge0. \]
This computes the underlying maxmin allocation, not merely an encoding of it. I expect Class A for this finite representation: the LP has size polynomial in \(\tau\), \(q\), and the input bit length. That tractability claim belongs to the stated mirror and its LP formulation; Proposition 4.6 itself gives convergence, not a polynomial running-time bound. The paper states no named P/NP-hardness classification for its problem, and its conclusion leaves computational efficiency open.

This covers the paper’s weighted cooperative maxmin allocation objective and the convergence result in Proposition 4.6. It does not continuize the later Shapley-value calculation or claim that fractional outcomes alone make the paper a population-continuous model.

The weak point is that Proposition 4.6 works over finitely many coalitions, and the paper’s own framework already permits coalitions weighted by cardinality. The mirror could therefore look like replacing integer cohort sizes with normalized masses. I think it still qualifies: mass is the per-capita quantity in the limiting question, and the finite high-multiplicity dictionary is exact. But the proposition supplies no new complexity classification or population-size asymptotic theorem; the strongest algorithmic case comes from the finite-cell LP for the mirror itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only strong anchor is Proposition 4.6, but its computational content is narrower than the proposed mirror suggests. It proves convergence of a subgradient sequence over the simplex of \(m\) coalitions, assuming one can compute each PVV exactly. It gives no bit-complexity or iteration bound; the paper itself leaves computational efficiency open.

The cohort mirror does not add a new optimization structure to that result. For type \(t\) with \(n_t\) identical members, the paper’s cardinality-weighted coalition objective is
\[ \max_{(B_t)}\min_t \frac{\nu_t(B_t)}{n_t}. \]
Setting \(\rho_t=n_t/n\) makes the proposed continuous objective exactly \(n\) times this value. Since the cake is nonatomic, each cohort’s bundle can be split among its members into equal-value pieces. So every rational mass instance has an exact finite-cohort realization: the allocation and its fairness trade-offs are already represented by the paper’s weighted coalition model. Holding \(\tau\) fixed while increasing the headcount does not increase the algorithm’s dimension; it changes the coalition weights.

The finite-cell LP is a reasonable way to make that instance computable, but it does not rescue the anchor as a distinctive population-continuization result. The paper already says that piecewise-constant preferences reduce special cases to linear programming. The proposed LP’s size is governed by the cake representation and the number of types; its tractability comes from that finite encoding. For general measurable preferences, a meaningful complexity question would first need a finite representation or an oracle model. Either way, the unresolved computational issue is how to represent and optimize over the cake, not what happens when the population becomes continuous.

A stronger version could ask for bit-complexity bounds in \(\tau,q,L\), or oracle-complexity bounds for computing PVVs. Those could be useful results. But the first is a complexity analysis of the paper’s weighted finite-coalition problem on a standard LP encoding, and the second is driven by the choice of density oracle. Neither supplies a clear new computational question created by continuous population. That makes this a weak case for the programme, though I cannot honestly claim it rules out every worthwhile formulation.
