The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a computational problem on the paper’s own kind of society. In a large labor market, for example, many applicants and employers could share each complete type: preferences over contracts, feasible contracts, and capacity. Let \(\nu_t\) be the fraction of society of type \(t\). When the population is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported types, an outcome can split each type’s mass across bundles while every individual still receives an integral bundle.

One concrete follow-up is High-Multiplicity Tree-Stable Outcome. Given finite sets of types \(T\) and contracts \(Y\), a contract limit \(N\), rational type masses \(\nu\), feasible bundles \(\chi(t)\), and utilities \(u_t(W)\), output rational weights for an outcome \(\mu\) over feasible type-and-bundle pairs. The weights must give type marginal \(\nu\), balance the two sides of every contract, and make the outcome tree-stable: no finite tree of agents drawn from its positive-mass pairs can all strictly improve through feasible new contracts. The bundles themselves remain multisets of at most \(N\) contracts. This is a precise construction problem in a high-multiplicity market; the type is the complete description that makes its agents interchangeable.

The paper’s nearest results are Theorem 1 (proved here), which transfers \(G\)-stable-outcome existence from all corresponding finite matching problems to the Polish-space model; Lemma 4 (cited to Jagadeesan and Vocke, 2024), which says a tree-stable outcome exists when type and contract sets are finite; and Corollary 1 (proved here from Theorem 1 and Lemma 4), which says tree-stable and therefore pairwise-stable outcomes exist for general type and contract spaces. These are existence results, not results that classify a computational problem as polynomial-time, hard, or parameterized. The paper supplies neither an algorithm nor a hardness result for computing an outcome. So the construction problem above is a plausible computational follow-up, but it is not a valid computational anchor from this paper, and the paper does not establish which complexity class it belongs to.

That is the weak point I would concede up front: the paper already makes the population continuous, and its named results are analytical existence theorems. The positive case is only that those results point directly to an algorithmic question about a high-multiplicity matching market. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is decisive but narrow: this paper states no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 transfers existence of \(G\)-stable outcomes to Polish type and contract spaces; Lemma 4 gives tree-stable-outcome existence for finite type and contract sets; Corollary 1 combines them. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a bound for a computational problem. So none is an eligible computational anchor.

The proposed High-Multiplicity Tree-Stable Outcome problem is still a coherent follow-up. Its finite-input version—explicit finite \(T,Y\), rational masses, and a rational outcome over type-and-bundle pairs—fits the paper’s model and preserves integral bundles for individuals. But that makes it a new computational problem inspired by an existence theorem, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper’s proof uses approximation, compactness, subsequences, and measure-continuity arguments; it supplies no effective construction or complexity guarantee. Extending the task to arbitrary Polish spaces would also need an input representation for the spaces, distributions, utilities, and correspondences that the paper does not provide.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case airtight. The paper treats population as a distribution, and its finite-support matching markets make high multiplicity a sensible regime. There is no fundamental modeling objection to asking how to compute a stable outcome there. The defensible conclusion is that this paper has no valid computational anchor under the stated rule. Claiming that no worthwhile continuous computational project could arise from it would go further than the evidence supports.
