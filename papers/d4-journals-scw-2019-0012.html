Why no mirror

The proposed type-mass school-choice extension is a sensible continuous-population question, and its formulation would likely be tractable. However, none of Propositions 7, 8, or 29 states an algorithm, complexity or hardness result, or computational formulation used to compute. The paper therefore fails the mandatory computational-result gate even though a statable mirror exists.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the fractional-stability feasibility analogue of Propositions 7–8 and the generalized non-wastefulness and individual-rationality translation in Proposition 29; it leaves the lottery-decomposition distinctions among ex-ante, robust ex-post, ex-post, and claimwise stability without a computational analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-mass feasibility problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm with weak priorities, unacceptable pairs, and unequal supplies?
  • When does a continuum mass solution correspond to a decomposition into stable finite high-multiplicity matchings?
  • Can the ex-ante, robust ex-post, and claimwise distinctions be defined on type masses without retaining named identities?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow one: this paper admits a natural continuous-population mirror, especially for school-seat allocation, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under the programme’s rules.

A plausible regime is a large school-choice or labour-market population with finitely many interchangeable types. An agent type \(a\) records the agent’s complete preference and priority-relevant profile; an object type \(b\) records the object’s priority rule and capacity class. Let \(\mu_a\) be the fraction of agents of type \(a\), and \(\nu_b\) the fraction of object supply of type \(b\), with both vectors summing to \(1\). Many students may share a preference and priority profile, while many seats may share a school and priority rule.

The decision variable is a mass-matching matrix \(x=(x_{ab})\), where \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of type-\(a\) agents assigned to type-\(b\) objects. It satisfies

\[ \sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a,\qquad \sum_a x_{ab}=\nu_b,\qquad x_{ab}\ge 0. \]

This is population continuization, not merely fractionalizing an outcome: in an atomless population, \(x_{ab}\) can be implemented by partitioning the mass of type \(a\), with every individual receiving one integral object in each realization. The fractions describe the population aggregate or lottery, not divisible objects assigned to named individuals.

The strongest candidate question is:

Continuum Fractional-Stability Matching. Given rational type masses \(\mu,\nu\), strict preference orders \(\succ_a\) over object types, and strict priority orders \(\succ_b\) over agent types, find a feasible \(x\) satisfying, for every \(a,b\),

\[ \frac{x_{ab}}{\mu_a} +\sum_{b'\succ_a b}\frac{x_{ab'}}{\mu_a} +\sum_{a'\succ_b a}\frac{x_{a'b}}{\nu_b} \ge 1. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \sum_{b'\succ_a b}\frac{x_{ab'}}{\mu_a} \ge \sum_{a'\prec_b a}\frac{x_{a'b}}{\nu_b}. \]

The left side is the fraction of type-\(a\) agents receiving objects better than \(b\); the right side is the fraction of type-\(b\) supply assigned to lower-priority agent types. This is exactly the paper’s fractional-stability condition translated from named agents and objects to type masses.

The natural expectation is Class A. For explicitly represented type sets, this is a rational linear program with \(O(|A||B|)\) variables and constraints. Rational masses can be expanded into finite high-multiplicity instances, while repeated finite profiles can be compressed back into \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). A minimum-cost variant with type-dependent assignment costs would also remain an LP. The genuinely interesting follow-up would be whether the paper’s ex-post decomposition interpretation survives this atomless setting.

The paper’s closest named structural support is Proposition 8, proved in the paper using the cited polyhedral results of Vande Vate and Rothblum: every fractionally stable random matching in the strict, equal-size, fully acceptable model is ex-post stable, because the fractional-stability polytope has deterministic stable matchings as its extreme points. Together with Proposition 7, this gives equivalence between fractional and ex-post stability. Proposition 29 gives the corresponding generalized statement: a generalized random matching is fractionally weakly stable exactly when its associated matching is fractionally stable and respects non-wastefulness. These results make the mass-transport mirror recognisably the authors’ problem rather than an unrelated allocation model.

There is, however, no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary in the paper asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, W[1]-hardness, approximation, or any other computational classification. The references to deferred acceptance and linear programming are either unnumbered observations or results attributed to other papers. Thus Proposition 8, Proposition 29, and the surrounding taxonomy cannot honestly be presented as computational anchors. Formally, the paper has zero qualifying anchors, so there is no anchor-indexed list of continuous problems satisfying the programme’s mandatory standard.

The mirror is still substantively plausible. The paper’s finite \(p(i,o)\) matrix makes lotteries over named individuals primitive; the proposed model instead makes the society itself a distribution over interchangeable agent and object types. That distinction is real, and school-seat cohorts with common priority tiers provide a credible high-multiplicity regime. It is not a collision merely because the paper uses fractional matchings.

The weakest point is that the resulting computational story is modest. Stable matching is already polynomial in the discrete model, so this mirror does not demonstrate that discrete hardness dissolves. If priorities are individualized tie-break orders, the number of types may grow with the population and the high-multiplicity interpretation weakens. Moreover, a sceptical referee could regard \(x\) as only a compressed representation of the paper’s existing fractional matrix unless the atomless integral implementation and the finite-to-continuous dictionary are made explicit.

So I would argue for a natural, credible continuous-population reformulation, expected to be tractable, but not for a strong ChoCo positive case: the decisive computational anchor is absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is that this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results are definitions, logical implications, counterexamples, convexity statements, and polyhedral characterizations of stability notions. Proposition 8 does not say how to solve an input problem; Proposition 29 does not classify an algorithmic task; the deferred-acceptance and LP remarks are background or citations. Thus a continuous optimization problem built from this paper would be a new problem inspired by the model, not a computational mirror of a result in the paper. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is enough to reject the paper.

The proposed school-choice construction is nevertheless a sensible high-multiplicity model. Its mass matrix \(x_{ab}\) is a legitimate population-level object, and the normalized inequalities are a reasonable aggregate analogue of the paper’s fractional-stability inequalities. But that does not rescue the anchors.

Proposition 8 concerns an equivalence between two descriptions of a finite random-assignment polytope: fractional stability and convex decomposability into deterministic stable matchings. It is not an algorithmic statement. The proposed question “find a feasible \(x\)” adds an optimization problem absent from Proposition 8. If \(x\) represents population shares, then it is a deterministic continuum assignment implemented by partitioning each type’s mass; it is not the paper’s lottery over named agents. If \(x\) represents each individual’s lottery, then it is simply a compressed version of the paper’s existing fractional matrix. Preserving the paper’s ex-post notion would require a probability law over complete measurable matchings, which reintroduces outcome-space randomization rather than giving a population-only continuization.

There is also a more structural mismatch. To have many genuinely indistinguishable agents of one type, an object must treat them identically, so the strict individual priorities of Proposition 8 must be replaced by priority ties between type members. The paper explicitly shows that the corresponding weak-priority theory behaves differently: Proposition 17 gives a fractionally weakly stable random matching that is not ex-post weakly stable. One can certainly formulate the weak-priority continuum problem, but it is then a new model and a new theorem, not a computational mirror of Proposition 8.

Proposition 29 is even less transferable. Its associated-instance construction uses a personal null object \(\phi_i\) for every agent and a personal dummy agent \(d_j\) for every object. Those identities are exactly what encode individual rationality and non-wastefulness. In a continuum, preserving the construction requires a continuum of personal null and dummy types, destroying the finite type space. Collapsing them into common null and dummy types preserves finite multiplicity only by changing the construction and losing the equivalence. Thus Proposition 29 supplies no genuine continuum computational object; it is a finite identity-preserving gadget.

The same problem affects the surrounding taxonomy. Ex-ante stability, robust ex-post stability, and the distinction between different decompositions are properties of lotteries over assignments of named agents to named objects. A type-level mass matrix does not determine those decompositions. One must either retain individual identities, defeating high multiplicity, or quotient them away, changing the very stability concepts being classified. In an atomless population, every feasible mass flow can already be realized by partitioning each type into subpopulations receiving integral objects, so the paper’s random-versus-deterministic distinctions no longer have a canonical interpretation.

The honest concession is that a continuous school-seat matching problem could be worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is therefore not that no sensible mass model exists. It is that this paper contributes no named computational result for such a model: the strongest proposed mirror is a new high-multiplicity stable-matching project, while the paper itself is an axiomatic taxonomy of fractional outcomes. That is insufficient for a ChoCo mirror, and the proponent’s anchors do not survive the programme’s computational gate.
