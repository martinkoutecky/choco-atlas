The continuous mirror question

For rational \(\mu,a,b,\gamma\), with \(0\le\gamma<1/2\) satisfying Theorem 4.2’s parameter bound, consider a unit-mass population with truthful mass \(1-\gamma\) and adversarial mass \(\gamma\). Let \(Q_T\in[0,1-\gamma]\) be the truthful mass reporting \(H\); nature chooses any joint law of \((\omega,Q_T)\) with \(\Pr[\omega=1]=\mu\), \(\mathbb{E}[Q_T\mid\omega=1]=(1-\gamma)a\), and \(\mathbb{E}[Q_T\mid\omega=0]=(1-\gamma)b\). After observing \((\omega,Q_T)\), adversaries choose \(Q_A\in[0,\gamma]\); the rule sees \(Q=Q_T+Q_A\) and outputs \(f(Q)\in[0,1]\). The benchmark knows nature’s law, observes \(Q_T\), and chooses the L1-optimal decision. Determine the minimax rule and worst-case regret over all such laws and adversarial strategies, including whether \(f(q)=0\) for \(q\le\gamma\), \(f(q)=(q-\gamma)/(1-2\gamma)\) for \(\gamma<q<1-\gamma\), and \(f(q)=1\) for \(q\ge1-\gamma\) remains optimal with the paper’s regret value.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass cohort has truthful mass \(1-\gamma\) and adversarial mass \(\gamma\). A joint law over the state and truthful high-report mass represents arbitrary correlations among truthful signals; adversaries choose high-report mass after observing the state and truthful reports. The aggregator maps total high-report mass to a forecast, while the benchmark uses the information law and truthful report mass to make its L1-optimal decision.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.2’s adversarial L1 result and Theorem 4.6’s non-adversarial L2 approximation result. It leaves the adversarial L2 result, the multi-state extension, the general-model negative results, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the truncated-mean mass rule minimize worst-case L1 regret over every admissible law on the full interval of truthful report masses, with the same value as Theorem 4.2?
  • Can the paper’s moment and convexity argument establish the result uniformly for fractional masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s adversarial aggregation problem. Imagine a very large cohort of otherwise interchangeable forecasters or model replicas: the truthful members share the same prior and signal accuracy, while a fraction \(\gamma\) are compromised and can coordinate their reports. Their private signals may be arbitrarily correlated, as in the paper. The cohort could contain millions of agents but only two relevant role types, truthful and adversarial. An individual’s realized \(L\) or \(H\) report is not a separate persistent type.

The continuous instance is \(\mu,a,b,\gamma\), where \(\mu=\Pr[\omega=1]\), \(a=\Pr[H\mid\omega=1]\), \(b=\Pr[H\mid\omega=0]\), and \(\gamma\) is the adversarial mass. Let \(q_T\in[0,1-\gamma]\) be the mass of truthful experts reporting \(H\), and \(q_A\in[0,\gamma]\) the adversarial mass reporting \(H\). The observed mass is \(q=q_T+q_A\). Nature may choose any joint law of \((\omega,q_T)\) with prior \(\mu\) and conditional means \(\mathbb E[q_T\mid\omega=1]=(1-\gamma)a\) and \(\mathbb E[q_T\mid\omega=0]=(1-\gamma)b\); this leaves signal correlations unrestricted. The adversaries may choose \(q_A\) after observing the state and truthful reports. The aggregator sees \(q\) and outputs a forecast \(f(q)\in[0,1]\). The benchmark knows the information structure and observes \(q_T\), then takes an L1-optimal decision. The question is to find \(f\) minimizing worst-case expected regret against that benchmark.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper, with its proof in Appendix B.1. It says that for \(k=\gamma n\), when
\[ \gamma\le \min\left\{ \frac{a}{1+a}, \frac{1-b}{2-b}, \frac{-\bar\mu b+\mu a}{\mu-\bar\mu b+\mu a}, \frac{\bar\mu(1-b)-\mu(1-a)} {(1-b)\bar\mu+\bar\mu-\mu(1-a)} \right\},\qquad \bar\mu=1-\mu, \]
the optimal L1 aggregator is the \(k\)-truncated mean. On a report count \(x\), it outputs \(0\) for \(x\le k\), \(1\) for \(x\ge n-k\), and \((x-k)/(n-2k)\) between those thresholds. Its worst-case regret is
\[ \frac{(1-\gamma)\bigl(1-(1-\mu)(1-b)-\mu a\bigr)}{1-2\gamma}. \]
This is an exact optimizer for a minimax decision-rule problem, and its closed form gives a direct polynomial-time computation from the rational parameters.

For the continuous mirror, the prover would receive rational \(\mu,a,b,\gamma\) satisfying the same bound and the mass-based model above, and must determine the minimax rule and value. The natural conjectured answer is
\[ f_\gamma(q)= \begin{cases} 0 & q\le\gamma,\\ (q-\gamma)/(1-2\gamma) & \gamma<q<1-\gamma,\\ 1 & q\ge1-\gamma, \end{cases} \]
with the same regret expression. This is exactly the finite formula after converting report counts to fractions. I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A): the finite proof uses moment constraints and convexity, and the continuous instance has the analogous mass constraints. The required result is to establish that those arguments still control every admissible distribution over fractional masses, rather than only distributions supported on an integer count grid.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 4.6, also proved here, though its proof invokes the cited FPTAS in Lemma B.3. It states that in the non-adversarial L2 setting, an algorithm costing \(O(1/\epsilon)\) finds an \(\epsilon\)-optimal aggregator: its maximum regret over the allowed information structures is at most the optimal regret plus \(\epsilon\). The proof reduces the finite problem first to structures with at most two report counts conditional on each state, then to extreme counts \(0,1,n-1,n\).

Its continuous counterpart has all experts truthful. Nature chooses any joint law of \((\omega,q)\) with prior \(\mu\), \(\mathbb E[q\mid\omega=1]=a\), and \(\mathbb E[q\mid\omega=0]=b\). Here \(q\in[0,1]\) is the mass reporting \(H\); the benchmark observes \(q\) and uses the posterior \(\eta(q)=\Pr[\omega=1\mid q]\). Given rational \(\mu,a,b\) and \(\epsilon>0\), the task is to return a finite, rational piecewise-affine rule \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) whose worst-case L2 regret, \(\mathbb E[(f(q)-\omega)^2-(\eta(q)-\omega)^2]\), is within \(\epsilon\) of the minimax value. I expect this to be tractable as well, through a continuous moment reduction and convex optimization. Proving that a concise finite rule suffices is part of the mirror’s computational question.

These anchors cover robust binary forecast aggregation and its non-adversarial L2 counterpart; they do not mirror the paper’s multi-state extension or its general-model negative results. The supplied paper has no named NP-hardness or other hardness-classification result, so I would not manufacture one. Its positive anchors are exact optimization and an approximation algorithm.

The main weakness is that the paper already parameterizes its finite model by \(\gamma=k/n\), and the lead formula is scale-invariant. The continuous answer may turn out to be almost immediate from the finite theorem. Also, the theorem’s finite proof does not by itself cover arbitrary laws over fractional \(q\); extending the moment and extremal-support arguments is real work. That weakness does not erase the mirror: the continuous model admits mass profiles unavailable on any one finite count grid, and equality of the two answers would be a substantive discrete-to-continuous result. The paper’s existing symmetry and proportion-based analysis is supporting high-multiplicity evidence, not a novelty collision.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would concede that the paper has a plausible high multiplicity regime: a large cohort of interchangeable forecasters or model replicas, with a fraction compromised. The rule is anonymous, and the truthful and adversarial roles give a natural small type space. Dismissing the mirror because experts have individual identities would miss the model.

For Theorem 4.2, the strongest mirror is the proponent’s mass model: truthful report mass \(q_T\), adversarial mass \(q_A\), and a rule \(f(q_T+q_A)\), with nature ranging over laws for \(q_T\) that satisfy the stated moment constraints. That is a coherent continuous-population question, and the authors could recognize it as theirs. The case against is about its computational yield. The theorem’s optimizer is already a formula in the normalized ratio \(\gamma=k/n\) and three scalar parameters. Its proof reduces the worst case to a few support points; in the mass formulation, the same moment-and-convexity structure appears on an interval. There is no growing type support, pricing problem, or instance-size parameter for continuous optimization to tame. This could be a useful formal extension, but it looks like a short extremal-distribution result attached to an already normalized policy-design theorem, rather than a new complexity landscape.

For Theorem 4.6, the proponent improves on a literal count-to-mass substitution by letting nature choose arbitrary laws of the report fraction \(q\), with the benchmark using the posterior given \(q\). Even on that stronger formulation, the continuum seems to degenerate. The paper’s reduction places the relevant finite report counts at \(0,1,n-1,n\); after normalization they are \(0,1/n,1-1/n,1\), which collapse to the endpoints \(0\) and \(1\). In the continuous version, the same convexity and moment argument appears to leave only those endpoint masses relevant to the worst case. Then the rule’s interior values do no work: the remaining optimization concerns \(f(0)\) and \(f(1)\), with the benchmark posteriors fixed by the moments. That is a small finite-dimensional decision problem, not a substantive use of continuous-optimization machinery.

So the negative case is strongest against prioritizing this paper for ChoCo: one anchor appears to add little computational structure, and the other likely loses its continuum in the limit. It is weaker as a universal rejection. Theorem 4.2’s mass formulation is genuinely well posed, and proving the continuous identity could count as a useful bridge result under the programme’s standards. I do not see an honest argument that no scenario could make that result worthwhile; the case against is that its likely payoff is modest, not that the mirror fails to exist.
