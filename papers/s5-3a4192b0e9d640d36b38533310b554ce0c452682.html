What the mirror covers

The proposal targets Theorem 2’s solid single-interval setting; it does not retain the theorem’s physical cut guarantee or individual incentive notion, and it leaves Corollary 4’s piecewise-uniform extension aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For a cake \(C=[0,1)\), \(n\) players, and solid single-interval valuation types \(C_i=[\alpha_i,\beta_i)\), it says the modified Asano–Umeda mechanism runs in \(O(n^3)\) time and returns a partition \(A_N\) with \(A_i\subseteq C_i\) for every player, such that the allocation is envy-free and truthful and uses at most \(2n-2\) cuts. The theorem is algorithmic rather than a hardness result; the paper has no numbered hardness theorem for this mechanism problem. The cited PPAD result concerns much more general valuations, so it is not an anchor for this mirror.

My proposed mirror is Mass-Truthful Minimum-Macrocut Cake Allocation. The continuous object is the *population*: let \(T\) be a finite set of rational interval types \(I_t=[\alpha_t,\beta_t)\), and let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over them with support size \(\tau\). A plausible regime is a very large population of households with repeated geographic service intervals along a corridor: perhaps millions of households but only hundreds of distinct intervals. Each type values a location uniformly inside its interval and zero outside.

The mass allocation records each type’s scaled average bundle. For a finite population of size \(n\), take \(q_t(x)\) to be \(n\) times the probability that a uniformly chosen type-\(t\) agent receives location \(x\). This normalization keeps per-capita utilities nonzero in the limit. A mechanism \(F\) maps each report distribution \(\nu\) to nonnegative, piecewise-constant densities \(q^\nu_r(x)\) for reports \(r\in T\), with
\[ \sum_{r\in T}\nu_r q^\nu_r(x)=1 \quad\text{for almost every }x\in[0,1). \]
Type \(t\)’s scaled utility from the bundle associated with report \(r\) is
\[ u_t(q^\nu_r)=\int_{I_t}q^\nu_r(x)\,dx. \]

Given \(T\) and \(\mu\), the task is to find a succinct rule \(F\), including its outputs for nearby report distributions, that minimizes the number of common breakpoints in the vector \(q^\mu\). Require type-level envy-freeness,
\[ u_t(q^\mu_t)\ge u_t(q^\mu_s) \]
for every populated \(t,s\), and vanishing-mass truthfulness: for every true type \(t\) and alternative report \(s\),
\[ u_t(q^\mu_t)\ge \limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow0} u_t\!\left(q^{\,\mu-\epsilon e_t+\epsilon e_s}_s\right). \]
A solution is the finite breakpoint representation and rule satisfying these conditions, with running time measured in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). This is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocation and strategic-reporting problem; the cake remains the same outcome space.

I would tentatively expect this problem to be Class A: once the interval endpoints are sorted, the type utilities and mass-feasibility constraints look finite-dimensional and linear. The hard part is whether truthfulness across nearby population distributions and the minimum-breakpoint objective can be handled efficiently. Natural follow-up questions are whether an optimum uses \(O(\tau)\) breakpoints, whether such a mass mechanism can be lifted to exact deterministic mechanisms for large finite replications, and whether the limiting truthfulness condition is sufficient for that lift.

The main weakness is substantial: the proposed mass object preserves envy and truthfulness in an *ex-ante, type-average limit*, and counts macro breakpoints in average allocations. Theorem 2 guarantees pointwise envy-freeness and truthfulness for deterministic individual bundles, with a physical cut bound. An author might accept this as a high-multiplicity relaxation of their problem, but could reasonably object that it changes precisely what their small-cut guarantee measures. The case rests on that relaxation being a useful, recognizable version of their question—not on the cake already being divisible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives an \(O(n^3)\) algorithm, a cut bound, and envy-freeness and truthfulness. I also can’t argue that interval types lack plausible multiplicity; many households could share the same acceptable service interval. So the negative case turns on what the theorem’s cut bound and incentive guarantee mean after aggregation.

Take the simplest replication: every player has valuation interval \([0,1)\). The mechanism gives each of \(n\) players an interval of length \(1/n\), using \(n-1\) cuts. But the proposed aggregate \(q_t\), which scales the average allocation density by \(n\), is just \(1\) everywhere. It has no breakpoints. Minimizing breakpoints of \(q_t\) therefore does not mirror the paper’s cut objective: aggregation erases the very cuts the theorem counts. It also records average bundles, while the theorem guarantees truthfulness for each named player.

A stronger mirror could track a distribution of individual bundles by type, use per-capita cut rates, and test truthfulness with a negligible deviator. That is a more faithful proposal, but it needs additional structure beyond the type-mass allocation in the case for. With fixed cake supply, a nonatomic individual’s physical share vanishes; scaling supply or using lotteries can preserve per-capita utility, as the programme allows, but then the model must define how cuts and individual incentives work in that scaled or randomized allocation. Those choices may yield a worthwhile problem, yet they are not specified by the paper’s theorem.

The proposed finite type set also needs care. Players in the paper can misreport any interval, including one absent from the population. Restricting deviations to \(s\in T\) weakens truthfulness. A full mirror must provide outcomes for off-support reports and a succinct way to represent the mechanism across nearby populations. That could be done, but it makes the computational object a family of menus over interval reports, rather than simply an algorithm on a distribution of the observed types.

These points weaken the particular macrocut proposal; they do not establish that every continuous mirror fails. A per-capita cut criterion and a carefully defined individual incentive model could be a credible research question. My negative case is therefore weak against the universal claim: the strongest concern is that aggregation changes the theorem’s central guarantee, not that no worthwhile high-multiplicity version can be formulated.
