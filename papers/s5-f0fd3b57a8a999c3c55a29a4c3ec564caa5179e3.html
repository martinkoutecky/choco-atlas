The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: this paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered results are empirical comparisons in Tables 2 and 5. For example, Table 2 finds that exchange-resistance theory best fits predictions based on structural potential for exclusion, while Table 5 finds that expected value theory best fits when predictions use observed exclusions. Those findings are not complexity results, and I cannot present them as the required anchors.

A possible *future* mirror would study many repeated exchange networks whose participants share complete role types: the same position, network alternatives, exchange quota, pool size, and information conditions. Mass would be the fraction of participants in each type. Given a distribution over complete exchange patterns, the task could be to compute each type’s expected exclusion and the corresponding predicted earnings under the paper’s expected-value model. That is a concrete continuous-population prediction problem, and repeated organizations could make its regime plausible. But it is an extension of the paper’s empirical question, not a continuous form of a named computational result in the paper. The absence of a qualifying anchor is the decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the gap between what this paper establishes and what ChoCo studies. The paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its central findings are empirical comparisons: Table 2 favors exchange-resistance theory when predictions use structural exclusion potential; Table 5 favors expected value theory when they use observed exclusion. Those findings compare predictions with earnings in eight small experimental networks. They do not pose an algorithmic problem over a population.

The proposed repeated-network scenario does not close that gap. If the input already gives a distribution over complete exchange patterns, expected exclusions and earnings are weighted averages. Replicating identical finite networks makes those averages more precise, but leaves the calculation for each network unchanged. If the task is instead to generate the patterns, the hard part is the network’s combinatorial structure. A distribution over role types does not specify who can exchange with whom or which exchanges compete. Making types complete enough to preserve those relationships can require encoding much of the network itself, so the type support grows with the structure rather than giving a useful high-multiplicity representation.

A stronger version could use a continuum network with a type-to-type interaction kernel, then ask for expected exclusions or earnings under specified bargaining rules. That may be a worthwhile new model. But it adds the interaction structure and bargaining assumptions that this paper tests empirically; it is not a continuous formulation of a computational result in the paper. If those additions are removed, the proposed computation is just averaging supplied patterns. If they are added, the resulting algorithmic question needs its own motivation and formulation.

So the case against is strongest on scope, not impossibility: the paper supplies no computational anchor, and the suggested mirror either leaves the paper’s finite-network computation intact or changes the model substantially. The remaining weakness is that a well-motivated continuum network model could still be valuable as a separate research direction; this paper alone gives little basis for judging that broader possibility.
