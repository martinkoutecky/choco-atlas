The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is narrow: the paper’s full-information one-sided matching results for Max-of-Sum. The paper proves distortion bounds, not NP-hardness or a complexity-class separation, so this supports a continuous algorithmic question rather than a new hardness claim.

Take a large matching market with many agents and facility slots but few distinct observable agent types: for example, applicants with a small number of shared location or commute profiles matched to many positions at repeated sites. Groups could be sizeable districts or departments whose labels the allocator does not receive. The regime is \(n=m\to\infty\), with the number \(\tau\) of observable types much smaller than \(n\), a fixed or moderate number \(k\) of groups, and facility supplies scaled with population. A type is a complete description of an agent’s relevant observable characteristics; the latent group label is part of the true description but hidden from the allocator, as group membership is in the paper. The input gives the type masses and facility capacities, not the group partition.

Here is the continuous model. There is a finite metric over agent types \(T\) and facility types \(F\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), rational facility capacities \(q_f\) with \(\sum_f q_f=1\), an affinity \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), and \(k\) hidden groups. An underlying society is an atomless population of unit mass with those observed type proportions and some hidden assignment of its members to groups. A feasible matching assigns each individual to one facility, with mass \(q_f\) assigned to facility type \(f\). Thus the action is an integral assignment at the individual level, represented by splitting type mass among facilities; the capacities and mass split are the co-modifications that make one-to-one matching meaningful at continuum scale.

For an assignment \(a\), let \(\ell_a(z)\) be an individual’s distance to their assigned facility. The continuous analogue of individual cost for someone in group \(g\) is
\[ c_a(z)=\ell_a(z)+\alpha\frac{1}{\mu(g)}\int_g \ell_a\,d\mu. \]
This is the limit of the paper’s average over the other group members. Integrating costs over a group gives \((1+\alpha)\int_g\ell_a\,d\mu\), so the continuous Max-of-Sum objective is
\[ C_{\mathcal G}(a)=(1+\alpha)\max_{g\in\mathcal G}\int_g\ell_a\,d\mu. \]
The benchmark knows the group partition and chooses the best feasible matching; the allocator does not.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2.3, proved in this paper: for the Max-of-Sum objective, every full-information one-sided matching algorithm has distortion at least \(k\). Its continuous question is Continuous Full-Information Max-of-Sum Matching Lower Bound. Given the class of metric-population inputs above and an allocation rule that sees the metric, observed type masses, and facility capacities but not group membership, show that its worst-case distortion is at least \(k\); a solution is a lower-bound construction that applies to every such rule.

The same witness works with mass. Put all population mass at location \(0\), put facility capacity \(1-1/k\) at \(0\) and \(1/k\) at \(1\), and let the hidden groups each have mass \(1/k\). Whatever matching the rule outputs, a hidden group can consist of the mass assigned to the distant facility. Its group distance integral is \(1/k\). With that partition fixed, the group-aware benchmark can spread the distant assignment evenly, assigning mass \(1/k^2\) to it in each group. Its maximum group distance integral is \(1/k^2\), giving ratio \(k\).

The other anchor is Theorem 2.2, also proved here: any \(\rho\)-approximation for the total-distance SUM problem achieves distortion at most \(\rho k\) for Max-of-Sum. Its continuous question is Continuous SUM-to-Max-of-Sum Matching Guarantee. Given the metric support, population masses, facility capacities, \(k\), and \(\alpha\), construct a matching rule based on a \(\rho\)-approximation to minimum total distance and prove that its distortion against every hidden group partition is at most \(\rho k\). A solution is the measurable assignment together with the bound. The proof carries over: the sum of group costs is \((1+\alpha)\) times total distance, so the worst group has at least a \(1/k\) share of any benchmark’s total distance. The allocator’s total distance is at most \(\rho\) times the benchmark’s, yielding the bound.

Together, these questions give an exact continuous minimax result: \(D_k=k\) for full-information one-sided matching. Theorem 2.3 supplies the lower bound; Theorem 2.2 supplies the upper bound with \(\rho=1\). The paper notes that discrete one-sided matching solves SUM in polynomial time. In the mirror, minimum-total-distance matching is a rational transportation LP over the \(\tau\) observed types and facility types, solvable in time polynomial in the input dimensions and bit length. I therefore expect the mirror to be tractable in the programme’s Class A sense; the lower bound is informational, not computational hardness.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it retains metric one-to-one assignment, the hidden group structure, the affinity cost, and the same group-fair objective. It covers only their full-information one-sided matching results for Max-of-Sum—not clustering, ordinal information, or Sum-of-Max. It does not continuize outcomes as its goal; mass-scaling facility supplies is needed to retain matching in a continuous population.

The weakest point is that this paper studies distortion under limited information, rather than computational complexity, and the paper’s theorems are not complexity classifications. The mirror is therefore strongest as a continuous computational formulation of its matching guarantees, not as evidence that the paper itself contains a missing complexity result. A further question is whether the corresponding ordinal-information mass model has the paper’s \(2k+1\) lower bound, and how the minimax value changes for Sum-of-Max.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly make the universal negative airtight: the mass version of Theorem 2.3 is sound. With population mass \(1\) at location \(0\), facility capacity \(1/k\) at location \(1\), and \(k\) hidden groups of mass \(1/k\), the allocator’s distant mass can form one group, while a group-aware benchmark spreads it evenly. The ratio is \(k\). Theorem 2.2’s sum-to-maximum argument also carries over. Changing the type space or stating matching as a mass allocation does not defeat those mirrors.

The strongest case against is about fit with ChoCo’s target. The paper’s named theorems bound distortion under information constraints. Its lower bounds hold even for algorithms with unlimited computation; its upper bounds assume a \(\rho\)-approximation to SUM or MAX. The paper does not classify the computational complexity of finding an assignment or a rule. Its central results measure what an algorithm can guarantee without group information, not the computational cost of achieving that guarantee.

That distinction matters for the proposed anchors. In the continuous one-sided matching model, minimum total distance over finitely many types and facility capacities is a transportation LP. The \(k\) factor in Theorem 2.2 then follows from the same group-sum inequality as in the finite proof. Theorem 2.3 supplies a valid information-theoretic lower bound, but it says nothing about computational hardness or a new algorithmic bottleneck. Computing an optimal distortion-minimizing rule could be a new question, but it would be a separate mechanism-design problem, not the continuous counterpart of either theorem.

The same limitation applies to the ordinal and Sum-of-Max bounds mentioned in the case for. Their bounds concern information loss, and the lower bounds likewise do not depend on limited computational resources. A continuous formulation may faithfully reproduce them, but faithful reproduction alone does not reveal the complexity landscape ChoCo is set up to chart.

I would therefore argue against spending ChoCo’s effort on these anchors: they give good continuous distortion questions, but weak continuous computational-complexity questions. The case is narrower than the requested universal claim, though. If ChoCo treats algorithmic guarantees under information constraints as in scope, this paper has a coherent mirror, and the negative case fails.
