The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(X\) of alternatives, a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rankings of \(X\), an edge budget \(k\), and a threshold \(q\), does there exist an undirected graph \(H\) on \(X\) with at most \(k\) edges such that \(\sum_{\sigma:\,\sigma\text{ is a DFS-ordering of }H}\mu_\sigma\ge q\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types on \(X\). Choose a graph \(H\) with \(|E(H)|\le k\) to maximize the mass \(\sum_{\sigma:\,\sigma\text{ is a DFS-ordering of }H}\mu_\sigma\).

The objection that survived

At \(q=1\), the masses are irrelevant, and Theorem 3.5 establishes no hardness result for any fixed \(q<1\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.5 through its exact \(q=1\) case and extends it to weighted partial support; it leaves the other edge-bounded and degree-bounded hardness results and the DFS-tree recognition algorithm outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-hardness persist for any fixed threshold \(q<1\), or for a fixed positive slack from full coverage?
  • Can the maximum covered mass be approximated, or solved efficiently with parameters such as \(|X|\), \(\tau\), or \(k\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is Mass-Threshold DFS Support. Consider a large population choosing among a fixed set \(X\) of alternatives. Each type is a complete ranking \(\sigma\) of \(X\), and \(\mu_\sigma\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. Given an edge budget \(k\) and a target coverage \(q\), ask whether there is a graph \(H\) on \(X\), with at most \(k\) edges, such that the total mass of rankings that \(H\) can generate by DFS is at least \(q\): \(\sum_{\sigma\text{ is a DFS-ordering of }H}\mu_\sigma\ge q\). A solution is the graph \(H\); equivalently, the optimization version chooses an edge-bounded graph maximizing that mass. The input lists only the \(\tau\) positive-mass ranking types and their binary-rational masses.

A plausible regime is a large user population facing a modest shared catalogue—for example, training pathways or products linked by similarity or compatibility. Users with the same role and goals may share a full ranking; some may explore by following one branch deeply before backtracking. The graph is the proposed common structure behind those exploration-generated preferences. Here \(N\), the number of people, can be much larger than \(\tau\), the number of distinct ranking types. This is a stylized behavioral scenario, not a claim that DFS describes preference formation generally. It does, however, follow the paper’s own motivation of modeling preferences as sequential exploration of connected alternatives.

My anchor is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper: Edge-Bounded-DFS-SP is NP-hard. In full, the decision problem takes a vertex set \(V\), a set \(\Pi\) of orderings of \(V\), and an integer \(k\), and asks whether some graph on \(V\) with at most \(k\) edges admits every ordering in \(\Pi\) as a DFS-ordering. The authors prove hardness by reducing from Vertex Cover on 3-regular graphs; they also state that the problems considered in the section lie in NP. Thus this decision problem is NP-complete.

The connection is exact at \(q=1\): give each ordering in \(\Pi\) equal positive mass. Then Mass-Threshold DFS Support is yes exactly when the instance of Edge-Bounded-DFS-SP is yes. The hardness transfers, so the problem as stated—where \(q\) is part of the input and may equal \(1\)—is expected to be Class B. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the alternatives and their support graph, rather than in how many people hold each ranking; that is a reason to expect the hardness to survive a high-multiplicity representation.

This version also asks a population-weighted question the paper does not ask: how much of society can one sparse graph explain? Natural follow-ups are whether hardness persists for a fixed positive tolerance \(1-q\), whether the reduction can establish a coverage gap, and whether the maximum-coverage objective admits approximation or fixed-parameter algorithms in \(m=|X|\), \(\tau\), or \(k\). When masses are empirical fractions \(n_\sigma/N\), the threshold question also has a direct finite-population reading: can one graph explain at least \(qN\) people?

The weakest point is that at \(q=1\), masses are irrelevant: only the support of \(\mu\) matters, so that boundary case is the paper’s problem written in distribution notation. The genuinely mass-sensitive part is \(q<1\), and Theorem 3.5 does not establish its complexity for any fixed slack. The positive case therefore rests on the authors accepting weighted partial explanation as a natural population-level extension of their exact recognition problem. I would present that extension as plausible, while keeping the hardness claim confined to the stated family that includes the exact \(q=1\) case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.5 is a real computational anchor: Edge-Bounded-DFS-SP is NP-hard, and the paper says the section’s problems lie in NP, so the decision problem is NP-complete. The strongest objection is that the theorem concerns a *set* of distinct rankings. Its truth does not depend on how many people have each ranking.

That makes the proposed mirror exact but mass-blind at \(q=1\). Give every ranking in \(\Pi\) positive mass and require the graph to explain all the mass: the answer is precisely the paper’s answer. Repeating a ranking a million times changes nothing; adding a single new ranking can change the answer even if its mass is arbitrarily small. The theorem therefore gives no population-sensitive result. Its support-only version is already the discrete recognition problem in compressed form.

The proposed \(q<1\) version is the best repair. It asks how much of society one sparse graph can explain, and that is a plausible question for a large population with recurring ranking types. But it changes the paper’s universal recognition task into weighted partial fit. The Vertex Cover reduction in Theorem 3.5 establishes the exact \(q=1\) case; it gives no fixed-slack or coverage-gap result. That limits what this anchor supports for the genuinely mass-sensitive version. It does not show that version is uninteresting.

The same support-versus-mass issue applies to the paper’s other exact recognition results, including DFS-tree recognition: their constraints concern whether each supplied ranking is admitted, so prevalence does not affect feasibility. A continuous exact mirror is formally easy to define, but the population distribution contributes no substance beyond its support.

That is the strongest negative case, and it does not defeat the better weighted mirror. The authors could reasonably recognize “what fraction of society admits a common sparse DFS support?” as their problem. The scenario is plausible, and the objective makes population mass matter. I would therefore call the case against an exact, mass-blind mirror strong, but the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists weak. The proposed weighted partial-support question remains a credible survivor, even though Theorem 3.5 alone does not establish its complexity away from the exact boundary.
