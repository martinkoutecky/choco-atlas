The case FOR (proponent)

On the brief’s anchor rule, I can’t make a qualifying positive case: this paper has no named result about computational complexity. Its theorems and proposition are axiomatic existence or impossibility results; they do not establish that an input problem is in \(P\), hard, or fixed parameter tractable.

The nearest foothold is Theorem 2, proved here: an agenda is connected if and only if there is no aggregation function that maps every profile of consistent, complete individual judgment sets to a consistent collective judgment set while satisfying unanimity and minimal subgroup rights. Minimal subgroup rights means that two disjoint subgroups are each decisive on at least one proposition-negation pair. This is a substantial impossibility result, but it is about whether such a rule exists—not the computational difficulty of a problem.

A plausible follow-on would use a large population divided into a few expert or affected-resident cohorts, with many people sharing each complete judgment type. Let \(\mu_{i,J}\) be the fraction of society in cohort \(i\) with judgment set \(J\), where each \(J\) is complete and consistent and the support has size \(\tau\). Given the agenda and each cohort’s rights set \(R_i\), ask whether there is a consistent collective judgment set \(A\subseteq X\) that respects unanimity whenever the mass accepting a proposition is \(1\), and respects subgroup rights whenever all mass in a rights-holding cohort accepts or rejects a proposition in \(R_i\). A solution is such an \(A\), or a report that none exists. Here the type is the judgment set plus cohort; mass is a fraction of the population; the decision variable is \(A\). This scenario could have \(n\gg\tau\), and the authors might recognize it as an operational question adjacent to their model.

But that feasibility question is new, not one of the paper’s named results. Theorem 2 concerns existence of a rule over the whole universal domain, while this asks about a single mass profile. The continuum is also a weak fit: intermediate masses do not affect the stated constraints; only unanimity within the population or a cohort matters. I can’t honestly attach a Class A, B, or C prediction to this unanchored question based on the paper. The strongest honest positive claim is therefore a plausible new direction, not a continuous computational mirror anchored in this article.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only anchor raised, and it fails the programme’s computational gate. It says that for a connected agenda no aggregation function can satisfy universal domain, minimal subgroup rights, unanimity, and collective consistency. That is an axiomatic existence result, not a result about the complexity of an input problem. The paper’s other results are also axiomatic. The proponent acknowledges this, so the proposed follow-on is a new problem rather than a computational mirror of a named result.

The closest direct computational version would ask whether a particular population profile admits a consistent collective judgment satisfying its unanimity and rights constraints. But that question sees the population only through which judgment types have positive mass. The forced propositions are exactly those accepted by everyone, together with those forced by unanimous rights-holding subgroups. Their consistency determines feasibility; the numerical values of intermediate masses do not matter. If completeness is required, a consistent forced set can be extended under the paper’s logic assumptions. Thus the proposed mirror reduces to a consistency check on a set of propositions. Any complexity would come from how the agenda’s logic is represented, not from continuizing the population.

A stronger population-based variant could attach costs to overriding rights or to rejecting judgments, then minimize the total mass overridden. That may be a worthwhile new research question. But the paper supplies no such costs or trade-off: its rights are categorical decisiveness conditions, and unanimity is an all-or-nothing requirement. Choosing a measure of violation and an objective would create a different computational model; Theorem 2 gives no reason to prefer that model over other possible extensions.

A society with repeated judgment types and rights-holding cohorts is coherent, so I cannot rule out every useful extension. The negative case is strongest for the paper’s actual anchor: its central result has no computational problem to continuize, and the most direct profile-level repair makes population masses irrelevant. Any richer computational project would need a new objective and axioms, rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
