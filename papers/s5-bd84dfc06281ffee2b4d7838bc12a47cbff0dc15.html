The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), ordered good categories \(g_1,\ldots,g_q\), binary utilities \(u_{tj}\), rational per-capita supplies \(\lambda_j\), and a queried type \(t^\star\), start all type mass at load \(0\). For each category \(j\), allocate supply \(\lambda_j\) by the Balanced Like least-load rule: at the least load \(r\) with eligible mass \(D=\sum_{t:u_{tj}=1}\nu_{t,r}>0\), transfer \(\delta=\min(s,D)\) mass, taking \(\delta\nu_{t,r}/D\) from each eligible type's load-\(r\) cohort to load \(r+1\), and continue with remaining supply \(s\). Compute exactly \(U_{t^\star}=\mu_{t^\star}^{-1}\sum_j u_{t^\star j}z_{t^\star j}\), where \(z_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) served in category \(j\); establish that this equals the limit of finite-replica expected type-average utilities as population size grows through compatible multiples.

The model it lives in

A fixed-order online allocation over type mass, with each type distributed across integer load levels, each recipient still receiving whole goods, and per-capita category supply. Balanced Like advances eligible mass according to its least-load and uniform-tie rules; the output is exact average utility for the queried type.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's fixed-order Balanced Like ExactUtility result at the type-average level. It leaves the stochastic-arrival results, Theorem 3's NecessaryUtility result, Theorem 4's two-agent algorithms, and Theorems 5–6 on manipulation outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass-flow computation have polynomial bit complexity in \(q\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), and does its value equal the finite-replica limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I would defend is a high-multiplicity version of Balanced Like exact utility. Picture a large food-bank network serving millions of households, with many households sharing the same acceptance and utility profile over a modest number of food categories. Let \(N\) be the number of households and \(\tau\) the number of profiles, with \(N\gg\tau\). Deliveries also scale with the network: a category may arrive in a batch of \(\lambda\) goods per household. Each good still goes to one household, and Balanced Like still favors households who have received the fewest goods among those who like it. The continuous quantity is the mass of households in each profile, and each profile’s output is its average expected utility.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that, with more than two agents, binary utilities, and the Balanced Like mechanism, ExactUtility—given a fixed ordering of items, an agent, and the preferences, output that agent’s expected total utility—is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hard under arithmetic reductions. The paper’s reduction encodes a count of perfect matchings in a 3-regular bipartite graph into that expected utility. This is a strong anchor because the paper studies the underlying outcome question directly, and the item order is fixed.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Balanced-Like Exact-Type-Utility. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); an ordered list of good categories \(g_1,\ldots,g_q\); binary utilities \(u_{tj}\) for type \(t\) and category \(g_j\); and rational per-capita supplies \(\lambda_j\in[0,1]\). It also identifies a type \(t^\star\). Initially every agent has received zero goods. For a finite approximation, take \(N\) so that \(N\mu_t\) and \(N\lambda_j\) are integers: there are \(N\mu_t\) agents of each type and \(N\lambda_j\) copies of category \(g_j\), delivered together in the listed order. Ask for the exact terminal average utility of type \(t^\star\), in the limit as \(N\) grows through such multiples. The answer is the type-average counterpart of the paper’s expected utility; within each type, agents are exchangeable.

The limit can be stated without reference to individual agents’ identities. Let \(\nu_{t,r}\) be the mass of type \(t\) that currently has \(r\) goods. During batch \(j\), find the least \(r\) with \(D=\sum_{t:u_{tj}=1}\nu_{t,r}>0\). For a remaining supply amount \(s\), set \(\delta=\min(s,D)\), and move mass \(\delta\nu_{t,r}/D\) from each eligible type’s load-\(r\) group to its load-\((r+1)\) group. Record that mass as category-\(g_j\) goods received by type \(t\), reduce \(s\) by \(\delta\), and repeat until the batch is exhausted. This is the uniform tie-breaking rule expressed over population mass: each affected unit of agent mass gets one whole good, while type averages may be fractional. If \(z_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) served in batch \(j\), the requested output is
\[ \frac{1}{\mu_{t^\star}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}u_{t^\star j}z_{t^\star j}. \]

I expect this question to be Class A. Its state has \(\tau\) types and at most \(q+1\) load levels, and each batch advances through at most \(q+1\) levels. That suggests a polynomial-size exact mass-flow computation in \(q,\tau\), and the rational input length. The first proof obligations are to bound rational bit growth and to establish that the finite Balanced Like lotteries converge to this flow. The mirror is plausible because it preserves the paper’s allocation rule, integer bundles for individual recipients, fixed-order arrivals, and exact-utility question. It changes the population representation and scales supply per capita, which are natural co-modifications for a high-multiplicity population.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s fixed-order ExactUtility result; I would not stretch it to cover the paper’s stochastic-arrival or manipulation results. It also raises useful follow-up questions: does exact type-average utility have a polynomial algorithm with a proved bit bound; how quickly do finite populations approach the flow; and does hardness survive when the query concerns one named agent or when arrivals are stochastic?

The weak point is that Theorem 2’s reduction uses many agent profiles to encode graph incidence. It does not itself demonstrate a regime with \(\tau\ll N\), and this mirror replaces its one-off finite instance with per-capita batches and a limiting type average. So the anchor supports a meaningful continuous version of the paper’s problem, but does not show that its \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hardness dissolves. That remains the question to prove.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that Theorem 2’s hardness reduction does not describe a high-multiplicity society with a small number of types. Its edge agents encode the graph incidence, so the number of distinct preference profiles grows with the graph. In the proposed regime, with moderate \(\tau\) and \(q\) item categories, Balanced Like appears to reduce to tracking mass across types and load levels. That suggests a polynomial fluid computation, once its bit growth and convergence are proved. The mirror may therefore yield a short tractability result rather than a substantial complexity landscape.

That is a concern about the result’s likely depth, though. The programme counts tractable continuous formulations as successes, and the reduction’s growing support does not invalidate a different, plausible regime. A large food-bank network with many households sharing a modest set of profiles, and category deliveries scaling with the population, is a credible high-multiplicity instance. The type-average expected utility is a natural population outcome; the finite version still gives each recipient an integral bundle; and scaling supplies with population is an allowed co-modification. The limit need not be trivial: the least-load rule still determines which types receive each category.

The mirror also appears to preserve a two-way bridge: finite replicas provide the approximation, while the mass-flow process asks a computational question over a continuous society. The paper’s matching-count hardness may disappear in that regime, but discovering that it becomes tractable would itself be a useful result.

So I cannot honestly defeat the Theorem 2 anchor. The proposed mirror may be technically modest, and its fluid-limit and exact-arithmetic claims need proof, but none of the available objections establishes that it is ill-posed or outside ChoCo’s scope. The case against is weak here; this anchor survives.
