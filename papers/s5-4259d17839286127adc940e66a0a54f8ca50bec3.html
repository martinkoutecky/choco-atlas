The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), a finite set of complete applicant profiles \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), school capacities \(q_s\), and reserves \(r_{s,g}\), find a mass assignment \(x_{\theta s}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{\theta s}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta s}\leq q_s\). Require that whenever \(x_{\theta d}>0\) and profile \(\theta\) prefers \(s\) to \(d\), the mass of group \(g(\theta)\) at \(s\) is at least \(r_{s,g(\theta)}\), and \(s\) is full. Each profile specifies a group, a school preference order, and a priority tier at each school; ties within a tier do not count as violations. Minimize the mass assigned to schools for which the corresponding profile prefers some school where positive mass of a lower-priority tier is admitted.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population described by a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete profiles \(\theta\), with per-capita school capacities and reserves. The decision variables \(x_{\theta s}\) split each profile’s mass across schools; the objective is the mass of applicants whose priority is violated at some school they prefer.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4’s reduction uses strict individual priority comparisons, while grouping applicants into priority tiers changes those comparisons; its NP-hardness result therefore gives no complexity conclusion for the mass problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the multiple-school priority-violations minimization question in Theorem 4. It leaves Theorem 5’s additional within-type priority compatibility condition, the single-school characterizations, and the simulations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For binary-rational input, what is the complexity of minimizing priority-violating mass as a function of the number of schools, profile support size, and input bit length?
  • Does the support-dependent objective permit an efficient formulation or algorithm, or does hardness persist when profile mass may split?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s multiple-school priority-violation minimization problem. Theorem 4, proved in this paper in Appendix A.5, says that finding an assignment with the fewest applicants whose priority is violated, among assignments satisfying Axiom 5 (reserves-respecting) and Axiom 6 (non-wasteful), is NP-hard even with two protected groups. Axiom 5 requires that if an applicant prefers school \(s\) to their assignment, at least \(r_{s,t}\) applicants of their protected group \(t\) attend \(s\). Axiom 6 requires that any school an applicant prefers to their assignment be full. An applicant’s priority is violated if some school they prefer has admitted someone with lower priority. The authors prove the result here by reduction from Complete Bipartite Subgraph.

I would ask about the Priority-Violation Mass Minimization with Reserves problem. An instance has schools \(S\), rational school capacities \(q_s\), reserve amounts \(r_{s,g}\) for protected groups \(g\), and a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many complete profiles \(\theta\). A profile includes an agent’s protected group \(g(\theta)\), full school preference order \(P_\theta\), and priority tier at each school. Priority ties do not count as violations. The total population is normalized to one, so capacities and reserves are population shares.

A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{\theta s}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_s x_{\theta s}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta s}\leq q_s\). Here \(x_{\theta s}\) allocates a portion of a nonatomic type cohort to school \(s\); each individual still receives one school. The assignment must satisfy the continuous versions of the paper’s two constraints: whenever \(x_{\theta d}>0\) and \(\theta\) prefers \(s\) to \(d\), the mass of group \(g(\theta)\) at \(s\) must be at least \(r_{s,g(\theta)}\), and \(s\) must be full. For a profile \(\theta\), let \(\theta\succ_s\theta'\) mean that \(\theta\) has higher priority than \(\theta'\) at \(s\). The objective is to minimize

\[ V(x)=\sum_{\theta,d}x_{\theta d}\, \mathbf{1}\!\left[ \exists s\text{ preferred by }\theta\text{ to }d: \sum_{\theta':\,\theta\succ_s\theta'}x_{\theta's}>0 \right]. \]

Thus the problem asks for a feasible assignment of population mass minimizing the mass of people whose priority is violated at some school they prefer. Its decision version takes a rational bound \(B\) and asks whether some feasible assignment has \(V(x)\leq B\).

A plausible regime is a large district-wide admissions system in which many students share the same protected-group status, ranked-school-list pattern, and priority-tier profile—for example, cohorts associated with the same feeder area and application pattern. The relevant comparison is between the number \(n\) of students and the number \(\tau\) of complete profiles: the mirror is most useful when \(n\gg\tau\), not merely because a school-choice system is large. The theorem’s reduction also uses repeated preference patterns: for each graph vertex \(v\), it creates \(N\) applicants with the same school preferences. But those applicants have strict individual rankings in the paper’s model. Grouping them as one complete profile therefore fits best where policy priorities are coarse tiers and individual lottery tie-breakers are not counted as priority violations. That is a meaningful, though explicit, adjustment to the paper’s strict-priority model.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: the objective and the two feasibility conditions are exactly those of Theorem 4, with applicant counts and seat counts expressed as population mass and per-capita capacity. The co-modification is natural because reserves and capacities scale with the population. The outcome remains an assignment to discrete schools; the society is what becomes continuous.

For this mirror, my tentative expectation is Class C, continuum-specifically hard. A positive amount of lower-priority mass at a preferred school can make an entire cohort assigned elsewhere count as violated, so the objective depends on which type-school flows are positive. That creates combinatorial choices even though mass is divisible. The biclique reduction points toward difficult interactions among preference profiles and schools, but it does not prove hardness for this fractional problem: fractional assignments may dissolve the discrete reduction. A useful next question is whether that hardness survives when repeated cohorts may split, or whether the support-dependent objective admits an LP or another tractable formulation.

The weakest point is that Theorem 4’s “two types” are protected groups, not ChoCo’s complete preference-and-priority profiles. Refining the types can increase \(\tau\), and allowing profile mass to split can change the optimum. The paper’s theorem is therefore an anchor for a well-motivated question, not a complexity result for the mirror. This case covers Theorem 4’s multiple-school minimization result; the single-school characterizations and simulations are not computational anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s computational anchor is narrower than its contribution as a whole: Theorems 1–3 are axiomatic characterizations, and the simulations are empirical comparisons. The NP-hardness results in Theorems 4 and 5 concern choosing assignments while counting applicants whose priorities are violated. In the formal model, priorities are strict. A complete profile must therefore include an applicant’s position in each school’s priority order; the paper’s “two types” identify reserve groups, not necessarily high-multiplicity types. The reduction repeats preference patterns, but its applicants are not automatically indistinguishable in every respect the objective uses. So the theorem alone does not establish that its hard instances lie in a regime with \(n\gg\tau\).

The best repair is the proponent’s tiered version: group applicants with the same protected group, preferences, and priority tier, then minimize the mass whose priority is violated. That is plausible, especially since the paper’s simulations explicitly ignore random tie-breakers when counting violations. But it changes the strict-priority model behind the complexity result. More fundamentally, the exact aggregate objective is sensitive to whether lower-priority mass is positive at all. An arbitrarily small amount of that mass can make an entire higher-priority cohort count as violated. Keeping the paper’s applicant-counting criterion therefore gives a support-dependent objective with a sharp jump at zero; replacing it with a smoother pairwise measure would change the policy objective. Either way, the paper does not supply a clear high-multiplicity bridge for its computational theorem.

Theorem 5 has the same applicant-level assignment structure, so it does not resolve this mismatch. A tiered mass model could still be a worthwhile new question, but its complexity would have to be studied on its own; it would not be a direct computational mirror of the strict-priority results here. That is the strongest negative case I can make. It is weak at the decisive point: the paper’s tier-based applications make the repaired mirror coherent and recognizable, and the discontinuity is a modeling complication, not proof that the question lacks value.
