What the mirror covers

The attempted mirror covers computing least-squares rankings; it leaves the axiomatic claims, generalized row-sum family, and league-specific empirical findings outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The narrow positive case is for computing the least-squares ranking from a high-multiplicity population of match results. Its one computational anchor is Csató (2015, Theorem 2), cited in Section 3 and used in equation (1); it is cited from elsewhere, not proved here. The result gives a recursive computation of the least-squares rating: for a connected comparison graph with Laplacian \(L\), normalized score vector \(s\), and maximum degree \(r\), set
\[ q^{(0)}=\frac1r s,\qquad q^{(k)}=q^{(k-1)}+\frac1r\left[\frac1r(rI-L)\right]^k s. \]
Except when the graph is regular bipartite, these iterates converge to the mean-zero rating \(q\) satisfying \(Lq=s\). The paper uses that recurrence to calculate rankings in its league examples. It has no comparable hardness anchor; Proposition 1 is about axioms, not computational complexity.

A plausible regime is a long-format competition with a fixed set \(C\) of clubs and many repeated fixtures. Treat each match as a comparison report. A type is an unordered pair of clubs and its result—one club wins, the other wins, or they draw. Those matches are indistinguishable to the paper’s method because it uses the match counts and points totals, not match identities or score margins. With \(m\) clubs there are at most \(3\binom m2\) such types, so the number \(N\) of match events can be much larger than the support size \(\tau\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of match events of type \(t\); per-type average points and fixture counts may therefore be fractional, even though each match has an integral result.

Call the mirror Match-Mass Least-Squares Ranking. Its input is \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over match types, and a total match volume \(H\). From them derive the points \(p_i=H\sum_t\mu_tP_i(t)\), the fixture counts \(M_{ij}=H\sum_{t:\,\mathrm{pair}(t)=\{i,j\}}\mu_t\), the match totals \(d_i=\sum_jM_{ij}\), and the centered points-per-match scores \(s_i=p_i/d_i-\frac1m\sum_k p_k/d_k\). Let \(L\) be the Laplacian of \(M\). If the comparison graph is connected, the requested solution is the unique mean-zero rating
\[ q=\arg\min_{\mathbf 1^\top q=0}\left(\frac12q^\top Lq-s^\top q\right), \]
and the ranking orders clubs by \(q_i\). This is the paper’s least-squares question over fractional match masses, not a relaxation of the rating outcome.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: the weighted Laplacian system has dimension \(m\), and rational linear algebra solves it in polynomial time in the input size. The paper’s recurrence suggests an iterative method too, though its convergence conditions and rate for fractional match weights would need to be established. Further questions include how closely rounded match masses preserve the ranking, when a mass instance has an exact finite-tournament realization, and whether the recurrence has useful convergence bounds in terms of the weighted graph’s structure.

This covers only the least-squares computation and its league-ranking application. The paper’s axiomatic claims and its generalised row sum family are outside this mirror. The soft spot is that the paper studies ranking a small set of named teams, while this regime treats match events as the population units and requires many repeated comparisons. That extension is plausible because the method already aggregates matches into \(p\) and \(M\), but it is not the paper’s main contribution. If anchors must be numbered results proved within this paper, rather than cited results it computes with, then there is no eligible computational anchor here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper gives no computational result to continuize. Its own contribution is axiomatic: Proposition 1 verifies ranking properties. The recursive least-squares formula is imported from Csató (2015, Theorem 2) and used to compute the examples; this paper gives no complexity theorem or algorithmic analysis. The league tables are applications, not computational results. So the proponent’s sole anchor is a result from another paper, not one of this paper’s anchors. That is a substantial obstacle under the programme’s scope.

The proposed Match-Mass mirror also puts continuity on the wrong object. Its mass is over match reports, while the paper ranks a fixed set of named teams. More fixtures increase the volume of observations, not the population of agents. And match types include particular team pairs, so growing the league grows the type space with the number of teams. A more direct continuum of teams runs into the paper’s identity-dependent output: the ranking assigns places to specific clubs, which can determine finite promotion and qualification slots. If types retain each club’s schedule and record, clubs do not aggregate; if identical clubs are merged, the ranking cannot identify which club gets a slot.

One could define a new model with a distribution of team types, a measure over matchups, and a ranking of types. That may be mathematically coherent, but it adds the population structure and computational question that this paper does not study. The match-mass proposal is not a continuous-population mirror, and the more faithful team-population proposal loses the named-club ranking that motivates the paper. My negative case is strongest on that mismatch and the absence of an in-paper computational anchor; it cannot rule out a worthwhile new continuum tournament model, but that would be a new research problem rather than this paper’s mirror.
