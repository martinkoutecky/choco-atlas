  • Theorem 1 characterizes the weighted Shapley value by dummy elimination, homogeneity, additivity, and the shared facility axiom.
  • Theorem 2 characterizes it by efficiency, fairness, and shared additional facility.
  • Theorem 3 gives the symmetric Shapley value characterization by efficiency, symmetry, and fairness.
  • Theorem 4 describes weighted values when some players have zero weight, separating the positive weight players’ allocation from the zero weight players’ allocation.

None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The numerical examples are calculations, and the paper mentions Dragan’s separate work on computation but does not state it as one of its results. So there is no honest one-per-anchor continuous complexity question to pose or classify.

The strongest seed for a follow-on would be cost sharing among many similar firms or facility users: types share weights and have identical effects on coalition costs; \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population share; a cost functional \(C(x)\) describes the cost of a coalition with mass vector \(x\); and the output is a per capita allocation whose aggregate equals \(C(\mu)\). A fixed cost affecting types in \(T\) would be charged only to those types, in proportion to their weights, reflecting the fairness idea behind Theorem 2.

That is a plausible continuous *allocation* model, but not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. It also requires choosing how arbitrary finite coalition costs \(C(S)\) extend to coalition masses \(C(x)\); the paper supplies no such extension. This is the weakest point, and it prevents the idea from serving as the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that this paper supplies no eligible anchor. Theorems 1–3 characterize allocation rules by axioms; Theorem 4 describes the allocations possible with zero-weight players. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. That is decisive against calling any continuous model a mirror of one of the paper’s results.

Theorem 2 is the strongest seed, but its fixed-cost question does not rescue the case. A continuous model of many similar firms or facility users is plausible: group players with identical cost-relevant properties into types, let \(x\) describe coalition masses, and specify a cost functional \(C(x)\). Yet the theorem’s claim is about uniqueness under fairness axioms, not about computing an allocation. For a single fixed cost, the proposed proportional charge is already a direct formula. For general \(C(x)\), the paper gives no continuous cost model to compute with. Its finite-player game allows an arbitrary value for every coalition; extending that table to masses requires additional structure. An unrestricted continuum cost function has no finite computational input, while a structured class such as airport costs brings in assumptions and questions the theorem does not establish.

The best version of the proposal could ask how to compute weighted allocations for compactly represented, high-multiplicity cost games. That may be a worthwhile project, and the firm or facility-user setting makes the population regime credible. But its complexity would depend on the chosen representation of \(C\); it would be a new computational study motivated by this paper, not a continuous counterpart to a result in it. The same limitation applies to Theorem 4: its separation of positive- and zero-weight players is structural, not a complexity classification.

So the negative case is strong for this paper as a *mirror candidate*: there is no computational result here to continuize, and the most natural extension shifts the work to a newly specified cost model. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous study could arise from the paper. A structured high-multiplicity cost-allocation project could be valuable; the paper simply gives no basis for treating it as the computational mirror the programme seeks.
