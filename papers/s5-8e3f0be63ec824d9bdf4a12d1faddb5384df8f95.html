The continuous mirror question

Given a finite state set \(X\) and a distribution \(\mu\) over reflexive, acyclic preference relations \(Q_t\), form \(R_\mu=\bigcap_{t:\mu_t>0}Q_t\). Compute a reflexive, connected, acyclic relation \(R'\) extending \(R_\mu\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete preference relations \(Q_t\) over \(X\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). The decision variable is the output relation \(R'\); the objective is to find any reflexive, connected, acyclic extension of the strong Pareto relation induced by the positive-mass types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1's extension construction for a distribution-derived Pareto relation; Theorems 1 and 2 remain structural choice-set identities.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited: this paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror to. It proves results about acyclic social welfare relations over a finite set of states, but does not classify the complexity of a computational problem.

The most plausible population regime would be a large electorate or policy consultation with relatively few distinct preference types. A type would be a complete reflexive, acyclic preference relation \(Q_t\) over the finite state set \(X\); \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s share of the population. One could derive a Pareto relation from the supported types and ask for a connected, reflexive, acyclic extension. The output would be a relation, with feasibility as the objective. That is a recognizable high-multiplicity scenario, but it is an extension of the paper’s setup: the paper takes an abstract relation \(R_0\) as input, not a population distribution.

The closest named result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: \(E(Q)\), the set of reflexive, connected, acyclic extensions of \(Q\), is nonempty exactly when \(Q\) is acyclic. Its proof constructs an extension by adding all incomparable pairs. Theorem 1, also proved here, says that for every nonempty \(S\subseteq X\), the \(R_0\)-undominated states are exactly the union of the undominated states under the Paretian social welfare relations extending \(R_0\). These are structural existence and choice-set identities, not results that state a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

One can turn Proposition 1’s construction into a polynomial-time algorithm for the extension problem, then feed it a relation derived from \(\mu\). But that is a complexity interpretation of the proof, not a named computational result of the paper. Moreover, the construction depends on the resulting relation; the masses matter only insofar as they determine which types are present. So this candidate has a plausible large-population story, but little population-specific computational content.

That is the weak point I cannot honestly repair: under the requested anchor rule, there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover. The paper’s contribution is axiomatic, and treating its relation-extension theorems as computational-complexity anchors would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not spend a continuous-mirror slot on this paper. Its strongest named results are Proposition 1 and Theorems 1 and 2, but each is a structural identity, not a classification of a computational problem. Proposition 1 says an acyclic relation has a connected, reflexive, acyclic extension; its proof gives one directly by adding every incomparable pair. Theorem 1 reduces membership in the union of choice sets across extensions to being undominated by \(R_0\). Theorem 2 gives a similar identity for strict intermediate relations. None leaves an algorithmic problem for the paper’s results to anchor.

The strongest faithful population version would take a large electorate with finitely many types, each type being a reflexive, acyclic preference relation over the finite state set \(X\), and derive a Pareto relation \(R_\mu\) from the distribution \(\mu\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario; the objection is not that agents are grouped by type. The trouble is that Pareto comparisons use which types have positive mass, not their shares. Under the paper’s Pareto convention, the mass of each supported type can vary without changing \(R_\mu\). Once \(R_\mu\) is computed, Proposition 1’s extension is immediate, and Theorem 1’s choice-set query is just an undominance check. For explicitly represented \(X\) and support, these are routine finite computations. Continuizing the population adds no substantive optimization or complexity question to the results as stated.

A more ambitious version might ask how the choice set changes as masses vary, or optimize over Pareto-consistent extensions. The first mostly tracks changes in support: Pareto relations can jump when a type’s mass reaches zero, while the positive shares themselves do not matter. The second needs a new objective or tie-breaking principle; the paper supplies neither, and adding cardinal utilities or weighted welfare would change its ordinal Pareto problem. Those could be worthwhile projects, but they would be new computational questions, not continuous mirrors of these theorems.

The negative case is strongest against a *mirror of this paper*, not against every project one could build nearby. A continuous population of voters is entirely plausible here. What I cannot honestly claim is that no such project could ever be worthwhile; the case is that this paper gives no computational result to continue, and its natural population reconstruction collapses to support-based relation construction.
