The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result to use as an anchor. Its numbered results are mathematical characterizations, all proved in the paper: Theorem 1 characterizes when an opinion-adjustment game is symmetric; Theorem 2 says that if the set of possible outcomes is a singleton, it equals the set generated by stable winning coalitions; Theorem 3 says a true simple game has at most one stable outcome; and Theorem 4 relates a singleton stable outcome in the “defeat analysis game” to membership in the simple game’s core. None asserts tractability, hardness, or another computational classification. So there is no qualifying anchor, and I would not present any of these as one.

There is still a plausible continuous population model closely tied to Theorem 3. Consider a large party or association electorate with many members but relatively few recurring types. A type \(t\) records a member’s ideal strict ranking \(R_t\) and full set \(P_t\) of acceptable rankings, with \(R_t\in P_t\). The society is a finite support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). Use a strict majority rule: a coalition wins when its mass exceeds \(1/2\). This continues the population while retaining the paper’s concern with flexible opinions and coalition formation.

One possible computational question is Continuous Stable-Outcome Computation. For each alternative \(a\), define its available supporting mass by
\[ E_\mu(a)=\sum_{t:\exists r\in P_t,\ \operatorname{top}(r)=a}\mu_t. \]
A winning mass coalition for \(a\) is a vector \(z_t\) with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), zero for types unable to accept \(a\), and \(\sum_t z_t>1/2\). Call it stable if every type represented in it has no ideal-preferred alternative \(b\) with \(E_\mu(b)>1/2\). The task is to output all alternatives with such a coalition, and a coalition-mass vector certifying each one. With acceptable rankings explicitly represented, direct aggregation gives a tractable algorithm in the input size. Theorem 3 is the closest semantic guide: its claim that there is at most one stable outcome has a natural mass-based version for this majority regime. Theorem 4 also suggests a further question: how does this stable-outcome computation relate to membership in the continuous core?

This is a population mirror rather than a continuization of outcomes, time, or uncertainty. Its weakest point is also decisive for the requested anchoring standard: the computational problem is a proposed extension, not a complexity result established by the paper. The model selects the symmetric quota setting; the paper’s arbitrary simple games do not specify a unique continuum rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continuize. Its Theorems 1–4 are structural statements about opinion adjustment, stable outcomes, and the core. The paper specifies no computational input, encoding, or algorithmic problem. The other analyst acknowledges this, then uses Theorem 3 as a semantic guide for a new computation task. That task may be well defined, but it is not a complexity question the paper posed or answered.

The majority model they propose is coherent. A large association electorate with recurring types \((R,P)\) is a plausible high multiplicity regime, and a distribution over those types preserves the preferences and acceptable opinions the model uses. Coalition identity and mass are not fundamental objections in that setting. Theorem 4 can also suggest a continuous core question if one defines blocking coalitions by mass. But those proposals add the winning rule, the computational objective, and the input representation. For arbitrary simple games, the resulting complexity would depend on how the winning coalitions are encoded; for majority, the analyst has selected a particular subcase.

That is a strong reason not to treat this paper as a computational mirror for the programme. It is not a strong case that no worthwhile continuous study could be built around it. The association electorate remains a sensible scenario, and the proposed computation could be worth studying on its own. I can’t honestly defend the universal claim that no continuous mirror is worthwhile in any scenario; the negative case is strongest on programme fit, not on whether a plausible population model exists.
