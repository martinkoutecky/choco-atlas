The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based case under your criteria: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 6 gives the equilibrium success-probability formula; Theorem 7 characterizes optimal outcome-contingent bribe conditions; Theorem 9 relates plausible deniability to bribe margins; Theorem 10 bounds bribe margins for corrected noised tallies; Theorem 11 removes correlations between payment conditions; and Theorem 13 bounds margins under differential-privacy noise. Corollary 7.1 gives exact margins for winner-only and full-disclosure tallies. These are analytical results, not classifications of a problem as tractable or hard. Section 4 calls its subset-sum attack subproblem NP-hard, but that claim is not a numbered result and concerns ballot reconstruction, not the paper’s B-privacy computation. Section 6.2 and Appendix B compute B-privacy using fixed-point iteration and heuristic bribe allocations, without a complexity guarantee.

The strongest unanchored lead would be a continuous B-privacy budget problem for a large DAO electorate. Let each type \(t\) specify a voter’s weight and private-utility distribution, and let \(\mu_t\) be its population fraction. Given a tally algorithm and target success probability \(p\), the adversary chooses a per-voter bribe \(b_t\) and an outcome-contingent payment rule \(f_t\). Minimize \(\sum_t \mu_t b_t\), subject to the adversary achieving probability at least \(p\) at equilibrium. A plausible regime is a large holder population concentrated in a modest number of exact token tiers and utility-distribution classes.

The central weakness is that in an atomless population an individual voter has zero effect on the tally. The pivotality and vote-conditioned bribe advantage at the heart of Theorems 6–10 can therefore vanish. Keeping those effects may require positive-weight agents or coordinated blocs, which materially changes the game. So this is a plausible new research question, but I cannot present it as a mirror anchored in the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no anchor among Theorems 6, 7, 9, 10, 11 or 13: each gives a game-theoretic characterization or bound, not a complexity result. The subset-sum attack in Section 4 is computational, but its NP-hardness claim concerns reconstructing ballots from a tally; it is neither a numbered result nor a complexity classification of B-privacy. Section 6.2 instead computes B-privacy with heuristic bribe allocations and fixed-point iteration, without a complexity guarantee. That is a substantial mismatch with ChoCo’s aim of mapping computational complexity.

The proposed mirror—minimize bribes over a distribution of voter types—does not fix that mismatch, and its best natural version runs into a limit problem. Even granting a plausible high-multiplicity setting, such as many accounts with the same weight and utility distribution, each account’s share of normalized total voting weight vanishes as the population grows. In a nonatomic society, changing one person’s vote leaves every aggregate tally unchanged. The individual’s tally-conditioned bribe advantage and pivotality therefore both vanish; the finite-voter incentive formula in Theorem 6 no longer supplies a nontrivial limit. Keeping finite pivotality requires retaining macroscopic voters or coordinated blocs, which changes the object from a large population of voters to a game with exceptional agents or collective players.

There may be a useful limit theory after choosing a scaling for bribes, utilities and election-margin fluctuations. But those choices determine what happens to the full-disclosure baseline and the privacy comparison, and the result would be a new asymptotic game—not an evident continuous version of a named computational result in this paper. The cited large-population voting-game literature makes such a regime plausible; as the programme brief says, that analytic work is not a collision. It also means I cannot honestly claim that no sensible continuous scenario exists.

So the strongest negative is narrower: this paper offers little support for a ChoCo complexity mirror. The proposed B-privacy optimization is an unanchored extension of a heuristic computation, and the most natural population limit erases the individual-level incentive effects that make B-privacy distinctive. That is a strong reason to rank the paper low for this programme, but it does not prove that every carefully rescaled large-population version would be unworthy.
