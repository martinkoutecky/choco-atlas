The continuous mirror question

Given finite room classes \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over resident types \(t=(A_t,e_t)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(f_h\) of unowned rooms, maximize the mass receiving an acceptable room. Choose mass \(r_t\) retaining its endowment, mass \(y_{t,h}\) assigned to acceptable class \(h\), and unassigned mass \(u_t\), subject to \(r_t+\sum_h y_{t,h}+u_t=\mu_t\), S-IR restrictions, and \(\sum_t y_{t,h}\le f_h+\sum_{t:e_t=h}(\mu_t-r_t)\) for each \(h\).

The model it lives in

A rational capacitated flow or linear program over resident types and room classes, with variables for retained, reassigned, and unassigned mass; the objective is to maximize satisfied mass subject to type-mass, room-supply, and S-IR constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers polynomial-time welfare maximization subject to S-IR in Theorem 1. It leaves individual strategyproofness and the fully endowed case’s core-stability guarantee unclaimed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a meaningful positive-mass incentive notion preserve any of MSIR’s strategyproofness guarantee?
  • Does the fully endowed continuum model’s core-stability guarantee extend to blocking coalitions of positive mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the welfare part of Theorem 1, proved in this paper. The theorem says that, for house allocation with existing tenants and dichotomous preferences, MSIR is polynomial-time and strategyproof, assigns acceptable houses to as many agents as possible subject to strong individual rationality, and is core stable in the housing-market case where every house is initially owned. The paper has no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution story.

Lead problem: Continuum-SIR-Max-Housing. Consider a large university allocating rooms from a finite set \(H\) of interchangeable room classes. A class might capture features such as location, accessibility, and room size. A student type is \(t=(A_t,e_t)\), where \(A_t\subseteq H\) is the set of acceptable classes and \(e_t\in H\cup\{\bot\}\) is the class of the student’s existing room, or \(\bot\) if they have none. The input gives the positive-mass types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and the per-capita supply \(f_h\) of initially unowned rooms in each class. Thus \(\tau\) counts observed types, while \(m=|H|\) counts room classes; for a large university, the number of students could be in the tens of thousands while \(\tau\) is in the hundreds.

A solution consists of masses \(r_t\), \(y_{t,h}\), and \(u_t\). Here \(r_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) keeping their own room, \(y_{t,h}\) is the mass reassigned to class \(h\), and \(u_t\) is left without a room. For each type, \(r_t+\sum_h y_{t,h}+u_t=\mu_t\). Under strong individual rationality, anyone whose existing room is acceptable must keep that exact room; a student with an unacceptable existing room must either keep it or move to an acceptable class. Students without an existing room may receive an acceptable room or remain unassigned. Unacceptable assignments can be normalized to unassigned, since they add no satisfaction.

Write \(E_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\) for the mass of initially owned rooms in class \(h\). Reassigned rooms must satisfy \(\sum_t y_{t,h}\le f_h+E_h-\sum_{t:e_t=h}r_t\): the rooms available to others are the unowned supply plus rooms released by their owners. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[e_t\in A_t]r_t+\sum_{h\in A_t}y_{t,h}\bigr)\), the mass receiving an acceptable room.

This keeps the paper’s underlying problem recognizable: agents exchange existing housing, receive at most one room, have dichotomous preferences, and the objective is to maximize satisfaction subject to S-IR. A fractional \(y_{t,h}\) means that this fraction of a type receives a room in class \(h\); it does not mean any individual receives part of a room. The retention variable also lets the model preserve the exact-room requirement of S-IR while aggregating interchangeable rooms.

I expect this problem to be Class A: tractable. Its input has rational masses and supplies, and its linear program has \(O(\tau m)\) assignment variables and polynomially many constraints, so it is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The paper’s Theorem 1 is the discrete anchor; Propositions 3 and 5 establish, respectively, that MSIR runs in polynomial time and returns a maximum-satisfaction S-IR allocation. This mirror predicts tractability, though it does not claim a new tractability gain: the discrete problem is already polynomial-time.

My weakest point is that the mirror captures the welfare-allocation part of Theorem 1, not its whole mechanism-design claim. Individual strategyproofness is central to MSIR, but in an atomless population a single person’s report changes no type mass, making truthfulness vacuous unless the model adds tagged or positive-mass deviations. I would not claim that MSIR’s strategyproofness carries over. A further question is whether the core-stability claim in the fully endowed case extends to blocking coalitions of positive mass under this model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only strong anchor. It gives a genuine computational result, so the negative case cannot rely on the absence of a result to mirror.

The room-class model is also a serious candidate. In a large housing market, many residents can share the same acceptable room classes and endowment class. With rational type masses and per-capita supplies, maximizing the mass assigned an acceptable room subject to S-IR is a well-posed flow problem. Retention variables can preserve the requirement that owners of acceptable endowments keep their own rooms. Rational solutions can be scaled to finite assignments. So neither identity nor indivisibility rules out this mirror, and the population really can be the continuous object.

The strongest objection is that this keeps only a thin slice of the theorem. MSIR’s contribution is a mechanism that combines welfare maximization with strategyproofness and individual rationality, and obtains core stability in the fully endowed case. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass, so ordinary individual strategyproofness is vacuous. Giving a whole type positive mass and letting it misreport would instead ask about coordinated group deviations, a different incentive property. The proposed welfare problem remains meaningful, but it no longer tests the paper’s central mechanism-design claim.

There is a better welfare-only mirror: use a fully endowed continuum, preserve the type-level endowment and preference data, and ask for maximum satisfaction under S-IR. That can carry the core conclusion too: a positive-mass coalition that strictly improves its members would yield a higher-welfare S-IR allocation. But the optimization is then an ordinary transportation problem, and the core conclusion follows directly from optimality. The paper’s matching algorithm is already polynomial, so this mirror offers no clear complexity transition; its main contribution would be a compressed formulation for large populations.

That is a case against spending ChoCo effort on this paper’s mirror, not a proof that no worthwhile version exists. The weak point is substantial: the flow formulation is natural, population-based, and computationally well-defined. If the programme treats a useful high-multiplicity formulation as sufficient even when the discrete problem is already polynomial, this anchor survives.
