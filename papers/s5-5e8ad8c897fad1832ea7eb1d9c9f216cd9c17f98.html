The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(S\) of sending communities, receiving types \(R\) with rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational matrix \(P\) of receiving-type influence weights, an access matrix \(C\), and a target distribution \(q_r\) over the distinct sending-source states for each \(r\in R\), determine whether there is a matrix \(X\) of external influence weights such that \(q_r(s)=\sum_{r'\in R}P_{r',r}q_{r'}(s)+X_{s,r}\) for every \(s\in S,r\in R\), with \(X_{s,r}=0\) when \(C_{s,r}=0\), \(X_{s,r}>0\) when \(C_{s,r}=1\), and \(\sum_{r'\in R}P_{r',r}+\sum_{s\in S}X_{s,r}=1\) for every \(r\); if feasible, construct \(X\).

The model it lives in

A large receiving population represented by role types with rational masses \(\mu_r\), where users in each role share the same signal model, access to sending communities, and aggregate weights assigned to receiving roles. The matrix \(P\) records those per-user aggregate receiving weights, and \(X\) records the per-user shares assigned to sending communities. The question is whether every positive-mass role can attain its specified limiting belief distribution; there is no objective in the paper’s design problem.

The objection that survived

The role masses do not enter the feasibility criterion, so changing their values leaves the answer unchanged; this limits the mirror to exact role-symmetric networks and gives it no mass-sensitive tradeoff.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a type-level version of the paper’s attainable-belief control result. The paper’s named anchor is Theorem 1 (Attainable Beliefs), proved here. For a fixed receiving-network matrix \(T_{RR}\), it defines \(V=Q(I-T_{RR})\) and an access matrix \(C\), where \(C_{s,k}=1\) when receiving agent \(k\) has a link from sending subnetwork \(s\). It proves that the desired belief matrix \(Q\) is attainable if and only if \(V_{s,k}=0\) wherever \(C_{s,k}=0\), and \(V_{s,k}>0\) wherever \(C_{s,k}=1\). The theorem does not label this problem “in P”; that is the direct algorithmic consequence I would draw from its explicit rational equalities and strict-positivity checks. The authors use the characterization to construct weights in their examples.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large platform with a few sending communities and many receiving users in a small number of recurring roles. Users in one role have the same signal likelihood model, the same access to sending communities, and the same aggregate pattern of trust in receiving roles. The network partition must preserve those properties exactly—for example, through repeated or block-symmetric network structure—so the role-level model retains the mixing that matters to the paper’s result. Millions of users can then be represented by a rational mass \(\mu_r\) for each of \(\tau\) receiving roles, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the population. This is a scenario claim, not a claim that arbitrary social graphs have a useful finite type structure.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover, Continuous Type-Level Attainable-Belief Control. The instance has a finite set \(S\) of sending communities, a finite set \(R\) of receiving types with rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to one, and \(S\) distinct source states. It includes a fixed rational matrix \(P\), where \(P_{r',r}\) is the weight type \(r\) assigns to receiving type \(r'\), and an access matrix \(C\), where \(C_{s,r}=1\) if members of type \(r\) can receive information from sending community \(s\). The input also gives a target distribution \(q_r\) over the source states for each receiving type \(r\).

The question is whether there are type-level external weights \(X_{s,r}\) such that
\[ q_r(s)=\sum_{r'\in R}P_{r',r}q_{r'}(s)+X_{s,r} \]
for every \(s,r\), with \(X_{s,r}=0\) when \(C_{s,r}=0\), \(X_{s,r}>0\) when \(C_{s,r}=1\), and \(\sum_{r'}P_{r',r}+\sum_s X_{s,r}=1\) for every \(r\). A solution is the matrix \(X\), understood as the share of each type’s influence weight assigned to each sending community; if that community contains multiple linked senders, the share can be distributed among them. There is no objective in the paper’s design question: it asks whether the target beliefs can be imposed and how to choose weights that achieve them.

This is the role-level analogue of Theorem 1. With \(Q_{s,r}=q_r(s)\), the residual \(V=Q(I-P)\) must be zero wherever \(C\) is zero and strictly positive wherever \(C\) is one. For finite rational input, that gives an exact polynomial-time decision procedure in \(|S|,\tau\), and the input bit length, followed by a construction of \(X\) when feasible. The continuum enters through the population measure over roles; the control variables and target beliefs are shared across each role’s mass.

The mirror covers fixed-internal-network attainable-belief design in Sections III-A–C. I expect this type-level feasibility problem to be tractable by the same structural criterion. It does not cover the paper’s joint design of \(T_{RR}\) and \(T_{SR}\), its approximate least-squares variant, or its simulation results. Further questions include minimizing a mass-weighted cost of changing trust weights, finding approximate targets when exact attainability fails, and understanding how much the criterion changes when the role-level network structure is only an approximation.

The weak point is that this article has no named hardness result, and its main theorem already gives a simple finite criterion. The mirror therefore does not test the programme’s central question of whether continuization dissolves discrete hardness; it makes the paper’s existing attainability question a population-over-types problem in a restricted but credible regime. The authors could reasonably recognize that as their problem’s continuous form, but it is a modest computational-social-choice case rather than a strong hardness-to-tractability example.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only serious anchor. It gives a named characterization of attainable beliefs, and its residual test yields a polynomial time decision procedure on rational input. So I would not dismiss it simply because the paper does not state a complexity bound.

The problem with the proposed mirror is more basic: its population masses do no work. Given \(P\), \(C\), and the target beliefs \(Q\), feasibility depends on \(V=Q(I-P)\). Any two positive mass vectors on the same type support produce exactly the same constraints and the same answer. The algorithm need not read the masses or their bit lengths. In the paper’s model, the operative object is a column-stochastic network matrix whose columns describe individual receivers. The proposed “types” replace repeated columns and neighborhoods with a finite quotient; they do not turn population mass into a computational variable. That may be a useful compression of a replicated graph, but it is not yet a continuous-population question in ChoCo’s sense.

The strongest version of the role scenario does preserve the paper’s dynamics: if members of each role have identical signal models, access, and aggregate weights to every other role, their limiting beliefs can be represented exactly at the role level. But that observation cuts both ways. The model reduces to Theorem 1 on a smaller finite graph, and changing how much population each role represents does not change attainability. The headline result is then a support-compression exercise, not a high-multiplicity relaxation whose mass distribution affects the control problem.

One could try to repair this by charging for campaigns in proportion to the mass whose trust weights must change. That would make total cost depend on \(\mu\), but the paper supplies neither a campaign cost model nor a baseline from which weight changes are measured. With fixed targets and independent per-type weight choices, a mass-weighted cost simply rescales each type’s separate subproblem; it does not create a new tradeoff. A shared campaign budget or a goal of persuading the largest possible mass could create one, but that changes the paper’s all-receivers attainability question and adds a resource-allocation problem the authors did not formulate.

This is a real objection to the proposed mirror’s computational payoff, but it does not establish the requested universal negative. The exact repeated-role regime is plausible, and a mass-sensitive campaign formulation might be something the authors would recognize. The honest negative case is therefore narrow: Theorem 1 has a coherent type-level analogue, but the version on offer leaves the continuous population measure inert. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario.
