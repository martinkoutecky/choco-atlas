The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible lead is a mass version of the paper’s regret-based allocation problem. There is an important limit on the claim: the paper has no named result establishing a complexity classification for a problem in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its nearest anchor is Theorem 2, a result of this paper whose proof is deferred to the longer version: for a regret-based partial revelation mechanism with partial Groves payments, if the maximum regret is at most \(\varepsilon\) for every reported partial-type vector, then the mechanism is \(\varepsilon\)-efficient and \(\varepsilon\)-dominant incentive compatible. That is a mechanism guarantee, not a complexity result. Section 5 describes a tractable heuristic for choosing partial types, but does not give it a numbered complexity theorem. So this is a credible continuous analogue of part of the paper, but it does not qualify as a formal anchor under your rule.

The most convincing regime would be a large population choosing among public service or infrastructure packages. A complete type \(t\) is a resident’s utility vector over a finite outcome set \(X\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that utility vector. The regime is plausible when the population is large and the number \(\tau\) of distinct utility types is comparatively small. A partial type is a cell in a fixed partition \(\Pi\) of the possible complete types, and \(q_A\) is the fraction reporting cell \(A\).

A precise candidate question is Mass-Regret Allocation for Partial Reports. Its input is finite \(X\), a finite set \(T\) of possible complete utility types with rational values \(v_t(x)\), a partition \(\Pi\) of \(T\), and rational reported masses \(q_A\) summing to one. For each \(x\in X\), define

\[ R(x;q)= \max_{\substack{\nu_t\ge 0\\ \sum_{t\in A}\nu_t=q_A\ \forall A\in\Pi}} \ \max_{y\in X} \sum_{t\in T}\nu_t\bigl(v_t(y)-v_t(x)\bigr). \]

The question is to return an outcome \(x^\star\) minimizing \(R(x;q)\), together with its worst-case per-capita regret and a certificate of optimality. Here the population is the continuous object: \(\nu_t\) is type mass, the action is the collective outcome, and the objective is worst-case welfare loss per person given the reported partial-type masses. For explicit finite \(T\) and \(X\), this candidate looks tractable: for each outcome pair, the adversarial regret calculation is a linear program, and the outcomes can be enumerated. It covers the allocation side of Theorem 2’s regret guarantee, not the paper’s whole mechanism-design result. Further questions include optimizing the partition under a communication budget and determining what incentive guarantees survive when individual reports are replaced by population masses.

The soft spot is substantial: in an atomless population, one person’s report cannot change the aggregate report masses, so the paper’s unilateral incentive question can become vacuous. Also, the paper’s payments and strategic reports are attached to individual agents. The mass problem is therefore a plausible continuation of its regret-based allocation model, but it is not a faithful continuous version of its full strategic mechanism. Under the requested anchor standard, the positive case remains unanchored.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 is a guarantee about efficiency and incentives, conditional on a regret bound; Theorem 1 and the propositions concern implementation and impossibility. Section 5 gives a myopic partitioning heuristic and experiments, but no complexity classification or formal runtime bound. That leaves no result here about tractability, hardness, or algorithms for ChoCo to chart.

The proposed mass-regret problem does not restore that anchor. It asks which outcome minimizes worst-case welfare loss given reported proportions of partial types. That is a coherent aggregate-uncertainty problem, and its finite version can be expressed with LPs. But it omits the central content of Theorem 2: individual reports, payments, and the guarantee against a unilateral lie. In an atomless population where reports affect only aggregate proportions, one person cannot change those proportions, so the incentive question becomes vacuous. Giving a reporting group positive mass can restore influence, but then the strategic units are finite, identity-bearing cohorts; replacing individual deviations with coalition deviations instead changes the solution concept.

A stronger version could use a continuum market: let the population distribution determine aggregate feasibility, while each reported partial type determines an individual’s allocation and payment. That can preserve pointwise incentive constraints, so it is a more serious mirror than the proposed welfare-only LP. But it requires a new type-contingent mechanism model and a computational question about that model; it does not continuize a computational result established in this paper. The paper gives no formal complexity result for its own partitioning procedure to carry over.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchor for ChoCo’s complexity landscape. It is weak against the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: the continuum-market partition-design problem could be worth studying on its own, and I cannot honestly rule it out. The case against succeeds only if the standard is a continuous mirror of a named computational result of this paper.
