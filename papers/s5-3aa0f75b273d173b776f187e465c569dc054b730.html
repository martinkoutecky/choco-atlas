The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: the paper supports a natural continuous-population extension of its main manipulability theorem, but it does not contain a qualifying named computational-complexity result. No numbered result here proves NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or a comparable algorithmic classification. The references to computational hardness and average-case ease are discussion or prior work, not results proved in this paper.

If the programme permits a quantitative algorithmic theorem as a near-anchor, my lead is Theorem 1.2, proved in this paper. It says that for \(n\ge1\), \(k\ge3\), and \(f:S_k^n\to[k]\) satisfying

\[ D(f,\mathrm{NONMANIP})\ge\varepsilon, \]

where distance is uniform Hamming distance over ranking profiles and \(\mathrm{NONMANIP}\) consists of dictatorships on a nonempty subset of alternatives together with monotone two-valued social choice functions,

\[ \Pr_{\sigma\sim S_k^n}[\sigma\in M(f)] \ge \Pr_{\sigma\sim S_k^n}[\sigma\in M_4(f)] \ge \frac{\varepsilon^{15}}{10^{39}n^{67}k^{166}}. \]

Here \(M_4(f)\) is the set of profiles at which some voter can obtain a preferred outcome by changing that voter’s ranking through a permutation of at most four adjacent alternatives. The theorem further states that if one samples a voter and a local four-alternative permutation at random, the resulting pair is a manipulation pair with probability at least

\[ \frac{\varepsilon^{15}}{10^{41}n^{68}k^{167}}. \]

That last statement is the bridge to a continuous population.

The corresponding problem I would hand to a prover is Continuous Random \(4\)-Block Mass Manipulation. Let \(C\) be the candidates, \(T=S_C\) the ranking types, and \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) a rational population distribution. The population contains many homogeneous agents of each type \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\). Let \(F:\Delta(T)\to C\) be an anonymous-in-voter-names deterministic social choice functional, possibly nonneutral in the candidates, and assume \(F\) has an exact finite representation. Fix a positive rational action scale \(\delta\).

For a type \(t\), let \(N_4(t)\) be the rankings obtained by permuting a contiguous block of at most four alternatives in \(t\). A mass-\(\delta\) manipulation by type \(t\) to \(t'\in N_4(t)\) changes the society to

\[ \mu^{t\to t'}=\mu-\delta e_t+\delta e_{t'}. \]

It succeeds when

\[ F(\mu^{t\to t'})\succ_t F(\mu). \]

The objective is the success probability of a random mass-bearing type and a random admissible local report,

\[ \operatorname{CR4}(F,\mu,\delta) = \sum_{t:\mu_t\ge\delta}\mu_t \sum_{t'\in N_4(t)} Q_t(t')\, \mathbf 1\!\left[ F(\mu^{t\to t'})\succ_t F(\mu) \right], \]

where \(Q_t\) is the prescribed distribution over local four-block permutations. The computational question is: given \(F,\mu,\delta\), and a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether \(\operatorname{CR4}(F,\mu,\delta)\ge q\), and return the successful type/report pairs as certificates.

This is recognisably the paper’s problem. The candidates, complete rankings, deterministic winner, strict preference improvement, and four-adjacent-alternative locality are unchanged. Only the voter population is replaced by rational type mass. In an anonymous \(N\)-voter election, setting \(\delta=1/N\) recovers the usual one-voter change exactly after normalising counts by \(N\). The genuinely continuous version lets \(\delta\) be a positive population fraction: a homogeneous cohort, rather than a named individual, changes its report.

A plausible regime is a large election or virtual preference-aggregation system with millions of agents but relatively few ballot types: the same rankings recur because voters share a party affiliation, demographic cohort, query context, or automated voting policy. Then \(\tau=|T|=k!\) is fixed or modest compared with population size. The mass is the honest quantity: a report change by a \(0\)-mass individual has no effect, whereas changing the report of a measurable homogeneous cohort does.

For score-based or threshold-based \(F\), I would expect this problem to be Class A. Each candidate comparison after a fixed mass transfer is a linear inequality in \(\mu\), and the local neighbourhood \(N_4(t)\) can be enumerated. More general polyhedral winner regions yield a finite collection of linear feasibility problems. Any hardness caused by a succinct circuit representation of \(F\) would come from the rule representation, not from population multiplicity.

The full analogue of Theorem 1.2 would require additional choices. One would need a probability law \(\Lambda\) over population distributions, a continuous class \(\mathrm{NONMANIP}_\infty\) of rules immune to positive-mass deviations, and a distance

\[ D_\Lambda(F,\mathrm{NONMANIP}_\infty) = \inf_G \Pr_{\mu\sim\Lambda}[F(\mu)\ne G(\mu)]. \]

The open theorem would then ask whether being \(\varepsilon\)-far from \(\mathrm{NONMANIP}_\infty\) forces an inverse-polynomial lower bound on \(\operatorname{CR4}\), perhaps for a nonnegligible set of societies.

That is also the weakest point. The original theorem concerns unilateral manipulation by a named voter and probability over labelled profiles. In an atomless population, unilateral deviations have zero mass, so replacing them by positive-mass bloc deviations is a substantive extension, not a literal restatement. Moreover, the paper’s distance \(D\) has no canonical population analogue: uniform labelled profiles, uniform count vectors, and a continuous simplex measure lead to different limits. The definitions of dictatorship and nonmanipulability also require redesign once voter identities disappear.

I would therefore cover only Theorem 1.2. I would not use Theorems 1.3–1.5 as additional anchors: Theorem 1.3 is an existence result, Theorem 1.4 is especially vulnerable to the one-voter/atomless problem, and Theorem 1.5 is a structural reduction rather than a computational result.

So the positive case is that the paper points naturally toward a meaningful continuous-population problem—one that could plausibly be tractable through polyhedral computation and could expose whether inverse-polynomial manipulability survives mass aggregation. But under the programme’s strict named-computational-anchor gate, this remains an author-recognisable extension of Theorem 1.2, not a fully qualifying mirror of a computational result proved by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at the programme’s formal gate: Theorem 1.2 is not a computational-complexity result. It proves a quantitative combinatorial statement about manipulation frequency. The paper’s claims that computational hardness cannot hide manipulation, and that random manipulation is easy, are discussion of other literature, not numbered complexity theorems proved here. Thus there is no qualifying computational anchor.

Even relaxing that gate, the proposed mirror does not preserve the theorem’s object. The paper studies an arbitrary labelled-coordinate function \(f:S_k^n\to[k]\), with distance measured over uniformly random labelled profiles. A population distribution \(\mu\) forgets voter identities, so a general \(f\) does not descend to a function of \(\mu\). Encoding each coordinate as a separate “type” preserves the theorem only by retaining \(n\) essentially singleton types; that abandons high multiplicity. Restricting to anonymous \(f\) is a legitimate new restriction, but it discards the general nonneutral result and does not supply a canonical extension from the finite count-vector grid to all of \(\Delta(S_k)\).

More fundamentally, the theorem’s manipulation is unilateral. For a population of size \(n\), one report change produces

\[ \mu'=\mu+\frac{1}{n}(e_{t'}-e_t), \]

which converges to \(\mu\). In an atomless population, an individual has zero effect, so every rule is vacuously immune to literal unilateral manipulation. Choosing a fixed \(\delta>0\) repairs that only by changing the problem to positive-mass coalition manipulation. Choosing \(\delta=1/n\) retains the disappearing finite population scale and is merely a reparameterization of the discrete anonymous model. Neither version is what Theorem 1.2 proves.

The probability statement also does not survive naturally. The paper averages over a uniformly random labelled profile and then a random voter and local move. The proposed \(\operatorname{CR4}(F,\mu,\delta)\) fixes one society and averages only over its types and reports. To recover the paper’s outer probability one must introduce a new law \(\Lambda\) over societies. The push-forward of uniform labelled profiles is multinomial and concentrates near the uniform distribution as \(n\) grows; uniform measure on the simplex is a different model altogether. Likewise, the proposed \(\mathrm{NONMANIP}_\infty\) has no canonical definition: dictatorships depend on named voters and disappear under anonymity, while positive-mass immunity is not the Gibbard–Satterthwaite notion.

The suggested computational problem is weakly anchored as well. Given an explicitly evaluable \(F\), one simply enumerates \(t\) and its \(4\)-block neighbours and evaluates \(F\) after each transfer. Any substantial hardness would have to come from a deliberately succinct or difficult representation of \(F\), or from the newly invented distance and measure problem—not from continuizing the population in the paper’s sense.

A theory of bloc manipulation over anonymous population distributions could certainly be worthwhile. But it would be a new continuous social-choice project requiring new notions of distance, nonmanipulability, population sampling, and probably new proofs. The paper supplies none of its computational content. Thus the proposed anchor cannot establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper; the honest limit is that a valuable follow-up may exist, but it would not be a continuization of a named computational result here.
