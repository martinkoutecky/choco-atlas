The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 3.4 characterizes egalitarian Shapley values by consistency and standardness, and Theorem 4.3 characterizes them by axioms including weak monotonicity. Both are classification results about solution concepts, not claims about the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous version would ask how those axioms or solutions extend to a new domain; it would not continue a computational question established here.

The strongest possible anchor is Theorem 5.1: the bidding mechanism implements the \(\alpha\)-egalitarian Shapley value as a subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome. One could propose a computational question about finding that outcome for a society with a distribution over player types. But the paper supplies neither a compact representation of games nor an algorithmic problem. With an explicit characteristic-function table, the Shapley value is computable in time polynomial in the table’s size. With a succinct representation, the complexity depends on that representation, which the paper does not choose or study.

A stronger modeling rescue would restrict the game to a few player types, make coalition worth depend on type masses, scale worths per capita, and define a continuous analogue of marginal contribution and of the bargaining mechanism. That could be a coherent new project. It would also require choices that determine the question’s substance: how coalition worth is represented, how marginal contributions are measured, and how a proposer or departing player is modeled when each individual has zero mass. The paper’s mechanism specifically selects and removes individual players, so its strategic steps do not directly persist in an atomless society. A type-level replacement could be devised, but its computational problem would come from those added modeling decisions rather than from a result in this paper.

Thus the proponent’s three named results leave no computational anchor standing: Theorems 3.4 and 4.3 are axiomatic, and Theorem 5.1 is strategic implementation without a complexity claim. The negative case is strongest as a judgment about this paper’s fit for ChoCo; it cannot establish that no continuous cooperative-game project would be worthwhile. The plausible extensions look like new continuous cooperative-game theory, rather than continuous computational mirrors of the paper’s results.
