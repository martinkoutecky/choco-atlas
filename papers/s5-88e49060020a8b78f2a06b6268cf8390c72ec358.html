The strongest honest positive case is a plausible *follow-on* problem about rotating shifts, not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. The closest numbered results do not meet the anchor requirement: Lemma 1 states Sperner’s lemma, so a Sperner-admissible coloring has an odd number of fully labeled triangles and therefore at least one; Proposition 1, proved here, states that \(3\mid |V|\) exactly when \(3\nmid q\). These support the construction and equal assignment of salary proposals to the three workers, but neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The paper reports an algorithm and simulations, but no numbered result asserting such a classification.

A reasonable population setting would be a large employer with many workers but relatively few complete worker types. A type would specify a worker’s full salary-conditioned shift preferences and relevant constraints; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s workforce fraction. For three shifts, let \(w_j\) be the salary for shift \(j\), with \(\sum_j w_j=Q\), and let \(x_{tj}\) be the mass of type \(t\) scheduled on shift \(j\). Given required coverage fractions \(\rho_j\) and rational preference maps \(p_t(w)\), ask for \(w,x\) satisfying \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tj}=\rho_j\) that minimize
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(\frac{x_{tj}}{\mu_t}-p_{tj}(w)\right)^2. \]
Finite rational piecewise-linear preference maps would make this a specified computational problem. It extends the paper’s salary choices, shift coverage, and least-squares correction to a workforce whose mass can be split across schedules. A standardized large workforce with \(N\gg\tau\) makes the high-multiplicity regime credible.

That extension is plausibly recognizable to the authors, especially because it preserves the second-stage correction they define. But its complexity is open on the evidence here: the paper provides no anchor from which to predict whether hardness transfers, dissolves, or arises specifically from the continuum. The paper’s simplex of salary allocations and probabilistic preferences already make outcomes or preferences continuous; they do not make the *population* continuous.

The weak point is decisive for the requested format: the paper studies exactly three named workers, and the proposed mass-allocation problem is a new extension rather than the continuous counterpart of a numbered computational result. So there is a sensible research question to take forward, but no valid anchor-based positive case for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Lemma 1 is Sperner’s parity theorem, used to guarantee a fully labelled triangle. Proposition 1 says that the number of grid vertices is divisible by three exactly when \(q\) is not; it supports an equal assignment of salary proposals to the three workers. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The algorithm and three simulations provide no running-time or complexity result either. The paper’s continuous objects are salary allocations and preference probabilities; its society consists of exactly three named workers.

The best population-scale scenario is a large employer with many workers drawn from a small set of complete preference and constraint types. One could choose shift salaries \(w\), allocate type mass \(x_{tj}\) to shifts, enforce coverage, and minimize deviation from type preferences. That is a plausible high-multiplicity staffing problem, and the paper’s least-squares correction gives it a recognizable starting point. It still requires adding a growing workforce, type masses, coverage targets, and a population allocation problem. The paper provides no discrete complexity result whose high-multiplicity relaxation this would be. Its grid parameter \(q\) controls salary resolution, not workforce size; increasing the population does not relax the problem the article analyzes.

That distinction matters to the proposed extension’s value as a mirror. With many workers, the relevant questions would depend on the added staffing model: the coverage constraints, legal rules, schedule horizon, and how preferences vary with salary. Those may make a worthwhile computational social choice problem. They do not follow from the paper’s Sperner construction or its three-worker experiments, and their answers would characterize the new staffing model. The extension is plausible as a separate research direction; its plausibility does not establish a continuous mirror of this paper.

On the evidence here, that would be a new problem inspired by the application, not a continuous counterpart of a computational result in the paper.
