The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result that can serve as an anchor under your criteria. Theorem 4, proved here, is the closest result: for any finite number of agents with monotone disutilities over connected bundles of a finite path of indivisible chores, a connected EF1outer partition exists. It is an existence theorem with a constructive proof; it does not classify the complexity of finding such a partition. The paper’s conclusion explicitly says that complexity is unknown. Lemma 3 establishes an inequality used in the rounding proof, and Theorem 2 is Sperner’s lemma; neither gives a complexity result for an allocation problem.

A high-multiplicity setting might be plausible—for example, many workers with a small number of shared disutility types dividing a growing sequence of tasks into connected blocks. But turning that into a continuous-population problem would require specifying how the path and chore granularity scale, and what “up to one item” means in the limit. The paper supplies no named computational result to ground those choices. So I can’t give a compliant continuous problem per anchor or predict its complexity without inventing a research question beyond the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no continuous question anchored in a named computational result. The closest candidate is Theorem 4, but it proves existence; its proof is constructive without giving a complexity bound. The paper itself leaves the complexity of finding an EF1outer division open. That makes computation a plausible follow-up, but it is not a complexity result for a continuous mirror to reproduce.

The strongest proposed mirror would use many workers in a few shared disutility types, dividing a long ordered sequence of chore slots into connected bundles, and ask how efficiently to find an EF1outer allocation. That is a credible high-multiplicity regime; the paper’s small examples do not rule it out. The difficulty is that its defining guarantee operates at the scale of each individual bundle: an agent compares bundles, and the EF1outer allowance removes one boundary chore from that agent’s own bundle. Under a regular continuum model, removing a boundary point has zero cost, so the allowance disappears and the condition becomes envy-freeness. Preserving a nonzero “one chore” allowance would require a per-agent cell scale and enough ordering information to recover each person’s bundle. That may be a worthwhile new model, but it needs additional structure beyond a distribution over types.

So the best negative case is about fit, not impossibility: the paper’s proved result is existential, while the computation it flags is an open follow-up whose natural continuous form requires extra modeling choices. Continuous chore-cutting existence results are analytic relatives, not computational mirrors. I cannot rule out a compelling two-scale model that the authors would recognize; that is the weak point in a universal “no scenario” claim. Still, the case for this paper rests on a newly specified research question, rather than a computational result of the paper that continuization would transform.
