The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the required kind: no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity bound. It proposes the TPSMA algorithm and illustrates it on two instances, but does not formally establish the algorithm’s correctness or running time. The introduction’s cited NP-hardness result for maximum-cardinality weakly stable matching concerns a different, pairwise problem; it is not an anchor for this paper’s three-person satisfaction problem. So I cannot honestly supply an anchor-compliant case.

There is still a plausible continuous mirror of the paper’s central task. Consider a large residential programme that assigns people to rooms of three. Participants fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of cohorts—perhaps by programme, year, language, and schedule—and everyone in a cohort has the same ranking, ties, and acceptability judgments about the other cohorts. A cohort is a complete type for this model: its members are interchangeable in every respect used by the assignment. The regime might have thousands of residents but only dozens of types.

Call the candidate problem Continuous Three-Person Satisfactory Roommates. Its instance is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and, for each type \(t\), a ranking with ties and incomplete lists over the types. Treat an unlisted type as unacceptable, and consider instances that admit a feasible full assignment. To extend the paper’s rank-based satisfaction to cohorts, give type \(s\) the score \(u_t(s)=1-\sum_{r\succ_t s}\mu_r\): one minus the mass of cohorts that \(t\) ranks strictly above \(s\). Tied cohorts receive the same score.

For each acceptable room configuration \(H=\{t_1,t_2,t_3\}\), let \(x_H\ge 0\) be the mass of rooms of that composition. Require \(\sum_H a_{tH}x_H=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), where \(a_{tH}\) counts how many occupants of type \(t\) are in \(H\). Maximize

\[ \sum_H x_H\,50\sum_{i=1}^{3}\bigl(u_{t_i}(t_j)+u_{t_i}(t_k)\bigr), \]

where \(j,k\) are the other two occupants. This is the paper’s aggregate satisfaction objective, extended to type masses. Each person still receives one whole room; \(x_H\) describes what fraction of a cohort is assigned to each room composition.

I think the authors could recognize this as their problem: it still asks for a full partition into three-person rooms maximizing the members’ satisfaction, while preferences over cohort types express the ties and repeated profiles needed for a high-multiplicity regime. The continuous version has \(O(\tau^3)\) room variables and linear constraints, so with explicitly listed types it is an LP and is tractable. That makes it a credible Class A *candidate*, not a result proved by this paper. It also prompts a useful follow-up: compare the LP with the discrete version having integer cohort counts, including whether the discrete optimum can be rounded with a small loss.

The weak point is substantial: the paper ranks named individuals, while this mirror ranks cohort categories and uses a population-scale rank score. That is a considered co-modification for high multiplicity, but the paper neither proposes it nor establishes a result that survives it. The positive case is therefore that the paper’s underlying assignment objective admits a sensible continuous analogue—not that the paper supplies a qualifying result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. It gives no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a complexity bound for TPSRP or proving TPSMA correct. The NP-hardness and parameterized results in the introduction concern cited work on pairwise stable roommates, not this paper’s three-person satisfaction problem. That is the strongest objection, and under the requested standard it is decisive: a continuous room-assignment problem may be interesting, but it cannot stand in for a computational result this paper never establishes.

The proposed mass-transfer LP is a coherent new formulation, and a large residential programme with repeated preference cohorts is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But its variables and objective come from choices the analyst supplies: preferences over cohort types, a population-quantile score, and a sum of those scores over room configurations. The paper does not specify that optimization problem precisely enough to identify those choices as a continuization of one of its proved results. Its examples illustrate the proposed algorithm; they do not establish what it optimizes over all partitions or that it finds an optimum. Calling the LP tractable would not fill that gap.

So the negative case is strong against a *result-based mirror*, but it cannot honestly prove that no useful continuous roommate problem exists in any scenario. The cohort model could be worth studying on its own. Its weakness as a mirror is that it extends an underspecified task rather than continuizing a computational claim made by this paper.
