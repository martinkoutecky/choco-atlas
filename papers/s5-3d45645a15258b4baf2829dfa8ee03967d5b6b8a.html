The case FOR (proponent)

The honest limitation is decisive: this paper has no numbered computational-complexity result to anchor a formal positive case on. Its Theorems 1–6 characterize rules by axioms; they do not establish that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classify its complexity. The closest result is Proposition 3, proved here: on the conditionally lexicographic domain, an allocation is individually-good efficient if and only if it is Pareto efficient. Theorem 6, also proved here, says that a rule on that domain satisfies balancedness, individual-good efficiency, the worst-endowment lower bound, and drop strategy-proofness if and only if it is ATTC. Neither is a computational anchor. The paper’s NP-completeness statement about detecting Pareto improvements under additive preferences is attributed to other work, not given as a numbered result here.

There is nevertheless a plausible high-multiplicity setting: recurring shift exchanges among a large workforce, where many workers share the same preference representation, initial schedule pattern, and quota. A type would include all of those features. Slot classes and their supplies would scale with the population; each worker would still receive an integral bundle, while mass records how many workers of a type receive each bundle. This makes the population, rather than the bundle, the continuous object.

A concrete follow-up problem would be Mass Pareto-Improvement Search for Reallocation. An instance has finitely many slot classes \(J\), finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and, for each type \(t\), an endowment bundle \(e_t\) and a conditionally lexicographic preference \(P_t\), given by an LP tree. Slot supply is \(s_j=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[j\in e_t]\). A solution is a set of rational masses \(y_{t,B}\geq 0\), one for each type and integral bundle \(B\subseteq J\), such that \(\sum_B y_{t,B}=\mu_t\), total use of each slot class \(j\) is \(s_j\), and \(y_{t,B}>0\) only when \(|B|=|e_t|\) and \(B\mathrel{R_t}e_t\). The question is whether such a solution exists with positive mass assigned a bundle strictly preferred to its endowment. Proposition 3 is relevant context: in this domain, finding a Pareto improvement also addresses individual-good efficiency. But this search problem is not what Proposition 3 asserts.

For additive preferences, the mass version looks like a plausible flow or LP problem. For the full LP-tree domain, feasibility has columns for integral bundles, and the paper does not settle whether the resulting pricing problem is tractable. I would leave its complexity open rather than assign it a class on this evidence. The proposed mirror covers only the paper’s efficiency discussion; it does not continuize the TTC or ATTC characterizations, their incentive axioms, or their uniqueness results.

The weakest point is that this is a new computational question attached to the paper’s efficiency results, not a mirror of a named computational result in the paper. The repeated-shift scenario is credible, but the supply-by-slot-class model is a co-modification, and the authors may view the search problem as separate from their axiomatic contribution. So the positive case is a reasonable programme lead, but it does not satisfy the requested anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper’s main results are about axiomatic characterizations, not computational problems. Theorems 1–6 identify TTC or ATTC from properties such as efficiency, participation, and resistance to particular manipulations. They do not classify the complexity of finding an allocation or computing a rule. So there is no computational result here for ChoCo to continuize directly.

Theorem 6 is especially hard to carry over. A high-multiplicity shift-exchange setting is plausible, but it requires scaling object supplies and grouping copies of shifts into classes. The paper’s TTC and ATTC procedures instead follow ownership of named, indivisible objects through cycles among particular agents. A type-mass allocation could be defined, but it would need a new aggregate procedure. More fundamentally, a single agent has zero mass: changing that agent’s report cannot affect the aggregate allocation, so the paper’s drop and truncation incentive conditions become vacuous. Replacing individual deviations with deviations by a positive mass of a type would make them meaningful, but would change the axioms and hence the characterization being mirrored.

Proposition 3 offers the better foothold, and the proponent’s mass Pareto-improvement search is a coherent adjacent question. But Proposition 3 establishes that Pareto efficiency and individual-good efficiency coincide on the finite-agent conditionally lexicographic domain; it does not assert a search algorithm. The proposed mass problem adds type-level splitting and scaled object classes. Its feasibility constraints allow one type’s mass to receive several different bundles, whereas the paper’s trading-cycle argument concerns individual agents and their particular endowments. The proposition does not establish that the same equivalence survives that change. It would be a new aggregate-allocation problem, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

I would not claim that no sensible high-multiplicity reallocation model exists: recurring shift exchanges could support one. The negative case is strongest against the paper’s central characterization results. It is weaker against the proposed efficiency-search direction, which may be worth studying if the authors recognize the aggregate model as part of their problem.
