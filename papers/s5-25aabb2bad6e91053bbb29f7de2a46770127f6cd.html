The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for a proper anchor: it does not classify a problem as tractable or hard, or give an algorithm with a complexity bound. The closest result is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper: for a stochastic bankruptcy game, if every claimant’s utility is \(u_i(x^i)=\sum_{s\in S}\rho_s w(x_s^i)\), with objective state probabilities \(\rho_s\) and a differentiable concave function \(w\), then the allocation given by the Constrained Equal Awards rule belongs to the Weak Sequential Core. Theorem 4.2, also proved here, says that the Weak Sequential Core is nonempty for stochastic bankruptcy games. Both are stability or existence results, not computational results. Theorem 3.8 is a characterization, but the paper attributes it to Habis and Herings (2011). So I can offer a plausible research lead, but I cannot honestly present it as anchored to a computational result of this paper.

The best lead is a high-multiplicity version of Theorem 4.3’s stability question. Imagine a large insolvency with many claimants under a small number of standardized contracts. A type \(t\) records a claimant’s complete state-dependent claim vector \(d_t=(d_{t,s})_{s\in S}\) and utility; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of claimants. Give the instance rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-claimant claims \(d_{t,s}\), estate per claimant \(e_s\) in each state, and the state probabilities and utilities. The many claimants share types because they have the same claims and utility-relevant characteristics; their number can be large while the number of supported types \(\tau\) is small.

Call the problem High-Multiplicity Stochastic Bankruptcy Stability: find a state-contingent award \(x_{t,s}\) for each type that is feasible and lies in the Weak Sequential Core, allowing coalitions to select any measurable fraction of each type and defining credibility by the paper’s recursive rule. A coalition of mass \(\nu_t\leq\mu_t\) has worth in state \(s\)
\[ \max\left\{e_s-\sum_t(\mu_t-\nu_t)d_{t,s},\,0\right\}. \]
The solution is a stable allocation, not an allocation maximizing a separate objective. The natural candidate is the mass-weighted CEA allocation \(x_{t,s}=\min\{d_{t,s},\alpha_s\}\), where \(\alpha_s\) satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_t\min\{d_{t,s},\alpha_s\}=e_s\).

This lead covers Theorem 4.3 alone. Its continuous version looks tractable: compute \(\alpha_s\) by sorting the \(\tau\) claim levels in each state, then establish that fractional coalitions cannot create a credible deviation. Theorem 4.3 motivates that expectation, but does not prove the atomless version. That is the weak point: allowing fractional subcoalitions adds possible deviations, and the finite-player theorem does not automatically rule them out. More broadly, this is a plausible computational extension of the paper’s stability question—not a computational result the paper itself supplies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 4.3 is the paper’s main result about a named rule: under the stated utility assumptions, the Constrained Equal Awards allocation belongs to the Weak Sequential Core. Theorem 4.2 proves nonemptiness, while Theorem 3.8 is attributed to earlier work. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

The proposed creditor-class scenario is plausible: many bondholders can share complete claim and utility types. I would not dismiss it for lack of multiplicity, and high-multiplicity work would support its plausibility. But its proposed task—find a stable allocation under Theorem 4.3’s assumptions—turns that stability theorem into an input/output problem. If the intended question is instead how to compute or recognize the Weak Sequential Core for broader utility classes, that needs additional choices about how utilities are represented and what the algorithm must output. It becomes a new computational cooperative-game problem, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The distinction is not that the continuous answer might match the finite one; that could itself be a useful result. It is that the paper’s result concerns stability, and the extra question raised by fractional coalitions concerns extending the Weak Sequential Core to a population model. That may be worthwhile economics or game theory, but it does not supply the complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

So the negative case is strongest on scope: no result in this paper provides a computational anchor. It is weaker as a universal claim. The high-multiplicity bankruptcy setting is sensible, and a continuous stability extension could be valuable even if it falls outside this programme’s computational focus.
