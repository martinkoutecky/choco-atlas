The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest case with the required computational anchors: the paper contains no named result about computational complexity or algorithms. Its numbered results classify equilibria analytically.

The closest candidate is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: for \(n\ge3\) and almost all unimodal, log-concave voter distributions \(F\), there is a unique equilibrium up to candidate identities, with two entrants at the uniquely characterized locations \((\dot y_1,\dot y_2)\). Proposition 4, also proved here, gives a unique equilibrium for the \(h\)-AVR under a uniform voter distribution, with \(h\) entrants at the specified equally spaced locations. Neither result says an equilibrium-finding problem is in P, gives an algorithm, or establishes hardness.

A plausible follow-up would ask for an algorithm to find or certify a pure-strategy equilibrium from a finitely represented distribution of voter ideal points, with candidates choosing whether to enter and where to locate. But that would add a computational problem the paper does not study, and it would need a careful input model. The paper already models a unit-mass population with a continuous distribution of ideal policies, so it does not supply a discrete-population result whose continuous mirror these propositions could anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational result. Proposition 1 proves equilibrium existence and uniqueness for an already continuous, atomless unit-mass electorate with distribution \(F\). Proposition 4 gives an explicit equilibrium for a uniform electorate. Neither studies the complexity of an election problem or gives an algorithm. This paper has already taken the population continuum as its model; it does not give a discrete-population problem whose computational landscape a continuous mirror could chart.

The best version of Proposition 1 as an algorithmic question would be: given a finite encoding of \(F\), compute the equilibrium locations \((\dot y_1,\dot y_2)\), or certify that the equilibrium exists and is unique. But the proposition characterizes those locations through scalar quantile conditions, and the paper supplies no canonical encoding of \(F\), precision requirement, or computational task. Choosing those would create a new equilibrium-computation problem. That might be worthwhile, but it would computationalize an already continuous model rather than continuize a population in the programme’s sense. Proposition 4 is weaker still as an anchor: its equilibrium locations are given directly by a formula.

The paper’s discussion of external manipulation offers a stronger possible direction. One could let an outside actor choose which voter types to bribe or coerce, assign costs to those choices, and ask for the minimum cost that changes the election probability after candidates respond. That would use population masses and connect to the paper’s motivation. But the paper assumes a fixed, exogenous committed fraction; it does not specify a targeting decision, costs, or a computational objective. Adding those would define a new campaigning or bribery problem, not mirror one of the paper’s results. The same gap applies to proposed robustness questions: the paper gives comparative equilibrium claims, not an optimization problem over population changes.

So the case against is strong for these particular anchors: they offer no computational result to continuize, and their population is already continuous. It would be a mistake, though, to claim that no worthwhile computational question could be built from the paper. A costed mass-manipulation problem is plausible and close to its subject. The honest negative conclusion is narrower: the paper does not itself support a continuous *computational mirror*; it could inspire a new computational project.
