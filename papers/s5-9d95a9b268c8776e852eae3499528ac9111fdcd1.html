The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes a unique Owen-type value by QCE, FDSU, QDL, and UDL, and gives its formula. Theorem 2 characterizes a unique Kamijo-type value by QCE, QDL, UDL, COV, UNRGP, and UCPIIC, and gives its formula. Both are results about axioms and allocation rules; neither establishes a problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The paper gives no input model or complexity analysis for computing those values.

There is a plausible population setting in the paper’s examples: many users grouped by city, or many residents grouped by institution, with a small number of shared local roles and communication patterns. Mass could represent the fraction of users of each type. But the paper’s values depend on individual coalition worths and graph components, and it does not show how to represent those structures by a type distribution while preserving the questions the authors study.

So the strongest honest positive case is only that the application stories invite a continuous-population model. There is no named computational result to anchor it, and therefore no per-anchor continuous problem or expected complexity classification I can responsibly give. A computational study of the population versions of these allocation problems could be worthwhile, but it would be a new question rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize allocation rules through axioms and give formulas. The other stated results are also characterizations; the paper names no computational problem and proves no complexity bound or algorithmic theorem. Its closing remark about choosing computationally efficient combinations is a motivation, not a result. So the proponent’s city example suggests a possible population regime, but it cannot anchor a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The best reconstruction would define a high multiplicity version of the games and ask how efficiently to compute the resulting allocations. That requires choosing how coalition worths and communication links are represented. An arbitrary TU game assigns a worth to every coalition, and a distribution over agent types does not capture those interactions by itself. The paper also allows varied communication graphs, whose individual links and connected components matter to its axioms. A useful population model would therefore need further structure, such as worths determined by type counts and a specified block network. Those are reasonable modeling choices, but the paper gives no computational question that selects among them. With an explicit table of coalition worths, complexity depends on the table’s size; a succinct representation would define a new input model.

This rules the paper out as a strong mirror candidate, though it does not establish that every continuous study of these values would be worthless. A count-based, networked TU-game model could support a worthwhile computational project. The honest negative case is narrower: that project would introduce a new computational question, rather than continuize a named computational result of this paper.
