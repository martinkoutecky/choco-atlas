The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s problem of finding a welfare-optimal decomposable allocation. A plausible regime is a large membership-based foundation or cooperative that divides a public budget among projects, where members choose from a fixed library of allocation plans. With, say, hundreds of thousands of members but only dozens or hundreds of distinct plans, a type is a member’s complete reported allocation vector \(p_t\in\Delta^{(m-1)}\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of members reporting that plan. The paper’s weighted setting, including its donor-coordination interpretation, makes this a credible extension: aggregate members with identical preferences and scale their total entitlement by their mass.

Here is the lead problem, Continuous Decomposable Welfare Threshold (C-DWT). An instance gives \(m\) alternatives, a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) rational preference vectors \(p_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(Q\). A solution consists of type-level contributions \(c_{t,j}\ge 0\) and an aggregate allocation \(a\), where \(\sum_j c_{t,j}=\mu_t\), \(a_j=\sum_t c_{t,j}\), and \(a_j\le p_{t,j}\) whenever \(c_{t,j}>0\). It must also satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j\min(a_j,p_{t,j})\ge Q\). The question is whether such a solution exists. Mass is each type’s share of the population and, correspondingly, its total budget entitlement. The objective is the paper’s utilitarian welfare divided by population size.

My anchor is Theorem 3 in §5, proved in this paper. It states that DecomposableWelfareThreshold is NP-complete: given a finite voter profile \(P\) and rational \(C\), decide whether there is a decomposable allocation \(a\), with voter contributions certifying decomposability, whose social welfare is at least \(C\). It also concludes that computing the outcome of \(\mathrm{UtilDecomp}\) is NP-hard. The authors prove the theorem by reduction from restricted Exact Cover by 3-Sets, whose NP-completeness they cite from Gonzalez (1985). This is the right anchor because it is a hardness result about the underlying allocation problem, not merely a fractional-outcome or encoding result.

I expect C-DWT to be hardness that transfers. If a finite profile has \(n_t\) voters of each type, set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(Q=C/n\). Aggregating those voters’ contributions gives a C-DWT solution with exactly the same feasibility condition and welfare ratio. Conversely, a type’s total contribution can be divided among its \(n_t\) identical voters, each with entitlement \(1/n\). Thus the paper’s NP-hard instances embed exactly. With rational input, C-DWT should also be in NP: guess which type-alternative contributions are positive, then certify feasibility and the welfare threshold with rational values. That suggests NP-completeness for the proposed problem, although the paper itself proves the finite-profile theorem, not this compressed formulation.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: the population is now represented by masses of indistinguishable voters, while their decomposability condition and welfare objective are preserved at the type level. The continuous simplex of outcomes is already in their model; the mirror’s continuous object is the society \(\mu\). The scope here is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 3’s welfare optimization under decomposability, not the paper’s moving-phantom, truthfulness, or mechanism-dominance results.

The weak point is the strength of the high-multiplicity evidence. In the paper’s X3C reduction, the number of distinct types still grows with the reduction instance; grouping duplicate voters compresses it, but does not show hardness when \(\tau\) stays small as the population grows. So the reduction supports the general type-mass problem and points to further questions: whether C-DWT is tractable or fixed-parameter tractable for small \(\tau\), whether hardness can be shown in a regime with \(\tau\ll n\), and what approximation guarantees extend to rational type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this anchor is that the paper already contains its proposed population model in weighted form. In Appendix E, a voter of weight \(\omega_i\) receives entitlement \(\omega_i/b\), where \(b\) is total weight; decomposability uses those entitlements, and welfare weights each voter’s utility by \(\omega_i\). Grouping weighted voters with the same preference vector \(p_t\) gives precisely the proposed type mass \(\mu_t\). The contribution constraint and normalized welfare become
\[ \sum_j c_{t,j}=\mu_t,\qquad a_j=\sum_t c_{t,j},\qquad a_j\le p_{t,j}\ \text{when }c_{t,j}>0, \]
and \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j\min(a_j,p_{t,j})\). Conversely, for rational masses, choose a common denominator \(b\) and give one weighted voter of type \(t\) weight \(b\mu_t\). The threshold \(Q\) is just the weighted threshold \(bQ\).

The paper says its results, including the hardness of welfare-optimal decomposable allocation, extend to weighted profiles. So C-DWT is an exact type-level restatement of the paper’s weighted problem. Its NP-hardness is worth knowing, but it does not establish a new continuous mirror; the paper has already crossed the population-weighting bridge for this anchor. The fact that the paper also mentions real-valued weights for its mechanisms further narrows the modeling gap.

This defeats the specific anchor and its stronger type-mass formulation as a *new* mirror. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. For example, a complexity result parameterized by support size \(\tau\) would ask something the cited weighted hardness statement does not settle. And the proposed finite-library setting makes a continuous population sensible. The negative case is therefore strong on novelty collision, but weak as an argument against every possible mirror.
