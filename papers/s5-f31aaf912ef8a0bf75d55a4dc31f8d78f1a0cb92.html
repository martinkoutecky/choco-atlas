The continuous mirror question

Fix a path \(P_m\) with \(m\ge 4\), and let \(T=V(P_m)\times\{\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\}\}\). For each population size \(q\), consider \(q\) agents at public path nodes, allowing repeated locations, who report approval sets; represent the population by its empirical distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). A unilateral report changes mass \(1/q\) between types at the same node. Let \(\rho_q(P_m)\) be the infimum, over deterministic strategyproof mechanisms on these profiles, of the worst-case ratio \(\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(M(\mu))/\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\), with the mechanism required to be optimal whenever \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=0\). Determine \(\liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(P_m)\); in particular, does the asymptotic best ratio remain \(4/3\), or improve?

The model it lives in

For each \(q\), the input is a distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t=(v,A)\), with masses that are multiples of \(1/q\). A deterministic mechanism maps each such profile to distinct facility nodes and must satisfy individual strategyproofness when one agent changes their reported approval set. The objective is to minimize the worst-case social-cost ratio against the optimal distinct-node placement, then study the limit inferior of that best ratio as \(q\) grows.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is an asymptotic high-multiplicity version of the mechanism-design problem. The paper has no named running-time or decision-complexity theorem—nothing establishing that a problem is in P or NP-hard. It does, however, have named constructive approximation results for deterministic strategyproof mechanisms. Theorem 4.10 is the best lead among those: it gives a lower bound on the approximation achievable by such mechanisms.

For a fixed graph \(G=(V,E)\), let a type be \((v,A)\), where \(v\in V\) is an agent’s public location and \(A\in\{\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\}\}\) is their approval set. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types. Its social cost at distinct facility locations \(z_1,z_2\) is \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(z_1,z_2)=\sum_{v,A}\mu_{v,A}\sum_{k\in A}d(v,z_k)\); the objective is to minimize this over \(z_1\ne z_2\).

To retain the paper’s individual strategyproofness, define the mechanism first on populations of size \(q\), so every mass is a multiple of \(1/q\). A unilateral report changes \(\mu\) by moving \(1/q\) mass from \((v,A)\) to \((v,A')\), and the agent’s true cost is still \(\sum_{k\in A}d(v,z_k)\). Let \(\rho_q(G)\) be the best worst-case approximation ratio over deterministic strategyproof mechanisms on that domain; the continuous question is to determine \(\liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(G)\). Here \(q\) can grow while the number of types stays at most \(3|V|\). A plausible regime is a city network whose nodes represent neighborhoods: many residents share a node and an approval type.

Theorem 4.10, proved here, is the lead anchor. It states that on connected graphs every deterministic strategyproof mechanism has approximation ratio at least \(3/2\); the lower bound is witnessed by the claw \(K_{1,3}\) with one agent at each node. Its continuous mirror is: determine \(\liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(K_{1,3})\), and in particular whether the \(3/2\) lower bound persists when many agents share each location-and-approval type. A solution would give matching asymptotic strategyproof mechanisms and a lower-bound proof. I expect the claw’s outcome geometry and strategic constraints to leave a nontrivial lower bound, so hardness transfer (Class B) is plausible; the four-agent proof does not itself establish that, since its deviations move a whole profile type whereas the scaled model permits one-agent changes.

A second anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved here: on the line, for \(n\ge4\), there is a deterministic strategyproof mechanism with approximation ratio at most \(4/3\). The mirror is to determine \(\liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(P_m)\) for each fixed path \(P_m\), asking whether \(4/3\) remains attainable or the asymptotic ratio improves. The paper’s fixed-parity median construction makes this a recognizable continuation of its problem. I would provisionally expect Class A for fixed paths, since the outcome geometry is simple and the input has only \(3m\) types, though the paper does not prove that the mechanism or its analysis extends to repeated occupancy.

The authors could recognize these as mirrors because the graph, two heterogeneous facilities, approval reports, individual incentives, distinct facility locations, and social-cost objective are all retained. The co-modification is to allow multiple residents at a public node; that is what makes \(q\) grow on a fixed graph while the type count stays small.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper assumes agents occupy distinct nodes. Its lower bounds on optimal cost use that assumption, so they cannot simply be reused when residents may share a node. Also, as \(q\) grows, a single report changes only \(1/q\) of the society; preserving exact strategyproofness in that limit is precisely the unresolved issue. This makes the mirror a real research question, but it means the paper’s \(3/2\) and \(4/3\) guarantees are anchors for the questions, not answers to them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither anchor is a computational complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to continuize. Theorem 4.10 gives a lower bound on the approximation ratio of strategyproof mechanisms; Theorem 3.1 gives an upper bound. Neither studies the complexity of a decision or optimization problem, or an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm for one. The charitable mirror would ask for the best asymptotic distortion achievable by strategyproof mechanisms. That is a coherent mechanism-design question, but it is a new question about the class of permissible rules, not a continuous version of a computational problem this paper analyzes.

Both proposed anchors also run into the same difficulty: individual strategyproofness needs an agent whose report can affect the outcome. On an atomless society, a single agent has zero mass, so changing their report leaves the distribution unchanged. Every rule is then strategyproof in this individual sense; choosing a socially optimal outcome gives distortion \(1\). To keep incentives meaningful, one must retain finite populations and ask about a sequence such as \(\liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(G)\), where a report moves \(1/q\) mass. That sequence may be worth studying, but it keeps the atomic strategic model at every finite \(q\). It is not the usual continuous-population problem, and the paper’s finite-profile arguments do not establish anything about its limit.

For Theorem 4.10, the witness is exactly one agent at each of the four nodes of \(K_{1,3}\). Increasing the population on a fixed claw requires allowing multiple agents at the same node, which changes the paper’s distinct-position assumption. Its anchor profiles and unilateral-deviation chains use whole-agent changes; after normalization, those changes have mass \(1/q\), and the proof supplies no lower bound that survives as \(q\) grows. Alternatively, growing the graph to retain distinct positions makes location part of each agent’s complete type, so the number of types grows with the population. A weighted-claw version is plausible, but its asymptotic incentive constraints and distortion would need new analysis.

Theorem 3.1 has a parallel problem. On a fixed path \(P_m\), distinct occupancy caps the population at \(m\); scaling the population therefore again requires repeated occupancy or a growing path. Repeated occupancy changes the cost bounds that support the parity-median analysis: a parity rounding loss depends on how much mass sits at the median, not simply on an additive constant of at most two. Growing the path makes location types grow with the population. In either version, the theorem’s \(4/3\) guarantee is a starting point for a new mechanism-design analysis, not a transferred algorithmic result.

This negative case is weaker than a claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The objective is anonymous and additive, and a city-neighborhood interpretation with many residents at each location is credible. A weighted facility-location model could therefore be a sensible object. But its natural question is the asymptotic distortion of strategyproof mechanisms, while the paper itself contributes distortion bounds rather than computational complexity results. That leaves a real possible research direction, but a poor fit for the continuization programme as stated.
