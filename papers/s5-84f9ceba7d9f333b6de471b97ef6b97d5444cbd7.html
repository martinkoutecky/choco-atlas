What the mirror covers

The paper's results concern stochastic convergence, diffusion, and steady states in finite and arbitrarily large networks; none is computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case for a continuous angle is that the paper already treats large societies as meaningful: in Section 4 it studies sequences of growing networks and asks when an action spreads to a non-negligible share of the population. A plausible application would be technology adoption among large cohorts whose members share an action, payoff distribution, and bounded local network role. Mass would mean the fraction of agents in each role and action. Preserving the network roles matters: a distribution over actions alone would lose the information flows that drive the model.

The closest named result is Proposition 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that in an arbitrarily large network, if exactly one action is initially chosen by a non-negligible share of the population, and agents follow the imitate-the-best rule with bounded neighborhoods, then the network converges with probability one to the monomorphic steady state in which everyone chooses that action.

That proposition supports the plausibility of studying a continuum-population version of the paper’s diffusion question. But it is an analytical convergence result, not a computational result: the paper defines no input decision or optimization problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic classification. Its other named results are also about stochastic dynamics and convergence. So there is no qualifying computational anchor here, and I cannot honestly give the requested anchor-backed continuous problem or predict its place in the tractable/hardness trichotomy.

One could formulate new computational questions about diffusion in finite-role population models, but those would be follow-on problems, not mirrors of computational results in this paper. The strongest honest positive case is therefore for an analytical continuous-population analogue; under ChoCo’s computational scope, the paper supplies no anchor for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a category mismatch: this paper proves stochastic convergence properties; it poses no computational problem. Its theorems and propositions ask what happens over time in a network, not for an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an optimization over a population distribution. That leaves no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror.

Proposition 4.1 is the strongest anchor, and a continuous population model for it is quite plausible. A type could record an agent’s action, payoff distribution, and bounded-neighborhood role, with mass assigned to each role-action pair and links between roles preserving who observes whom. That would capture a natural large-cohort society. But the resulting question—whether an initially prevalent action takes over—is still an analytical question about stochastic dynamics, which is exactly what the proposition studies.

One could add a computational task: for example, find the smallest initial mass that ensures diffusion, or choose whom to seed. Those could be worthwhile projects, but they add an objective or a decision to the paper’s model. They are not mirrors of Proposition 4.1. Preserving the network structure may require a richer continuous object, such as masses of roles and links; the paper’s key contrast between bounded and unbounded neighborhoods makes that structure matter. This is a legitimate co-modification of the population model, but it does not supply the missing computational result.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper supports a continuous-population analogue, but supplies no computational anchor for a continuous mirror. I cannot honestly extend that into a claim that no useful computational project could ever be built from this model. Such a project would be new work inspired by the paper, rather than a continuization of a computational result it contains.
