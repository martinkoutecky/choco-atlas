The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest ChoCo case from this paper’s named results: it contains no theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally complex. Its main result, Theorem 1, is proved here and says that, for a tree \(G\) and a minimally rich domain \(D\), every extreme rule—projecting a fixed leaf onto the path hull of reported peaks—is strategy-proof on \(D\) if and only if every preference in \(D\) is single-peaked on \(G\). That is a characterization of incentives and preference domains, not a complexity result.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large electorate with \(n\) voters but only \(\tau \ll n\) distinct complete rankings, represented by masses \(\mu_t\). The continuous extreme rule could select the projection of its designated leaf onto the path hull of peaks with positive mass. But Theorem 1 concerns a single voter changing their report. In an atomless society, one voter has zero mass, so that deviation cannot affect the outcome; allowing a positive mass to change reports instead asks about coalitional manipulation, which the theorem does not characterize. Keeping voters as atoms preserves the original question but does not provide the population continuum the programme requires.

So the closest mirror has a sensible electorate regime, but no qualifying named anchor and no non-vacuous continuous version of the paper’s central incentive question that Theorem 1 supports. Under the requested criterion, the honest positive-side finding is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper names no computational problem whose complexity it studies. Theorem 1 characterizes a preference domain: for a minimally rich \(D\), every extreme rule is strategy-proof exactly when \(D\) is single-peaked on the tree. That is an incentive characterization, not a result about algorithms, hardness, or computational complexity. The large-electorate regime is plausible, but plausibility of high multiplicity does not supply the missing computational question.

The closest population mirror makes the issue clear. Let \(\mu\) distribute mass over rankings, and let an extreme rule project its designated leaf onto the hull of the peaks with positive mass. In an atomless society, one voter’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Strategy-proofness then holds regardless of whether the available rankings are single-peaked, so the characterization loses its content. Giving a voter positive mass preserves the possibility of a unilateral deviation only by retaining an atom; as that atom shrinks with population size, its effect disappears in the continuum.

The best repair is to ask whether a positive-mass coalition can manipulate, or how much mass must change reports to alter the outcome. Those can be meaningful questions, but they replace the paper’s unilateral incentive condition with a coalitional one. The rule also depends on whether a peak type has any mass at all: an arbitrarily small positive mass can add a peak to the hull, while eliminating a peak may require moving all mass of that type. The paper’s theorem does not characterize these mass deviations, and studying their computational complexity would be a new project rather than a continuous version of its result.

One could instead ask whether the rankings in a society’s support form a single-peaked domain on a supplied tree. That is a computational question one can pose, but it extracts a recognition task from the characterization; it does not mirror a computational result in the paper. So the negative case is strong on ChoCo’s scope, though it cannot rule out every worthwhile project inspired by the model: a study of mass manipulation could stand on its own.
