The continuous mirror question

Given a catalog \(J=[m]\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita item supplies \(b_j\), and explicit monotone submodular valuations \(u_t:2^{R_t}\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) with \(|R_t|\le15\), find an allocation maximizing total welfare. Represent it by masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) assigned to integral bundles \(S\subseteq R_t\), subject to \(\sum_{S\subseteq R_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j\) for every item \(j\); maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}u_t(S)\).

The model it lives in

A distribution of population mass over complete valuation types, with per-capita supplies of interchangeable items. Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle; the decision variables \(x_{t,S}\) split each type's mass among bundles, and the objective is total welfare.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.4's APX-hardness result for constant-size utility functions, using support size at most 15. It leaves Theorem 1.2's rounding result, the integrality-gap examples, and the paper's other hardness cases unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does tractability persist when type valuations have unbounded relevant support or are given through demand oracles?
  • How does the additive loss when rounding to a finite population depend on the number of types \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 1.4. It says that Submodular Welfare is APX-hard: for some constant \(\rho<1\), it is NP-hard to approximate the maximum welfare within a ratio better than \(\rho\). The hardness still holds when every player’s utility depends on only a constant number of items; the paper’s concrete construction uses support size \(15\). The reduction is proved here in Section 4.3, using the 3-coloring gap in Lemma 4.2, which the authors cite from [8].

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large buyer population choosing from a fixed catalog of product categories. There are \(N\) buyers but only \(\tau\) standardized valuation types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Each category has \(N b_j\) interchangeable copies, for a fixed per-capita supply \(b_j\); some categories are scarce. Each buyer still receives an integral set of categories, and a type’s utility is monotone submodular and depends on at most \(15\) categories. The type records the complete valuation table and its relevant categories, so agents of a type are indistinguishable for everything the allocation problem uses. Replicating category supplies with population size is a co-modification of the model, but it keeps the welfare-allocation question intact and gives the market nonzero resources per capita.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover, High-Multiplicity Submodular Welfare. The input is a catalog \(J=[m]\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\in[0,1]\), and \(\tau\) explicitly described types. Type \(t\) has a relevant category set \(R_t\subseteq J\), \(|R_t|\le15\), and an explicit monotone submodular valuation \(u_t:2^{R_t}\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\); categories outside \(R_t\) have zero value. Find an allocation of the population to integral bundles that maximizes total welfare, subject to each type receiving its full mass and no category being allocated to more than its available supply.

Concretely, a solution can specify masses \(x_{t,S}\) of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(S\subseteq R_t\). They must satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq R_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}\le b_j, \]
and maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}u_t(S)\). These masses mean that different members of a type receive different *integral* bundles; no individual receives a fractional item. This is the allocation problem itself, not merely an LP encoding.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. Each type has at most \(2^{15}\) relevant bundles, so the allocation can be optimized by an explicit rational LP with \(O(\tau 2^{15})\) bundle variables and \(m+\tau\) constraints. It is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) includes the bit lengths of the masses, supplies, and valuations. For a finite population with counts \(N\mu_t\) and stocks \(Nb_j\), mass assignments can be rounded down bundle by bundle and the leftover members assigned the empty bundle. With \(\tau\) fixed and bounded per-agent utilities, this loses only an additive amount independent of \(N\), so the per-capita loss vanishes as the population grows.

This mirrors the paper’s restricted hardness result: the objective is still total submodular welfare from assigning disjoint, indivisible bundles, while the continuous population lets a type’s mass be split among bundles. The consequential question is whether the discrete APX-hardness survives that high-multiplicity relaxation; here the bounded support makes the relaxed problem exactly solvable. The further boundary questions are whether tractability persists for general submodular type valuations without bounded support, especially under demand-oracle input, and how much finite-population error remains as \(\tau\) grows.

The weakest point is that the paper’s hardness construction does not itself exhibit a small type catalog: its graph-based players have local, instance-specific utility supports. My proposed market is a different, plausible regime for the same allocation problem, not a limit of that reduction. That makes the mirror less direct than one built from repeated copies of the paper’s hard instances, but it does not make high multiplicity artificial for welfare allocation. I am only claiming a mirror of Theorem 1.4’s constant-size-utility hardness; I am not stretching it to the paper’s rounding theorem or its other results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1.4, and it is a genuine computational result. The strongest case against this mirror is that the paper’s hard construction does not carry over: each vertex player’s 15 relevant items are tied to that vertex’s incident edges, so those players are generally distinct complete types. Keeping the original unique items also makes each item’s supply vanish per capita as the population grows. To avoid that, the proposed model replicates category supplies and groups buyers with identical valuations. That produces a different instance regime, and the graph reduction gives no evidence that its hardness mechanism survives there.

That weakens the connection, but it does not defeat the mirror. A large market with repeated buyers, a fixed catalog, and standardized valuations is plausible; scaling supplies with population is the kind of co-modification the programme allows. The model really does continuize the population: each buyer still receives an integral bundle, while a type’s mass can be distributed across bundles. With at most 15 relevant categories per type, there are at most \(2^{15}\) bundle variables per type, so the mass-allocation problem has an explicit polynomial-size LP. That is a clear tractability question about the high-multiplicity version of welfare maximization.

Calling the LP routine or observing that it convexifies the allocation problem is not enough to reject it under the programme’s criteria. It does mean the mirror is less direct than a limit of the paper’s hard instances, and the bounded-support assumption makes the tractable version narrow. But the obvious stronger question—what happens for general submodular type valuations, or under different oracle models—still has computational content. A dull or already familiar answer would affect what is worth proving, not whether the continuous question is well-posed.

So the honest negative case is weak: it can challenge the directness and likely novelty of this particular mirror, but it cannot rule out a worthwhile scenario. Theorem 1.4’s anchor survives.
