The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a result-anchored case for a continuous mirror from this paper: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as tractability or hardness. Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2 describe sequential procedures, and the paper reports numerical performance, but neither is a named complexity result.

The paper’s continuous objects are the proposal space \([0,1]^D\) and distributions over a single user’s latent parameters—not a continuous society. A population of users sharing finitely many preference-model types could motivate a new high-multiplicity preference-learning problem, but it would not be anchored in a qualifying result here. So there are no per-anchor mirror questions to classify as tractable or hard under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is decisive for a paper-anchored mirror: there is no named result here that establishes the complexity of a computational problem. The maximin strategy in Section 3.3 is an information-theoretic claim about learning one user’s unknown parameters. Algorithms 1 and 2 give approximate procedures, and the paper reports simulations and a user study; it does not analyze the complexity of an input-defined problem. So there are no qualifying result anchors, and no continuous questions attached to them to defend.

The best stronger candidate is a population version of preference learning: let \(\mu\) describe users’ preference parameters \(\phi=(\theta,\Lambda)\), then choose comparisons to learn either users’ individual preferences or the population distribution. That could be a worthwhile research question. But it changes the paper’s objective. Its agent repeatedly queries one user to estimate that person’s preferred setting. Learning \(\mu\) does not by itself recover each user’s setting; retaining individual histories restores the identity-specific problem. If users share the same \(\phi\), pooling their responses reduces to collecting more observations of one common model. If their parameters differ, the population-level inference problem is new, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The posterior over \(\phi\) in the paper is uncertainty about one user, not a society of users.

I would therefore reject this paper as an anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme. I would not claim the stronger universal statement that no scenario could make population preference learning worthwhile: a sufficiently large cohort with a shared or modeled distribution of preferences is plausible. The negative case is strong about the absence of a paper-anchored mirror, and weaker about ruling out every worthwhile adjacent problem.
