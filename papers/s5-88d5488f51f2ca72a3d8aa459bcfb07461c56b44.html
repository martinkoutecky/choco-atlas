The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a committee size \(q\), a finite support \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^{C}\) of approval types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(B\geq 0\), does there exist a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=q\), such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_H(\mathbf{1}_W,t)^2\leq B\)?

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over approval-ballot types \(t\in\{0,1\}^{C}\); the decision is an integral committee \(W\) of size \(q\), and its objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t d_H(\mathbf{1}_W,t)^2\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s approval-committee problem. It already treats a committee election as minimizing the Hamming-distance \(p\)-norm from voters’ approval ballots, so this stays close to the authors’ own question.

My anchor is Corollary 2, proved in this paper. It states that for every fixed rational \(p>1\), p-Norm Approval Committee is NP-hard and, unless ETH fails, admits neither a \(2^{o(\hat n+\hat m)}|I'|^{O(1)}\)-time algorithm nor a \(2^{o(k^{p/(p+1)})}|I'|^{O(1)}\)-time algorithm. Here \(\hat n\) is the number of candidates, \(\hat m\) the number of voters, and \(k\) the \(p\)-norm bound. The corollary follows from the paper’s hardness construction with the committee constrained to have exactly the prescribed number of members.

For a clean continuous version, I would take the \(p=2\) case. Given candidates \(C\), a committee size \(q\), a finite support \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^{C}\) of approval types, rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(B\geq 0\), ask whether there is a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=q\), such that
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,d_H(\mathbf 1_W,t)^2\leq B. \]
A solution is the committee \(W\). Each type is a complete approval ballot; its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ballot. The objective is the mean squared Hamming distance, equivalently the square of the per-capita RMS distance.

This is a natural problem for a large approval election with many voters but relatively few distinct ballots: for example, a large electorate voting on a fixed slate where approval patterns recur across blocs. The same approval set makes voters indistinguishable for the paper’s objective, so grouping them into types preserves everything the problem uses. This is a property of that election regime, not of elections generally.

The hardness transfers exactly. If a discrete electorate has \(N_t\) voters of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=N_t/N\), where \(N=\sum_t N_t\). Then
\[ \sum_t\mu_t\,d_H(\mathbf 1_W,t)^2 =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_H(\mathbf 1_W,s_i)^2. \]
Thus the paper’s \(p=2\) threshold \(k\) is equivalent to \(B=k^2/N\). Conversely, rational masses can be scaled to integer multiplicities. This is a high-multiplicity version of the same committee problem, not a change to the committee outcome or the question being asked. I expect hardness to transfer; the \(p=2\) instance of Corollary 2 gives the discrete hardness anchor.

This mirror covers the approval-committee result, not the paper’s unrestricted clustering problem or all its algorithmic results. It also suggests follow-up questions: whether the weighted version is fixed-parameter tractable in support size \(\tau=|T|\), and how the ETH lower bounds should be expressed in terms of candidate count, support size, and mass bit length.

My weakest point is that this case establishes a credible continuous-population problem more clearly than it establishes a tractability gain. The committee remains an integral subset, and the hardness construction’s combinatorics appear to grow with the candidate and type structure. That puts this anchor on the hardness-transfer side of the programme’s trichotomy; it does not show that continuization dissolves this hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed mirror changes the encoding more than the optimization problem. Let \(D\) be a common denominator of the rational masses, and form a discrete electorate with \(D\mu_t\) voters of each approval type \(t\). For every committee \(W\),
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,d_H(\mathbf 1_W,t)^2 =\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}d_H(\mathbf 1_W,s_i)^2. \]
Thus the proposed \(p=2\) question is exactly the paper’s committee problem with its threshold rescaled. Every rational society in this mirror is an empirical distribution of a finite electorate, and every finite electorate gives such a distribution.

That leaves a possible research contribution in the succinct high-multiplicity encoding: binary masses can describe electorates too large to list, and algorithms measured in support size \(\tau\) and bit length \(L\) may differ from algorithms measured in voter count. But that is an input-representation question, and the programme explicitly treats high multiplicity as supporting evidence for a mirror. The paper’s hardness result also already applies to the corresponding discrete instances, so this anchor offers no evident tractability gain.

I cannot honestly turn that into a decisive objection under the stated criteria. The proposed society is sensible: in a large approval election, many voters can share a ballot, and ballot type preserves everything this objective uses. The exact rescaling does not make the mirror already continuous in the paper’s model, and a known hardness answer is not a collision. The negative case is therefore weak; this anchor survives.
