The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rational belief-matrix types \(P^t\) on signal alphabet \([m]\), compute a shared payment matrix \(M\in[0,1]^{m\times m}\) maximizing \(\delta\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_j P^t_{s,j}(M(s,j)-M(x,j))\ge\delta\) for every \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\), signal \(s\), and alternative report \(x\ne s\). Return whether the optimum is positive, which is exactly whether one strictly truthful mechanism serves every type in the society.

The model it lives in

Types are agents' complete conditional belief matrices \(P^t\); \(\mu_t\) is their population mass and the mechanism is a single pair-payment matrix \(M\). The LP maximizes a common strict-truthfulness margin over the supported types; the mass values select the support but do not weight the individual incentive constraints.

The objection that survived

The masses do not affect individual incentive constraints, so this mirror has no mass-sensitive objective and its LP size is driven by \(\tau\), not total population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's mechanism characterization for a finite support of belief-model types; it leaves Theorem 8's robustness formula, effort-incentive optimization, and data-driven power-diagram fitting outside the population feasibility problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow yes. This paper has no named NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity result. Its named results are geometric characterizations and mechanism-construction results. The mirrors below are therefore computational questions suggested by those results; where I claim tractability beyond what the paper proves, I say so.

My lead is Population-Robust Peer Prediction Synthesis, anchored in Theorem 8, proved here. The theorem says that, given the mechanism designer’s conditional belief model \(p(\cdot\mid s)\), the mechanism
\[ M(x_i,x_j)=p(x_j\mid x_i)-\frac12\sum_{s=1}^{m}p(s\mid x_i)^2 \]
is maximally robust to agents’ belief models deviating from \(p\). Its proof identifies the limiting robustness threshold as half the minimum Euclidean distance between two posterior rows: if
\[ d=\min_{s\ne s'}\|p(\cdot\mid s)-p(\cdot\mid s')\|_2, \]
the mechanism guarantees truthfulness for every deviation radius below \(d/2\), and no mechanism can guarantee a radius at or above that threshold.

The continuous mirror is: a large standardized crowdsourcing task has a nominal peer-belief model \(p\), while actual worker types \(t\) have conditional belief matrices \(P^t\). A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\); \(N\) workers may be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is moderate. Given \(p\), find one minimal pair-payment rule and the largest radius \(\varepsilon\) such that every worker in every such society reports truthfully whenever each row of each \(P^t\) lies within \(\varepsilon\) of the corresponding row of \(p\). The solution is the mechanism in Theorem 8 and the threshold \(d/2\). This is a tractable, closed-form case. The masses do not enter the guarantee because truthfulness is required for every worker, including those in a small cohort. That makes the mirror robust to different population compositions, rather than an optimization of average welfare.

A broader heterogeneous-population question is Truthful Mechanism Feasibility for a Type Distribution, anchored in Theorem 4, proved here. The theorem states that any minimal mechanism induces a power diagram over posterior beliefs, and conversely every power diagram can be induced by a minimal mechanism. Here the types are complete conditional belief models \(P^t\) for the shared signal alphabet \([m]\), and \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population fraction. Given the rational type list and its masses, ask whether there is one payment matrix \(M\) that makes truthful reporting strictly optimal for every positive-mass type after every signal, and return such a matrix if one exists.

For this finite-support version, strict truthfulness is a finite system of linear inequalities:
\[ \sum_j P^t_{s,j}\bigl(M(s,j)-M(x,j)\bigr)\ge\delta \quad \text{for every }t,\ s,\text{ and }x\ne s. \]
Normalize payments to \(M(x,j)\in[0,1]\) and maximize \(\delta\); a positive optimum gives a strictly truthful mechanism. This gives a direct LP with \(m^2\) payment variables and \(O(\tau m^2)\) constraints. I expect this mirror to be in \(P\), but that complexity claim is my derivation, not a result stated in the paper. The power-diagram characterization gives the geometric interpretation: one mechanism must assign each supported posterior to the cell for its observed signal.

These are plausible mirrors because the paper explicitly models a group of agents and allows their belief models to differ; the mechanism remains the same pairwise payment rule. Large annotation or survey tasks with recurring worker cohorts are a credible regime, and the complete type here includes the belief information the incentive constraints use. The first question covers the paper’s robust mechanism result; the second covers its characterization of truthful mechanisms. I would not extend the claim to every result in the paper. A natural follow-up is whether a single mechanism can maximize mass-weighted effort incentives across heterogeneous belief-model centers, subject to a payment budget.

The soft spot is that population mass is not where these results’ mathematical difficulty lies. Theorem 8 uses one nominal belief model and is unchanged when the workforce is represented by a continuum; the finite-support feasibility LP is a population-level formulation of per-agent incentive constraints. If every worker has a distinct belief model, then \(\tau\) may be as large as \(N\), and the finite-type regime offers little compression. Nor does this paper supply discrete hardness for continuization to dissolve. So the positive case is for a well-posed, recognizable continuous-population version of peer-prediction mechanism synthesis—not for a new complexity landscape across the whole paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither anchor makes population composition the computational object. I would not argue that crowdsourcing has no high multiplicity regime: recurring cohorts of workers on a standardized task are plausible. The problem is that repeating a type changes neither theorem’s central calculation.

Theorem 8 gives the supremal robustness radius \(d/2\), where \(d\) is the minimum distance between posterior rows for different signals. Its mechanism and guarantee depend on the signal alphabet and the nominal belief model, not on how many workers hold each belief model. If the requirement is that every positive-mass type report truthfully, the masses drop out; only the support matters. Adding more workers of a type does not change the robustness calculation. A population-weighted variant could optimize total effort incentives, but that changes the objective from the paper’s individual truthfulness guarantee. With finite support and a common payment matrix, the natural version has \(m^2\) payment variables and type-specific linear incentive constraints; the masses enter as weights in the objective. That may be useful mechanism design, but it does not expose a complexity question caused by a continuous population.

Theorem 4 characterizes truthful mechanisms by power diagrams over the simplex of possible posterior beliefs. That simplex is a continuous *belief space*, not a continuous population. Given a finite support of complete worker types, requiring one mechanism to truthfully elicit every type gives inequalities of the form
\[ \sum_j P^t_{s,j}\bigl(M(s,j)-M(x,j)\bigr)>0 \]
for each type \(t\), signal \(s\), and alternative report \(x\). After normalizing payments, maximizing a common strictness margin is a finite LP. Again, population mass affects only which types have positive mass, not the constraints. For an uncountable distribution of types, the computational question would depend on how that distribution is represented—something the paper does not specify.

The best mass-sensitive repair is to ask for a mechanism that truthfully incentivizes the largest fraction of a heterogeneous population, or maximizes mass-weighted effort subject to a budget. The first permits some workers to remain unincentivized; it becomes weighted fitting of a power diagram to labeled posterior data. The second changes the paper’s individual objective to an aggregate one. Both could be worthwhile adjacent problems, but neither is the result Theorems 4 or 8 establish. The paper itself points toward fitting power diagrams from labeled data, which makes the first repair look more like a learning extension than a missing continuous-population complexity landscape.

There is no numbered computational-complexity result here to transfer or dissolve. Theorem 4 is a geometric characterization, and Theorem 8 is a closed-form robustness result. A population distribution over complete belief types is sensible, but under the paper’s original guarantees it either reduces to checking its support or supplies weights for a newly chosen aggregate objective. That is why these anchors do not make a strong case for a ChoCo mirror. I cannot honestly rule out every useful population-aware follow-up; the case is weakest against weighted power-diagram fitting. But that follow-up changes the target, rather than revealing a population-complexity question latent in the paper’s results.
