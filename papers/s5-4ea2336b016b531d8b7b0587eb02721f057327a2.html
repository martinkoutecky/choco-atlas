The continuous mirror question

Given an agenda whose conclusions are positive monotone clauses of length two and their negations, a distribution \(\mu\) with binary-rational masses over complete consistent premise assignments, a consistent desired set \(J\), and a rational budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), decide whether there are transfers \(x_{t,u}\ge 0\) from supported types \(t\) to complete consistent destination types \(u\), with \(\sum_u x_{t,u}\le\mu_t\) for each \(t\) and \(\sum_{t,u\ne t}x_{t,u}\le\beta\). The resulting mass is \(\mu'_u=\mu_u-\sum_v x_{u,v}+\sum_t x_{t,u}\). Let \(F(\nu)\) accept a premise exactly when its mass under \(\nu\) exceeds \(1/2\), then derive conclusions as in the paper; decide whether \(|J\setminus F(\mu')|<|J\setminus F(\mu)|\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete, consistent assignments to the premises, so every conclusion judgment is determined by its formula. The briber chooses mass transfers \(x_{t,u}\) at unit cost per mass moved, with total moved mass at most \(\beta\); the objective is to strictly reduce the number of desired formulas missing from the collective majority outcome.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5 for UPQR Bribery with positive monotone clauses of length two. It leaves the fixed-budget result in Theorem 4, the Microbribery results in Theorems 6 and 7, and other results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this decision problem in agenda size \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does searching over reachable majority outcomes give continuum-specific hardness, or can it be handled by a polynomial-time method?
  • How does the mass problem relate to discrete instances with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(\beta=k/n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a mass version of UPQR Bribery. The strongest anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper by reduction from Optimal-Lobbying; the paper cites Christian et al. (2007) for that problem’s hardness. The theorem says that Bribery remains NP-hard, and is W[2]-hard when parameterized by the number \(k\) of judges bribed, even when every conclusion is a positive monotone clause of length \(2\). The reduction uses majority quota \(q=\tfrac12\). In the underlying problem, a briber changes up to \(k\) judges’ complete judgment sets and asks whether this strictly reduces Hamming distance from the desired collective judgment.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large pool of experts assessing the same short checklist of startup features, with many experts sharing the same complete pattern of judgments. There might be millions of evaluators but only \(\tau\) recurring judgment types. This fits the paper’s expert-evaluation and lobbyism setting, and the uniform cost of changing one judge gives the types a common bribery cost. The type is the expert’s complete, consistent judgment set: an assignment to the premises, with all conclusion judgments determined by the formulas.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance consists of the agenda from Theorem 5, with positive monotone two-literal conclusions; a baseline distribution \(\mu\) over complete judgment types, given by its \(\tau\) supported types and their binary-rational masses; a consistent desired set \(J\); and a rational budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), the fraction of the population that may be bribed. For each supported type \(t\) and any complete consistent destination type \(u\), choose transferred mass \(x_{t,u}\ge 0\), subject to
\[ \sum_u x_{t,u}\leq \mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t,u\ne t}x_{t,u}\leq \beta. \]
The resulting distribution is
\[ \mu'_u=\mu_u-\sum_v x_{u,v}+\sum_t x_{t,u}. \]
Under the continuous majority rule, a premise is accepted exactly when more than half the resulting mass accepts it; conclusions are then evaluated from those premises as in the paper. The question is whether some feasible transfer makes the Hamming distance from that collective outcome to \(J\) strictly smaller than it was under \(\mu\). Thus \(\beta\) is the per-capita counterpart of \(k\), and the objective and briber’s action remain those of the paper. Only the society has become a distribution.

I expect this to be a Class C candidate, though that is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 5. For any fixed collective premise outcome, the mass-transfer feasibility question is linear. The remaining choice among binary premise outcomes can still couple many formulas through Hamming improvement, leaving a combinatorial search over the agenda. Theorem 5’s reduction, however, relies on choosing whole judges, so its NP- and W[2]-hardness cannot simply be carried over: fractional mass may dissolve precisely that selection problem. A tractability result would be valuable too, perhaps by finding an efficient way to optimize over the reachable outcomes.

My weakest point is that the anchor’s clearest hardness mechanism is the integral choice of bribed judges, while the mirror deliberately removes that granularity. I therefore claim a credible continuous research question, not inherited hardness. The mirror covers Theorem 5 only. Natural follow-up questions are whether the mass version is polynomial-time solvable for these two-literal clauses, whether hardness persists when \(\tau\) is small, and how its answers relate to discrete instances with proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and normalized budgets \(k/n\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 5. It gives a named computational result, so the “no result to mirror” objection does not apply. Nor does bribery track particular people for their own sake: each judge’s complete judgment set captures everything the rule uses about them. A mass of interchangeable judges is therefore a coherent model.

The best negative case is about where this theorem’s hardness comes from and whether its high-multiplicity regime is compelling. The reduction from Optimal-Lobbying encodes matrix rows as judges and asks which \(k\) rows to alter. In a typical instance, those rows could all be distinct judgment types, so \(\tau\) may grow with the number of judges. The paper’s stated setting is an expert panel, where judges are individually appointed and large populations of experts with identical judgments may be unusual. That makes the proposed crowd-evaluation scenario do significant work: it is plausible, but it extends the application beyond the paper’s motivating committee.

A better mirror is still straightforward: take a pool of people repeatedly evaluating the same checklist, represent each complete premise assignment as a type, and let a briber transfer mass between types subject to a per-capita budget \(\beta\). The quota rule still produces a meaningful majority outcome, and Hamming distance still measures improvement. Prices or other individual differences could be included in the type. I do not see a fundamental modeling flaw in this version.

The reduction’s row-selection hardness does not establish that the mass-transfer version stays hard; fractional transfers may remove that mechanism. But that weakens a claim about the expected answer, not the case for asking the question. A tractability result would itself be useful under the programme’s stated aims.

So the negative case is weak. It can question how often large, repeated populations of judgment types arise in the paper’s expert-panel applications, but it cannot rule out the crowd-evaluation regime or show that the mirror is unworthwhile. For this sole anchor, I cannot honestly make the requested universal case against a continuous mirror.
