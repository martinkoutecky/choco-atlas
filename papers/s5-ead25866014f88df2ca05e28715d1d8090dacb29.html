The anchor — hardness

Theorem 4.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4.1 — survived: The opponent accepts that the fresh-agent argument extends to fresh mass: any stage with repeat allocations has a bad EFX comparison, and only finitely much mass can be fresh.
  • Corollary 4.2 — survived: The continuous low-success-probability statement yields the same zero expected guarantee by bounding the score on success and failure.

The continuous mirror question

Given a mass-one society with finitely many additive valuation types of rational masses and a fixed finite online stream of cohorts, where cohort \(r\) supplies mass \(q_r\) of identical indivisible goods, determine whether every randomized nonanticipating policy has worst-case expected population EFX score zero. A policy moves mass among type-and-integral-bundle states, assigning cohort \(r\)'s goods to mass \(q_r\) of agents without knowing later cohorts or the stopping horizon. Equivalently, for every policy and \(\alpha,p>0\), is there a fixed finite cohort stream with probability less than \(p\) of terminal EFX score at least \(\alpha\), where the score is the minimum EFX comparison over positive-mass agent states?

The model it lives in

A mass-one population with finitely many rational-mass types, each specifying a complete additive valuation; integral bundles remain indivisible at the individual level. The online state is \(x_r(t,B)\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents holding bundle \(B\); decisions \(a_r(t,B)\) move mass \(q_r\) to bundles augmented by the arriving good. The objective is the supremum over randomized nonanticipating policies of the infimum over fixed finite cohort streams of expected terminal population EFX score.

The objection that survived

The policy learns the current cohort size and can coordinate assignments within it, unlike the paper's item-by-item model; the fresh-mass argument supports the lower bound, but the exact correspondence between these online information structures is not formalized.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the EFX impossibility in Theorem 4.1 and the zero expected guarantee in Corollary 4.2, including their identical-valuation setting. It leaves the PROP1 results and the EF1 bound in Theorem 4.3 unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the zero-guarantee proof persist when identical copies are processed one by one without revealing the cohort size?
  • How does the guarantee change with multiple valuation types or if a small fraction of the population is excluded from EFX comparisons?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the paper’s EFX impossibility, not its PROP1 algorithm. Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper, says that for every \(n\ge2\), every randomized fully online algorithm \(A\), every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), and every \(p\in(0,1)\), there is a fixed finite input \(I\), chosen independently of \(A\)’s random choices, on which \(\Pr[\rho_{\mathrm{EFX}}(A,I)\ge\alpha]<p\). The theorem still holds when all agents have identical valuations. Corollary 4.2, also proved here, concludes that every such algorithm has worst-case expected realized EFX guarantee zero, even on identical-valuation inputs. These are named online algorithmic impossibility results, though the paper does not give a classical \(P\)-versus-\(NP\) classification.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Let the society have mass \(1\), divided among finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\). A type is a complete additive valuation function \(v_t\) over a finite catalogue \(\mathcal G\) of good classes. An input is a fixed, finite online stream \(((q_r,g_r))_{r=1}^K\): cohort \(r\) supplies \(q_r\in(0,1]\) mass of indivisible copies of class \(g_r\). In a finite realization with \(N\) agents, this means \(Nq_r\) identical goods arrive at that stage. The cohort is revealed when it arrives; the policy does not know later cohorts or the stopping horizon.

Represent the population’s state after stage \(r\) by \(x_r(t,B)\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents holding integral bundle \(B\). The policy’s action is \(a_r(t,B)\), the mass of agents in that state who each receive one copy. It must satisfy \(0\le a_r(t,B)\le x_{r-1}(t,B)\) and \(\sum_{t,B}a_r(t,B)=q_r\). The resulting state is \(x_r(t,B)=x_{r-1}(t,B)-a_r(t,B)+a_r(t,B-e_{g_r})\), omitting the last term when \(B\) contains no \(g_r\). Thus the decision variable moves mass between bundle states while every individual still receives whole goods.

Define population EFX as the worst EFX comparison between any two positive-mass type-and-bundle states: for an agent of type \(t\) with bundle \(B\), compare \(v_t(B)\) with \(v_t(B')-\min_{g\in B'}v_t(g)\) for every other positive-mass bundle state \(B'\), using the paper’s convention that a zero denominator gives score \(1\). The continuous problem is to determine whether
\[ \sup_{\pi}\inf_I \mathbb E[\rho_{\mathrm{EFX}}(x_K)] \]
is zero, where \(\pi\) ranges over randomized nonanticipating mass-allocation policies and \(I\) over fixed finite cohort streams. Equivalently, does every policy, for every \(\alpha,p>0\), admit a fixed stream with \(\Pr[\rho_{\mathrm{EFX}}(x_K)\ge\alpha]<p\)? The answer supplies either a policy with a positive worst-case guarantee or an impossibility proof.

The regime is a large online distribution program whose recipients fall into a small number of stable valuation groups and whose supplies arrive in bulk—for example, households with the same value schedule for successive categories of relief goods. In the anchor family there is only one type, so the number of agents tends to infinity while the number of types stays at \(1\). This is exactly the sort of high-multiplicity regime that makes the population-mass formulation natural.

I expect the negative result to transfer in a Class B-like way. The proof’s useful invariant is that each unit of agent mass can be “fresh” only once. With cohorts of mass \(q\), at most \(1/q\) cohorts can be assigned entirely to fresh agents. If any positive mass in a later cohort receives a good despite already holding one, sufficiently rapidly increasing values make the population EFX score small. Choosing an unknown stopping prefix from many possible stages should then reproduce the theorem’s fixed-input averaging argument. The likely further questions are whether this survives for several types, what changes when a small fraction of agents may be excluded from the fairness requirement, and how the best guarantee depends on total goods per capita.

The weakest point is the co-modification: the paper has a stream of individual arrivals, while this mirror groups high-multiplicity identical goods into cohorts and reveals cohort supply. The continuous state keeps bundles integral, but the online information structure is not identical. I think bulk supply is a credible version of the authors’ problem, especially because the impossibility anchor already holds for a single valuation type; I would not claim that Theorem 4.1 automatically proves the continuous statement. My proposed scope is just this EFX lower bound, not the paper’s PROP1 or EF1 results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed cohort model reveals each batch’s mass before allocation. In the paper, goods arrive one at a time, and the algorithm does not know where a run of identical goods ends. Batch disclosure gives the policy extra information and exact control over which mass receives each copy, so the proposed theorem would not automatically mirror Theorem 4.1.

That objection does not defeat the better version. Even with this extra control, the fresh-agent argument appears to transfer. Take one valuation type and cohorts of mass \(q<1\), with common values \(s_t\) chosen so that \(s_t>\epsilon^{-1}\sum_{h<t}s_h\). At any stage where positive mass receives the current good despite already holding an older good, there is also positive mass that did not receive it. The latter has value at most \(\sum_{h<t}s_h\); after removing the least-valued good from the repeated bundle, its EFX comparison still includes the current good, worth \(s_t\). The population EFX score is therefore below \(\epsilon\). A stage can avoid this only by giving its entire cohort to fresh agents. At most \(1/q\) stages can do that. If the stopping prefix is selected uniformly from \(K\) possible stages, a fixed policy succeeds on at most \(1/q\) of them; averaging over the policy’s randomness gives a fixed bad stream when \(K\) is large enough.

So the batch formulation needs justification as the authors’ intended online model, but its central impossibility is not obviously an artifact of that choice. An infinitesimal-arrival formulation or a more faithful per-item model looks capable of preserving the same mass-capacity obstruction. This is one well-posed population-continuous online question, anchored in a named algorithmic theorem. Its answer might be an easy transfer of the paper’s proof, but that is not a reason to dismiss the question. I cannot make an honest case that this anchor fails, and the proponent’s case for it remains strong.
