The continuous mirror question

Given a tree airline network, a coalition \(S\), and a finite support \(T\) of passenger types \(t=(o_t,d_t,r_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), let \(P_t\) be the unique route for type \(t\) and \(A(P_t)\) its carriers. Given rational per-capita leg capacities \(\kappa_e\), find a served-mass vector \(x\) maximizing \(\sum_{t:A(P_t)\subseteq S} r_t x_t\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:e\in P_t}x_t\le\kappa_e\) for each leg \(e\) owned by a carrier in \(S\).

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over passenger types defined by origin–destination pair and fare; the decision variable \(x_t\) is the served mass of each type, the objective is total revenue, and per-capita leg capacities constrain feasible service. The resulting rational LP is polynomial-time solvable in its encoded input size.

The objection that survived

The paper's base model has no finite passenger demand, so the population-bearing version is an extension rather than an exact relaxation of its perfectly elastic-demand model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the maximum-revenue optimization associated with Lemma 1 in a bounded-demand cohort setting; leaves the axiomatic sharing-rule characterizations, Shapley-value result, and core and Owen-set results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • As market size grows, what additive rounding bound relates this cohort LP's optimum to the finite integer-demand optimum in terms of \(|T|\) and \(|E|\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mirror of the paper’s maximum-revenue problem, not its axiomatic characterizations. The useful anchor is Lemma 1, proved in this paper: for any airline coalition, the integer program that maximizes revenue over feasible flights, with nonnegative integer flight quantities and integer leg capacities, can be solved by its LP relaxation. The proof claims that an optimal LP solution can be taken integral, so the integer and relaxed problems have the same optimum.

I would call the mirror Continuous Airline-Alliance Revenue Maximization. Its instance is a tree network whose legs are partitioned among airlines; a finite support \(T\) of passenger types, each an origin–destination pair with a fare \(r_t\); type masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) summing to one; and rational per-capita leg capacities \(\kappa_e\). A type’s route \(P_t\) is the unique path between its endpoints. For a given coalition \(S\), ask for an optimal served-mass vector \(x\): maximize \(\sum_{t:\,A(P_t)\subseteq S} r_t x_t\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:\,e\in P_t}x_t\le\kappa_e\) for every leg \(e\) owned by \(S\). A solution is the vector \(x\) and its maximum revenue.

The population in this model is a large passenger market: each type is a cohort with the same itinerary and fare, and its mass is its share of that market. Think of a seasonal booking market with many passengers but relatively few OD-and-fare types. Capacities scale with market size, so the variables are served shares rather than counts of named passengers. In the supply-rich regime where every type’s mass is at least the bottleneck capacity on its route, the demand bounds do not bind; that case matches the paper’s perfectly elastic demand assumption. The bounded version also models finite passenger cohorts directly.

This problem is tractable: it is a rational LP with \(\tau\) route variables and \(|E|\) capacity constraints, so standard polynomial-time LP algorithms apply in the input bit length. That is a direct tractability claim for the continuous underlying revenue problem, rather than for an encoding. It also makes the connection to Lemma 1 concrete: in the supply-rich regime, the continuous variables are precisely fractional seat quantities on the paper’s routes. The paper’s simplex-based algorithm is relevant as a proposed method, though simplex alone does not establish a worst-case polynomial bound. This mirror covers the maximum-revenue result; it does not continuize the paper’s axiomatic sharing rules.

The serious weakness is that Lemma 1’s integrality claim is false as stated. Consider a three-leaf star, with one airline owning each spoke, unit capacity on every spoke, and the three leaf-to-leaf routes having distinct revenues \(1.01\), \(1.00\), and \(0.99\). The capacity constraints are \(x_{12}+x_{13}\le1\), \(x_{12}+x_{23}\le1\), and \(x_{13}+x_{23}\le1\). Setting all three quantities to \(1/2\) is LP-feasible and earns \(1.5\), while any integer solution can use at most one route and earns at most \(1.01\). So the paper’s claimed bridge from its integer problem to the LP cannot support an exact discrete-to-continuous identity in general. The continuous mirror remains a sensible and efficiently solvable problem, but its tractability is the tractability of the relaxation itself; the paper’s lemma does not establish that the original integer problem has the same answer. The further questions are whether the continuous model’s revenue shares admit the paper’s core interpretation, and how its fractional optimum relates to finite markets when demand bounds or integer capacities matter.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The maximum-revenue problem is the strongest anchor, but the proposed passenger interpretation does not make its continuous variables population masses in the paper’s model. The paper’s inputs are routes, fares, and leg capacities; its decision variables are quantities of flights. It assumes demand is perfectly elastic, so there is no passenger population or demand by type. In the proposed supply-rich mirror, the bounds \(x_t\le\mu_t\) are slack: the masses do no work, and the problem is simply the LP relaxation of the route-quantity model. If those bounds bind, passenger demand has been added. That is a plausible extension, but it changes the problem whose result Lemma 1 concerns.

The strongest repair is to start with finite cohorts of passengers sharing an origin–destination pair and fare, then ask about fractional service when cohort sizes and capacities scale with the market. The paper itself flags demand constraints as future research, so this is a credible question the authors might recognize. Still, its continuous version is a compact LP with one variable per listed route type and one capacity constraint per leg. Its polynomial solvability comes directly from standard LP algorithms; the paper provides no complexity classification for a passenger-demand problem, and the mirror would introduce that problem rather than continuize one of its computational results. Studying the discrete version’s integrality gap or rounding could be useful, but that is a neighboring operations-research project.

Treating airlines as the population is a poorer repair. Revenue shares track named carriers whose ownership of particular legs determines which routes involve them. Aggregating carriers into types risks erasing those network positions; a continuum of carriers also makes the paper’s equal split per flight degenerate unless the sharing rule is redesigned.

So the negative case is strongest against this paper as a ChoCo mirror: its population is absent, and its main optimization already uses quantities of routes. But the universal claim is weak. A finite-demand airline model is explicitly adjacent to the authors’ stated future work, and its high-multiplicity version could be worth studying. I would reject the case that the proposed mirror directly captures Lemma 1, while stopping short of claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror can be made.
