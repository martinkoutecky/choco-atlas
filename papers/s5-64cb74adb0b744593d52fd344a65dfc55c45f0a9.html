The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible continuous apportionment question from this paper, but I cannot honestly give it a required anchor. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifiable by complexity. Its unnumbered “Theorem” characterizes apportionment methods: a method is house monotone and consistent if and only if it is a Huntington method. That is a mathematical characterization proved here, not a computational result. The numbered lemmas and their sublemmas establish parts of that proof; they do not provide complexity results.

The strongest plausible mirror would be Continuous Webster Apportionment. Take many residents distributed across \(\tau\) constituencies, with each constituency’s population represented by a positive rational share \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and give an integer house size \(h\). Ask for an apportionment \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_i a_i=h\), generated by repeatedly giving the next seat to a constituency maximizing \(\mu_i/(a_i+1/2)\); ties may yield multiple valid outputs. The residents are the large population, constituencies are the types, and their shares are the masses. Seats remain integral. In a national apportionment setting, millions of residents and a fixed, much smaller number of constituencies make this a credible high-multiplicity regime.

That question is close to the paper’s subject, but it is not a mirror of a named computational result in the paper: Webster is one of the methods discussed, and the characterization theorem is about a class of rules, not the complexity of computing this rule. I would expect this specific computation to be tractable, but that expectation is not an anchor and cannot support the requested complexity comparison. So the positive case is only a credible direction for a follow-up problem. Under the required anchor standard, there are no qualifying anchors and therefore no anchored continuous problems to put to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with a gap in the proposed anchors: this paper has no result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Its central theorem characterizes apportionment methods by house monotonicity and consistency. The lemmas support that characterization; they do not establish an algorithmic or complexity result. So there is no computational result here for a continuous mirror to anchor to.

The proposed Continuous Webster Apportionment problem is a sensible high-multiplicity model. Constituencies can be types, their rational population shares can be masses, and seats can remain integral. I would not reject it on grounds of named constituencies or indivisible seats. But it asks for a new computational analysis of a method mentioned in the paper; it does not mirror a result the paper proves. A stronger version—computing apportionments for continuous shares, or studying their sensitivity—would still be a follow-up question, not a continuization of the paper’s contribution.

The closest mirror of the actual theorem would extend its characterization from integer populations to rational or real population shares. That preserves the paper’s subject, but remains an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational remit. To make it computational, one would need to specify a finitely encoded input for the apportionment method itself. The paper’s generalized Huntington methods allow an arbitrary rank index, but give no representation of that index as an algorithmic input. Choosing an encoding and a restricted family of indices would define a new problem rather than recover a computational question from the theorem.

This is a strong case against treating the paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror, but it does not establish that every new computational study of apportionment with population masses would be unworthy. The universal negative is weaker: the population-as-mass interpretation is natural, yet this paper supplies no computational result for it to continue.
