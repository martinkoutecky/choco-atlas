The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a continuous version of the paper’s preference-aggregation route. A plausible regime is a large public consultation or membership body using one shared argumentation framework and a fixed vocabulary of values. Each participant has a complete type given by their strict ranking of those values; the mass of a type is the fraction of participants with that ranking. If there are \(n\) participants but only \(\tau\) distinct rankings, then \(\tau\) can be small relative to \(n\).

Concretely, given a fixed VAF \( \langle A,\rightarrow,V,val\rangle \) and a finite support \(S\) of audience rankings with rational masses \(\mu_P\) summing to \(1\), compute each value’s mass-weighted Borda score
\[ B_\mu(v)=\sum_{P\in S}\mu_P\,\mathrm{rank}_P(v). \]
Return the resulting collective audience, using a fixed tie-break, and the defeat graph it induces on the VAF. This is a continuous analogue of the preference-based aggregation illustrated in Example 2.2: the inputs are type masses rather than individually listed experts, and the output remains a collective justification and attack graph. No objective is minimized. Direct score calculation suggests it is tractable in \(\tau\), \(|V|\), the argument graph size, and the mass bit length. That is an inference about this proposed problem, not a result proved in the paper.

There is an instance of this paper’s main weakness for the programme: it has no named computational-complexity result to anchor that question. The nearest relevant result is Proposition 1, proved in the paper: being a defeat graph is not preserved by any quota rule. That is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a complexity classification. Theorem 3, also proved here, says that any graph aggregation rule restricted to justifiable profiles that preserves being a defeat graph must violate independence, unanimity, or non-dictatorship. Theorem 4, proved here, says that the only unanimous preference aggregation rule corresponding to a graph aggregation function is a dictatorship. These are also axiomatic results. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are cited characterizations and impossibility results, not computational ones. The apparent Proposition 5 about polynomial-time computation is inside a comment environment in the supplied text, so I would not count it as a result of the paper.

Thus, the Borda-mass problem is a plausible population-level extension of a method the authors study, but it is not a continuous mirror of a qualifying named result. Its expected tractability follows from weighted score aggregation; it does not show that continuization changes the complexity landscape. Further questions could study how approximation in the masses affects the induced defeat graph, or whether mass-threshold graph aggregation preserves justifiability. The latter would continue the paper’s axiomatic line, outside ChoCo’s computational focus. The weakest point is decisive: the proposed mirror is natural but computationally elementary, and the paper supplies no named complexity anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is at the anchor gate: the paper contains no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its contributions are impossibility and characterization results about aggregation axioms. That leaves no result for a continuous-complexity mirror to carry forward.

The proponent’s strongest concrete proposal is still a sensible population model. In a large consultation with a shared VAF, people can be grouped by their complete ranking of the values, and a mass-weighted Borda rule can produce a collective ranking and defeat graph. But this extends the illustration in Example 2.2; it does not mirror a result in the paper. The paper neither studies the computational complexity of evaluating Borda aggregation nor presents that calculation as a theorem. A mass version of the example would be a new, elementary aggregation task, rather than a continuization of one of its results.

The named results the proponent points to do not supply a stronger anchor. Proposition 1 says quota rules cannot preserve being a defeat graph. Its mass version replaces each support count with the corresponding fraction of society and retains the same axiomatic question. Theorem 3 trades preservation of defeat graphs against independence, unanimity, and non-dictatorship; Theorem 4 links unanimous preference aggregation and correspondence to graph aggregation with dictatorship. A distribution over rankings gives natural inputs for restating those claims, but the claims remain about properties of aggregation rules, outside ChoCo’s computational focus. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are cited characterizations and impossibility results of the same kind. Proposition 5 cannot provide an anchor here: in the supplied text it appears inside a comment environment.

A stronger computational proposal could add costs and ask for the least mass of participants whose rankings must change to produce a target collective graph. That could lead to algorithmic questions over ranking types and perhaps optimization over permutations. But the paper defines no intervention, costs, or target-outcome problem; those additions would supply the central computational problem themselves. They may be worthwhile new work, but they cannot turn these aggregation results into computational results of this paper.

I would not claim that a high-multiplicity regime is implausible: large consultations can have many participants sharing rankings over a fixed set of values. The negative case is weaker on that ground, and the weighted Borda extension is coherent. It rests instead on the mismatch between the paper’s actual contributions and ChoCo’s computational remit. If the requirement is merely to find any computational question inspired by the paper, the universal negative claim cannot be made convincingly.
