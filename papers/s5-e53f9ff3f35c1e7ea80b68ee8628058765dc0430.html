What the mirror covers

The potential mirror covers high-multiplicity NSW with \(K=3\), repeated indivisible item types, and fractional mass over integral bundles; it leaves unrestricted-bundle NSW and the cited Vertex Cover theorem itself untreated.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity Nash-social-welfare allocation with integral bundles. It preserves the paper’s central object—additive utilities, indivisible items, and the geometric-mean objective—while making the population, rather than the goods received by each agent, continuous.

There is one bibliographic caveat. Lee’s own APX-hardness claim is not assigned a theorem number. The only numbered computational hardness result is Theorem 2.1, cited from Chlebík and Chlebíková [2], not proved in this paper:

Given a \(3\)-regular graph with \(N\) vertices and \(M=1.5N\) edges, it is NP-hard to distinguish whether the graph has a vertex cover of size at most \(c_{\min}N\), where \(c_{\min}\approx0.5103\), or every vertex cover has size at least \(c_{\max}N\), where \(c_{\max}\approx0.5155\). Consequently, approximating Vertex Cover on \(3\)-regular graphs within a factor of approximately \(1.01\) is NP-hard.

I use Theorem 2.1 as the formal named hardness anchor, while the object being mirrored is Lee’s unnumbered result proved from it: there exists a constant \(\mu>1\) such that maximizing Nash social welfare with indivisible items is NP-hard to approximate within factor \(\mu\). The reduction obtains \(\mu\approx1.00008\) for the displayed parameters.

My lead question is:

\[ \textbf{HM-NSW}_{\infty}(3). \]

An instance consists of:

  • finitely many complete agent types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\);
  • finitely many item types \(J\), with rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\);
  • rational additive utilities \(u_{tj}\), where an agent of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\) has utility \(u_t(b)=\sum_{j\in J}u_{tj}b_j\);
  • the requirement that every agent receive an integral bundle containing at most three item units.

Let \(\mathcal B_3=\{b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}:\sum_jb_j\le3\}\). The decision variable is \(x_{t,b}\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(b\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B_3}x_{t,b}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b\in\mathcal B_3}b_jx_{t,b}\le\rho_j \]

for every item type \(j\). The objective is the normalized Nash social welfare

\[ \operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(x) = \exp\!\left( \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b\in\mathcal B_3} x_{t,b}\log u_t(b) \right), \]

with zero utility interpreted as zero Nash welfare. The problem is to compute an optimal feasible \(x\), or an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal solution in logarithmic value.

This is a genuine continuous-population mirror. A finite realization takes many cloned agents of each type and many copies of each item type. If the masses and supplies have denominator \(R\), then \(R\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(R\rho_j\) copies of item \(j\) give a discrete instance. Conversely, any such finite allocation induces an \(x\). Every individual still receives an integral bundle; only the mass of agents receiving each bundle is fractional.

The regime is plausible in public-benefit or household-package allocation: millions of households fall into a moderate number of complete need/preference types, while standardized indivisible goods or service slots are supplied in large multiplicities. The type includes the entire utility vector, eligibility, and package constraints, so no relevant individual-specific information is discarded. The population is large relative to the number of types and item types.

The cap of three is not arbitrary in relation to Lee’s reduction. Lemma 2.2, proved in the paper, shows that an optimal allocation can be chosen with at most one vertex item per vertex agent. Lemma 2.3, also proved there, gives the exchange rules for shared items; together with the corresponding edge item, agents in the reduction need at most three items. Lemma 2.4 then proves the soundness bound

\[ \operatorname{OPT} \le \left(\frac{2(1+\alpha)}{3}\right)^{\gamma/2.5} (1+\alpha)^{\beta/2.5}, \]

when every vertex cover has size at least \(c_{\max}N\), where \(\beta=3(c_{\min}-1/2)\) and \(\gamma=(c_{\max}-c_{\min})/3\). Thus the bounded-bundle mirror still tracks the structure actually used in the hardness construction.

I expect \(\textbf{HM-NSW}_{\infty}(3)\) to be Class A. Its formulation is the linear program

\[ \max \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\log u_t(b) \]

over the constraints above. Since

\[ |\mathcal B_3|=\binom{|J|+3}{3}, \]

the number of variables is polynomial in \(|T|\) and \(|J|\). Hence the continuous problem is polynomial-time solvable to arbitrary requested precision. The discrete APX-hardness can disappear because many identical agents of one type may be split across different integral bundles, while the items themselves remain indivisible. That is precisely the high-multiplicity effect the programme is meant to expose.

The further boundary question is important: if the bundle-size bound \(K\) is part of the input, the LP has \(\binom{|J|+K}{K}\) possible bundles, and the relevant pricing problem becomes

\[ \max_{b:\|b\|_1\le K} \left[ \log(u_t\cdot b)-\lambda\cdot b \right]. \]

That may yield a continuum-specific hardness boundary. Thus the mirror does not claim that all continuous NSW variants are easy; it identifies a precise tractable regime and a natural pricing problem beyond it.

My weakest point is that the paper does not number its own NSW APX-hardness theorem, and \(\textbf{HM-NSW}_{\infty}(3)\) is a bounded-bundle high-multiplicity version rather than the completely unrestricted problem. A referee could therefore demand a second mirror with unbounded bundles. Nevertheless, the proposed question preserves the paper’s objective and indivisibility, is justified by the paper’s own normal-form lemmas, has a realistic high-multiplicity regime, and turns the paper’s discrete hardness into exactly the kind of meaningful continuous question the ChoCo programme is designed to study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that it is not really a mirror of Lee’s problem. The paper’s hard instances do not contain a small population of repeated types: every vertex agent, edge agent, shared item, and edge item is distinguished by graph-incidence information. In the reduction, one would have \(\Theta(N)\) agent and item types, each with mass about \(1/N\). Splitting such a type across bundles is therefore a fractional assignment relaxation, not a high-multiplicity population limit.

The cap of three is also not a property of Nash social welfare. Lemmas 2.2 and 2.3 establish a three-item normal form only for the particular reduction instances. They do not show that arbitrary NSW allocations can be restricted to bundles of size three. Thus \(\textbf{HM-NSW}_{\infty}(3)\) is a new bounded-configuration allocation problem whose easy LP arises from the imposed cap. Calling it a mirror of the unrestricted problem overstates what the paper supplies. The named Theorem 2.1 makes this weaker still: it is a Vertex Cover hardness theorem cited from elsewhere, not a numbered NSW result proved by Lee.

There is a further technical discontinuity. In a finite NSW instance, one agent with utility zero makes the product zero, whereas a zero-measure set disappears from \(\int \log u\). The proposed objective is therefore not an unrestricted literal limit of finite NSW unless one imposes positivity or treats zero-utility types carefully. That can be repaired, but it shows that the claimed two-way dictionary is not automatic.

These objections defeat the proposed formulation as a faithful mirror of Lee’s theorem, but they do not establish the requested universal negative. The proponent’s stronger public-benefit scenario is coherent: millions of households may share finitely many complete utility and eligibility types, standardized indivisible goods may have repeated copies, and per-capita supplies are then the natural scaling. For positive utilities, the identity

\[ \frac{1}{R}\log\!\left(\prod_i u_i\right) = \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\log u_t(b) \]

makes the continuous objective exactly the normalized high-multiplicity objective. No individual identity is needed, and indivisibility remains at the level of each bundle and item unit.

Removing the artificial cap would give an even better mirror: a configuration LP over arbitrary integral bundles, with a genuine pricing problem. Its complexity would be an interesting boundary question, not a reason to reject it. Consequently, the negative case can fairly say that the anchor needs to be relabelled as “bounded-bundle high-multiplicity NSW,” rather than presented as a direct continuization of Lee’s APX-hardness theorem. It cannot honestly say that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
