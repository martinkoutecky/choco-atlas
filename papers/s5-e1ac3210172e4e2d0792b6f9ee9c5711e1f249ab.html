The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its contributions are the CAMS method and empirical comparisons on J1, T1, and T2. SMV, SMS, and RASA are defined and evaluated, but the paper gives no numbered complexity or algorithmic result about them. So I cannot honestly restate an anchor or claim a continuous problem is the mirror of one.

The strongest candidate for a mirror is an unanchored population version of the paper’s extractive SMS aggregation. For one close-ended question, let \(A\) be the finite set of candidate text answers, and let \(T\) be the support of worker types. A type is a complete answer-behavior profile for the task, including its response distribution \(p_t(a)\) over \(A\); \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the worker population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Define \(P_\mu(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_t(a)\). Given a similarity function \(S\) on candidate answers, the Distributional SMS Aggregation problem is: choose an answer \(\hat a\) with \(P_\mu(\hat a)>0\) maximizing \(\sum_{b\in A}P_\mu(b)S(\hat a,b)\). The instance is the explicitly listed answer set, rational type masses and response distributions, and similarity values; a solution is a maximizing candidate answer.

A plausible regime would be a large annotation service handling many repeated or templated tasks with stable cohorts of interchangeable workers, where the number \(N\) of workers is much larger than the number \(\tau=|T|\) of behavioral types. Mass represents population share, and the decision remains the paper’s underlying extractive aggregation choice. With an explicit finite answer set and similarity matrix, this version is tractable by direct enumeration. It would raise further questions about implicit similarity models, approximation, and whether type reliabilities can be estimated jointly as in RASA.

This candidate does not supply the missing anchor. It also narrows the paper: it models SMS-like extractive selection, not the full CAMS pipeline or abstractive crowd and LLM aggregators. Its weakest point is the regime: exact text answers may be almost all distinct, making \(\tau\) large unless one assumes recurring templates or canonicalization, which could change the problem. The paper’s reported experiments do not establish that this population model is sensible or that its continuous version has any particular complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The paper defines SMS, SMV, and RASA, then reports empirical comparisons, but it proves no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity of an aggregation problem. Q1–Q3 are experimental questions. Under the requested anchor discipline, there is nothing to anchor a continuous question to.

The proposed Distributional SMS model is the strongest rescue. A large annotation service with recurring tasks and interchangeable worker cohorts could make type masses sensible. But that yields a new problem, not a continuization of a computational result in this paper. SMS already selects from the observed answers for each instance. With an explicit answer list and similarity values, the population version just scores each candidate against the weighted answer distribution. Making the problem more ambitious—by searching over arbitrary texts, learning worker response laws, or optimizing the mix of creators and aggregators—adds a search, learning, or allocation problem that the paper neither formulates nor studies. The paper’s central contribution is the CAMS pipeline and its measured answer quality, not such an optimization problem.

So I would reject this paper as a worthwhile ChoCo mirror on the evidence presented, while conceding that the universal claim is weaker: a plausible high-multiplicity setting exists, and a population SMS model is well-posed. What is missing is a computational result from the paper for that model to mirror.
