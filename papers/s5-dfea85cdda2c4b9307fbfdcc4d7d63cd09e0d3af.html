The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its central finding—that the sequential treatment produced higher social surplus than the simultaneous treatment—is an experimental result in §4.1. The paper also reports how often subjects’ actions matched equilibrium predictions. Neither is a complexity result. Section 2’s claim that each finite sequential game has a unique SPNE is unnumbered and makes no algorithmic claim. So there are zero anchors, and I can’t honestly give the requested one continuous problem per anchor.

The strongest adjacent research question I can see is a high-multiplicity invitation game. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete preference types, with rational population masses \(\mu_t\). Let \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) be the mass of type \(t\) that joins. Each type has specified piecewise-linear payoff functions \(U_t^1(q)\) and \(U_t^0(q)\) for joining and declining, based on the aggregate composition \(q\). In the simultaneous version, an equilibrium \(q\) must satisfy: all of type \(t\) joins if \(U_t^1(q)>U_t^0(q)\), none joins if the reverse holds, and any joining mass is possible when the payoffs tie. A computational question would be whether there is such an equilibrium with total surplus at least a given threshold, where
\[ W(q)=\sum_{t\in T}\bigl(q_tU_t^1(q)+(\mu_t-q_t)U_t^0(q)\bigr). \]
This could fit a large membership drive with many people and relatively few stable preference types.

That is a plausible population-level invitation model, but it is not an anchor-backed mirror of this paper. It replaces preferences over coalitions of named people with preferences over aggregate composition, and it covers the simultaneous game only. The paper’s main comparison depends on sequential moves among atomic players; that order and its backward-induction logic have no direct counterpart in an atomless population. I would therefore offer this as a possible follow-on question, not as a continuous mirror supported by the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no result here that meets the programme’s computational anchor. The paper’s central claim is an experimental finding: in these three-player games, the sequential treatment produced higher social surplus. Its equilibrium discussion gives predictions for the experiment, not a complexity result or an algorithmic problem. Even the claim that each finite sequential game has a unique SPNE is a game-theoretic observation, not a computational classification. So there is no named computational result to mirror.

The proposed mass-participation model is coherent, but it changes the payoff primitive. In the paper, an outcome is a subset of three identified players, and each player’s list ranks such coalitions. The experiment runs separate three-person games; its 90 participants are not one large society. To get utilities \(U_t^1(q)\) and \(U_t^0(q)\), one must define new preferences over aggregate participation. That could suit a large membership drive, but it is an anonymous participation game. It does not preserve the paper’s experimental question about how observing predecessors changes strategic choices and welfare.

A stronger follow-on would model both simultaneous and sequential procedures for a large population with type-level preferences over aggregate composition, then ask for the complexity of finding an equilibrium above a welfare threshold or choosing an order. But the sequential half faces a structural limit: in an atomless population, one person’s action changes the aggregate by zero. The individual pivotality that supports the paper’s backward-induction story then vanishes. Treat types as coordinated, positive-mass blocks to preserve pivotal moves, and the strategic players are those blocks; multiplicity within each block is no longer doing the strategic work. Keep individual moves pivotal, and the model must retain finite agents or add fine-grained threshold structure that the aggregate distribution alone does not capture. This is a plausible case of the continuum degenerating at the feature the paper studies.

The simultaneous equilibrium question may still be worth studying on its own. The honest limit of the negative case is narrower: I cannot rule out that an independently motivated anonymous participation model would be valuable. But it would be a new computational model, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s results; the best version that retains the paper’s central sequential comparison runs into the loss of individual pivotality.
