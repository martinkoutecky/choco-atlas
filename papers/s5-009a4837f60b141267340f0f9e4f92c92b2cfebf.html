What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers the paper’s IAC volume calculations for Condorcet events, voting-rule efficiencies, Borda paradoxes, and Condorcet classes. The paper reports these computations but gives no qualifying numbered computational result or complexity classification.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s IAC limit. For five candidates, let the 120 rankings be the voter types and let \(\mu\) be their mass vector in the simplex. Given an event \(E\), such as “there is no Condorcet winner” or “the Condorcet winner wins plurality,” define \(P_E\) as the region of mass vectors satisfying the event’s homogeneous inequalities. The continuous problem is: given those inequalities, compute the normalized volume of \(P_E\), exactly or with a certified error bound. A solution is the volume, which is the event’s probability under the limiting IAC measure. The regime is \(k\to\infty\) voters over at most 120 types. Section 2.1 identifies that limit with the ratio of discrete profile counts, and the paper computes volumes for concrete five-candidate events. This is a credible population-continuous question the authors would recognize.

There is a substantial catch: this continuous probability-volume problem is already the paper’s own object. It is evidence that the population continuum is natural, and the finite-profile limit to volume is a meaningful discrete-to-continuous identity, but presenting the volume computation as a new mirror would mostly restate the paper. It also averages over all societies under IAC rather than taking one society \(\mu\) as the input, so it is not quite the ChoCo model of computation on a given population distribution.

I find no qualifying named computational-complexity anchor in the paper. The exact probability and volume computations are reported in sections and tables, not as numbered theorems, lemmas, corollaries, or propositions asserting a complexity classification. The closest numbered result is Proposition 1, proved here: if an event defined by strict homogeneous rational inequalities is nonempty, it has positive probability under IAC. That is a positivity statement, not a result that a computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Conjecture 2 is also not an anchor: it conjectures that the listed probability series decrease with the number of candidates. So I can state the IAC volume problem precisely, but cannot honestly attach it to a compliant anchor or infer its complexity class from this paper. Its complexity as the number of candidates and types grows is a further question the paper leaves open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor has no qualifying computational complexity result behind it. Proposition 1 says that a nonempty event defined by strict homogeneous inequalities has positive IAC probability; Conjecture 2 predicts how certain probabilities change with the number of candidates. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem. The exact probabilities and computational reports are numerical results, not numbered complexity claims.

The proposed volume problem also already *is* the paper’s computation. The 120 rankings are voter types, their proportions form the population simplex, and the paper computes event volumes there as limiting IAC probabilities. That finite-profile-to-volume identity is a real result, but it is already derived and used here. Asking again for the volume of the same event polytope would restate the paper’s continuous model, not supply a new mirror. This is a genuine collision: the paper does not merely study fractional outcomes or cite continuous methods; its main calculations are over the continuous space of populations.

A stronger input-conditioned version changes the task. Given one mass vector \(\mu\), deciding whether it has a Condorcet winner or whether a specified rule elects one is just evaluation of the paper’s linear inequalities. Asking for the volume under IAC returns to the calculation the authors already perform. Other proposals, such as optimizing over mass changes, introduce intervention objectives that the paper does not study.

There is no good objection that these voters lack multiplicity: the ranking types and their masses are exactly a high-multiplicity population. The negative case is instead about novelty and anchoring. I cannot rule out a worthwhile new project on generalized polytope-volume complexity, but that would extend the paper’s computational geometry; it would not mirror one of its named computational-complexity results.
