The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over weak orders on \(C\), listed by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, does there exist an axis \(A\) such that every type \(v\) with \(\mu_v>0\) is existentially single-peaked with respect to \(A\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a sparse rational distribution \(\mu\) over types in \(\mathcal W(C)\). The decision variable is an axis \(A\), a linear ordering of \(C\); feasibility requires every type in the support of \(\mu\) to satisfy the paper's existential single-peaked condition. The consecutive-ones construction and PQ-tree algorithm decide feasibility in polynomial time in the support representation.

The objection that survived

The opponent's strongest point is that exact consistency ignores mass values and reduces to the finite support test, so this mirror contributes little to mass-sensitive computation or continuous-optimization methods.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper's existential single-peaked, single-plateaued, and single-peaked consistency results and their consecutive-ones formulations. It does not cover separate weighted near-consistency or mass-removal questions.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous population version of the paper’s existential single-peaked consistency problem. Let \(C\) be a fixed set of candidates and let \(\mathcal W(C)\) be the weak orders on \(C\). An instance is a sparse rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal W(C)\), listed by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and their masses. The question is whether there is an axis \(A\), a linear ordering of \(C\), such that every type \(v\) with \(\mu_v>0\) is existentially single-peaked with respect to \(A\). A solution is such an axis; a no-instance has none.

A plausible regime is a large electorate ranking a short list of candidates or policy options. Many respondents submit the same weak order, and the analyst wants to know whether one ideological axis can explain the whole response distribution. A type is the complete weak order used by this consistency question; its mass is the fraction of respondents with that order. The natural high-multiplicity case has \(N\) respondents much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct observed orders.

This is an exact population-level version of the paper’s problem, not an outcomes or noise model. A finite profile with counts \(n_t\) gives \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). For consistency, repeated copies of a type add no new constraint, so the answer is determined by the positive support. That makes the continuous problem polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass encoding length \(L\), using the paper’s construction on the support. The paper itself gives a polynomial-time result in the finite-profile input size; the support-sensitive bound follows because duplicate types can be discarded.

My lead anchor is Corollary 15, proved in this paper: existential single-peaked consistency for weak orders is in \(\mathrm P\). Its accompanying Theorem 14, also proved here, states that a weak-order profile is existentially single-peaked consistent if and only if the matrix built by Construction 12 has the consecutive ones property. The paper uses the polynomial-time consecutive-ones algorithm, including Booth and Lueker’s PQ-tree algorithm, to obtain Corollary 15. In the continuous mirror, the same matrix is built from the positive-mass weak-order types. An axis is exactly an ordering of the columns that gives the required consecutive-ones property. This is the broadest of the three variants and the paper presents the result as resolving the general weak-order case left open by Lackner.

Two further anchors support the case, each for a distinct restriction on the same type space. Corollary 21, proved here, says single-plateaued consistency for weak orders is in \(\mathrm P\); Theorem 20, proved here, characterizes it by the consecutive-ones property of the matrix from Construction 18. Its continuous question asks whether one axis makes every positive-mass type single-plateaued: each type’s most-preferred candidates form its plateau, with preferences strictly increasing and decreasing along the respective sides. The expected class is tractable, by the paper’s matrix construction and consecutive-ones test.

Likewise, Corollary 26, proved here, says single-peaked consistency for weak orders is in \(\mathrm P\); Theorem 25, proved here, characterizes it by the consecutive-ones property of the matrix from Construction 24. Its continuous question asks whether a common axis makes every supported type single-peaked in the paper’s sense: the order has one most-preferred candidate, with strict increase and decrease on the two sides. This mirror is also expected to be tractable. I include these two as meaningful companion anchors because the permitted patterns of indifference differ, rather than treating the three variants as one blended problem.

All three mirrors are Class A candidates. The paper constructs the matrix with rows for each voter type and uses the resulting consecutive-ones test; the PQ-tree approach can also return all consistent axes. In a support representation, the mass values matter only through whether they are positive. That is appropriate for exact consistency: every type present in the population must fit the common-axis hypothesis, however small its share. A distinct follow-up would ask how much mass must be removed to make a distribution consistent, or whether an axis can explain all but an \(\varepsilon\) fraction of society. Those weighted repair questions use mass substantively, but they are not the anchors proved in this paper.

The paper has no named hardness result, so there is no hardness anchor here and no claim that continuization dissolves hardness. Its contribution to this case is a tractable computational problem over voter types, with a natural large-electorate regime and a direct algorithmic bridge to the continuous instance.

The weakest point is that this mirror does not make the numerical proportions affect the answer: a type’s mass matters only through being zero or positive. The resulting computation is close to the paper’s finite-profile problem after coalescing duplicate orders, and it does not bring continuous-optimization machinery into play. Still, the mass-insensitive predicate is the authors’ own consistency question: their problem asks whether one axis works for every voter, and in the distribution model that quantifier naturally ranges over every positive-mass type. The case is therefore a credible, if modest, population continuization of the paper’s central computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that all three results concern the *support* of a profile, not the population distribution. I can’t use “there is no computational result to mirror”: the paper proves named results in Theorems 14, 20, and 25, with the corresponding polynomial-time claims in Corollaries 15, 21, and 26.

But for each of those anchors, the continuous question has the same form: does there exist an axis compatible with every preference type of positive mass? Repeating a weak order adds duplicate rows to the paper’s consecutive-ones construction and cannot change its answer. The masses do not enter. The existential single-peaked mirror in Theorem 14 and Corollary 15, the single-plateaued mirror in Theorem 20 and Corollary 21, and the single-peaked mirror in Theorem 25 and Corollary 26 therefore all reduce to the same finite support test. The PQ-tree algorithm already solves that test; the distribution supplies no new continuous optimization problem, and its rational bit lengths are irrelevant.

This is not a claim that high multiplicity is implausible. Large electorates with many respondents sharing weak orders are a natural regime. Nor is existing high-multiplicity work an objection: it supports the regime. The narrower point is that these particular predicates discard multiplicity entirely. Once duplicate orders are coalesced, the exact question is unchanged. That makes the mirrors poor candidates for a programme whose distinctive payoff is to understand what computation looks like when population mass is the mathematical object.

A stronger proposal would ask for an axis that explains at least a fraction \(\rho\) of the society, or maximizes the compatible mass:
\[ \max_A \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[v_t\text{ is compatible with }A]. \]
This gives the proportions real work to do, and it applies to all three variants. But it changes the paper’s universal consistency question into a weighted repair or near-consistency problem. The paper itself points toward nearly single-peakedness and distance measures, so this is a credible adjacent direction; it is not a continuous version of the named results. And because the axis is still a permutation of finitely many candidates, the weights make this a weighted combinatorial problem, not automatically a new use of continuous-optimization machinery.

That is a case for giving the three exact mirrors low priority, not a convincing proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper has a plausible high-multiplicity regime, no identity dependence or continuum degeneration, and no continuous computational treatment that would settle the question. The weighted near-consistency variant is difficult to dismiss honestly. My negative case is therefore weakest precisely at the universal claim: the exact anchors add little beyond support compression, but I cannot rule out a worthwhile, mass-sensitive mirror.
