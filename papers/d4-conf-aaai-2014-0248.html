The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant anchor-based case for this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Algorithm 1 describes its active-learning procedure, and the paper says the sparse-coding optimization in Eq. (7) is convex and efficiently solvable, citing prior work. Neither is a numbered complexity result for the underlying active-learning problem. The reported accuracy gains are experimental.

So there are no qualifying anchors, and no tractability or hardness classification to carry over to a continuous mirror. Treating Algorithm 1 or the experiments as such an anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no computational-result anchors to defeat: the proponent raised none, and that is the paper’s strongest obstacle to a mirror. Algorithm 1 gives a query heuristic and the experiments report accuracy; neither states a complexity, approximation, or parameterized result for the active-learning problem. Equation (7) is a sparse-coding optimization subproblem that the paper says can be solved by iterative methods, not a complexity result about active learning. The paper therefore gives the ChoCo programme no named computational claim to continuize.

The strongest alternative would make the labelers the population. For a large crowdsourcing service, one could group workers by their complete expertise and cost types, then ask how to allocate a query budget across those types to minimize expected classification error. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but it changes the paper’s sequential choice of an individual instance–labeler pair into a stochastic allocation problem. The paper supplies no complexity result about that allocation problem either. Its target instances remain specific feature vectors whose uncertainty changes as labels arrive, so replacing workers by masses alone does not yield a continuous mirror of a stated result.

I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no such scenario could be worthwhile. A theory of query allocation over worker types might be worthwhile as a new project. The case against is narrower but strong: this paper offers no computational theorem for that project to mirror, and the most natural population-level reformulation would need a new formal problem and objective before its computational value could be assessed.
