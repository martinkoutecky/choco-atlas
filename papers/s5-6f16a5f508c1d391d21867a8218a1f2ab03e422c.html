The continuous mirror question

Given binary issues, a common legal order \(O\), a finite support \(T\) of complete compact acyclic CP-net types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), type weights \(w_t\), target outcome \(p\), action \(A\in\{IV,DV,IV+DV\}\), and reachable transitions with \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\) costs \(\gamma(t,t')\), choose transfers \(x_{t,t'}\ge0\) for \(t\ne t'\) with \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\) to minimize \(\sum_{t\ne t'}\gamma(t,t')x_{t,t'}\), subject to the resulting masses satisfying the weighted sequential-majority inequalities for \(p\) and forbidding transitions from a type whose top outcome is not \(p\) to one whose top outcome is \(p\); decide whether the optimum is at most \(\beta\).

The model it lives in

High-multiplicity weighted sequential-majority campaigning with CP-net types: rational mass is transferred between complete types, each unit follows one coherent CP-net transition, costs are linear \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\) transfer costs, and feasibility is determined by weighted sequential-majority and negative-bribery constraints.

The objection that survived

The compact CP-net representation leaves the reachable-type and transition-cost pricing problem unspecified; an explicit transition table yields a clean LP, while preserving compact input may introduce additional complexity.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper does admit a serious positive mirror. The strongest anchor is the paper’s weighted negative bribery result, because its reduction puts the combinatorics entirely in the indivisibility of weighted voters.

My lead problem is Continuous Negative Sequential-Majority CP-Net Campaigning. An instance contains binary issues \(M=\{X_1,\ldots,X_m\}\), a common legal issue order \(O\), a preferred outcome \(p\), and a finite set \(T\) of compact acyclic CP-net types. Type \(t\) has rational population mass \(\mu_t\), per-agent influence weight \(w_t\), and all the preference, dependency, and campaign-cost information relevant to the problem. The population may have millions of agents but only \(\tau=|T|\) distinct types.

For an allowed action \(A\in\{IV,DV,IV+DV\}\) and cost scheme \(C\), let \(\gamma_{A,C}(t,t')\) be the minimum cost of changing a type \(t\) into a reachable type \(t'\). The campaign chooses nonnegative mass transfers \(x_{t,t'}\), with \(\sum_{t'}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\). The resulting type distribution is \(\mu'\), and the campaign cost is \(\sum_{t,t'}\gamma_{A,C}(t,t')x_{t,t'}\). Negative campaigning imposes \(x_{t,t'}=0\) whenever \(t\)'s top outcome is not \(p\) but \(t'\)'s top outcome is \(p\). The question is whether there is such a mass-transfer plan of cost at most \(\beta\) for which \(p\) wins sequential majority, using weighted mass \(w_t\mu'_t\) at each issue.

This is a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large referendum, policy platform, or organizational electorate contains many cohorts sharing the same conditional preference template, influence weight, and campaign-price schedule. The type is the complete relevant description, so grouping agents is not an artificial loss of information. A rational mass profile is exactly the normalized profile of a large finite electorate.

The named anchor is Theorem 20, proved in this paper:

“Weighted-\((SM,A,C)\)-negative-Bribery with a bribery action \(A\in\{IV,DV,IV+DV\}\) and any cost scheme \(C\) is NP-complete.”

The theorem concerns weighted voters with compact acyclic CP-nets, negative bribery, and all five cost schemes \(C_{\mathrm{equal}},C_{\mathrm{flip}},C_{\mathrm{level}},C_{\mathrm{any}},C_{\mathrm{dist}}\). Its reduction from Partition uses weights \(a_1,\ldots,a_\ell\) summing to \(2\psi\). The preferred candidate initially has weight \(1\); another candidate has weight \(2\psi\); and the only successful negative bribery splits that \(2\psi\) weight equally between two alternatives, which is possible exactly when some subset of the \(a_i\)'s sums to \(\psi\). The reduction uses only a constant number of binary issues and works without relying on costs; consequently the result holds for every cost scheme and all three bribery-action models.

I would instantiate this anchor with \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\). In the continuous problem, the Partition choice becomes fractional: instead of selecting an indivisible voter \(i\), the campaign can select an amount \(z_i\) with \(0\le z_i\le\mu_i\). The crucial condition becomes a linear mass constraint such as \(\sum_i a_i z_i=\psi\), after normalization. Since fractional amounts can be taken from the relevant types, the subset-sum obstruction disappears. More generally, for a fixed target \(p\), each sequential-majority condition is linear in the final masses: along \(p\)'s prefix, the weighted mass supporting \(p_j\) at issue \(X_j\) must be at least the opposing mass. Campaign arcs, capacities, negative-bribery prohibitions, and \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\) costs are also linear. This strongly suggests a min-cost flow or LP formulation, hence Class A.

The point is not merely that the continuous version is “easier.” Theorem 20’s hardness has no candidate-side source: the reduction has only a constant-size agenda, and all difficulty lies in choosing whole weighted voters. It is therefore exactly the kind of multiplicity-driven hardness that continuization should dissolve. If this mass problem turned out to be hard, that would be an interesting continuum-specific obstruction—probably arising from implicit CP-net pricing—not a transfer of the Partition argument.

A second, cleaner mirror comes from Theorem 12, also proved here:

“Weighted-\((SM,A,C_{\mathrm{level}})\)-Bribery with bribery action \(A\in\{IV,DV,IV+DV\}\) is, for the special case that \(Q[i]=1\) for each voter \(v_i\), solvable in time \(O(n^2m^2)\).”

This is the positive, non-negative version. The paper processes the issues one at a time and reduces each issue to bounded-value Knapsack: choose the cheapest weighted voters whose issue votes must be flipped. The bounded \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\) costs make the dynamic programme polynomial.

Its continuous counterpart, Continuous \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\)-SM Campaigning, has the same type distribution and asks for the minimum cost of a divisible campaign that makes \(p\) the sequential-majority winner, without the negative-bribery restriction. For each issue \(X_j\), let \(z_{t,j}\in[0,\mu_t]\) be the amount of type-\(t\) mass whose relevant cp-statement is flipped. The objective is \(\sum_{t,j}c_{t,j}z_{t,j}\), subject to the weighted-majority inequalities and the action restrictions. Each issue is now a fractional covering problem: sort types by cost per unit of electoral influence and take the required mass, possibly splitting one type. Thus the discrete dynamic programme should collapse to an ordinary LP or fractional-knapsack computation.

The paper’s own campaign-management interpretation makes these mirrors especially plausible. It explicitly treats bribery as convincing voters through systematic actions on their CP-nets. A campaign directed at a large cohort can naturally affect \(37\%\) of that cohort rather than an individually named voter. The paper itself, however, never makes the electorate a distribution, never uses mass-transfer variables, and never studies the resulting optimization problem; this is therefore not a novelty collision.

My weakest point is that the paper’s weighted voters may sometimes represent genuinely indivisible blocs. If a weight \(a_i\) is a delegation, district, or organization that must be bribed as a whole, fractionalizing it would change the problem rather than continuize it. The mirror is strongest for large populations of ordinary agents sharing CP-net templates, with influence weights that are common properties of types. I also would not claim automatic polynomiality for every implicit CP-net cost representation: the reachable-type pricing problem must still be analyzed. But for sequential majority with \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\), the winner constraints and issue-level campaign costs have exactly the separable structure needed for a credible Class A theory.

This case deliberately covers only Theorem 20 at \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\) and Theorem 12. It does not claim that every result in the paper has a useful continuous analogue; in particular, the negative \(C_{\mathrm{equal}}\) result may retain a different combinatorial obstruction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent has not actually shown a faithful mirror of either theorem.

Theorem 20’s Partition reduction does not encode multiplicity in the ordinary population sense. It uses one voter \(v_i\) of voting weight \(a_i\), with each \(a_i\) potentially different. The hardness lies in indivisible voting powers, not in many interchangeable voters. Giving type \(t_i\) mass \(\mu_i\) and weight \(a_i\), then allowing an arbitrary fraction of \(\mu_i\) to be bribed, changes the atomic unit from a weighted voter into divisible influence. Replacing weight \(a_i\) by \(a_i\) ordinary clones is also a substantive re-modelling: the paper’s action is applied to voters, whereas the mirror permits bribing arbitrary fractions of a previously indivisible bloc.

That objection is serious, but it is not decisive under ChoCo’s rules. A natural scenario with many ordinary agents sharing a CP-net and unit weight does exist, and type-specific influence weights can also be defended as a complete type property. One cannot reject the mirror merely because the continuous version removes the Partition obstruction; that is precisely the phenomenon the programme seeks.

The more substantial problem is algorithmic. The proposed model is an LP only if the type set and all admissible transitions \(t\to t'\) are explicitly available. In the paper, however, a voter is given by a compact CP-net, while the possible post-bribery CP-nets and their costs may be exponentially numerous. If the mirror preserves that compact representation, the relevant pricing problem over reachable CP-nets has not been specified or solved. If it instead lists every type and transition explicitly, it obtains a straightforward finite LP but no longer reflects the compact CP-net input that makes the paper’s setting interesting. Theorem 20 therefore does not itself support the proponent’s claim that the continuous problem is a min-cost flow or polynomially solvable.

The same issue affects Theorem 12. Variables \(z_{t,j}\) for each type and issue can assign different fractions of one type to different issue changes without ensuring that the resulting agents receive one coherent CP-net modification. A faithful formulation needs mass-transfer variables \(x_{t,t'}\) between complete CP-net types. With an explicit transition graph, that formulation is indeed linear; with compact CP-nets, its separation and cost-oracle problems remain the real question. The paper’s dynamic programme proves neither representation has an efficient continuous analogue.

These points weaken the proposed anchors, especially their claim that LP structure follows automatically. They do not, however, defeat the stronger mirror. One can plausibly take a large electorate of ordinary agents with finitely many complete CP-net templates, a common legal issue order, per-capita \(C_{\mathrm{level}}\) costs, and rational type masses. For a fixed target \(p\), sequential-majority constraints along \(p\)’s path are linear in the final masses, and the paper’s weighted reductions become fractional mass-allocation questions. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity computational problem, even if its compact-type pricing complexity is open.

So the honest negative case is weak. It can argue that the proponent has oversold the immediate LP conclusion and has not fixed the type-space representation. It cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. Theorem 20, and even more clearly Theorem 12, survive under a natural high-multiplicity re-modelling.
