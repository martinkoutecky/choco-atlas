What the mirror covers

The proposed continuum idea concerns Theorems 5 and 7 on efficient informative aggregation and quota rules; no paper result supplies a computational anchor, and the other characterization results remain uncovered.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population story is a very large jury or citizen assembly in which members share the same signal process and common preference for correct collective judgments. Each person’s type is one of the four evidence patterns \(T=\{pq,p\bar q,\bar p q,\bar p\bar q\}\); with many members but at most four realized types, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction holding each pattern. A rule maps those masses to one of the three consistent decisions. One could ask whether a rule—perhaps specified by fractional quota thresholds—makes informative reporting a Bayesian Nash equilibrium and selects the posterior-optimal decision.

That is a plausible continuous-population *modeling* direction, but it has no qualifying anchor in this paper. Theorem 5, proved here, is the closest: under consequentialist preferences, an informative voting rule yields efficient decisions exactly when the paper’s two likelihood-weighted prior quantities \(A\) and \(B\) both exceed \(\pi_{pq}\), equivalently when \(pq\) is efficient only for the profile in which every voter’s type is \(pq\). Theorem 7, also proved here, says no quota rule makes informative voting efficient under consequentialist preferences. The other numbered theorems characterize or rule out efficient information aggregation under different assumptions. Here, “efficient” means Bayesian decision-theoretic efficiency, not polynomial-time solvability. None of these results asserts a computational complexity classification.

There is also a serious obstacle to treating that candidate as a mirror of the paper’s question. In an atomless population, one person’s report has zero effect on the outcome, so the finite model’s pivotal-voter incentives disappear. And if signals are independently generated conditional on the state, the continuum’s exact type proportions may reveal the state by a law-of-large-numbers effect. A model that preserves the strategic problem would need to explain how those incentives and residual uncertainty survive.

So the honest positive case is limited: a high-multiplicity setting is imaginable, and the paper offers named results about rule design that could motivate a new continuum study. But under the requested anchor criterion, this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Turning its mechanism-design or equilibrium claims into a computational problem would add a new question, not supply a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 5 characterizes when informative voting can be efficient under consequentialist preferences; Theorem 7 rules out that outcome for quota rules. The other theorems concern equilibrium, efficiency, and properties of voting rules. None studies the complexity of computing a solution from an input. Turning one of these characterizations into an algorithmic decision or search problem would add a new target, rather than continuize a computational result in the paper.

The best version of the Theorem 5 mirror would put a very large jury into a continuum, represent the four signal types by their masses, and ask whether a rule over those masses makes informative reporting efficient and an equilibrium. But the limit removes the strategic force the theorem studies: an individual in an atomless population cannot change the outcome, so any report is a best response. With the paper’s conditionally independent signals, the exact limiting proportions also reveal the state, since the signal frequencies differ across states. The mirror therefore loses both pivotal incentives and residual uncertainty. Adding common shocks could preserve uncertainty, but would not make an individual pivotal; keeping pivotality requires retaining finite agents.

Theorem 7 has the same problem even under its strongest natural reformulation: allow fractional quota thresholds and ask whether they support efficient informative equilibrium in the continuum. The quota question can be posed, but the individual incentive condition has collapsed. A model that instead optimizes a collective decision from known signal masses would be a different planner problem, not this paper’s strategic information-aggregation question.

A computational mechanism-design project built on these ideas could still be worthwhile. The case against commissioning it as a continuous mirror is stronger: it would need both a new computational target and a change to the feature that makes the paper’s question distinctive. That is a substantial negative case, though it cannot establish that no adjacent continuum project could ever be valuable.
