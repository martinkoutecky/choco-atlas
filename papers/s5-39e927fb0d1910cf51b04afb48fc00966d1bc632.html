What the mirror covers

The proposed angle concerns type-level power indices in weighted voting; it does not mirror a computational result established in this paper. The paper’s propositions and its examples involving graphs, exercises, and other domains are left without a continuous-population computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

On the paper’s own results, there is no eligible named computational anchor. Its numbered results are Proposition 1, which states the duality between minimal sets and minimal hitting sets; Proposition 2, which equates a nonzero characteristic-function difference with criticality; and Proposition 3, which says each element of a minimal set is critical. None asserts a complexity bound or an algorithmic result. The discussion of exact computation and approximation points to results from other work, without presenting a numbered computational result here.

The nearest positive lead is high-multiplicity weighted voting: voters with the same weight are one type, and their mass is their fraction of a large assembly. A continuous question could ask for type-level limits of power indices as a finite weighted voting game is replicated with fixed weight proportions. That is a plausible population mirror of the paper’s voting-power setting. But the paper defines its indices over subsets or permutations of a finite set and neither defines nor computes those limits. So this lead cannot supply the required anchored continuous problem; calling it a result of this paper would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The attached case does not leave a computational anchor standing: it says none is present. The numbered results are Propositions 1–3, which establish structural identities about minimal sets, critical elements, and hitting sets. The paper proves no complexity bound or algorithm for computing an importance measure. Its section on exact and approximate computation points to results from other work. The paper’s contribution is to extend definitions across domains and illustrate their use. That gives a continuization programme no computational result of this paper to mirror.

The strongest repair is the weighted-voting example. Give voters types by weight, represent their proportions by \(\mu\), replicate the electorate, and ask for the limiting aggregate power of each type. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and it would be wrong to dismiss it merely because clones have identical weights. But it changes what is being computed: each individual’s criticality disappears as their mass tends to zero, while a continuum supplies no canonical uniform random coalition or permutation. A useful limit therefore needs choices about aggregation, normalization, quota scaling, and the replication sequence. Those choices could define a worthwhile asymptotic voting-power project, but they are not computational results of this paper, and the paper’s general MSMP framework does not determine them.

The other examples do not provide a better population axis. Making trainees a continuum does not change that the paper measures the importance of distinct exercises; graph vertices retain their positions and adjacencies; constraints and formulas retain their own identities. Taking masses over those agents would average application outcomes without continuizing the set whose importance is being measured.

So the strongest negative case is that a continuous weighted-voting study may be worthwhile on its own, but this paper supplies no computational result for it to mirror. The weak point is that a reader might count a new algorithmic study of type-level limits as a sufficient mirror despite that gap. I would regard that as a promising neighboring project, not a continuization of a result established here.
