Why no mirror

But preserving its strategic question requires an individual to have positive mass; in an atomless society, unilateral reporting leaves the aggregate unchanged and an efficient no-transfer rule attains \(\alpha=1\). The proposed unit deletion retains atomic agents, while fractional multiplicities do not specify a coherent population of such agents, so the stated mirror changes the question.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets Theorem 1’s worst-case mechanism guarantee. It leaves Theorem 2’s i.i.d. probability guarantee, Theorem 3’s finite-population ratio, and the paper’s other comparisons untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make is a narrow one: ask whether the paper’s near-optimal redistribution result survives when valuation classes have rational masses rather than integer populations. That continues the population. The fact that individual valuations already range over \([0,1]\) is not itself the mirror.

A plausible regime is a citywide public project, such as a bridge or broadband network, with many households but relatively few recurring willingness-to-pay levels. A type is a household’s valuation \(v\) for the project; the project cost and the no-build cost share are common parameters. Write \(T_C=\{p/q\in[0,1]:q\le C\}\), and let \(\lambda_v\) be the agent-equivalent mass of type \(v\), with total mass \(N\). The support size is \(\tau\), which may be far smaller than \(N\); the masses have binary encoding length \(L\). The project still has two outcomes: build if \(\int v\,d\lambda(v)\ge1\), and otherwise do not build. Its first-best total utility is
\[ S(\lambda)=\max\left\{\int v\,d\lambda(v),1\right\}. \]
No-build utility remains \(1/N\) per unit of mass, so total no-build utility is \(1\).

The decision variable is an anonymous Groves rebate rule \(h_N\), evaluated on the other agents’ mass profile. To retain the paper’s incentive question, a tagged type-\(v\) agent is removed as \(\lambda-\delta_v\); the rebate must depend on that profile, not on the tagged agent’s report. A precise mass-design problem is: for fixed \(N\) and a finite type set \(T\subseteq T_C\), find a finitely represented \(h_N\) maximizing \(\alpha\), subject for every allowed rational mass profile \(\lambda\) to
\[ (N-1)S(\lambda) \le \sum_{v\in T}\lambda_v h_N(\lambda-\delta_v) \le (N-\alpha)S(\lambda). \]
These are the paper’s weak-budget-balance and welfare-ratio constraints with sums over voters replaced by mass-weighted sums. For the tagged-agent version, require every represented type to have at least one unit of mass so that the deletion is defined. This gives a concrete prover’s question: does a rebate rule exist for fractional multiplicities that attains the paper’s guarantee, and can it be represented and evaluated efficiently in \(N,\tau,L,C\)?

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Under the assumption that every valuation is rational with denominator at most the constant \(C\), it constructs an anonymous VCG redistribution mechanism with worst-case ratio
\[ \alpha_N = 1-\frac{4C(4C-1)(3N-2-8C)}{N(N-2)}, \]
which tends to \(1\) as \(N\) grows. Its construction uses the following function:
\[ f(a,b,z)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{a+b}{2}+\dfrac z3, & z\ge1,\\[4pt] \dfrac z3+\dfrac{\max\{a+b,1-z\}}3 +\dfrac{\mathbf 1_{a>0}\bigl(\max\{a+b,1-z\}-\max\{b,1-z\}\bigr)}6 +\dfrac{\mathbf 1_{b>0}\bigl(\max\{a+b,1-z\}-\max\{a,1-z\}\bigr)}6 +\dfrac{\mathbf 1_{a>0\land b>0}(1-z)}3, & z<1. \end{cases} \]
The paper reports an \(O(N^3)\) implementation for its finite-population mechanism. This is a constructive algorithmic anchor, though the paper gives no named NP-hardness or complexity-class theorem. I would not describe Theorem 1 as proving that the continuous problem is already in P.

For the mass version, my expectation is Class A, as an inference from the construction rather than a result of the paper. With fixed \(T_C\), the formula is piecewise linear in the aggregate mass and uses only a constant-size sample of types. That suggests a finite LP or pricing formulation whose work can be organized around \(\tau\), rather than enumerating \(N\) individuals. The central follow-up questions are whether the stated \(\alpha_N\) remains valid for rational masses, and what the optimal ratio is if it does not.

This mirror covers Theorem 1’s worst-case mechanism-design guarantee. It does not use the paper’s separate i.i.d. probability result as its anchor, and it does not continue the project’s outcome space.

The weakest point is the passage from distinct named agents to fractional type masses. The construction samples distinct voters and its proof uses finite-population counting. A mass profile has no automatic “three distinct voters” interpretation, and a fractional cohort can change those counting identities. The tagged-agent deletion above preserves the strategic question explicitly, but the theorem’s proof does not transfer just by replacing sums with integrals. That is why this is a real continuous-mass problem for a prover to settle, rather than a claim that the paper has already solved it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mass formulation has to preserve the paper’s incentive constraint, and that constraint depends on an individual agent being an atom. In the finite model, changing one report can change the build decision and the rebate arguments \(h(\theta_{-i})\). The construction also averages over triples of distinct agents.

In a genuinely nonatomic society with normalized type distribution \(\mu\), one person’s report changes neither \(\mu\) nor its aggregate value. If the reported society determines whether to build, a zero-transfer mechanism can choose the efficient outcome, satisfy weak budget balance, and make truthful reporting weakly dominant: an individual is indifferent among reports because none changes the outcome. Its welfare ratio is \(1\). The strategic redistribution problem that makes Theorem 1 interesting has disappeared. Requiring the limit mechanism to retain a VCG rebate structure could prevent that trivialization, but then the VCG restriction is being imposed after individual incentives no longer require it.

The proposed \(\lambda_v h(\lambda-\delta_v)\) formulation tries to keep individual influence by deleting one unit of mass. If every unit is a strategic agent, type masses must be integer counts. Allowing noninteger \(\lambda_v\) treats fractional amounts of a cohort as full strategic agents in the incentive constraint. One can define that extension, or give agents different positive weights and include weight in their type, but neither follows from the paper’s model. Each requires a new choice about how agents’ influence and the sampling of distinct agents work. The question “does the guarantee survive?” therefore has no unique continuous meaning until that choice is made.

The asymptotic guarantee has a further mismatch with an ordinary population limit. The paper’s proof says that any nonzero valuation is at least \(1/C\); whenever the total valuation is below \(4\), there can be at most \(4C\) agents with nonzero valuations. Those are the profiles relevant to the theorem’s nontrivial loss. In normalized population mass, their share tends to zero as \(N\) grows, so the profiles converge to the all-zero society. Any fixed positive mass of positive valuations instead makes total valuation grow with \(N\), and Lemma 1 gives ratio \(1\). To retain the exceptional agents’ effects, the model must track an \(O(1/N)\) boundary layer or preserve those agents as atoms. Either way, the central object is no longer just a continuous distribution over voter types.

This defeats the proposed fractional-mass mirror on its own terms. A finite-size study with agent mass \(\varepsilon\) might still be worthwhile, but it would study how atomic incentive effects change as \(\varepsilon\) shrinks; at each positive \(\varepsilon\), the substantive problem remains finite-agent mechanism design. I cannot honestly rule out every useful model of that kind. The negative case is strongest against treating Theorem 1 as a direct continuous-population problem: its key incentive effects live in the atoms that the limit removes.
