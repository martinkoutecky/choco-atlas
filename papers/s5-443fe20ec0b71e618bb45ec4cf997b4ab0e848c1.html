The case FOR (proponent)

There is no anchor in the required form: the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. It does say in §2.1 that the general RMV-\(n_1\)-\(n_2\) problem is NP-complete, with a reduction from Set Packing, but that claim is unnumbered. The introduction’s claim that Set Packing is NP-hard is also unnumbered and cites earlier work. Algorithm 1 and the experimental tables do not supply eligible anchors.

If the paper’s unnumbered Set Packing claim can serve as a provisional lead, the strongest mirror I see is Continuous High-Multiplicity Set Packing. An instance has job classes \(R\), a finite support \(T\) of offer types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-capita job supplies \(q_r\), and, for each type \(t\), a bundle \(a_{rt}\) and profit \(w_t\). Type \(t\) describes an offer with the same bundle and profit for every bidder of that type. Choose accepted mass \(x_t\) to maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T} w_t x_t \]
subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}a_{rt}x_t\le q_r\) for every \(r\in R\). A solution is a feasible mass vector attaining the maximum. Each unit of accepted mass receives its whole bundle; only the aggregate allocation is fractional.

A plausible regime is a large procurement exchange with many recurring supplier offers and standardized job classes: many bidders, but relatively few distinct bundle-and-price types. With an explicit support, this is a rational LP, so I expect it to be tractable (Class A), even though discrete winner determination is NP-hard. Further questions include how the continuous optimum relates to discrete allocations and whether rounding it gives useful guarantees.

The weak point is that this mirror needs repeated full offer types and interchangeable jobs. Without that regime, it risks being merely the familiar fractional relaxation of the paper’s instance. And under your anchor rule, it remains only a provisional mirror: the paper does not provide a numbered result to anchor it to.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper gives little to anchor a continuous mirror to. Its contribution is a simulated annealing heuristic and experimental comparisons; it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational result. The closest candidates are the cited Set Packing hardness claim in the introduction and the unnumbered NP-completeness claim for RMV-\(n_1\)-\(n_2\) in §2.1. The latter concerns a preprocessing certificate problem, not the paper’s main winner-determination contribution. Under the requested anchor standard, neither supplies a qualifying named result.

RMV is also a poor target for continuization. Its definition depends on counts of selected bids, disjoint families, and inclusion between the unions of their job sets. Replacing families by masses makes “coverage” depend on whether a type has any positive mass at all, so an arbitrarily small amount can change the condition. Keeping unit masses preserves the original discrete problem. One could define a mass-based dominance-certificate problem, but it would be a new question with no clear connection to the paper’s preprocessing result.

That does not defeat the proponent’s stronger mirror of winner determination. In a market with repeated offers and job classes, let a complete bidder type specify its bid menu, and let \(y_{t,b}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned bid \(b\). With per-capita job supplies \(q_r\) and type masses \(\mu_t\), the continuous allocation is the LP
\[ \max \sum_{t,b} w_{t,b}y_{t,b} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_b y_{t,b}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,b:\,r\in b} y_{t,b}\le q_r,\qquad y_{t,b}\ge 0. \]
Each unit of mass can still receive a whole bid; only the aggregate allocation is fractional. A market with many bidders, standardized offers, and scaled job supplies is a plausible regime. The objective needs aggregate profit, not bidder identity. This is therefore a well-posed continuous-population computation, even if the LP itself is a familiar relaxation.

So the negative case is strong only in a narrower sense: the paper has no qualifying named result to mirror, and RMV’s particular complexity claim resists a natural mass version. I cannot honestly extend that to “no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario.” The continuous winner-determination question survives the stronger modeling choices; the case against it is that it may be a modest, standard LP result, not that the mirror is ill-posed.
