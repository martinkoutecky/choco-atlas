The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that strategic voting here has a natural continuous-population extension. But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a complexity mirror to.

The paper’s strongest conceptual anchors are Theorem 2, proved here, which says every onto, non-dictatorial social choice rule with at least three alternatives is safely manipulable; and Theorem 3, also proved here, which says there is a profile, a report \(L\), and a voter \(i\) such that \(i\) strictly prefers the outcome after reporting \(L\), and \(L\) is safe for \(i\). Neither theorem asserts an algorithm or a complexity classification. The polynomial-time results mentioned in the related-work section are attributed to Hazon and Elkind (2010) and Ianovski et al. (2011); this paper does not state them as numbered results of its own. I would not present any of these as qualifying complexity anchors.

The population mirror itself is plausible. In a large election, a type can be a complete strict ranking, and its mass the fraction of the electorate holding that ranking. A public campaign might prompt some fraction of a like-minded bloc to cast the same strategic ballot while the rest vote sincerely. Millions of voters can then be represented by a much smaller support of ranking types. This is precisely a continuous population, rather than a fractional outcome or a noise model.

A concrete candidate question, inspired by the paper’s safe-vote definitions, is Safe Strategic Mass Voting under Borda. An instance gives candidates, a fixed tie-breaking order, and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings. Given a type \(r\), let \(w_0\) be the sincere Borda winner. A report \(L\ne r\) moves mass \(q\) from \(r\) to \(L\), producing \(\mu^q=\mu-qe_r+qe_L\). Ask whether there is a report \(L\) and rational \(q>0\) such that the winner under \(\mu^q\) is strictly preferred to \(w_0\) by type \(r\), while for every \(q'\in(0,\mu_r]\), the winner under \(\mu^{q'}\) is at least as preferred as \(w_0\). A solution is such a report and mass, with the exact mass ranges checked. The objective is a safe improvement; the paper assigns no cost to changing votes.

This is a genuine analogue of safe and unsafe strategic voting, but it does not inherit Theorems 2 or 3: those quantify over finite profiles and, in Theorem 3, a pivotal individual. In a continuum an individual has zero mass, so the group-mass incentive in Theorem 2 is the more natural bridge. The paper’s unnumbered Borda Example 3 makes that bridge tangible: among 94 voters, between 4 and 8 of the 17 \(ABC\) voters switching to \(ACB\) elects \(A\), while 10 or more elect \(C\). Those thresholds become population fractions in the mirror, but the example is not a computational anchor.

The mirror’s scope is therefore limited to mass-based safe-vote questions for scoring rules; it does not cover the paper’s universal claims for arbitrary social choice rules. Its weakest point is the shift from a single voter’s strict gain to a coordinated positive mass, which changes the pivotality question. For a fixed report, checking outcomes along the transfer is tractable: scores vary linearly with \(q\), with changes only at pairwise score crossings. Finding a report when the number of candidates varies is a separate computational problem. The paper gives no basis for assigning that search to Class A, B, or C.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 says every onto, non-dictatorial rule is safely manipulable; Theorem 3 sharpens that existence claim to a single voter. Neither gives an algorithm, complexity bound, or even a computational problem with an input representation. The Borda examples illustrate the definitions but do not state a complexity result. The algorithms mentioned in related work belong to other papers.

There is also a real obstacle to continuizing the headline theorem as stated. Theorem 2 quantifies over arbitrary social choice rules, including rules that may depend on voter identities. A distribution over rankings does not determine the outcome of such a rule: two profiles with the same type masses can yield different winners. Adding identities to the types preserves that information, but leaves each type with mass \(1/n\), so the aggregation that motivates continuization disappears. Restricting to anonymous rules such as Borda avoids this problem, but changes the theorem’s domain.

Theorem 3 has a separate limit problem: one voter has zero mass in a continuum. Replacing that voter with a positive mass of like-minded voters changes the claim from individual safe manipulation to a coordinated mass campaign. Theorem 2 does involve groups, but supplies no lower bound on their share of the population that would ensure a non-vanishing mass in a limit.

The Borda example is the positive case’s best foothold. Its thresholds for switching \(ABC\) voters to \(ACB\) translate naturally into proportions, and the resulting mass-transfer question is coherent. But the example itself is a fixed profile and a fixed report, not a computational result. Searching over reports in a new mass-based Borda problem would be a researcher-defined question, not a complexity mirror established by this paper.

So the negative case is strong if “mirror” means a continuous counterpart to a computational result in the paper: none of the proposed anchors meets that standard. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could be built from the paper. The mass-based safe-voting problem is recognizable and plausible; the case against it is that it is an adjacent new problem, not a continuization of the paper’s computational contribution.
