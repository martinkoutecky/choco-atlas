The continuous mirror question

Given \(k\ge2\), \(q\) rational elementary cake intervals, and a finite type distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t\), where each type has a family label \(f(t)\), mass \(\mu_t\), and a normalized rational piecewise-constant valuation \(V_t\), let \(p_j=\sum_{t:f(t)=j}\mu_t\). Find a permutation \(\pi\) of the families and cut points \(0=x_0\le x_1\le\cdots\le x_k=1\), defining \(X_{\pi(r)}=[x_{r-1},x_r]\), such that for every family \(j\), \(\sum_{t:f(t)=j,\;V_t(X_j)\ge 1/k}\mu_t\ge p_j/k\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type high-multiplicity family cake-cutting model in which types carry masses \(\mu_t\), family labels, and piecewise-rational valuation densities; the decision variables are the connected interval cuts and family assignment, and the objective is feasibility of democratic proportionality.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a Class-A mirror of the paper’s democratic-fairness result. I would not claim a hardness result: this paper contains no NP-hardness theorem. Its best computational anchor is the efficient-protocol theorem.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6, labelled pos-mpr-connected in the supplied manuscript, proved here using a moving-knife construction. It states that, for every \(k\ge 2\), with \(k\) equal-entitlement families, there exists a connected division such that at least a \(1/k\) fraction of each family values its own piece at least \(1/k\) of the cake; moreover, such a division can be found by an efficient protocol. The moving-knife idea is attributed to Dubins and Spanier, but the family-level theorem is proved in this paper. The paper is [Fair Cake-Cutting among Families](https://econ.biu.ac.il/sites/econ/files/shared/efrat/fair_cake-cutting.pdf).

Here is the continuous-population problem I would hand to a prover.

Call it \(\mathsf{CDemConn}_\infty\). The cake is \(C=[0,1]\), divided into \(q\) elementary intervals with rational endpoints. There are \(k\) fixed families with equal entitlements. A type \(t\) consists of:

  • its family \(f(t)\in\{1,\dots,k\}\);
  • a rational, piecewise-constant valuation density over the \(q\) elementary intervals;
  • a mass \(\mu_t\ge 0\).

The valuation is normalized so that \(V_t(C)=1\). Let

\[ p_j=\sum_{t:f(t)=j}\mu_t \]

be the mass of family \(j\). The input is the finite type distribution \(\mu\), not a list of named individuals.

The action is to choose an ordering of the families and cut points

\[ 0=x_0\le x_1\le\cdots\le x_k=1, \]

giving one connected interval \(X_j\) to each family. The output is valid if, for every family \(j\),

\[ \sum_{\substack{t:f(t)=j\\ V_t(X_j)\ge 1/k}}\mu_t \;\ge\; \frac{p_j}{k}. \]

Thus at least \(1/k\) of the population mass in every family approves its family’s share as proportionally fair. The objective is to find any such connected allocation.

This is genuinely a population continuization. The cake remains continuous because it already was continuous in the paper; the new object is the society \(\mu\). A type is a family member with the same valuation profile and family affiliation as every other member of that type. Millions of residents may occupy only a few land-use types—for example, citizens of several states dividing a riverbank, with repeated agricultural, recreational, and conservation profiles. The natural regime is \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the underlying population and \(\tau=|T|\) is the number of distinct family–valuation types.

The high-multiplicity bridge is exact. A discrete instance with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\) induces \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Conversely, rational masses can be expanded into a finite population by multiplying by a common denominator. Because identical agents always receive the same value from a given family piece, replacing their count by mass does not alter the fairness question.

The paper’s protocol also survives the translation. Each type has a cut location at which a prefix reaches the relevant value threshold. Within each family, ordinary medians become weighted medians or weighted quantiles of these type-level marks. The moving-knife recursion then cuts off a family and continues on the remaining interval. With piecewise-rational valuations, these marks and cuts can be computed exactly, and the work is polynomial in \(k\), \(q\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length—not in \(N\). I therefore expect \(\mathsf{CDemConn}_\infty\) to be tractable.

The mirror is plausible because it preserves precisely what the authors study: fixed families, public family shares, individual valuations rather than an average valuation, the \(1/k\) proportionality threshold, and connectedness. It does not replace democratic fairness by average fairness or make the geometry trivial. It merely replaces repeated indistinguishable members by their mass.

The natural further question is the type-sensitive optimization version: given \(h\), find a democratic-fair allocation minimizing the total number of connected components. The paper’s component lower bounds suggest that the discrete dependence on \(n\) should become dependence on the number and masses of distinct valuation types. In particular, repeating each type arbitrarily many times should not increase the required number of components, while introducing genuinely different valuation types may still create the same obstruction.

My weakest point is that “efficient protocol” in the paper belongs to the cake-cutting query model, not to a formal bit-complexity model with finite inputs. For arbitrary nonatomic valuation oracles, saying “polynomial time” is not meaningful. The Class-A claim therefore requires the explicit finite-type, piecewise-rational encoding above. I regard that as a natural high-multiplicity instance regime, not a change to the underlying problem—but the paper itself does not prove this exact Turing-model formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem \(\ref{pos-mpr-connected}\) is not actually a finite-input complexity result. Its “efficient protocol” is in the cake-cutting query model, with arbitrary nonatomic valuation oracles. The paper specifies neither a bit representation of valuations nor polynomial-time arithmetic or output encoding. Thus \(\mathsf{CDemConn}_\infty\), with rational piecewise-constant densities, is an additional computational model invented by the proponent, not a complexity theorem already present in the paper.

Grant that repair, however, and the negative case becomes much weaker. In every faithful finite-type model, a type is fully determined by its family and valuation. The moving-knife proof then uses the population only through weighted approval counts. For each type one computes the relevant cut location, and the family’s stopping point is simply a weighted quantile of those locations. Recursing on the residual cake repeats the same operation. Replacing multiplicities by masses therefore produces an exact weighted execution of the existing protocol; it does not expose a new population-dependent combinatorial problem, pricing problem, or hardness boundary. This is a useful compression of the query transcript, but arguably not a worthwhile continuization for the ChoCo programme.

The obvious repairs do not decisively improve the case. Refining the finite type space merely supplies more valuation descriptions; identities that do not affect valuations or family membership cannot matter under the paper’s fairness criterion. Making the type distribution genuinely uncountable removes the finite computational input unless an oracle model is specified. Adding identity-sensitive constraints would create a different fairness problem, not a mirror of democratic fairness. Varying family masses or entitlements changes thresholds but not this basic weighted-quantile structure.

The proposed component-minimization variant is a more serious escape route: it could produce a genuine optimization problem. But that would be a new result built around the paper’s geometry, not a computational mirror of the theorem the proponent actually anchored. Its difficulty would be driven mainly by distinct valuation profiles and cake structure, rather than by population multiplicity.

So the negative case can legitimately downgrade the anchor: the paper supplies a query protocol, not a formal complexity result, and its most direct high-multiplicity translation is close to a weighted restatement. But it cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The finite-type democratic-fairness problem is a sensible population continuum, and the component-optimization extension may itself be valuable.
