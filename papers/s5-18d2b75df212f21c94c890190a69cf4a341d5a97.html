What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror concerns Proposition 5.3’s average-tree allocation formula, motivated by the production application; it leaves the axiomatic characterizations and core and precore results without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

Proposition 3.3 proves uniqueness of an allocation rule from axioms; Propositions 4.1–4.2 show that the average tree solution satisfies those axioms and is the unique such rule; Proposition 5.1 establishes precore and, under an extra condition, core membership. These are substantive results, but they are characterization and stability results, not computational-complexity results.

The closest positive lead is Proposition 5.3, which represents the solution through hierarchical outcomes, and the paper’s explicit calculations in its production and Hotelling applications. But neither gives a named complexity bound for computing the solution. Calling that an “in P” anchor would require choosing an input representation and complexity measure the paper does not supply. So there is no qualifying anchor here, and no honest per-anchor tractability or hardness prediction to report.

A plausible, but unanchored, population mirror would be a large industry with many interchangeable firms in each of a finite set of technological roles. A type would specify a firm’s role, activity levels, and economic characteristics; mass would be the fraction of firms of that type. The action could be a distribution of each type’s mass across activity levels, and the objective could be to compute the mass-weighted average-tree allocation of production surplus. That setting has a credible high-multiplicity story, and the paper’s production application gives it some motivation. Its weakness is fundamental: the paper does not define how worth, communication, or the average-tree solution extends when mass within a type splits across activity levels, nor does it analyze the computational problem that results. It is a research direction, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named computational anchor, correctly. Propositions 3.3 and 4.2 characterize a solution axiomatically; Proposition 5.1 proves stability. None states the complexity of a computational problem, and the paper does not specify an input representation or complexity measure for computing the allocation. So there is no computational result here whose complexity landscape a continuous mirror could chart.

The strongest candidate for a mirror is a large industry made up of repeated, finite firm networks. A type could specify a firm’s position in a network, its activity levels, and its economic characteristics; the task could be to compute the average-tree allocation by type. This has a plausible high-multiplicity story, especially for repeated local production or market clusters. But in a forest, the restricted game separates across components. Repeating a fixed component lets you compute its allocation once and scale by its mass; multiplicity adds no new allocation problem. For a single growing connected network, preserving the paper’s payoffs requires types to encode the firm’s position and its coalition-level worth data. Grouping firms only by a coarse role would lose information the allocation uses. Restricting the game enough to avoid that problem might yield an interesting new model, but it would not mirror a computational result established in this paper.

That is the strongest negative case: this paper is a poor ChoCo target because its contributions are axiomatic and stability results, not complexity results. It does not prove that every population-continuous extension would be uninteresting. A repeated-network model could be worth studying on its own; the case against it is that it would supply a new computational question, rather than continue this paper’s computational landscape.
