Its numbered Results are experimental findings: Result 1 ranks observed outcomes across mechanisms, and Result 3 explains PFU’s performance in terms of tentative reports and best responses. Neither asks for an algorithm or establishes a complexity bound. That leaves no result here to continue into a computational question about a continuous society.

The strongest rescue would start from Result 1: imagine a large population grouped by peak and behavioral type, randomly matched in pairs, and ask for the limiting frequency of Uniform outcomes under each mechanism. That is a coherent high-multiplicity experiment. But it aggregates the paper’s two-person interactions; it does not extend a computational result. Given the type distribution and each pair’s outcome behavior, the expected frequency is just an average over pairs. Making the model richer by adding learning histories or response policies may raise substantive questions, but those are new behavioral questions, not complexity mirrors of this paper.

Result 3 has the same limit. A population distribution of tentative reports might summarize what subjects show one another, but the finding concerns how two paired subjects react over time. Aggregate report masses alone do not capture those pairwise histories. Adding a continuum of time or a new model of population feedback would change the object of study; it would not supply the missing computational result.

One could instead generalize Uniform rationing to a continuum of agents and ask how to compute its allocation. That is a plausible model, but it strips away the paper’s comparisons of strategic mechanisms and behavior; with finitely many peak types, the allocation reduces to a scalar cutoff calculation. A more ambitious question about designing feedback for a large population might be worthwhile, but it would be a new project rather than a mirror of the paper.
