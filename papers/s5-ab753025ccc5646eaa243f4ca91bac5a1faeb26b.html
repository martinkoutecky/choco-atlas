The continuous mirror question

Given a bounded rational polygon \(P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2\), compute the exact Lebesgue area of \(P\cap D\), where \(D=\{(\alpha,q):0<\alpha<1,\ 0\le q<1-\alpha\}\). Here \(\alpha\) is the population mass of veto type \(A\), type \(B\) has mass \(1-\alpha\), and \(q\) is the threshold for \(B\): a coalition wins exactly when \(x_A=\alpha\) and \(x_B\ge q\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of two voter types with masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). A rule is parameterized by its threshold \(q\), with coalition masses \((x_A,x_B)\); the quantity computed is the area of valid society-rule pairs in \(P\), equivalently \(\int_{P\cap D}1\,d\alpha\,dq\).

The objection that survived

The area ranges over society compositions and rule thresholds, whereas Proposition 1(2) counts games at fixed \(n\); the lattice normalization links them but does not make the area a query on one supplied society.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1(2) only; it leaves the paper’s other enumeration formulas and bijections unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For three or more types, can normalized counts of complete simple games be represented and computed as volumes over continuous type-mass and shift-minimal-profile parameters?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Proposition 1(2), proved in this paper. It states that \(\mathrm{CGV}(n,2)=n(n-1)/2\) for \(n\ge2\): the number of complete simple games, up to isomorphism, with \(n\) voters, exactly two equivalence classes, and at least one vetoer. In the proof, each split \(n=n_1+n_2\) with both parts positive has \(n_2\) possible minimal winning profiles \((n_1,a)\), where \(0\le a<n_2\). Summing over splits gives \(\sum_{n_1=1}^{n-1}(n-n_1)=n(n-1)/2\). This is an exact counting result; its formula makes the associated count computable in polynomial time, though the proposition does not state a complexity class.

Here is a continuous version of that counting question. Consider large federations with two voter-role types: a veto bloc \(A\), and a second, less powerful bloc \(B\). Members of each bloc are indistinguishable for the rule, and the number of members grows while the number of types stays at two. Let their population masses be \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). A coalition is represented by its yes-vote masses \((x_A,x_B)\). For \(0<\alpha<1\), a complete rule with \(A\) as the veto type is determined by a threshold \(q\), with \(0\le q<1-\alpha\): it passes exactly when \(x_A=\alpha\) and \(x_B\ge q\). Thus the society is continuous, while the collective outcome remains yes or no.

Call the resulting problem Two-Type Veto-Design Volume. An instance is a rational polygon \(P\) in the \((\alpha,q)\)-plane describing allowed bloc sizes and thresholds. The question is to compute the exact Lebesgue area of the valid continuous designs in \(P\), where each pair \((\alpha,q)\) counts as one design. The answer is a rational number. For the full design region
\[ D=\{(\alpha,q):0<\alpha<1,\ 0\le q<1-\alpha\}, \]
the answer is \(\int_0^1(1-\alpha)\,d\alpha=1/2\). The discrete result supplies a natural normalization: its games correspond to grid points \((\alpha,q)=(n_1/n,a/n)\), so \(n^{-2}\mathrm{CGV}(n,2)\) tends to the area of \(D\). This is a continuous population parameterization, not a fractional outcome model.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: clip the input polygon against \(D\) and compute its area. A fixed society with mass \(\alpha\) has a threshold interval of length \(1-\alpha\). The paper’s result therefore supports a precise, continuous counterpart to one of its exact counting results, though it does not suggest hardness that could dissolve under continuization.

The scenario is a federation or large membership organization where every member of one homogeneous bloc must support a proposal, alongside a second homogeneous bloc whose support is measured by its share of the whole membership. The two blocs may each contain many people, while the number of distinct voting roles remains two. A type here means the paper’s equivalence class of voters under desirability, not a preference ranking.

The mirror covers only Proposition 1(2). It does not cover the paper’s Fibonacci enumeration for three types or its bijections among distinguished voter classes. A natural next question is whether the volume formulation extends to more types and multiple shift-minimal winning profiles, and whether properties such as weightedness correspond to measurable subsets of those parameter spaces.

The weak point is that this is a continuous design-space enumeration, rather than a manipulation or intervention problem on one fixed society; the measure is motivated by the normalized lattice count, but it is an extra modeling choice. Also, an atomless model cannot preserve literal pointwise veto power: omitting a measure-zero individual does not change \(x_A\). The mirror captures the bloc-level requirement that any positive loss of \(A\)’s support defeats the proposal. That is plausible for a large institution with a unanimous bloc, but it is the point an author or referee would need to accept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1(2) is the strongest anchor, and it is a real exact enumeration result. The two-bloc scenario is also plausible: large groups can share a voting role, so there is no convincing objection based on the absence of multiplicity or on individual veto power disappearing in an atomless limit. A bloc-level unanimity requirement is a reasonable continuous version.

The problem is what the proposed volume counts. At a point \((\alpha,q)\), \(\alpha\) describes a society’s composition, but \(q\) describes the voting rule. The area integrates over both, counting a space of society–rule designs. Proposition 1(2), by contrast, counts rules for each fixed number of voters. Its normalized lattice count does converge to the stated area, but that makes the area a continuous counterpart to an asymptotic enumeration—not a computational question on a society supplied as an instance.

Fixing \(\alpha\) makes the distinction clearer: the continuous question becomes the measure of admissible thresholds for that society. That can be a useful design statistic, but the paper gives no constraints or objective that turn it into a computational problem about intervention, robustness, or optimization on a given society. Adding such costs or constraints might produce a worthwhile continuous problem; they would also supply the problem’s main content rather than mirror the paper’s counting result. The choice to count each \((\alpha,q)\) equally is natural for the particular lattice limit, but it is a measure on design choices, not a consequence of the society distribution itself.

So the strongest negative case is one of scope: the proposed mirror is mathematically coherent and meaningfully tied to the proposition, but it moves from population-as-input computation to measuring a rule-design space. That distinction matters for ChoCo’s stated programme. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no scenario works: a large federation could make this extension worthwhile to researchers studying continuous voting-system design. If the authors regard such design-space enumeration as their problem, Proposition 1(2) survives the objection. The negative case is strongest only under ChoCo’s narrower remit of computational questions on a given continuous society.
