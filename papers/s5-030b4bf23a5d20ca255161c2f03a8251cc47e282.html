What the mirror covers

The proposed feasibility task adapts Theorem 10's fair-core criterion; it does not cover Theorem 12's efficient variant, Theorem 14's stable core, or Theorem 19's one-deviation principle.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this feasibility problem for fixed and unrestricted \(|T|\), under a precise encoding of \(X\) and \(G\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is a plausible *population extension* of the paper’s dynamic market model. Imagine a large consortium of small suppliers, with many firms but few standardized production types. A type records everything the model uses: technology, endowment, and maintenance or decay parameters. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of firms of type \(t\); a coalition is described by the mass \(s_t\) it contains from each type. Its size, and thus any externality depending on consortium size, is measured by \(\sum_t s_t\). Allocation streams and discounting remain dynamic as in the paper; the continuization is of the population.

There is a serious limit on how far I can take this as an anchored case: the paper contains no named computational result in the sense required here. Theorems 10, 12, and 14 characterize core existence, and Theorem 19 gives a one-deviation principle. They are mathematical existence or characterization results, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar; the paper does not compute with an LP or other algorithmic encoding. So none qualifies as a computational anchor, and I cannot honestly present a theorem-to-complexity mirror as though the paper supplied one.

The closest intellectual starting point is Theorem 10, proved in this paper. It says that when the grand coalition’s worth is a constant \(d\), the transition \(V(N;\cdot)\) is continuous, and each coalition’s worth is bounded, then for every \(\varepsilon>0\), all discount factors sufficiently close to \(1\) have a nonempty \(\varepsilon\)-fair core if and only if there is an allocation \(x\in\Delta(d)\) and a game \(v\in\operatorname{conv}V(N;x)\) for which \(x\) is in the core of \(v\). This is a useful characterization to computationalize, but it is not itself a computational result.

A concrete follow-on question would be Type-aggregated Fair-Core Feasibility. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), a compact rational polytope \(X\) of per-capita allocations \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=d\), and a rational piecewise-linear map \(G(y,s)\) giving the worth of coalition mass \(s\in[0,\mu]\) after allocation \(y\), with \(G(y,\mu)=d\). Ask whether there are \(x\in X\), allocations \(y_j\in X\), and weights \(\alpha_j\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), such that
\[ x=\sum_j\alpha_j y_j \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t s_t x_t\geq \sum_j\alpha_j G(y_j,s) \quad\text{for every }s\in[0,\mu]. \]
A yes solution is such an allocation and mixture satisfying every coalition constraint; the piecewise-linear input makes those constraints finitely checkable. This is the type-mass analogue of the static convexification witness in Theorem 10. It is a proposed computational problem, not an anchor supplied by the paper. With fixed \(|T|\), finite polyhedral representations, and efficient separation over coalition masses, I would tentatively expect a Class A route. The paper does not establish that route or justify a classification for unrestricted transition maps.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a continuous-population version of their market example: technologies and externalities are preserved, while coalitions select mass rather than named firms. My weakest point is also the decisive one for the requested assessment: the paper never asks a computational question. Moreover, its finite-player core constraints become constraints over a continuum of coalition masses, and the paper gives no pricing or separation method for them. So there is a credible setting and a precise follow-on problem, but no qualifying anchor to carry the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 10 characterizes when the \(\varepsilon\)-fair core is nonempty for discount factors near \(1\); Theorems 12 and 14 give related existence characterizations, and Theorem 19 gives a one-deviation principle. None establishes a complexity bound, hardness result, or algorithm for an encoded computational problem. That defeats Theorem 10 as a computational anchor. It does not, by itself, show that a new continuous-population problem would be worthless.

The proposed Type-aggregated Fair-Core Feasibility problem is the best attempt to go beyond that gap, but it adds the computational object the paper lacks: a finite type representation, a rational polytope, and a piecewise-linear worth map. It is a plausible follow-on, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result. Its proposed tractability also needs real support. Piecewise linearity alone does not show that the existential mixture and all coalition-mass constraints have an efficient algorithm; “efficient separation over coalition masses” is a condition to establish, not a result supplied by the paper. Depending on the representation and restrictions on \(G\), the resulting complexity could reflect those choices more than the paper’s core theory.

There is a more specific modeling strain in the market example. A firm’s allocation affects its future productivity, through the factor \(\gamma^{1/(1+x^i_{t-1})}\). Firms with the same initial technology and endowment can therefore acquire different states after different allocations. A fixed list of initial types will not generally be closed under the dynamics. Tracking the resulting productivity and allocation histories may require a distribution over a continuum of states; after a coalition splits, its continuation game depends on the state distribution it takes with it. One can impose symmetric allocations or a finite-state anonymous transition to avoid this, but those are additional restrictions, not consequences of the paper’s results. The fair-core characterization may be easier to extend than the stable and credible cores, whose deviations make this state-closure problem more pronounced.

This is a case against treating the proponent’s Theorem 10 proposal as an anchored ChoCo contribution: it offers no demonstrated complexity landscape, and a realistic dynamic market extension may lose the finite-type structure that made the proposed formulation attractive. But the universal claim goes too far. A large consortium of otherwise identical suppliers is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a restricted anonymous model could yield a sensible mass-based feasibility problem. I can defeat the claimed anchor; I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror. The negative case is strong against this paper as a source of computational results and weak against such a new, carefully bounded research problem.
