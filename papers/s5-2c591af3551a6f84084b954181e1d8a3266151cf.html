Why no mirror

Both anchors are computational hardness results, but the stated role-mass models change the paper’s graph structure: each category is valued by every member of endpoint types, while each paper edge is valued only by its two particular endpoints. The proposed supplies do not encode those individual pairings. A graphing model might motivate further work, but neither anchor has a stated continuous question that preserves those incidences and passes the authors-would-recognise test.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors cover only Theorem 6 (existence of \(EFX^0_0\) allocations) and Theorem 2 (existence of \(EFX^+_-\) orientations); they leave the paper’s other results untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of EFX allocation, with Theorem 6 as the lead. It keeps the paper’s underlying question—whether indivisible items can be allocated so that envy is eliminated under the specified EFX rule—and makes populations of agents, rather than items, the continuous object.

A plausible regime is a large network of local service teams assigning recurring jobs, hosting duties, or shared-resource tasks. There may be many thousands of teams but only a modest number \(\tau\) of complete role types: teams of a type have the same valuations and eligibility for each job category. Each job copy remains indivisible; a team still receives an integral bundle. What scales continuously is the mass of each team type and the supply of each recurring job category. This is an instance-regime claim, not a claim that every fair-allocation problem has a useful population limit.

Here is a precise version. Let \(R\) be a finite set of agent types and \(F\) a finite set of item categories. Each \(e\in F\) joins two types in a role graph \(H=(R,F)\). Type \(r\) has population mass \(\mu_r>0\), with \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). Category \(e\) has supply \(q_e>0\) item copies per unit population. Its additive value to type \(r\) is the rational number \(v_r(e)\), and is zero for non-endpoint types. Values may be positive, negative, or zero.

A bundle is an integer vector \(b\in\mathbb N^F\) with finite total size; \(b_e\) counts copies of category \(e\), and \(u_r(b)=\sum_e v_r(e)b_e\). A solution is a finite list of rational masses \(\nu_{r,b}\ge0\): the mass \(\nu_{r,b}\) of type \(r\) receives the integral bundle \(b\). The plan must satisfy

\[ \sum_b \nu_{r,b}=\mu_r \quad\text{for each }r, \qquad \sum_{r,b}\nu_{r,b}b_e=q_e \quad\text{for each }e. \]

Thus copies of an item category may be distributed across people, but no person receives a fractional item. A finite instance is recovered by clearing the rational denominators and assigning the resulting numbers of people to bundle classes.

Lead question — Role-Mass EFX\({}^{0}_{0}\) Allocation. Given \(H\), the rational masses \(\mu\), supplies \(q\), and additive values \(v\), does such a plan exist in which every positive-mass bundle class is EFX\({}^{0}_{0}\) against every other positive-mass class? For a type \(r\) with bundle \(b\) and another type with bundle \(c\), if \(u_r(c)>u_r(b)\), require both:

  • for each item copy \(e\) in \(c\) with \(v_r(e)\ge0\), \(u_r(b)\ge u_r(c)-v_r(e)\);
  • for each item copy \(e\) in \(b\) with \(v_r(e)\le0\), \(u_r(b)-v_r(e)\ge u_r(c)\).

These are the paper’s two EFX\({}^{0}_{0}\) tests for additive valuations, applied to every pair of agents represented by positive-mass bundle classes. This is a population allocation question: the solution is a distribution of people among bundles, not a fractional allocation of the bundles themselves.

The anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It states that, for the paper’s mixed-item graph allocation model with additive valuations, deciding whether an EFX\({}^{0}_{0}\) allocation exists is NP-complete. Items may be allocated to any agent; only their endpoint agents can value them nonzero. The paper proves the theorem by reducing from Circuit-SAT using gadgets for logical gates and wires. The mirror keeps the mixed values, graph-based item interests, unrestricted allocation, and the same fairness test. It covers this existence result, not the paper’s whole landscape.

My initial expectation is hardness in the type-support parameters, because the circuit logic in the reduction is carried by the graph and valuation pattern. But the theorem does not itself establish hardness for this continuous question: mass can be split among different integral bundle classes, potentially weakening the gadget logic. The substantive computational question is whether that flexibility dissolves the discrete hardness or whether the logic can be made robust to it.

A second, distinct mirror is worthwhile for Theorem 2, also proved here. The theorem says that deciding whether a mixed instance has an EFX\({}^{+}_{-}\) orientation is NP-complete even for additive valuations. An orientation assigns each item to one of its two endpoint agents. The proof reduces from \((3,B2)\)-SAT: each variable occurs exactly twice positively and twice negatively, and each clause contains three distinct literals. In the continuous version, impose the same role graph and supply constraints, but require that every copy of category \(e\) be assigned to an agent of one of its endpoint types. For any envying pair with bundles \(b,c\), the EFX\({}^{+}_{-}\) conditions are: removing any copy \(e\) from \(c\) with \(v_r(e)>0\) eliminates the envy, and removing any copy \(e\) from \(b\) with \(v_r(e)<0\) eliminates it. The question is whether a feasible mass plan over integral bundles satisfying those conditions exists.

I would also initially put this orientation mirror in the “hardness may transfer” camp: the SAT structure lives in the role graph and type support, not in the number of people of each type. The mass orientation can divide item supply between endpoint populations, though, so the discrete reduction is not already a proof. This anchor is useful on its own because it tests whether endpoint restrictions change the effect of population scaling.

The programme that follows these mirrors is clear: establish a discrete-to-continuous dictionary for replicated instances; determine whether finite-support bundle plans suffice for the unrestricted measurable version; and study complexity in the number of agent types, item categories, and rational bit length. For both questions, one should test whether the configuration space of integral bundles admits a tractable separation or optimization method, and whether the corresponding discrete hardness survives population splitting.

The weakest point is the move from individual edges to recurring item categories between populations of endpoint types. In the paper, an edge is incident to two particular agents. In this mirror, a category summarizes repeated items associated with role types, so it is a natural high-multiplicity generalization rather than a literal rescaling of every finite graph instance. The finite-support policy is also an explicit modeling choice; whether it is without loss for an unrestricted continuum population is open. Those are real qualifications. The case for the mirror is that it preserves the central object the authors study—indivisible bundles judged by their EFX conditions—while making the population large and type-homogeneous. Fractional mass records how many agents receive each integral bundle; it does not make a person’s bundle divisible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that both proposed mirrors lose the feature that makes these theorems about allocations on graphs. Theorem 6 and Theorem 2 are named computational results, so “there is no result to mirror” is unavailable; nor does the paper already solve their continuous-population versions.

In the proposed role-mass model, an item category joins two *types*. But an edge in the paper joins two particular agents, and only those two value it. If every agent of either endpoint type values every copy of the category, the item is no longer local to two agents. If each copy instead has two designated individual endpoints, the masses and category supplies do not specify those pairings. Keeping the pairings as part of each agent’s complete type makes types carry their neighborhoods and item identities; type support then grows with the graph rather than capturing a high-multiplicity regime.

That distinction is central to both anchors. The circuit reduction for Theorem 6 uses particular agents, priceless edges, and exact wire connections. The \((3,B2)\)-SAT reduction for Theorem 2 likewise relies on particular variable and clause endpoints, with orientation assigning each edge to one of its two agents. Replacing these endpoint agents with populations does not simply let mass split while preserving the construction: it changes which agents value each item or discards the incidence data that connects the gadgets. That is a modeling objection, not a claim that the reductions’ hardness would survive.

A stronger faithful proposal could retain the incidence structure in a graphing or another model of large sparse networks. But then the input must encode the links among individuals as well as the distribution of their roles; it is no longer just a distribution over finitely many complete types. Alternatively, one could scale by repeating fixed finite gadgets. That preserves the local interactions, but EFX comparisons across disjoint copies see only zero-valued items, so the population becomes a mixture of local gadget allocations rather than the proposed type-and-supply optimization. Either route may be worth studying, but neither is the claimed finite-type mirror of these theorems.

A generic high-multiplicity EFX problem with shared item categories might also be worthwhile. The difficulty for the negative case is that it drops the paper’s exact-two-endpoint valuation structure, making it a neighboring fair-allocation problem rather than a mirror of either anchor. I would not use existing high-multiplicity work against it; under the programme’s rule, that would support the regime’s plausibility.

So the objections defeat the stated role-mass formulations and make the faithful versions substantially less direct. They do not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. If the authors accept a broader role-based EFX question, or find a useful graphing formulation, either anchor could still motivate one. The honest negative case is therefore weak at the universal claim the task demands.
