The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is unusually natural, but it does not meet your anchor requirement. The paper contains no named result about computational complexity or algorithms: its theorems and propositions characterize which mechanisms satisfy incentive and coalition-proofness conditions.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for a mechanism \(F=(Q,P^0,P^1)\) with \(P^1-P^0\notin V\), robust implementability together with the paper’s UB-robust coalition-proofness condition holds exactly when provision is monotone in the number of net beneficiaries: for every pair of valuation profiles \(v,v'\), if \(s_1(v)\ge s_1(v')\), then \(Q(v)\ge Q(v')\), where \(s_1(v)=|\{i:v_i>P^1-P^0\}|\). In other words, the provision decision may depend on how many people favor provision, but not on their preference intensities.

A plausible continuous-population extension would take \(V\) as the set of valuation types and \(\mu\in\Delta(V)\) as the fraction of society with each valuation. Payments remain \(P^0\) when the good is not provided and \(P^1\) when it is. Define \(s_1(\mu)=\sum_{v>P^1-P^0}\mu_v\). The question would be: characterize anonymous provision rules \(q:\Delta(V)\to\{0,1\}\) that are robustly implementable and coalition-proof in a nonatomic society. The natural conjecture is that they are exactly the rules satisfying \(s_1(\mu)\ge s_1(\nu)\Rightarrow q(\mu)\ge q(\nu)\). A plausible regime is a large municipality deciding whether to provide a public facility, with many residents but a small set of shared valuation types.

That is a credible continuous-population mirror of the theorem’s *economic question*. Its weak point is that extending the paper’s coalition concept to a continuum—where coalitions have mass and individuals have zero mass—needs careful definition. More decisively, even if the extension works, it is a mechanism-characterization result, not a computational problem or complexity result. I would not present it as a qualifying ChoCo computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor, but it is a characterization of mechanisms, not a computational result. It says when robust implementability and coalition-proofness coincide with monotonicity in the number of net beneficiaries. It defines no computational problem, input representation, algorithm, or complexity claim. The same is true of the paper’s other numbered results. Under ChoCo’s gate, there is no computational result here to mirror.

The best continuous version is easy to state: represent the population by a distribution \(\mu\) over valuations, let \(q(\mu)\) determine provision, and replace the count of net beneficiaries with their population mass \(s_1(\mu)\). A large municipality deciding on a public facility is a plausible setting. Identity is no obstacle; the paper’s anonymity assumption and focus on population shares make this a particularly natural population continuization. The cited large-economy work strengthens the case that this is a sensible regime. It is supporting analytic work, not a collision with a computational result.

But that stronger mirror still asks for an analytic characterization of incentive and coalition-proof mechanisms. Turning it into a computational question requires adding something the paper does not supply. “Evaluate \(q(\mu)\)” depends on how \(q\) is represented and is just a wrapper around a specified rule. “Find the welfare-best rule” needs an objective and beliefs; the paper explicitly leaves its second-best problem uncharacterized. Those could become worthwhile new research questions, but they are not computational mirrors of Theorem 1.

So the negative case is narrow: the continuous economic mirror is credible, but this paper supplies no computational anchor for ChoCo. The case against a continuous mirror in every broader sense would be weak; the case against treating this theorem as a ChoCo computational mirror is decisive.
