The continuous mirror question

Given rational candidate positions \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a uniform RIV with rational segment probabilities, let \(\mu_B\) be the induced mass of each approval type \(B\subseteq C\). Find a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\), such that for every \(\ell\in[k]\) and submasses \(0\le g_B\le\mu_B\) with \(\sum_B g_B\ge\ell/k\), if all types with \(g_B>0\) share a candidate outside \(W\), then \(\left|W\cap\bigcup_{B:g_B>0}B\right|\ge\ell\).

The model it lives in

Treat the RIV distribution as the population, with each approval set \(B\) a type of mass \(\mu_B\); choose \(k\) candidates satisfying PJR+ for every qualifying submass of those types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers computing a PJR+ committee for the exact RIV-induced type masses. It does not cover Theorem 2’s per-voter query bound, the full-preference elicitation and core result, or the paper’s open EJR+ direction.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is the paper’s PJR+ committee problem, with the RIV distribution treated as the population’s exact mass distribution. The paper itself motivates RIV as aggregated preference information based on historical data. The continuization step is to treat that aggregate as the society, rather than as a generator from which \(n\) individually queryable voters are sampled.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for a uniform RIV, Algorithm 3 always finds a PJR+ committee; when \(n=\Omega(k^2\log m)\), it uses \(O(\log(\sigma k))\) queries per voter in expectation and with high probability. The query bound does not depend on \(m\). This is an algorithmic, query-complexity result, not a hardness result, and the paper offers no named hardness anchor. So the case here is for mirroring its proportional committee objective, not for claiming that the paper establishes a complexity-class boundary.

Call the mirror Exact-RIV PJR+ Mass Committee. An instance consists of rational candidate positions \(C\subset\mathbb R\), a committee size \(k\), and a uniform RIV with \(\sigma\) segments and rational segment probabilities \(p_t\). For each contiguous approval set \(B\subseteq C\) that a voter can have, let \(\mu_B\) be the probability that an RIV voter approves exactly \(B\). This is the mass of that ballot type; with rational candidate positions and rational \(p_t\), these masses are rational. The voter type is the complete approval set \(B\), since PJR+ uses only approvals. There are at most \(1+\sum_t {m_t+1\choose 2}\) such types, where \(m_t=|C\cap I_t|\).

The task is to output a committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\) that satisfies continuous PJR+. Precisely, consider any group described by masses \(g_B\in[0,\mu_B]\), with total mass at least \(\ell/k\), for some \(\ell\in[k]\). Let \(S_g=\{B:g_B>0\}\). If the group’s types share an unselected candidate, \((\bigcap_{B\in S_g}B)\setminus W\ne\varnothing\), then its types must collectively approve at least \(\ell\) members of the committee: \(|W\cap\bigcup_{B\in S_g}B|\ge\ell\). The solution is the committee itself; there is no extra objective in the paper beyond meeting the representation guarantee.

A plausible regime is a large participatory-budgeting electorate or civic deliberation with relatively few projects or comments. Residents’ ballots can be grouped by their approval intervals along the paper’s one-dimensional spectrum; many residents then share each ballot type, while the number of types is bounded by the candidate set rather than by the number of residents. The paper’s RIV segments can represent broad blocs, and their endpoint distributions can describe variation within each bloc. That makes mass the natural quantity in the quota \(\ell/k\), just as the paper’s finite version uses the fraction \(n\ell/k\).

This is a credible analogue of the authors’ PJR+ question: the same candidates, interval ballots, and proportionality requirement remain, while the finite group-size test becomes a mass threshold. The paper’s finite electorate is the empirical distribution with masses \(n_B/n\); the mirror asks for a committee against the exact RIV-induced distribution. The RIV is already a probability model, but that does not make the mirror a restatement: in the paper, the realised finite electorate is the object queried and guaranteed PJR+, whereas here the distribution itself is the society.

I expect the mass problem to be tractable (Class A). The paper states that the Method of Equal Shares outputs PJR+ committees in polynomial time for explicit elections. A weighted version that aggregates voters with identical ballots looks like a natural route to a running time polynomial in \(m\), the number \(\tau\) of positive-mass ballot types, and the bit length \(L\). That compressed-input bound is not proved by Theorem 2, however; it is the main computational question to hand a prover. Further questions include whether an exact weighted MES implementation achieves that bound, and whether the same mass formulation can handle the stronger EJR+ guarantee that the paper leaves open.

The weakness is that the mirror preserves the representation goal but drops the paper’s central operational contribution: asking individual voters a small number of queries. Theorem 2’s query bound and its fallback procedure concern a finite sample of named voters; they do not establish an algorithm for the exact mass problem above. So this is a good positive case for the paper’s PJR+ committee-selection problem in a continuous population, but a substantially weaker case for mirroring its incomplete-information and elicitation contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 2’s contribution is tied to a finite electorate whose members can be queried. Its \(O(\log(\sigma k))\) bound is per voter, and its guarantee is about the committee for the realized sample. In an exact continuum with a known RIV distribution, there are no individual preferences to elicit: the distribution already specifies the mass of every approval type. If the distribution is unknown, sampling voters brings back the finite-sample problem the paper already studies. If cost is measured per capita, the same per-voter query bound survives without any benefit from making the population continuous.

The best replacement mirror is the one the other analyst proposed: exact-mass PJR+ committee selection over the RIV-induced approval types. That is a sensible high-multiplicity object, especially for participatory budgeting or civic deliberation. The usual objections about identity, type aggregation, or mass being meaningless do not fit this paper.

But this particular mirror appears to be settled by the paper’s own proof. The distribution-only committee construction in Algorithm 3 is designed from the segment probabilities. Lemma 4 bounds each event \(Y_{\ell,c,j}\)—a voter approves an unselected candidate \(c\) but approves only a limited block of committee members—by \(\ell/k-1/(4k)\). A violating continuous PJR+ group would have to lie within one such event, with \(j\) determined by the winners it supports to the left of \(c\). Its mass therefore falls short of the required \(\ell/k\). The concentration and query steps are needed to check the guessed committee against a finite realization; they are unnecessary for the exact distribution. So the strongest mass version is not an open computational question awaiting a prover. It is, in substance, a consequence of the paper’s geometric argument.

That leaves no convincing continuous mirror of the paper’s distinctive result: the query-efficient guarantee for a sampled electorate. A continuum version either removes the queries because the mass distribution is known, or retains individual sampling and the finite population. The exact-mass committee variant is the better case for a mirror, but its immediate derivation from the paper makes it a weak candidate for new complexity work.

This is not an airtight case that no worthwhile scenario exists. The paper plainly admits large electorates with repeated approval types, and exact-mass PJR+ is a coherent question. Under the programme’s stated standard, the fact that its answer follows from the paper does not by itself make the mirror unworthy; it may count as a valuable discrete-to-continuous result. My negative case is therefore narrow: the paper’s central elicitation contribution does not gain a population-continuum counterpart, while its strongest alternative seems already answered by the paper’s own analysis.
