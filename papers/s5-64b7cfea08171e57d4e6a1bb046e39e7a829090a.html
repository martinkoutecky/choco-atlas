The continuous mirror question

Given two sides with type masses \(\mu_t\), capacities at most \(3\), acceptable counterpart types, and a strict ranking of counterpart types for each type, is a proposed feasible population matching \(p\) popular? Each individual receives an integral multiset \(B\) of counterpart types with \(|B|\le b_t\); compare bundles by the highest-ranked type whose multiplicity differs. A rival \(q\) is feasible when the aggregate mass of each type-pair contract agrees on both sides. Allow any coupling \(z_{t,B,B'}\) of the candidate and rival bundles within each type, and ask whether the maximum mass-weighted lexicographic vote for a rival is at most zero.

The model it lives in

Represent each type’s population as mass \(\mu_t\) over integral multisets of counterpart types, with capacity at most \(3\). Fix the candidate bundle masses \(p\); optimize the rival bundle masses \(q\) and within-type coupling variables \(z\) to maximize the rival’s mass-weighted vote, subject to bundle-mass, coupling-marginal, and type-pair matching constraints. The candidate is popular exactly when this maximum is at most zero.

The paper uses strict rankings of named partners, while the type model treats members of a counterpart type as interchangeable; that changes the preference representation and the reduction’s identity-dependent structure. This limits direct hardness transfer, but does not make the type-mass verification question unrecognizable.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the verification question in Theorem 16; it leaves the theorem’s existence hardness, the paper’s polynomial-time result for its other popularity notion, and its matroid exchange results aside.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 16, proved in Section 6 of this paper via a reduction from Exact 3-Cover. It says that deciding whether a lexicographically popular \(b\)-matching exists is coNP-hard, and verifying whether a given \(b\)-matching is lexicographically popular is coNP-complete. Both statements hold even when every agent has capacity at most \(3\). I would mirror both questions, with verification as the lead: it gives the cleanest continuous problem and the clearest expected algorithmic result.

Consider a large two-sided market: for example, a regional hiring platform with many applicants and employers, where agents fall into a relatively small number of role and preference classes. A type \(t\) specifies an agent’s side, capacity \(b_t\le 3\), and strict ranking of acceptable counterpart types. Each type has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is \(N\gg\tau\): many agents, but comparatively few distinct types. Members of a type are identical in every way the matching problem uses. The categories they rank are meaningful contract or partner roles; an agent’s bundle contains at most one partner from each category.

Here is the concrete mirror, Continuous Lexicographic Popularity (CLP). An instance consists of the finite type sets on the two sides, their binary-rational masses, the acceptable pairs of types, and each type’s ranking of its acceptable opposite-side types. A population matching assigns every unit of mass an integral bundle \(B\) of acceptable counterpart types, with \(|B|\le b_t\). Its aggregate table \(p_{t,B}\) records how much mass of type \(t\) receives bundle \(B\). It must satisfy \(\sum_B p_{t,B}=\mu_t\); for every acceptable pair \((t,u)\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents matched to type \(u\) must equal the mass of type-\(u\) agents matched to type \(t\). Because cohorts are atomless, those equal masses can be paired into contracts. The population average may be fractional, but each individual still receives an integral bundle.

To compare a candidate matching \(p\) with a rival \(q\), let \(z_{t,B,B'}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents who receive \(B\) under \(p\) and \(B'\) under \(q\). Its row sums are \(p_{t,B}\), its column sums are \(q_{t,B'}\), and \(q\) must itself satisfy the matching constraints. Let \(\sigma_t(B,B')\) be \(+1\), \(-1\), or \(0\) according to whether \(t\) lexicographically prefers \(B\), prefers \(B'\), or is indifferent; the preference is decided by the highest-ranked category in \(B\triangle B'\). The rival’s net vote is
\[ \Delta(q,z)=\sum_{t,B,B'} z_{t,B,B'}\,\sigma_t(B',B). \]
Each unit of mass casts one vote, just as each agent does in the paper. CLP verification asks whether a given feasible \(p\) is popular: does every feasible rival and every feasible assignment of its bundles to the same type cohorts have \(\Delta(q,z)\le 0\)? CLP existence asks whether any such \(p\) is popular.

For verification, the most adversarial rival is found by a finite linear program over \(q\) and \(z\): bundle choices and cohort reassignments are the variables, matching constraints and coupling marginals are linear, and the vote is the objective. With capacity at most \(3\), a type of degree \(d\) has only \(O(d^3)\) possible bundles, so the LP has polynomial size in the type graph and the input bit length. My expectation for CLP verification is Class A: the discrete coNP-complete verification problem has a natural continuous counterpart that appears to reduce directly to LP optimization.

CLP existence is the next question the mirror generates: can one find a \(p\) for which the challenger LP never finds a positive vote? I would weakly bet on Class C for this existence version. Choosing \(p\) against all challengers is an outer problem around the LP, and the type-compatibility and ranking structure that drove the paper’s Exact 3-Cover reduction could remain relevant. But the paper’s reduction does not establish hardness here, and I would not predict hardness transfer without an embedding argument.

The mirror keeps the paper’s underlying question: two-sided matching, bounded capacities, one vote per agent, and lexicographic comparison of integral bundles. It changes the population representation and makes the type-level allocation fractional, which is exactly the high-multiplicity move. Its scope is the lexicographic popularity results of Theorem 16; it does not try to continuize the paper’s polynomial-time result for the different, non-lexicographic popularity notion.

The weak point is that Theorem 16’s reduction uses a detailed graph of agents with individual preference lists. In a market with \(N\gg\tau\), grouping agents by complete preference type may erase distinctions needed by that reduction, while allowing a cohort to split across bundles may remove the obstruction altogether. Thus the theorem is a strong anchor for asking the continuous question, but it is not evidence that its hardness survives. The natural follow-ups are whether CLP existence is tractable, whether popular population matchings always exist, and how either answer changes as the support size \(\tau\) grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is a modeling one. In the paper, a lexicographic vote compares bundles of particular edges under strict rankings of particular partners. The reduction’s Exact 3-Cover structure is encoded in those identities and adjacency lists. If a type instead ranks counterpart *categories*, members of a category become interchangeable, and the model needs to say how that equivalence interacts with strict rankings and bundles containing multiple partners from one category. Keeping every identity and its rank position as part of the type preserves the paper’s formalism, but can make the number of types grow with the population. So the reduction itself does not exhibit the high-multiplicity regime the mirror needs.

That objection weakens against the better mirror, though. A role-based market can treat members of a counterpart category as interchangeable, permit ties within a category, and represent each agent’s integral bundle as a multiset of categories. With capacities at most \(3\), each type then has only polynomially many possible bundles. The proposed coupling variables \(z\) can represent how the same cohort’s members fare under a candidate and a rival, while aggregate edge-mass constraints enforce matching feasibility. Subject to specifying the type-level preference and matching semantics carefully, verification appears to be a finite linear program. That is a coherent continuous computational question, and a tractable answer would itself be an informative contrast with Theorem 16.

Existence is less settled: asking whether any population allocation has no majority-winning rival under those comparisons remains a natural problem. The paper’s reduction does not establish hardness for it, but that lack of transfer is not a reason to dismiss the question.

So I cannot honestly defeat Theorem 16 as an anchor. The strongest objection is that the proposed role types change the paper’s identity-specific strict preferences, and the reduction depends on precisely those details. But a plausible type-level model repairs that issue, and the resulting verification question survives. The case against “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” is weak here.
