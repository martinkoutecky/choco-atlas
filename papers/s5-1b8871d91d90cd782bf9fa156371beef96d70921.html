The case FOR (proponent)

I do not find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I cannot make a positive case that meets the requested standard.

The closest named results are Theorems 1–6, proved in the paper. They characterize the \(k\)-SED value by different axiom sets; for example, Theorem 1 says that efficiency, symmetry, linearity, and the \(k\)-nullifying null player property characterize the same value as the paper’s explicit \(k\)-SED formula. These are characterization results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. Proposition 7 says that, for a zero-monotonic TU game and a distribution \(\alpha\), Mechanism (M) implements the \(\alpha\)-SED value in subgame perfect Nash equilibrium. That is a strategic implementation result, not a complexity result.

There is a plausible *research direction*: model a large population with finitely many player types, let coalition worth depend on the mass of each type, and ask for a continuum analogue of the \(k\)-SED allocation. But the paper does not define that problem or analyze its computation. In particular, its \(k\) is a coalition size among finitely many players, and the worth function may specify arbitrary values for all coalitions. Choosing a mass threshold and a representation for continuum coalition worths would require new modeling decisions.

So there is no named computational result here to anchor a precise continuous problem to. The paper offers a candidate allocation rule and a finite strategic mechanism; turning either into a continuous-population computational question would be a new extension, not an honest mirror of a result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–6 characterize the \(k\)-SED value by different axiom sets. Proposition 7 establishes a subgame-perfect implementation. Neither asks whether a computational problem is tractable, hard, or approximable. The formula in Section 3 gives a way to calculate the value, but with an explicit table of all coalition worths that calculation is polynomial in the table’s size. For succinct game descriptions, the paper specifies no input model or complexity question. A continuous version would have to supply those first.

The best population-based extension is quite natural: take finitely many player types, let coalition worth depend on the mass of each type, scale worth extensively, and let \(k/n\) approach a threshold \(\kappa\). One could then study a limit of the \(k\)-SED allocation along a random coalition-formation process. That is a credible continuous-population model, and the paper’s tribal-community motivation gives it a plausible setting. But it requires choosing a restricted class of anonymous games and a representation for its worth function. The paper instead permits arbitrary \(v:2^N\to\mathbb R\). Keeping arbitrary coalition-specific interactions would require types rich enough to encode them, which may defeat finite-support aggregation; restricting to type-count games creates a new problem. Either way, the paper states no algorithmic question for the proposed limit.

The implementation result offers a similar path: one could define a continuum bargaining mechanism and ask about its equilibria. But the result in the paper is an existence and implementation claim, not a computational analysis of finding equilibria or constructing the mechanism. Asking for an efficient equilibrium algorithm would add a new question; studying continuum equilibrium behavior would be analytic work outside ChoCo’s computational remit.

So the proponent’s central observation survives every stronger version of the proposed mirror: a worthwhile continuous-population extension may exist, but the paper does not anchor a continuous *computational* question. I cannot honestly rule out such an extension being worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is strongest—and decisive for this paper as a ChoCo mirror—when kept to that narrower claim.
