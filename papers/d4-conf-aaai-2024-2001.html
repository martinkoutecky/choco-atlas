The continuous mirror question

Given finite rational mass distributions \(\mu^B\) over buyer values and \(\mu^S\) over seller costs in \([0,1]\), does the rule that trades when \(\bar v>\bar c\) and sets the shared per-member payment and receipt to \((\bar v+\bar c)/2\) achieve group IR and first-best gains from trade in the exact-mass population, while remaining IC and SBB?

The model it lives in

A society consists of buyer and seller types with rational masses \(\mu^B_v\) and \(\mu^S_c\), each summing to one. The mechanism outputs a shared trade decision \(x\in\{0,1\}\) and a common per-member payment and receipt; its rule computes \(\bar v=\sum_v\mu^B_v v\) and \(\bar c=\sum_c\mu^S_c c\), trades iff \(\bar v>\bar c\), and sets both transfers to \((\bar v+\bar c)/2\).

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the setting: the paper’s clubs and countries are natural high-multiplicity societies. In a fixed finite-type regime, a buyer type could be a valuation \(v\), a seller type a cost \(c\), and \(\mu^B_v,\mu^S_c\) their population fractions, with \(n\gg\tau\). Their shared trade decision and per-member transfers also make a reasonable co-modification of the model. That is a plausible population-continuous setting.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the required kind: none classifies an underlying problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest named results, all proved here, concern mechanism existence and welfare:

  • Theorem 4.3 proves that Algorithm 1—trade iff \(\mathbb E_F[v]>\mathbb E_G[c]\), charging and paying each side half the sum of those expectations—is IC and SBB; when that inequality holds, its group IR probability and GFT-to-first-best ratio are each \(1-e^{-\Omega(n)}\).
  • Theorem 4.4 says that when \(\mathbb E_F[v]\le\mathbb E_G[c]\), no IC mechanism in the deterministic-mechanism section is efficient in the limit.
  • Theorem 5.2 says that under the same mean inequality, no IC twice continuously differentiable randomized mechanism achieves a constant GFT approximation to first best in the limit.

These are substantive mechanism-design results, but their approximation statements concern welfare, not computational approximation, and they give no complexity classification. The characterizations in Theorems 4.1, 4.2, and 5.1 likewise do not analyze computation. So there are zero eligible anchors, and the requested one-problem-per-anchor deliverable is empty; assigning one would invent an anchor the paper does not provide.

The population interpretation also has a real modeling wrinkle: the paper draws individual values and costs from priors and retains individual deviations for its IC constraints. In an exact atomless population, a single agent’s report changes no aggregate mass, so individual IC becomes vacuous unless the mirror adds a finite-cohort or coalition-level incentive model. That may be a worthwhile research direction, but it changes a central part of the paper’s question.

So the positive case is that this is an unusually natural *high-multiplicity regime*. Its weakest point is decisive for the requested task: the paper studies asymptotic mechanism feasibility, not computational complexity, and the atomless mirror does not preserve its individual incentive constraints automatically.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The clubs, countries, and communities are credible high-multiplicity settings. That establishes that a continuous population is sensible for this domain, but it does not supply the kind of computational problem ChoCo is trying to mirror. The paper’s named results concern mechanism feasibility and welfare as \(n\) grows; none classifies the complexity of computing a mechanism, allocation, or optimum.

Theorem 4.3 is the strongest candidate, but its mechanism ignores every report: it trades according to the known priors’ means and sets a fixed payment. In an exact population distribution, those means are already aggregate quantities. The theorem’s incentive claim is therefore not a computational challenge, and its finite-\(n\) probability guarantees concern sample fluctuations. Computing the means from a finite support would be a possible implementation detail, not a complexity question the theorem poses.

Theorems 4.4 and 5.2 are also welfare and feasibility impossibilities, not computational hardness results. Their individual incentive constraints are the deeper obstacle to a faithful mirror: in an atomless population, one member’s report changes no mass, so individual incentive compatibility becomes vacuous for mechanisms based on aggregate reports. If reports by a positive mass of members can change the outcome, the deviation is collective, replacing the paper’s individual IC with a different incentive concept. Theorem 5.2’s smoothness condition concerns the allocation function; it does not make the population continuous or create a computational task.

The characterizations in Theorems 4.1, 4.2, and 5.1 have the same difficulty. They describe how outcomes depend on named agents’ bids and asks. Compressing those coordinates into type masses would change the object being characterized, and the paper gives no algorithmic problem over that new object. One could certainly formulate a follow-up about computing good mechanisms for a distributional or coalition-incentive model, but that would be a new mechanism-design question rather than a continuous mirror of these results.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed anchors: none is a computational result, and the natural population limit removes the individual strategic constraint that makes the paper’s results distinctive. It is not airtight as a universal claim. The setting is genuinely high-multiplicity, and a carefully designed algorithmic mechanism-design problem for it could be worthwhile; the paper simply does not provide that problem to continuize.
