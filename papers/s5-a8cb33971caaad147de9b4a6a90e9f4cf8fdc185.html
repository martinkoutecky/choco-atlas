The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper has a natural population-level extension, but it has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your rules. Theorem 1 is attributed to Rochet; Theorems 2 and 3 give characterizations of dominant-strategy implementability; and Theorem 4, proved here, characterizes incentive compatibility for multi-unit auctions with diminishing marginal utilities. These are mathematical characterizations, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The paper gives no computational algorithm or complexity analysis to anchor a ChoCo mirror.

The closest candidate is Theorem 4. It says that, in the paper’s homogeneous multi-unit auction with diminishing marginal values, an allocation rule is dominant-strategy implementable exactly when its allocation changes satisfy the stated nondecreasing-marginal-utility condition. A plausible population version would have unit mass of buyers, with finite support \(\mu\) over types \(t=(t_1,\ldots,t_K)\), where \(1\ge t_1\ge\cdots\ge t_K\ge0\). Each buyer receives an integer quantity \(q\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\) and pays a transfer; utility is \(\sum_{j=1}^{q}t_j-p\). Scale the supply with population mass, so feasibility is \(\int q\,d\lambda\le\kappa\). The question would be whether a feasible allocation rule admits transfers making each buyer truthful against any unilateral misreport.

That scenario is recognizable as a large-market version of the paper’s multi-unit auction: many buyers, relatively few distinct valuation types, and per-capita supply. But it is only a candidate analytic extension, not a computational mirror supported by an eligible anchor. The paper does not establish how to represent or efficiently check such a continuum mechanism, so I cannot honestly assign it a tractability or hardness class.

The weakest point is therefore fundamental to this task: the paper studies implementability, not the computational complexity landscape the programme asks analysts to chart. Calling Theorem 4 an “in P” result, or presenting the proposed continuum question as a computational consequence of it, would overstate the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s first point is the strongest one: this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 2–4 characterize which allocation rules are dominant-strategy implementable; Theorem 1 is attributed to Rochet. None classifies an algorithmic problem by runtime, hardness, approximation, or parameterized complexity. Theorems 2 and 3 therefore do not provide separate computational anchors, and Theorem 4 is the closest candidate only because it is the paper’s new characterization—not because it asks a computational question.

The proposed large-market auction is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many buyers can share marginal-value types, and supply can scale per capita. But that does not make “does a feasible allocation rule admit truthful transfers?” a computational mirror as stated. If the rule is the input, its representation and the allowed reports must be specified; a finite table over the supported types tests a different domain from the paper’s full diminishing-marginal-value domain. If the question is instead whether any feasible truthful rule exists, the proposal supplies no design objective or target allocation to compute. Adding one—say, revenue maximization—could define a worthwhile new problem, but it would not be a computational result of this paper.

There is also a real modeling seam. In an anonymous mechanism that responds only to the aggregate type distribution, one buyer’s report has zero mass and cannot change that input. One can preserve meaningful individual incentives by giving each buyer a report-dependent menu while imposing aggregate supply feasibility, but that is a new mechanism model. This is not a knock against the large-market scenario; it shows why the proposed mirror needs substantive modeling choices before it yields a computational task.

So the negative case is strong against a ChoCo mirror anchored in this paper: no eligible computational anchor survives, and the best candidate requires a new input model or objective. It is weaker as the universal claim that no scenario could produce worthwhile research. A distributional auction-design problem may be worthwhile on its own; the material here does not establish that every such problem fails.
