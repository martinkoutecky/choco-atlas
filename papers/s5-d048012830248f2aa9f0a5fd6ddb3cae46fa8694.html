The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set with candidate attributes, group quotas, committee size \(k\), voter populations with designated committees \(W_P\) and representation quotas, and a finite support \(T\) of complete ranking-and-population-membership types with positive rational masses \(\lambda_t\) summing to one, find a size-\(k\) committee maximizing \(\sum_{c\in W}\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_t\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_t(c)}\), subject to the paper's diversity and representation constraints and fixed tie-breaking.

The model it lives in

The electorate is a distribution \(\lambda\) over complete voter types; candidate groups, quotas, designated population committees, and the integral size-\(k\) committee remain as in the paper. Candidate scores are mass-weighted positional scores, and the task is to maximize their sum over feasible committees.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the DRCWD hardness in Theorems 1 and 3, including the stated odd-\(\mu\) cases; it does not address Theorems 2 and 4 or other multiwinner fairness problems.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Mass-DRCWD remain NP-hard when the type support size \(\tau\) is fixed or small while the candidate set scales?
  • Which restrictions on candidate groups or designated population committees yield efficient algorithms parameterized by \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is a high-multiplicity version of Diverse and Representative Committee Winner Determination. Its population is continuous; its committee remains an integral set of candidates. That is a faithful translation of the paper’s problem, not a relaxation of the committee decision.

For the lead anchor, Theorem 1 states that when the paper’s number of candidate attributes is \(\mu=1\), for every number \(\pi\ge1\) of voter attributes, DRCWD is NP-hard for the monotone separable committee rule arising from an arbitrary single-winner positional scoring rule. The theorem is proved here: its proof extends Theorem 5 of Relia (2022, full version), adding a candidate ranked first by every voter. A second useful anchor is Theorem 3, proved here by reduction from vertex cover on 3-regular graphs: for every odd \(\mu\ge3\), every \(\pi\ge1\), and every such positional rule, DRCWD is NP-hard. The paper also states that this latter hardness holds when either all diversity lower bounds are zero or all representation lower bounds are zero. Theorem 1 is my lead because it gives the cleanest mirror with just one candidate attribute; Theorem 3 shows the same population model applies to the paper’s broader attribute settings.

Here is the concrete continuous problem for both anchors. Call it Mass-DRCWD. An instance gives a finite candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), candidate attributes whose groups partition \(C\), and a diversity lower bound \(l^D_G\) for each group \(G\). It also gives voter attributes and their populations, plus a finite support \(T\) of voter types. A type \(t\) is a complete ranking of \(C\) together with its membership in each voter attribute’s populations. Each type has a positive binary-rational mass \(\lambda_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_t=1\); let \(\tau=|T|\). As in the paper, the instance supplies each population \(P\)’s designated winning committee \(W_P\), and a representation lower bound \(l^R_P\).

Fix a positional scoring vector \(\alpha\). The mass-weighted score of a candidate is
\[ q_\lambda(c)=\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_t\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_t(c)}, \]
and a committee’s score is \(F_\lambda(W)=\sum_{c\in W}q_\lambda(c)\). The task is to output a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) maximizing \(F_\lambda(W)\), subject to \(|W\cap G|\ge l^D_G\) for each candidate group and \(|W\cap W_P|\ge l^R_P\) for each voter population, with the paper’s fixed tie-breaking order. Mass-DRCWD\(_1\) is this problem with exactly one candidate attribute and \(\pi\ge1\) voter attributes; Mass-DRCWD\(_{\mathrm{odd}}\) is the same problem for each odd number \(\mu\ge3\) of candidate attributes and \(\pi\ge1\). These are the continuous problems posed by Theorems 1 and 3, respectively.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large membership organization electing a board from a fixed-size slate. Members submit rankings and belong to a small, fixed set of constituencies; candidates have attributes such as gender or professional area; each constituency has its designated local committee \(W_P\). If \(N\) members grow while the slate and constituency system stay fixed, then many members share exactly the same ranking and membership signature. Thus \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). Mass means a fraction of the electorate, and the decision remains which actual candidates to put on the board. The paper’s quotas count seats intersecting candidate groups and local committees, so they stay as integer constraints; there is no reason to turn them into fractional seats.

The hardness should be expected to transfer (Class B). Given any finite election with \(N\) voters, group voters by their complete type and set \(\lambda_t=n_t/N\). Then \(q_\lambda(c)\) is exactly the discrete score divided by \(N\), so every committee has the same relative score and the same tie-break outcome. The population labels preserve the voter populations, while the candidate-side feasibility constraints and supplied \(W_P\) remain unchanged. This embeds the discrete instances directly into Mass-DRCWD. In Theorem 3, the reduction’s graph structure is encoded through candidates and their attribute groups, which is further evidence that the hardness is not simply an artifact of counting many voters.

The mirror covers the DRCWD hardness in Theorems 1 and 3, not every possible fairness or multiwinner problem. Its weakest point is that the translation may preserve hardness without yielding much algorithmic relief: the theorems do not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is bounded or small. That makes the next questions concrete: does Mass-DRCWD remain hard at fixed support size, and which restrictions on candidate groups or population committees give efficient algorithms when \(\tau\) is modest? Finding that boundary would be useful even if the unrestricted mirror remains hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the likely payoff, not whether these mirrors are well-posed. In Mass-DRCWD, once the type masses have been used to compute each candidate’s average score \(q_\lambda(c)\), the optimization is over the same integral committees and quota constraints as in the paper. The voter distribution contributes coefficients; it does not create a mass-transfer problem, an exponentially large LP, or an evident pricing problem. The reductions put their combinatorics in candidate groups. That makes this a less promising place to expect ChoCo’s continuous-optimization tools to unlock an algorithm.

But that does not defeat either anchor. For Theorem 1, group voters by their complete rankings and population memberships and set \(\lambda_t=n_t/N\). Every committee’s score is then its discrete score divided by \(N\); the representation committees, quotas, and tie-breaking can stay as given. For Theorem 3, the same construction applies, including when the candidate-attribute structure varies across instances. Conversely, every rational mass vector has a common denominator and corresponds to a finite voter profile. So both proposed questions are coherent high-multiplicity formulations, not artifacts of a broken limit.

A stronger version would hold the number of voter types \(\tau\) small while the electorate grows, and study complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. The paper does not settle that regime. A fixed candidate slate would make the problem trivial as a complexity family, but scalable slates and structured, repeated rankings are plausible; that observation cannot rule out every useful scenario. Nor does existing high-multiplicity work defeat the mirror: under the programme’s stated standard, it supports the regime.

So the negative case is weak where it must be strongest. The unchanged candidate-side hardness and the absence of an obvious continuous-optimization advantage may justify lower priority, but they do not show there is no worthwhile mirror. I cannot honestly defeat either Theorem 1 or Theorem 3 under the burden set here.
