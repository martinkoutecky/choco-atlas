The continuous mirror question

Given a finite propositional language, a satisfiable integrity constraint \(\kappa\), a query \(\psi\), and a finite support of types \(t=(K_t,X_t)\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\lambda_t\) summing to \(1\), take each type's priority-level function \(\ell_t(w)\) as available after the paper's assumed offline stratification. Let \(R\) be the largest priority level and, for each world \(w\models\kappa\), set \(F_w(j)=\sum_{t:\ell_t(w)\le j}\lambda_t\) for \(j=1,\ldots,R-1\). Select the worlds lexicographically maximizing \((F_w(1),\ldots,F_w(R-1))\); decide whether every selected world satisfies \(\psi\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\lambda\) over complete source types \((K_t,X_t)\). Each admissible world is evaluated by the mass of sources ranking it at each priority level; the decision asks whether all worlds maximizing that weighted rank histogram under \(\kappa\) entail \(\psi\).

The objection that survived

The mirror treats repeated units as independent evidential contributors, while the paper has no provenance model to distinguish genuine cohort frequency from duplicated replicas; restricting the application to independent units bounds but does not kill it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 8's query-entailment complexity result for best-out, maxsat, and leximin strategies. It leaves the logical postulates and other complexity results, including preprocessing to construct the world stratifications, outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the binary-rational mass version remain \(\Theta_2^p\)-complete when each supported type is stratified once?
  • What is the parameterized complexity in support size \(\tau\) and number of atoms \(m\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is for the paper’s inference problem for merging: treat knowledge sources as the population. A source’s type is its complete stratified knowledge base together with its ordering strategy. Two sources share a type only when the problem treats them identically. The mass \(\lambda_t\) of type \(t\) is the fraction of sources using it. This fits, for example, a large federation of policy or diagnostic units using a small library of ranked knowledge templates: millions of units, perhaps dozens of distinct types.

The anchor is Proposition 8, proved by the authors, with its proof deferred to their accompanying technical report. It states that deciding whether \(\Delta_{\mathrm{PLMIN},\mu}^{X}(E)\models\psi\) is in \(\Theta_2^p\); when the ordering strategies are best-out, maxsat, or leximin, the decision problem is \(\Theta_2^p\)-complete. The paper assumes the world rankings induced by each knowledge base have been computed in an offline preprocessing stage. The decision is whether every model selected by merging the profile \(E\) subject to integrity constraint \(\mu\) satisfies query \(\psi\).

Here is a continuous version that keeps that decision intact. Call it Continuous PLMIN Entailment. An instance consists of a finite propositional language, a finite support \(T\) of types \(t=(K_t,X_t)\), positive rational masses \(\lambda_t\) summing to \(1\), a satisfiable integrity constraint \(\kappa\), and a query \(\psi\). For each world \(w\), let \(\ell_t(w)\) be its priority level under type \(t\)’s ordering strategy. Let \(R\) be the largest level, and define
\[ F_w(j)=\sum_{t\in T:\,\ell_t(w)\le j}\lambda_t,\qquad j=1,\ldots,R-1. \]
Among worlds satisfying \(\kappa\), select those that lexicographically maximize \((F_w(1),\ldots,F_w(R-1))\): at the first priority level where two worlds differ, prefer the one with more mass at that level or better. The question is whether every selected world satisfies \(\psi\); a solution is yes or no.

This is the population form of PLMIN’s lexicographic comparison of sources’ priority levels. It preserves the paper’s substantive question: which admissible interpretations best reconcile the ranked knowledge of a group of sources? It is not a continuous outcome space or a probability model over discrete profiles. When \(\lambda_t\) is the fraction of sources of each type in a finite profile, the rank histogram gives the same comparison as the profile’s multiset of priority levels. Thus the continuous question has a direct bridge back to the paper’s problem.

I expect hardness to transfer. Given a finite profile of \(n\) sources, assign each distinct type mass equal to its count divided by \(n\). The resulting continuous instance reproduces the original merging decision, so Proposition 8’s \(\Theta_2^p\)-hardness carries over. Under the same offline-stratification assumption, I would expect the continuous problem to remain \(\Theta_2^p\)-complete: it compares polynomial-length rational rank histograms while optimizing over worlds. This is a Class B mirror, not a claim that continuization makes this result tractable. It also raises follow-up questions about parameterized complexity in support size \(\tau\) and number of atoms \(m\), and about algorithms that exploit a small number of source types.

The mirror covers Proposition 8’s inference result, not the paper’s logical postulates or every computational question about merging. Its weakest point is the regime: many knowledge sources may be less naturally understood as a population than voters are, and the paper itself studies profiles of sources without assigning them population masses. But the masses here encode cohort proportions of otherwise identical sources, and the discrete problem already counts repeated bases in a multiset. That makes the continuous profile a plausible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ own model, rather than a simplified substitute for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 8 is the one anchor, and it survives the strongest objection I can make. The paper does have a named computational result: query entailment for \(\Delta_{\mathrm{PLMIN}}\), with a \(\Theta_2^p\)-completeness claim for several ordering strategies. Treating sources as the population also gives a coherent mirror. A better version than the proponent’s example would use a large federation of independent diagnostic or policy units drawn from a small library of stratified knowledge bases and ordering strategies. For type \(t\), mass \(\lambda_t\) is the share of units using it; worlds are compared by their rank histograms. When \(\lambda_t=n_t/n\), this reproduces the finite multiset comparison. Since the paper already treats profiles as multisets, the authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem.

The best negative argument is about the regime’s semantics. Repeated identical knowledge bases count repeatedly in the merge, so interpreting their frequency as evidential weight assumes that they are independent source instances. In a real information system, many copies may instead be replicas of one upstream source; counting them separately would amplify duplication rather than independent evidence. The paper’s model does not represent provenance or dependence. This makes the proposed application conditional on a substantive assumption, and it may limit how often the small-support regime is appropriate. It does not defeat the mirror: independent units generated from shared templates are plausible, and the paper’s own multiset formalism already gives multiplicity computational significance.

There is also a real qualification to the anchor. Proposition 8 assumes the world stratifications have been computed in an offline preprocessing stage. Its complexity claim therefore does not cover the full cost of starting from raw knowledge bases and constructing those strata. A continuous version inherits that qualification; it would need to state whether preprocessing is part of the input or part of the measured computation. That narrows the result’s reach, but the same question remains meaningful for preprocessed instances or when each supported type is preprocessed once.

Nearby work makes the novelty claim more demanding: belief merging has computational analyses for proportional operators, and weighted merging studies source weights and aggregation of source-relative distances ([proportional belief merging](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5671), [weighted merging of propositional belief bases](https://proceedings.kr.org/2023/22/kr2023-0022-everaere-et-al.pdf)). Those results do not, on the evidence available here, establish this paper’s continuous-population rank-histogram problem, so they are not a collision under the programme’s standard. They do make the precise distinction worth spelling out.

I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case is weak at its central point: it questions whether source frequency is a sensible population measure in a given application, while the mirror can specify independent, exchangeable source units and preserve the paper’s merge semantics.The strongest defensible verdict against this anchor is “plausible but application-dependent, and qualified by preprocessing,” not “no continuous mirror.”
