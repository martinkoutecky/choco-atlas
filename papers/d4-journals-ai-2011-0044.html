The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a defensible anchor based on this paper. It contains no named computational result of the kind this task requires: no theorem or proposition establishes that a computational problem is in P, NP hard, fixed parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity.

The closest result is Proposition 8, proved in the paper: it counts the simple preference rules on \(n\)-point intervals, giving \(\binom{2n}{n+1}\). The other nearby results characterize properties of the represented preference relations—for example, Proposition 9 gives a condition for transitivity, and Proposition 13 for the Ferrers property. These are representational and structural results, not complexity results about problems the paper computes with.

Nor is there a convincing population mirror hiding in the model. The paper’s finite set \(A\) is a set of alternatives, each represented by points on the real line; it is not a society of agents. A continuum of decision makers with interval-comparison rules as types could be invented, but the paper studies no aggregation or computational question for such a population. That would be a new problem, rather than a continuous analogue of a named result here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive gap is that the paper proves no computational complexity result. Proposition 8 counts simple comparison rules; Propositions 9 and 13 characterize when a represented preference relation is transitive or Ferrers. None classifies an algorithmic problem. Those results could inspire computational questions, but there is no named computational result here for a continuous version to mirror.

The model also has no population axis to continuize. Its set \(A\) contains alternatives, each represented by ordered real values. The paper’s \(n\) counts points describing an alternative, not agents or voter types. Taking \(A\) or its interval values to a continuum would continuize the alternatives or outcome representation, outside ChoCo’s scope.

A stronger population scenario is possible: a large recommender system could contain many users with the same complete preference type. But the paper’s characterizations apply to each user’s relation independently of how many users have it. With a distribution over such types, Proposition 8 still counts possible rules, and Propositions 9 and 13 still classify individual relations; the masses change none of those results. Asking how to aggregate users’ relations, or how to edit mass so an aggregate satisfies a property, would introduce a new social choice problem and a new aggregation rule. It would not be a mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for a *continuous mirror* of this paper, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile population-level research direction inspired by it. The proposed population problem would need its own justification and computational results.
