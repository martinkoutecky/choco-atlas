The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(A\), a finite support \(S\) of complete rankings with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a designated alternative \(d\), define \(x \succ_\mu y\) when more than half the mass ranks \(x\) above \(y\), with ties producing no edge. Use this relation for the paper's upward-covering relation: \(x\) upward covers \(y\) within \(B\) if \(x \succ_\mu y\) and every \(z \in B\) with \(z \succ_\mu x\) also satisfies \(z \succ_\mu y\). A covering set \(B\) has every member in \(\mathrm{UC}_u(B)\), and every \(x \notin B\) is outside \(\mathrm{UC}_u(B \cup \{x\})\); it is minimal if no proper subset is a covering set. Decide whether \(d\) belongs to some minimal upward covering set.

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings of the alternatives, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society with type \(t\). The majority relation derived from these masses determines the dominance graph. The decision certificate is a subset \(B\) that is a minimal upward covering set containing \(d\); there is no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers minimal upward membership, MCu-MEMBER, from Theorem 5.2. It leaves the paper's other upward and downward membership, size, uniqueness, testing, and search results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does complexity vary with the number of alternatives \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\)?
  • Which restrictions on ranking supports or their induced majority graphs yield useful algorithmic boundaries?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Majority Upward Membership. A type is a complete ranking of the \(m\) alternatives. An instance is a finite support \(S\) of such rankings, each with a binary rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\), plus a designated alternative \(d\). The mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. Define \(x\succ_\mu y\) when more than half the mass ranks \(x\) above \(y\); a tie gives no edge.

For \(B\subseteq A\), say \(x\) upward covers \(y\) within \(B\) if \(x\succ_\mu y\) and every \(z\in B\) that dominates \(x\) also dominates \(y\). Let \(\mathrm{UC}_u(B)\) be the alternatives in \(B\) that no alternative in \(B\) upward covers. A covering set \(B\) satisfies \(\mathrm{UC}_u(B)=B\) and, for every \(x\notin B\), \(x\notin\mathrm{UC}_u(B\cup\{x\})\). It is minimal if no proper subset is also a covering set. The question is whether \(d\) belongs to some minimal upward covering set; a yes instance has such a set \(B\) as a witness. The decision variable is the selected subset \(B\); there is no objective to minimize.

The anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper: deciding whether a designated alternative belongs to some minimal upward covering set is \(\Theta_2^p\)-hard and is in \(\Sigma_2^p\). The \(\Theta_2^p\)-hardness strengthens the earlier NP-hardness cited from Brandt and Fischer [BF08]; the paper proves the stronger lower bound and the upper bound here.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a large election with many voters but relatively few distinct ballots: for example, millions of voters sharing a much smaller set of complete rankings. The ranking is a complete type for this problem because the paper uses no other voter-specific attributes. The authors explicitly motivate dominance relations through pairwise majority. More decisively, they cite McGarvey’s result that every asymmetric dominance relation can be realized by a profile of linear rankings. Turning that finite profile into rational proportions gives a continuous society with the same relation. Thus the hardness reduction’s graph instances remain representable in this mirror; pairwise comparisons can also be computed from the supplied masses in polynomial time.

I expect hardness to transfer. The hardness is driven by the alternatives and the structure of their dominance relation, not by the number of voters. This is the sort of case where population continuization supplies a natural high-multiplicity formulation without dissolving the underlying complexity.

The mirror covers the paper’s minimal upward membership problem only. It does not claim to cover the paper’s downward or minimum-size variants. Further questions include whether restrictions on the number of supported rankings \(\tau\) help, how complexity depends on \(m,\tau\), and the bit length \(L\), and whether a useful algorithmic boundary appears for structured preference supports.

The weakest point is that the paper takes the dominance graph as its input rather than a voter distribution. In this mirror, the masses serve to generate that graph and can then be discarded. But the majority interpretation is already part of the paper’s framing, and McGarvey’s result means the distribution representation does not exclude its hard instances. The case is therefore strongest as a faithful high-multiplicity formulation of the paper’s problem, not as a claim that continuity changes its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that it looks like a change of input format, not a new continuum phenomenon. Given a society \(\mu\), compute each pairwise margin
\[ \Delta_\mu(x,y)=\sum_t \mu_t\bigl(\mathbf 1[x\succ_t y]-\mathbf 1[y\succ_t x]\bigr). \]
The induced majority graph determines the entire instance of Theorem 5.2; the exact masses play no further role. Conversely, by the McGarvey result cited in the paper, every asymmetric dominance relation can be realized by a polynomial-size profile of rankings, hence by rational masses with polynomial support. So the continuous and graph-input problems are polynomially intertranslatable. This makes the proposed mirror look like a direct corollary of the paper’s result, with little new algorithmic structure.

That is a real limit on the mirror’s novelty, but under the programme’s criteria it does not defeat it. Theorem 5.2 is a named computational result; pairwise majority is an explicit setting in the paper; and the ranking distribution is a sensible population model. Neither the graph encoding nor the fact that the answer transfers makes the question out of scope. The masses being discarded after majority aggregation is the strongest criticism, but it is not enough to show the authors would reject the mirror or that it is not worth asking.

I therefore cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case. This anchor survives: the case against it is weak, and the proponent’s main limitation is that the mirror may add little beyond an equivalent representation.
