What the mirror covers

The proposed recognition mirror restates the representability question underlying Theorems 3.1 and 3.2; it does not turn either theorem into a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 3.1 proves the existence of voter-deletion-minimal non-Euclidean profiles; Theorem 3.2 proves that one-dimensional Euclidean profiles have no finite forbidden-substructure characterization. Both are structural results, not complexity classifications. The polynomial-time recognition results mentioned in the introduction are credited to earlier work, not proved here. So there are no named results in this paper asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class.

The strongest nearby population formulation is still clear: let a type be a strict ranking, let \(\mu\) assign rational mass to each supported ranking, and ask whether there are positions for the alternatives and one voter position for each supported type that induce all those rankings by distance. A solution is such an embedding. For each \(k\ge2\), Theorem 3.1 gives a concrete instance: put mass \(1/(2k)\) on each of the \(2k\) rankings in \(P_k^*\). The resulting society is not one-dimensional Euclidean, but deleting any one supported type and renormalizing makes it Euclidean. This is plausible as a high-multiplicity regime—many people share each ranking—but the masses do not affect the recognition question. It repackages the paper’s support structure rather than opening a new continuous computational problem. Theorem 3.2 similarly carries over as a structural statement about forbidden support patterns, not as a hardness result.

That is a possible mirror, but it does not meet the requested anchor condition. A qualifying case for or against a computational mirror cannot honestly be built from this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.1 constructs voter deletion minimal profiles; Theorem 3.2 rules out a finite obstruction characterization. Neither classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The recognition algorithms mentioned in the introduction belong to earlier work. So there is no named computational result here for ChoCo to mirror.

The natural candidate is to take a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings and ask whether its supported rankings admit a common one dimensional Euclidean representation. That is coherent, but it does not turn the paper’s results into computational questions. Representability depends only on which rankings have positive mass: changing their rational weights changes neither the embedding constraints nor the answer. The input is still a finite preference profile, compressed to its distinct rankings. Theorem 3.1 then says the same thing it said before: the support of \(P_k^*\) is not representable, but removing any one of its rankings makes it representable. That is a support statement, not a result about population mass.

Theorem 3.2 fares no better as a continuous anchor. Its infinite obstruction family carries over unchanged when each ranking has positive mass. Asking whether a distribution’s support avoids those obstructions restates the structural theorem; weighting the support does not create a new computational problem. The paper’s minimality argument is about deleting a whole voter. In a population with mass assigned to types, removing only part of a type leaves the same support and cannot change representability.

A stronger candidate would ask for the least mass that must be removed to make a society Euclidean. For the paper’s \(P_k^*\) examples, deleting any one type suffices, while partial deletion cannot help, so the answer is exactly the mass of the lightest type. Since there are \(2k\) supported types, that mass is at most \(1/(2k)\); across the obstruction sequence, the repair mass therefore vanishes. For other societies, weighted repair could be a worthwhile high multiplicity optimization problem. But it is a new objective—maximum weight Euclidean subprofile—not a computational result established by this paper. Any complexity landscape for it would need its own models and results.

That is where the negative case is weakest: weighted repair is a plausible research direction, and the paper’s discussion of nearby structured profiles gives it some motivation. But it does not rescue a continuous mirror of the paper’s named results. The exact recognition mirror is support invariant, while the mass sensitive version changes the objective and leaves the paper’s results behind.
