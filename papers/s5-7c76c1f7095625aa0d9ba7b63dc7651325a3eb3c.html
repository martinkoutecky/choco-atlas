What the mirror covers

As a follow-on, this would extend Proposition 2’s equal-quota, uniform-ranking setting to type masses; it would not mirror the paper’s experimental findings or its other mechanism comparisons as computational results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the nonatomic Boston game have a well-defined equilibrium for general rational \(\mu\), and can one be computed efficiently?
  • Under what type distributions and capacities is truthful reporting an equilibrium, and when do Boston assignment-rank distributions first-order stochastically dominate those under Deferred Acceptance?
  • How, if at all, do equilibria and assignment distributions of the finite games converge to this nonatomic model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The most credible continuous setting is a large citywide school assignment, where thousands of applicants share a relatively small set of preference and priority types. Let a type \(t\) specify a student’s ranking of schools, utility over assignments, and priority class; let \(\mu_t\) be its population share. Scale each school’s capacity with the cohort, so \(\kappa_s\) is its seats per capita. Reports determine masses applying in each round of the Boston mechanism, and lottery ties divide the marginal mass fractionally. This is a natural high-multiplicity model of the paper’s ex ante school-choice problem.

A concrete follow-on question would be Mass Boston Equilibrium: given schools \(S\), finite complete types \(T\), rational type shares \(\mu\), per-capita capacities \((\kappa_s)\), utilities, priorities, and tie-breaking rules, compute a symmetric Bayesian Nash equilibrium of the continuum Boston game. A solution would give each type’s reporting strategy and the resulting type-to-school assignment masses, with a certificate that no type benefits from changing its report. One could then compare its assignment-rank distributions with those under truthful Deferred Acceptance. I would expect the general equilibrium-computation question to be continuum-specifically hard, though the paper itself gives no evidence for that prediction; further questions include when truthful reporting is an equilibrium and when Boston’s assignment distribution first-order stochastically dominates DA’s.

The nearest numbered result is Proposition 2, proved in the appendix. It says that when all schools have equal quotas and students’ preferences are drawn independently and uniformly from all rankings of the schools, truth-telling is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium under Boston from the ex ante perspective. It also says truth-telling is the unique equilibrium among strategies that apply the same permutation to every student’s true ranking. That result makes the proposed equilibrium question recognizable to the authors, but it is not a computational result: it establishes an equilibrium property, not a complexity classification or algorithm.

That is the decisive limitation under the requested standard. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. So Mass Boston Equilibrium is a plausible computational continuation, but it cannot serve as a qualifying mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results.

The weakest point is that Proposition 2 relies on a finite random preference profile with uniform preferences and equal capacities. In a continuum, type shares become fixed masses, changing the uncertainty that underlies the result. The proposed model is therefore a sensible large-cohort extension of the paper’s subject, while the positive case for a ChoCo mirror of its *results* remains weak under the anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 2, the proponent’s anchor, establishes a Bayesian equilibrium property for a particular finite environment; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s other formal results are also about mechanism properties and equilibrium outcomes. So “Mass Boston Equilibrium” would introduce a new problem, rather than mirror a computational result in this paper.

The most direct mass version of Proposition 2 would preserve its symmetry assumptions: uniform preference shares and equal per-capita school capacities. There, the truthful-equilibrium claim appears to carry over through the same symmetry and rank-monotonicity reasoning. That is a useful structural observation, but it does not open a complexity landscape. Asking for equilibria under arbitrary type distributions could be a genuine new project, but its computational value would need its own motivation; the paper’s result does not point to a hard search problem.

There is also a mismatch in what drives the paper’s ex ante comparison. With fixed masses, the realized preference composition is known. The variation across finite preference profiles that enables the Boston-versus-DA trade-off disappears in the natural high-multiplicity limit. One could add uncertainty over aggregate population measures, but that is an additional modelling choice, not the mass relaxation itself.

I would not claim that school choice has no plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large district with recurring preference and priority types is credible. The negative case is narrower: this paper is a weak ChoCo target because it supplies no computational result to mirror, and its most natural continuous version of Proposition 2 seems to transfer a symmetry argument rather than pose the kind of complexity question the programme is meant to chart.
