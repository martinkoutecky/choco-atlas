The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), a polynomial worth function \(V\) on \([0,\mu]\), and an efficient allocation rule for coalition compositions, does there exist a measurable partition with no positive-mass group that can reorganize among existing or new coalitions so that almost every deviator strictly improves? Theorem 1’s continuous promised subclass assumes \(\sum_t y_t\phi_t(\mu)\ge V(y)\) for every \(y\le\mu\), where \(\phi_t(\mu)\) is the grand-coalition per-capita payoff.

The model it lives in

A population consists of types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\). Coalitions have compositions \(x\in[0,\mu]\), worth \(V(x)\), and per-capita allocations \(\phi_t(x)\) satisfying \(\sum_t x_t\phi_t(x)=V(x)\). Under grand-coalition core domination, \(\sum_t y_t\phi_t(\mu)\ge V(y)\) for all \(y\le\mu\), the one-coalition partition is FX-FE strongly Nash stable against positive-mass deviations.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorem 1, proved here, says that if an allocation rule is efficient on every subgame and its grand-game allocation is in the core, then the grand-coalition partition is FX-FE strongly Nash stable. Corollary 3 applies that guarantee to the Shapley value on convex games, using the established fact that it lies in the core there. Theorems 2–5 give examples where stability is or is not possible; none classifies a problem as tractable or hard. The conclusion’s claim that deciding stable-partition existence “appears to be computationally hard” is an open direction, not a proved result. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no continuous question can honestly be presented as a mirror of a named complexity result in this paper.

The strongest prospective mirror I see is Continuous Shapley-Induced FX-FE Stability. Imagine a large workforce in which many workers share the same complete role and skill profile. Let \(T\) be the finite set of profiles, with rational population shares \(\mu_t\). A coalition is described by its composition \(x\in[0,\mu]\), and its total worth is a rational polynomial \(v(x)\), with \(v(0)=0\). Use the Aumann–Shapley extension of Shapley to assign each member of type \(t\) in coalition \(x\) the per-capita payoff
\[ \phi_t(x)=\int_0^1 \partial_t v(sx)\,ds. \]
A solution is a measurable partition of the population into coalitions. The decision question is whether there exists a partition with no positive-mass group able to leave and reorganize—joining existing coalitions or forming new ones—so that almost every deviator strictly improves their payoff. The partition is the decision variable; the objective is existence of FX-FE strong Nash stability.

This keeps the paper’s central question intact: an allocation rule induces preferences over coalition compositions, and the test is whether those preferences support strong stability. Its scope would be the paper’s stability and nonexistence results, not its broader XAI framing. Theorem 1 suggests a tractable promised subclass: if the grand-coalition allocation satisfies \(\sum_t y_t\phi_t(\mu)\ge v(y)\) for every \(y\le\mu\), the grand coalition is stable by the same efficiency-and-core argument. For the unrestricted polynomial-worth family, I would tentatively expect continuum-specific hardness, but the paper establishes no reduction or complexity result to support that guess. Useful next questions are whether stable partitions always have finite descriptions and whether hardness or tractability holds once the input and witness representations are fixed.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper studies a finite-player Shapley value, while this mirror uses Aumann–Shapley payoffs and a worth function over fractional coalition compositions. A replica-limit argument could make that a convincing extension, but the paper provides no such dictionary, and its finite examples do not automatically carry over. This is a plausible new ChoCo problem—not a computational mirror anchored in this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and its corollaries are conditional stability guarantees; Theorems 2–5 give finite examples. The conclusion’s claim that deciding stable-partition existence “appears” hard is an open question, not a complexity result. So the proposed continuous problem cannot be a computational mirror of a result established here.

The natural extension of Theorem 1 is easy to state: in a population with type masses \(\mu\), if the grand-coalition allocation satisfies \(\sum_t y_t\phi_t(\mu)\ge V(y)\) for every subcomposition \(y\le\mu\), then no positive-mass group can profitably deviate. The paper’s proof carries over directly. But deciding whether that condition holds would be a new core-membership computation, not a computational consequence of the theorem. Likewise, the mixed stability outcomes in Theorems 2–5 are three-player examples; turning them into population results requires choosing how coalition worth scales with mass. Their finite worth tables do not determine that choice.

The proposed Aumann–Shapley model makes a plausible choice: workers with repeated role profiles, a polynomial worth function over type masses, and stability against positive-mass deviations. But it adds the continuum worth function and changes the allocation rule. The paper does not supply a discrete-to-continuous dictionary showing that this model preserves its Shapley examples or stability question. Asking whether stable partitions exist in that new model may be a worthwhile ChoCo project. It is not a mirror extracted from this paper. The whole-partition version also needs a finite representation or a theorem reducing arbitrary measurable partitions to finite descriptions before it becomes a standard algorithmic problem.

That is a strong case against calling this paper a source of a computational mirror. It does not establish the broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile one. A large workforce with repeated complete role types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the polynomial Aumann–Shapley formulation could produce a well-posed finite-input question. I cannot honestly defeat that stronger version. The negative case is solid on the absence of an anchored result, but weak on the universal claim: the best proposed model survives as a new problem motivated by the paper.
