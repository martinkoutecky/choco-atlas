What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; Theorems 1–4 and the quota and popularity extensions give expected-rank bounds, while the simulations are empirical illustrations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: in i.i.d. one-to-one markets with \(n\) students and schools, where student preferences and school priorities are independent uniform rankings, EADA’s expected average student rank is at most \(4\log\log n+O(1)\). Theorem 2, also proved here, says that in the same market model, *any* Pareto-efficient matching that weakly Pareto-dominates student-proposing DA has expected average rank \(O((\log\log n)^2)\). Both make the population size central, and Theorem 1 studies a mechanism that improves assignments across that population.

But neither theorem is a computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. They bound expected welfare under random profiles; they do not classify a problem as tractable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. The paper has no eligible named anchor, so I cannot give a compliant continuous problem per anchor.

A tempting analogy would take masses of student and school types, describe assignments by a matching flow, and study rank under continuum DA or EADA. Its weakest point is substantial: the paper’s types are complete rankings over growing sets of named agents, so their number grows with the market. Aggregating them into a small fixed type set changes the model, while replacing rank by rank quantile changes the scale of the theorems. That could motivate a separate continuum-market analysis, but it does not supply the computational mirror this programme asks for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Neither Theorem 1 nor Theorem 2 is an eligible ChoCo anchor. Theorem 1 bounds expected rank in a random finite market; Theorem 2 bounds the rank of every matching in a class. Neither defines a computational problem or asserts anything about its complexity or algorithms. The quota and popularity extensions remain expected-welfare bounds. That is the decisive gap: there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The best rescue for Theorem 1 would build a continuum school-choice model with masses of student and school types, then define mass EADA and measure rank by the fraction of schools a student prefers to her assignment. But the paper’s random market gives students rankings over individually named schools and schools priorities over individually named students. Treating agents as complete types must retain how every school ranks each student; under the paper’s random model, those full descriptions are effectively unique. Grouping students by preference alone loses priority information that DA and EADA use. Replacing individual priorities with type-level priorities or lotteries could make a finite-type model, but it would be a different market.

There is also a scale problem for this theorem. The natural continuum rank is a percentile. The stated bound becomes \(\mathbb E[R(\mu^{\EADA})/n]=O(\log\log n/n)\), which vanishes; the DA rank scale vanishes too. The result’s distinction is in the slower-growing, unnormalized rank, a finite-resolution effect that a continuum percentile does not retain. Recovering it would require a sequence of finite discretizations, not just the continuous society.

For Theorem 2, one could newly ask for a Pareto-efficient mass assignment that improves on continuum DA and minimizes average rank. That may be a sensible school-choice optimization problem, but it is not what the theorem computes: the theorem makes no selection or algorithm for \(\nu\). Its finite acyclicity argument concerns cycles among particular students; an atomless version needs a new definition of efficiency in terms of positive-mass reallocations and a new analysis. Even if that reformulation is worthwhile, it is a new question rather than a computational mirror of the theorem.

So the strongest negative I can support is that this paper provides no worthwhile anchored ChoCo mirror: its results are probabilistic welfare theorems, and the most natural continuum restatement loses their rank scale. I would not claim the broader impossibility that no continuous computational market-design question could be built around school choice; the paper alone cannot establish that.
