The continuous mirror question

Given voter types \(k\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_k\), per-unit voting weights \(a_k\) satisfying \(\sum_k \mu_k a_k=1\), and quota \(q\), compute the allocation \(x\) minimizing \(\max\{1-\sum_k z_kx_k: 0\le z_k\le\mu_k,\ \sum_k a_kz_k\ge q\}\), subject to \(x_k\ge0\) and \(\sum_k\mu_kx_k=1\).

The model it lives in

A weighted-majority society described by a distribution over voter weight types; coalition variables \(z_k\) select mass from each type, and \(x_k\) is the allocation per unit mass. The objective is to minimize the largest winning-coalition excess.

The objection that survived

Proposition 2’s guarantee depends on integer replication counts and absolute weight scale, which a mass-only instance does not retain.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the first-stage nucleus and the weight-coincidence question connected to Proposition 2. Proposition 1’s convergence claim and the paper’s other asymptotic bounds remain analytic results outside this computational mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the type-mass nucleus be computed in polynomial time using a separation oracle based on fractional knapsack?
  • For which mass distributions and per-unit weights does the nucleus equal the normalized weight allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that a continuous weighted-majority nucleus is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s subject. Let type \(k\) be a voter’s weight class, with population mass \(\mu_k\) and per-unit voting weight \(a_k\), where \(\sum_k \mu_k a_k=1\). A coalition is a vector \(z\) with \(0\le z_k\le\mu_k\), and wins when \(\sum_k a_k z_k\ge q\). For an allocation \(x_k\) per member of each type, require \(x_k\ge0\) and \(\sum_k\mu_k x_k=1\). Its excess is
\[ e(z,x)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_k a_k z_k\ge q\right]-\sum_k z_kx_k. \]
The continuous problem is to find an allocation minimizing \(\max_z e(z,x)\). This is the paper’s first-stage “nucleus” notion. A full continuous nucleolus would need an additional definition of lexicographic minimization over the continuum of coalition excesses.

A credible regime is a large legislature or shareholder body with many members but only a few statutory voting weights. Weight is a complete type for the majority game: members of the same weight are interchangeable. The model therefore has \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of weight classes.

The paper gives meaningful support for that regime. Proposition 1, proved here, shows that when normalized quotas stay away from \(0\) and \(1\), and each individual’s normalized weight vanishes along a sequence of games, the nucleolus converges in \(L_1\) to the normalized weight vector. It further proves that nucleolus ratios converge to weight ratios for regular players. Proposition 2, also proved here, gives an exact finite-game condition: with integer weights, \(t\) distinct weight values, and \(m^\circ\) occurrences of the rarest weight, the nucleolus equals normalized weights if
\[ \min\{\bar q,1-\bar q\}\,m^\circ>2t w_1^2. \]
The authors note that sufficiently many replications meet this condition. These are not claims about a continuum computation, but they show that repeated weight types and large populations matter to the paper’s own results.

For the proposed nucleus problem, I would expect tractability: checking a candidate allocation reduces to finding a minimum-\(x\)-value winning coalition, a fractional-knapsack linear program over the \(\tau\) type masses. That is a reason to investigate this mirror, not a result established by the paper.

The required anchor test is decisive, though: neither proposition is a named computational-complexity result. The paper names no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Its discussion of computing the nucleolus by a sequence of linear programs does not supply such a result. So this is a plausible continuous follow-up, but there is no compliant complexity anchor here. The soft spot is that the paper already studies asymptotic population behavior, while the continuous computational question above—especially beyond the nucleus—is a new formulation rather than a complexity result this paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive for the proposed anchors: the paper proves no named computational-complexity result. Proposition 1 is a convergence theorem for nucleolus components; Proposition 2 is a sufficient condition for exact equality with normalized weights. Neither gives an algorithmic complexity classification. The remark that a nucleolus can be computed by a sequence of linear programs supplies no input model, running-time bound, or pricing result.

Proposition 1’s direct mirror is a limit question: what allocation does the nucleolus approach as each individual’s weight vanishes? That is already the proposition’s subject under its stated conditions. Defining the continuous allocation as the limit of replicated games therefore yields an analytic continuation of the result, not a computational result to mirror. One could ask for algorithms outside those conditions, though that would be a new question rather than an anchor supplied by Proposition 1.

Proposition 2’s exact threshold depends on the integer replication count \(m^\circ\) and integer weight scale \(w_1\). A society described only by type masses has discarded the absolute population size that determines whether the threshold is met. Keeping that size recovers the finite replication question; dropping it leaves a mass-level reformulation of a sufficient condition, not a computational result. Computing a continuous nucleolus could be a better follow-up, though it is again a separate question.

The proposed continuous weighted-majority model is coherent: weight class can be a complete type, and coalition choices can be fractional masses. Its first-stage nucleus also has a clean separation problem—fractional knapsack over the type masses. That makes it a plausible, perhaps tractable research question. The paper’s finite-coalition lexicographic definition of the full nucleolus does not directly specify its continuum counterpart; defining one through replica limits returns to the asymptotic analysis above. These points defeat Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 as computational anchors, but they do not establish that no worthwhile continuous computational question can be built from this subject. The negative case is weak precisely there: the high-multiplicity regime is sensible, and the nucleus mirror is well-posed even though it is not anchored by a computational result in this paper.
