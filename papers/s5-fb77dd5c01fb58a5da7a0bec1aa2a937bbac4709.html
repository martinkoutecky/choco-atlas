A direct high-multiplicity question is whether those winner problems remain hard when the electorate is supplied as \(\tau\) complete rankings with rational masses, and how complexity depends on \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). For Young, the continuous version can ask how much mass must abstain to make a candidate Condorcet-winning; for Dodgson, how much adjacent-swap cost must be applied across the mass. These are population continuizations, and fractional type-level changes are explicitly within ChoCo’s scope. The programme’s supplied examples even identify continuous Young and Dodgson as tractable, which makes this a concrete computational mirror.

There is also a natural question from Studies 3 and 4 in Table 2, which report Minimax’s winner-quality advantage in spatial simulations. Given a continuous society over complete rankings and costs for changing types, what is the least-cost mass transfer that changes the Minimax winner? That is a continuous campaigning or winner-robustness problem tied to the paper’s comparison of Minimax with Condorcet-Hare and its discussion of strategic voting. In a large electorate with relatively few ballot types, mass is a sensible quantity; the cost does not collapse when the population grows.

The important caveat is that the paper does not prove a numbered complexity theorem of its own. The Dodgson and Young hardness results are cited, and the simulation studies do not classify complexity.
