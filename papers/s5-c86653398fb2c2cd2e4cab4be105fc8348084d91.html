The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a theorem-anchored case from this paper: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. It does report complexity claims, including NP-completeness for finding a hedonic-game core and co-NP-completeness for testing membership, but attributes them to earlier work. Its own main evidence is the glove-game simulation reported in Figure 5, not a numbered complexity result.

The strongest unanchored mirror is the paper’s glove-game core problem. Let each type be an endowment \(t=(\ell_t,r_t)\in\{0,\ldots,9\}^2\), and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of a large population. A coalition with type-mass vector \(x\) gives each member payoff
\[ u(x)=\frac{\min(\sum_t \ell_t x_t,\sum_t r_t x_t)}{\sum_t x_t}. \]
Given a finite partition into coalition mass vectors \(x^1,\ldots,x^K\) summing to \(\mu\), ask whether it is core-stable: is there any nonzero mass vector \(y\), drawn from members whose current coalition payoff is below \(u(y)\), that would strictly benefit all its members? A solution to the finding version is such a stable partition. A plausible regime is a large exchange or reuse economy with many participants but relatively few repeated endowment types.

That is a credible population mirror of the paper’s underlying glove-game question, but it is not an anchor under your requirements. Figure 5’s reported success rate—96.1% in the Results text—comes from sampled games of only three to nine players, and does not establish the complexity of the continuous problem. The candidate’s weakest point is therefore evidential: this paper supplies no qualifying named result for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the strongest possible mirror, but it does not give this paper a named computational result to mirror. The paper’s NP-completeness claims are attributed to earlier work. Its own contribution is a six-routine stochastic heuristic and a finite glove-game experiment; Figure 5 reports how often that heuristic found a core member on games with three to nine players. The proposed continuous core problem replaces that contribution with an exact core-finding question. That may be a worthwhile problem, but it is not a continuous version of a result this paper establishes.

The glove-game mirror is coherent, and a large exchange economy with many people sharing a small set of glove endowments is plausible. The paper’s own glove payoff also ignores identities beyond endowments, so identity dependence is no objection here. But the mirror does not carry over the paper’s distinctive computational object: its local, randomized coalition-forming dynamics. In an atomless population, selecting one or two individuals and changing their coalition membership moves zero mass. Making the dynamics nontrivial would require choosing a time scaling or allowing positive-mass batches or blocks. The paper supplies no principle for choosing among those versions. Asking for a stable mass partition avoids that problem, but then the question is about the exact core, not the heuristic’s reported performance.

There is also a substantive change in blocking. For the proposed payoff,
\[ u(y)=\frac{\min(\ell\cdot y,r\cdot y)}{\mathbf{1}\cdot y}, \]
scaling a coalition by any \(\lambda>0\) leaves its payoff unchanged: \(u(\lambda y)=u(y)\). If a positive mass of each required type is eligible to deviate, any improving type mixture can be scaled down until it fits. Thus coalition size and the finite coordination thresholds that shape the paper’s local search disappear from the blocking test. That is a legitimate atomless model, not a reason to reject fractional mass; it does mean the proposed core question no longer captures the mechanism behind the paper’s experiment.

I would therefore reject the proponent’s anchor as a mirror of this paper’s computational result. The case against is weak at the broader level: the continuous glove-game core is sensible and could be worth studying in its own right. What I cannot support is the stronger claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror. The strongest negative point is narrower: this paper offers no named computational result, and its most plausible continuous reformulation changes the paper’s heuristic into a different computational problem.
