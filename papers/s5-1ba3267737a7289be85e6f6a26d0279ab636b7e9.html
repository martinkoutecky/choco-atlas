What the mirror covers

The proposed question concerns Theorem 9’s Type 1 classification; it covers no computational result, and the other numbered results are also structural classifications or restrictions.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper’s setting is a natural large-population regime: many voters, a relatively small support of distinct rankings, and each ranking represented by its fraction of the electorate. The authors would recognize that as their model. In fact, they already write the electorate as a normalized profile vector \(p\) in the unit simplex, so the population is already represented continuously.

The closest anchor is Theorem 9 (proved here). It says that any Type 1 stage satisfying SFBC, neutrality, anonymity, linearity, no turnout quota, and decisiveness is a point system: candidates receive points according to how many voters rank them in each position, and the candidate with the most points wins. The first and second positions must receive equal points; with strict, complete rankings, the method is a positional method.

A direct continuous question would be: given a finite set \(T\) of rankings and a linear Type 1 election rule on the mass simplex \(\Delta(T)\), classify the rules that satisfy those six conditions and return their score vectors. Theorem 9 gives the classification. A plausible regime is a large public election with many voters but relatively few distinct ballots.

But that is not a valid ChoCo anchor under the requested standard. Theorem 9 classifies voting rules; it does not establish a computational complexity or algorithmic result. None of the paper’s numbered theorems supplies an eligible computational anchor. And this proposed question would restate a classification the paper already performs over the continuous profile simplex, rather than open a distinct computational problem about a continuous society. The weak point is therefore decisive: while the population regime is convincing, the paper offers no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own anchor, Theorem 9, is not a computational result: it characterizes a class of voting rules. It gives no complexity bound or algorithm for an encoded problem. More fundamentally, its proposed continuous question—classifying Type 1 rules over a mass simplex—is already the question the paper answers. The paper defines each ballot type’s coordinate as its fraction of voters and develops its geometry over the unit simplex. This is not a case where continuity of the outcomes is mistaken for continuity of the population: the population is already represented by its distribution over rankings.

There is no high-multiplicity objection here. A large election with many voters sharing a relatively small set of rankings is a sensible regime, and the paper’s anonymity and no-turnout-quota assumptions fit it well. But that makes the continuous model a collision with the paper’s existing model, not a new mirror. The paper’s other numbered results do not rescue the anchor: they characterize rule properties or rule impossibilities, rather than computational problems.

The strongest repair would be to ask an algorithmic question about the rules Theorem 9 identifies. Given a fixed score vector and a society supported on \(\tau\) rankings, computing the winner is just tallying the scores. A harder question—such as the least-cost mass of voters a campaigner must persuade to change rankings—could be worthwhile. But it introduces a cost model, a target, and a coordinated intervention that the theorem does not study. The introduction’s campaigner motivation makes that a plausible new ChoCo problem; it does not turn Theorem 9 into a computational result to mirror.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as an anchor: the natural continuous formulation is already present, and no named computational result remains to continuize. The case is weaker only if “mirror” is broadened to include new algorithmic problems prompted by the paper’s discussion. Those might merit study on their own, but they do not establish a continuous mirror of the paper’s results.
