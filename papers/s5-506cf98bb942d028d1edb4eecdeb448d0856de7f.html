The continuous mirror question

For a single half-hour, the input is rational type masses \(\mu_t\), explicit contract menus \(J_t\), each offer's bid \(b_j\) and bundle size \(\ell_j\), rational per-capita demand \(D\), and nonnegative spot price \(m\). Choose accepted offer mass \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{j\in J_t}x_{tj}\le\mu_t\), so each EV receives at most one whole contract. Let \(q=\sum_{t,j}x_{tj}\ell_j\); maximize \(\sum_{t,j}x_{tj}(m-b_j)\ell_j-m\max\{0,q-D\}\).

The model it lives in

A large depot cohort of EVs with a small number of repeated offer types, each specifying its contract menu and attributes. The platform chooses what fraction of each type receives each whole contract; demand and accepted energy are measured per capita.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is HM-Hour-Scheduling Contract Selection. Its anchor is Proposition 1, which the paper states and attempts to prove here by reduction from Knapsack, rather than citing as an established theorem: given offered contracts \(J_{\mathrm{offered}}\), market prices \(M\), and demand \(D\), finding a subset \(J^*\subseteq J_{\mathrm{offered}}\) that maximizes the paper’s society-savings objective \(\mathrm{SocSave}(J,D,M)\) is NP-hard.

The mirror is a day-ahead market with many EVs but relatively few standardized vehicle and duty-cycle profiles. This is plausible for bus or delivery depots with repeated vehicle models and schedules. Each agent is a one-EV fleet unit, a case the paper itself allows in Remark 2. A type \(t\) specifies the EV’s exportable bundle, available half-hours, contract offers, bids, costs, and other relevant attributes. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the EV population with type \(t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). In this regime, the number of EVs \(N\) is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of types.

Here is the problem as I would hand it to a prover. The input is the rational type masses \(\mu_t\); each type’s explicit contract offers \(J_t\); for each offer \(j\), its half-hour \(h(j)\), bid \(b_j\), and bundle size \(\ell_j\); and rational per-capita demand \(D_h\) and nonnegative spot price \(m_h\) for each half-hour \(h\). Choose \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), the mass of type-\(t\) EVs assigned contract \(j\), with \(\sum_{j\in J_t}x_{tj}\le\mu_t\). Thus each EV gets at most one contract for its bundle; the contract and energy bundle remain whole for every EV.

Set \(q_h=\sum_{t,j:h(j)=h}x_{tj}\ell_j\). The goal is to maximize

\[ \sum_{t,j}x_{tj}\bigl(m_{h(j)}-b_j\bigr)\ell_j -\sum_h m_h\max\{0,q_h-D_h\}. \]

This is the paper’s \(\mathrm{SocSave}\) objective applied to accepted contract mass, with demand and energy measured per capita. A solution is an optimal mass assignment \(x\). With nonnegative prices, introduce \(z_h\ge 0\) and \(z_h\ge q_h-D_h\); maximizing the same objective with the overflow term replaced by \(-\sum_h m_hz_h\) gives a linear program of size polynomial in the number of offers, types, half-hours, and input bit length.

I expect this mirror to be Class A: the paper’s contract-selection question remains intact, but a continuum of repeated type members can be split across whole-contract choices. That is precisely the high-multiplicity relaxation; it does not make an individual EV’s energy divisible. The underlying problem is still “which offered contracts should the platform accept to maximize society’s savings?” The mirror covers Proposition 1’s optimization problem, not the paper’s VCG truthfulness claims or its Frequency Regulating mechanism.

One caveat belongs with the anchor. Proposition 1 is presented as proved here, but the printed Knapsack reduction appears to assign bids incorrectly for the stated objective: with the paper’s \(\mathrm{SocSave}\) formula, the displayed \(b_i=x_i/v_i\) does not make a feasible item’s contribution equal to its Knapsack value \(x_i\). The hardness claim appears repairable: for Knapsack item volume \(v_i\) and value \(x_i\), choose \(b_i=m-x_i/v_i\), with \(m\) large enough that every over-capacity selection has negative savings. That encodes the usual value-maximizing Knapsack instance, but the printed proof should be corrected before treating the anchor as fully established.

The main weakness of the mirror is the regime: many identical EV contract profiles are credible in standardized depots, but many *identical fleet offers and costs* may be less common, especially when legal fleet owners have idiosyncratic costs. The paper’s single-EV case and a depot-cohort interpretation make the regime plausible, though narrower than the paper’s general model. The next questions are how to round the mass solution for a finite number of EVs and bound the resulting loss, whether truthfulness and budget balance survive in this setting, and what changes when spot prices can be negative.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against the proposed anchor is that it may be a very thin mirror of the paper’s actual setting. The proposed LP gives each EV type a single constraint, \(\sum_{j\in J_t}x_{tj}\le\mu_t\). But the paper allows the same bundle to be offered at different half-hours in different peak periods. If an EV can accept one such contract in each peak, that constraint rules out allocations the paper permits. A faithful version would make each type’s options complete feasible schedules across the day, then allocate mass among those schedules. That is a sounder formulation, but its option set may be much larger, and the simple LP description no longer by itself settles the pricing or computational question.

This is a limitation of the proponent’s formulation, though, not a defeat of the mirror. The paper’s one-half-hour, one-bundle hardness construction is already a subcase with no cross-peak scheduling issue. In that subcase, repeated EVs with the same bundle and complete contract menu can be represented by type masses; each EV still receives a whole contract, while mass records how many members of the type take each option. Scaling demand per capita is also a plausible co-modification for a depot cohort. The allocation objective remains the paper’s contract-selection objective.

The nearby literature makes the case for studying that regime stronger. Meir, Ma, and Robu’s DR-VCG work studies finite-agent contract selection, efficient computation, and simulations with populations up to \(n=400\). That is relevant high-multiplicity context, not an existing continuous-population solution, so it is not a collision. [Meir, Ma, and Robu, “Contract Design for Energy Demand Response”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07300)

My strongest negative point is therefore about research priority: this anchor appears to be a standard convexification of a multiple-choice selection problem, and the proponent’s simplest model does not capture all of the paper’s cross-period choices. That may make it a less demanding test of ChoCo’s methods. But under the stated standard, an elementary LP answer still counts, and the paper’s single-period case gives a recognizable computational question with a credible repeated-agent regime. I cannot make a persuasive case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; this anchor survives.
