The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its results are experimental claims about neural predictions; the claim that cooperative-game solutions are hard is general background citing prior work, not a result proved here. The LPs in Appendix C are formulations, not numbered theorem statements. So I can’t honestly provide an anchor-specific mirror or claim that the paper proves a complexity boundary.

The most plausible *unanchored* lead is a continuum version of the paper’s least-core problem for weighted voting games. Take a finite set of types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), individual voting weights \(a_t\), and quota \(q\leq\sum_t\mu_t a_t\). A type comprises all traits relevant to voting and payoff; its mass is the fraction of members of that type. A coalition is described by masses \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), and wins when \(\sum_t a_tz_t\geq q\). The problem is to find nonnegative per-member payoffs \(p_t\) and \(\epsilon\geq0\) minimizing \(\epsilon\), subject to
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_t=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}z_t p_t\geq 1-\epsilon \ \text{for every winning coalition }z. \]
This asks for the least-core allocation of a unit surplus: the payoff per member of each type, and the smallest instability it can achieve.

A plausible regime is a very large association or union with many members but only a few standardized voting-weight classes. Then the population is large relative to the support size \(|T|\), and fractional coalition masses are a natural approximation to member-level choices. The authors could recognize this as their least-core allocation question with a high-multiplicity population model. For this version, I would expect tractability: for a proposed allocation, finding the winning coalition with the least total payoff is a fractional knapsack calculation over the types. Further questions include the exact bit complexity of solving the resulting optimization problem and how its solution relates to finite high-multiplicity games.

This candidate covers only weighted-voting least-core allocations, not the paper’s Shapley or Banzhaf experiments, nor its feature-importance application. Its weakness is central: it is a reasonable extension of a problem the paper computes with, but it is not anchored in a named computational result of the paper. The paper studies individually specified games with \(4\)–\(20\) players and reports predictive experiments; it does not establish a high-multiplicity complexity result.

The least-core proposal is the strongest extension, and I can’t honestly defeat it as a continuous-population problem. Grouping players by voting weight, assigning a per-member payoff to each type, and allowing coalitions to contain fractional masses is a coherent high-multiplicity model. Its separation problem is fractional knapsack, so it appears to give a tractable question in the programme’s terms. The type-level payoff does not lose relevant identity when players of a type are identical for the game.

Calling that proposal “not a mirror” because the paper reports experiments rather than a theorem only defeats it under the requested anchor-based test. It does not show the question is unworthwhile; the paper trains predictors for least-core allocations, so the authors could reasonably recognize this as a continuous version of one of their target problems. Nor can I dismiss it because the answer may be easy or tractable: that is exactly the kind of result the programme seeks.

So the honest negative case is weak beyond the anchor test. The paper has no eligible named result to mirror, but the proposed least-core extension appears to survive as a plausible continuous mirror. I can’t support the stronger claim that no scenario makes a worthwhile mirror.
