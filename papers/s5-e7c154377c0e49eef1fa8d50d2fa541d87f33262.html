The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 says no non-probabilistic pSCF satisfies population-consistency and cloning-consistency; Theorem 2 characterizes a pSCF; Theorem 3 gives an alternative characterization. None asserts that a computational problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. So I can’t honestly claim the paper has a named computational result to mirror.

The closest candidate, if the anchor requirement were broadened, is Theorem 2, proved here by transferring the fractional-profile result of Brandl et al. (2016): *maximal lotteries are the only pSCF satisfying population-consistency and composition-consistency.* A plausible computational follow-up would be: given candidates \(C\) and a support of \(\tau\) rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\), compute a maximal lottery \(p\). Each ranking is a type, and \(\mu_t\) is its share of a large electorate—for example, a national election with many voters but relatively few distinct ballots. Form the weighted majority-margin matrix \(M_\mu\); return \(p\in\Delta(C)\) with \(p^\mathsf{T}M_\mu\ge 0\). Equivalently, find a maximin strategy for the corresponding zero-sum game. This is an LP, so the natural expectation is tractability in \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length. That computational claim is an inference from the definition of maximal lotteries, not a result asserted by Theorem 2.

This is a plausible problem about a continuous society: the input is a distribution over voter types, and the output is the lottery the paper studies. Its scope would be computing maximal lotteries only; it would not mirror the axiomatic characterization or the impossibility theorem. The weakness is substantial: the paper’s main contribution is axiomatic, not computational, and it already works with fractional profiles in its appendix, identifying them with homogeneous finite profiles. The authors might accept the computation as a natural question about their object, but it is not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is an impossibility result about axioms; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize maximal lotteries. None identifies the complexity of a computational problem.

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s best anchor, but its proposed mirror—compute a maximal lottery from a distribution over rankings—repackages an object the paper already studies. In Appendix A, profiles are rational distributions over rankings, \(\mathcal R_A^*=\Delta_{\mathbb Q}(L(A))\), and the paper proves the characterization in that fractional-profile framework before transferring it to finite profiles. So the population is already represented by type masses. A question asking whether the characterization holds for a continuous society would repeat work the paper presents; asking for the lottery on that society turns its existing definition into a computational task, rather than mirroring a computational result.

There is a more ambitious possibility: choose mass transfers, with specified costs, to make a candidate enter or uniquely determine the maximal-lottery outcome. That would fit ChoCo’s population model well. Rankings are natural types, and large elections with repeated ballots are a credible high-multiplicity regime. But the paper supplies no transfer operation, costs, target, or intervention objective. Adding those would define a new problem around maximal lotteries, not continuize one of the paper’s results.

That distinction is the case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror. It is not a claim that no worthwhile continuous research about maximal lotteries is possible. The universal claim that no such scenario exists is too strong to establish from this text; the narrower negative conclusion is that the paper itself offers no computational result to mirror, and its strongest apparent candidate is already formulated over fractional populations.
