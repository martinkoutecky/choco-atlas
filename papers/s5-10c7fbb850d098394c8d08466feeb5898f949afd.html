The continuous mirror question

Given type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and two finite lists of weighted-majority clauses with rational quotas and coefficients in \(\{0,1\}\), decide whether the unions define the same winning function for every \(x\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), where each clause wins when its weighted mass reaches its quota.

The model it lives in

Types are contribution vectors across the given clauses; \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\), and \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) is its coalition mass. Each clause evaluates its linear threshold on \(x\), each union accepts when at least one clause accepts, and the task is exact equivalence over the full mass box.

The objection that survived

CUE can distinguish weighted-clause representations that define the same finite simple game, so it extends the clause presentations rather than an invariant fractional interpretation of the game.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4’s equivalence-testing problem. It leaves the paper’s dimension and codimension results, their hardness consequences, and its other propositions unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize when equivalence on the finite coalition grid implies equivalence throughout the type-mass box.

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one anchor do the work: Proposition 4. The paper states that checking whether one given union of weighted majority games is equivalent to another is coNP-complete, even when every weight is \(0\) or \(1\). It presents this as an extension of equivalence results from [2]; Proposition 4 is stated as a result of this paper, not merely cited from elsewhere.

A plausible high multiplicity setting is a large federation or membership organization whose members fall into a small number of roles. Members in the same role contribute identically to each of the weighted-majority clauses being compared. For an equivalence instance with \(r+s\) clauses and binary weights, a member’s complete task-relevant type is their vector of \(0/1\) contributions to those clauses. There are at most \(2^{r+s}\) such types, so a society with millions of members can still have a modest support size \(\tau\). A coalition becomes a choice of participating mass from each type.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous Union Equivalence (CUE). An instance consists of type masses \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and two finite lists of weighted-majority clauses, \(\mathcal A=((q_i,a_i))_{i=1}^r\) and \(\mathcal B=((p_j,b_j))_{j=1}^s\), where \(a_{it},b_{jt}\in\{0,1\}\) and the quotas are rational. The domain of coalitions is \(D=\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\); \(x_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) in the coalition. Define \(F_{\mathcal A}(x)=1\) iff some \(i\) satisfies \(\sum_t a_{it}x_t\ge q_i\), and define \(F_{\mathcal B}\) likewise. The question is whether \(F_{\mathcal A}(x)=F_{\mathcal B}(x)\) for every \(x\in D\). A “no” answer has a rational witness \(x\) where the two rules disagree.

This has a direct finite bridge. If \(n_t\) players have type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); a discrete coalition \(S\) maps to \(x_t=|S\cap N_t|/n\), and scale each original quota by \(1/n\). The clause outcomes then agree exactly on all discrete coalitions. CUE asks whether they also agree at every fractional type-coalition profile. That is the population-continuous extension of the same representation-equivalence question.

I expect CUE to be tractable. To test whether \(F_{\mathcal A}\subseteq F_{\mathcal B}\), enumerate each clause \(i\) of \(\mathcal A\) and ask whether there is an \(x\in D\) satisfying that clause while strictly losing every clause of \(\mathcal B\). This is linear feasibility with strict inequalities, handled by a slack variable; repeat in the other direction. The resulting algorithm uses polynomially many LP calls and is polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r+s\), and the input bit length \(L\). This would be a Class A mirror of Proposition 4’s finite coNP-completeness in the small-support regime.

The mirror is close to the paper’s subject: it still asks whether two unions of weighted-majority rules define the same winning rule. Its scope is deliberately limited to Proposition 4; it does not claim to mirror the dimension and codimension results. It also raises a useful follow-up: when does agreement on the discrete coalition grid imply agreement throughout the continuous domain?

The weak point is that the paper defines equivalence only over subsets of named players. A finite simple game does not uniquely determine its behavior at fractional coalitions; CUE uses the given weighted-majority clauses to supply that extension. So this is a plausible new high multiplicity question about the paper’s central object, but it is not a theorem that the original finite equivalence problem has become easy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is the paper’s clearest computational anchor, so the case against it cannot be that there is no result to mirror. The issue is whether CUE preserves the object being compared. The paper defines equivalence by the winning coalitions of the represented simple games; its computational result concerns that equivalence question. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.04328)

Consider two players and the one-clause unions \([1;1,1]\) and \([1/2;1,1]\). Both represent the same finite game: a coalition wins exactly when it is nonempty. But with normalized coalition mass \(x\), CUE compares \(x\ge 1/2\) with \(x\ge 1/4\), so it calls them unequal—for example at \(x=3/8\). That disagreement comes from the chosen quotas, not from the simple game: both quota values fall in the same gap between attainable discrete scores. More generally, equivalent finite representations can induce different fractional rules. CUE therefore tests whether two *chosen formulas* agree on fractional coalitions, not whether the two simple games are equivalent in the paper’s sense.

A more faithful mirror would need an extension determined by the finite simple game itself, invariant under changing its weighted-game representation. But the finite game specifies outcomes only for whole coalitions; it does not say how to classify fractional coalitions. Choosing a quota normalization, convexification, or some other interpolation adds modeling structure. Those choices might define worthwhile continuous problems, but they would not settle the representation-invariant ambiguity.

That is the strongest objection I can make, and it has a real limit. Large populations with repeated voting roles are plausible, and CUE’s type masses and fractional coalitions are a coherent model. If the authors treat the weighted clauses as primitive rules, they may recognize CUE as their problem. So I would challenge CUE as a mirror of Proposition 4, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous extension of the paper’s subject.
