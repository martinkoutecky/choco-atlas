Why no mirror

The paper has computational approximation results, but every proposed mirror depends on treating a whole label-type cohort as one strategic actor. With individual agents in a nonatomic population, a person's report has no effect on the aggregate mechanism; making the cohort influential changes the incentive problem and leaves a finite set of weighted strategic players. The risk guarantee of Theorem 2.3 can be averaged over a type distribution, but that drops the strategyproofness constraint central to the result.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors address the decision-theoretic results in Section 2; they leave the learning-theoretic results in Section 3 untouched, and no Section 2 anchor survives as a population-continuum mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible mirror is the paper’s decision-theoretic setting: many employees judge the same fixed set of emails, but their labelings fall into a relatively small number of stable patterns. A type is a complete labeling \(Y_t:X\to\{+,-\}\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that labeling. The organization chooses a classifier \(c\in C\), minimizing
\[ R_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t R_t(c),\qquad R_t(c)=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}\mathbf 1[c(x)\ne Y_t(x)]. \]
This keeps the shared inputs, private labels, classifier outcomes, and welfare objective intact. A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large organization where many employees share one of a few labeling patterns and a designated representative reports for each pattern.

That last detail matters: the continuous mechanism below treats a positive-mass cohort as the strategic reporting unit. A direct mechanism maps the cohorts’ reported labelings to a classifier or lottery over classifiers. It is strategyproof if no cohort can lower its members’ labeling risk by changing its report. This is a precise cohort-level version of the paper’s problem, though it changes individual agents’ reporting into delegated cohort reporting.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper. It says that for \(n\) agents there are concept classes for which every deterministic strategyproof mechanism has approximation ratio at least \(\Omega(n)\), even when all agents have equal weights. The continuous question is: given a fixed shared input set \(X\), a concept class \(C\), and \(\tau\) distinct labeling types with rational masses summing to \(1\), what is the best worst-case approximation ratio of a deterministic cohort-strategyproof classifier mechanism? The mechanism must satisfy the cohort incentive constraints across all possible labeling reports; a solution is either such a mechanism with a guarantee or a lower-bound proof ruling one out. I would expect the \(\Omega(\tau)\) obstruction to transfer for hard instances whose \(n\) report units remain \(\tau=n\) distinct types of equal mass. The distinct-support condition is a proof obligation, though: the theorem as stated does not say that its hard instances survive aggregation of identical types. This is an approximation-impossibility result, not an NP-hardness or runtime result.

A useful positive counterpart is Theorem 2.3, also proved here. It states that for every concept class, the weighted random dictator mechanism—choose agent \(i\) with probability \(w_i\), then return an empirical risk minimizer for that agent’s data—is strategyproof and a \(3\)-approximation; if every agent’s data is individually realizable by the class, it is a \(2\)-approximation. Its continuous question is whether, for the mass distribution above, selecting type \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t\) and returning a classifier minimizing that type’s risk is strategyproof and achieves
\[ \mathbb E[R_\mu(c)]\le 3\operatorname{OPT}_\mu, \]
improving to \(2\operatorname{OPT}_\mu\) when every type is individually realizable. The mechanism and guarantee are the solution. I expect this to extend structurally: the paper already proves the weighted finite-support case, and the risk calculation is a weighted sum over types. But this is not a polynomial-time claim for arbitrary \(C\); the cost of computing an empirical risk minimizer is outside the theorem.

For a sharper randomized lower bound, Theorem 2.5 is cited from Meir, Procaccia, and Rosenschein (2008), not proved here. It states that for every \(\varepsilon>0\), no randomized strategyproof mechanism achieves a \((2-\varepsilon)\)-approximation for the two-constant-concept class \(C=\{c_+,c_-\}\), even with two equal-weight agents. The continuous question is whether a strategyproof lottery over classifiers can achieve a factor below \(2\) when the society consists of two equally weighted, oppositely labeled types under this class. A lower-bound construction is a solution; the expected answer is that the barrier transfers, since this is the two-type, equal-mass cohort version of the stated finite case. Together with Theorem 2.3, this points to further questions about the best randomized factor for general \(C\) and arbitrary type masses.

The scope here is narrow: these mirrors cover the fixed-data, shared-input results in Section 2. They do not continuize the input space, classifier outcomes, or the learning-theoretic sampling process in Section 3. The paper does have named computational-mechanism results, but none establishes membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, or another running-time class.

The weakest point is the strategic unit. If the population is truly atomless and individuals report separately, one person’s report does not change \(\mu\). Strategyproofness then becomes vacuous, and a welfare-minimizing classifier can be optimal, so the paper’s deterministic impossibility need not survive. The positive case therefore rests on a specific, plausible delegated-reporting regime in which a cohort can act through one representative. An author could recognize the shared-input classification and approximation questions, but might reasonably object that cohort strategyproofness is not their individual strategyproofness problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that these theorems depend on who can make a strategic move, and the proposed population distribution does not specify that. Two people with the same label function can be combined when calculating welfare, but that does not make them one strategic actor: one person changing a report alters only their own contribution. The paper’s mechanisms and impossibility results are about those individual deviations.

If the population is genuinely atomless and a mechanism sees only the reported mass distribution \(\mu\), one person’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Strategyproofness then imposes no constraint: whenever a risk minimizer exists, choosing one for the reported distribution is both optimal at truthful reports and strategyproof. The incentive tradeoff behind the deterministic lower bound in Theorem 2.2 and the two-agent barrier in Theorem 2.5 has disappeared. Theorem 2.3’s random-dictator guarantee can still be stated, but the individual incentive condition no longer explains or constrains it.

Making a cohort into the strategic actor restores nontrivial incentives, but changes the problem. A cohort member’s deviation now moves the reports of the whole mass \(\mu_t\); that is collective manipulation, not the unilateral reporting studied in the paper. Under this interpretation, each supported type is simply one weighted player. The resulting problem is a finite-player mechanism-design problem with \(\tau\) players and weights \(\mu_t\), not a new continuum strategyproofness problem. Theorem 2.3 already permits arbitrary agent weights. Theorem 2.5’s two equal-mass types are just its original two agents under new names.

That distinction also undermines the proposed reading of Theorem 2.2. Its \(n\) counts strategic agents, whereas \(\tau\) counts distinct label functions. Combining identical label functions preserves their contribution to social risk, but it need not preserve unilateral incentive constraints: a single member’s deviation has a different effect from a representative changing the whole cohort’s report. The theorem as stated therefore does not establish a lower bound in \(\tau\). A stronger question about weighted cohort mechanisms could be asked, but it remains a finite-player incentive question parameterized by support size.

The missing granularity is fundamental. The same distribution \(\mu\) could describe two agents or two million agents. Individual deviations have different sizes in those populations, yet \(\mu\) records neither population size nor the size of one agent’s contribution. Keeping that information gives a high-multiplicity sequence of finite games; discarding it yields the atomless model where individual strategyproofness is vacuous. Allowing partial-mass deviations instead asks about coalitional manipulation, a further change in the solution concept.

There is also little connection to the programme’s computational target. These theorems bound the welfare approximation achievable under strategyproofness; they do not characterize running time or computational complexity. In particular, the random-dictator result invokes an empirical risk minimizer for arbitrary \(C\), with no tractability guarantee for computing one.

This negative case has a real limit: a large organization could plausibly delegate reports to representatives of label-pattern cohorts, and an author might recognize that as a useful model. But that would make the cohort the strategic unit by design. Without that additional institution, the proposed mirrors either erase the individual incentives that make the paper’s results interesting or restate a weighted finite-player problem. I would not prioritize them as continuous-population mirrors of these results.
