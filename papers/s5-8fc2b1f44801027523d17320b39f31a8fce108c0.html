The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly name an eligible computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern representation and when cycles can occur, not an algorithm or a complexity classification.

The nearest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper. Theorem 1 says that a relation satisfies completeness, continuity, independence, and mixture domination exactly when there is a closed convex cone \(U\) of utility vectors such that \(p \succeq q\) iff some \(u\in U\) has \(u\cdot p\ge u\cdot q\). Theorem 2 replaces independence with betweenness and gives a corresponding family of closed convex cones \(U_q\), one for each reference lottery \(q\). These are representation theorems, not computational results.

Propositions 4–7, also proved here, are the closest structural anchors. Proposition 4 says that with at most three outcomes, every comonotonic probabilistic decision-maker with core expected-utility preferences satisfies WST for every distribution over core preferences and every lottery set. Proposition 5 gives the same conclusion with at most three outcomes when all monetary-outcome core preferences share risk aversion or share risk seeking. Proposition 6 extends those guarantees to core betweenness preferences: with at most three ordered monetary outcomes, WST holds if all preferences respect first-order stochastic dominance, or if they share a risk attitude. Proposition 7 gives a WST guarantee for core dual-EU preferences on the paper’s specified two-state domain. None asserts an algorithm, running time, or hardness result. The paper’s examples of cycles and its four-outcome counterexample sharpen the structural boundary, but they do not supply a named computational anchor either.

The strongest positive case I can make is therefore a follow-on computational question, not a qualifying mirror. The paper’s group interpretation makes it plausible: it explicitly relates WST to Condorcet cycles and treats core preferences as potentially representing team members. Consider a large organization whose members evaluate risky options using EU preferences. A type is a member’s complete utility vector over the \(m\) possible outcomes; members of the same type share that vector, and \(\mu_t\) is their fraction of the organization. Let the input list the \(\tau\) supported rational utility types and their rational masses, with many members but relatively few types.

Call the problem Continuous WST-Cycle Search. Given that input, find lotteries \(p,q,r\in\Delta(X)\) for which
\[ \mu(p\succ q)>1/2,\qquad \mu(q\succ r)>1/2,\qquad \mu(r\succ p)>1/2, \]
where \(\mu(p\succ q)=\sum_{t:u_t\cdot p>u_t\cdot q}\mu_t\). A solution is an exact triple of lotteries witnessing a majority cycle, or a determination that no such triple exists. This preserves the paper’s underlying question about WST and Condorcet cycles across all lotteries; it does not simplify the members’ preferences. Proposition 4 is the closest behavioral anchor: it says such a cycle cannot occur in its three-outcome, comonotonic case. The paper’s other examples show that the broader question is nontrivial.

For fixed \(m\), an arrangement of the \(O(\tau)\) comparison hyperplanes in the \(3(m-1)\)-dimensional space of lottery triples suggests an exact \( \tau^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(L) \) search. That makes the fixed-outcome slice tractable; it leaves the joint complexity when \(m\) varies open. I would not claim a Class A, B, or C result for the full problem without resolving that.

The weakness is substantial: the paper already represents choice probabilities using a distribution over core utilities, so the proposed type-mass model is close to an existing mathematical object. It is a population interpretation the authors could recognize, especially for a team or electorate, but the paper’s distribution can instead describe a single decision-maker’s changing state across occasions. And the paper supplies no computational result to anchor the proposed complexity question. Under the stated anchor requirement, that prevents this from being a strong positive verdict.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with a mismatch: none of this paper’s named results is computational. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize preference relations through utility cones; Propositions 4–7 give conditions for weak stochastic transitivity. They state no algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or hardness result to mirror.

The proposed Continuous WST-Cycle Search is the closest candidate, but it adds an input-dependent computation to Proposition 4’s structural claim. Proposition 4 says when cycles cannot occur under particular assumptions; it does not ask for a cycle witness in a given society. More fundamentally, the paper already represents preferences with a probability distribution over core utilities, and its group interpretation treats those core preferences as team members. Section 4 studies WST under that distribution, and Section 6 connects it directly to Condorcet cycles. Giving the distribution finite rational support makes it an algorithmic input, but does not continuize the population: the population-as-distribution is already in the model. The same objection applies to a stronger version framed as a large electorate with repeated utility types.

That is a case against calling this a *continuous mirror*. It does not show that the search problem is uninteresting. The paper’s results do not settle its computational complexity, and the authors would likely recognize the cycle question as theirs. So the honest negative case is weak against the broader claim that ChoCo could study a worthwhile computational continuation; it supports only the narrower point that the paper offers no computational result for a population-continuization mirror to carry forward.
