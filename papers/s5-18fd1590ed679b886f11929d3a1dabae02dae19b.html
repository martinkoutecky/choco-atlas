What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers evaluating CELmin-style allocations across claim types. It does not mirror Proposition 1, Proposition 2, or a computational result in the ERDF application.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no valid named computational anchor in this paper. Its two numbered propositions are mathematical results about allocation rules, not results about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

Proposition 1, proved in the paper, says CELmin satisfies equal treatment of equals, anonymity, order preservation, resource monotonicity, supermodularity, order preservation under claims variation, and the Min lower bound. Proposition 2, also proved there, says CEA Lorenz-dominates \(\alpha_{\min}\), which Lorenz-dominates CELmin, which Lorenz-dominates CEL; it also says there is no Lorenz-dominance relation between CELmin and proportional allocation. Neither proposition establishes computational complexity. The paper’s numerical allocations do not supply a numbered complexity result either.

The strongest plausible mirror I can offer is therefore unanchored: computing the CELmin allocation for a high-multiplicity population of claimants. Imagine many local authorities applying to the same funding programme, with a moderate number \(\tau\) of distinct claim amounts. A type is a claim \(c\); its mass \(\mu_c\) is the fraction of applicants with that claim. Let \(e\) be the endowment per applicant, with \(\sum_c\mu_c c>e\). The task is to return each type’s per-applicant award \(x(c)\) under CELmin. Writing \(c_{\min}\) for the smallest claim and \(b=\min(e,c_{\min})\), the rule gives \(x(c)=e\) if \(e\le c_{\min}\); otherwise it gives
\[ x(c)=b+\max\{0,c-b-\lambda\}, \qquad \sum_c\mu_c\max\{0,c-b-\lambda\}=e-b. \]
This preserves the paper’s floor for every claimant and applies equal losses to the remaining claims. It is a plausible population-level version of the paper’s allocation problem, and the authors could recognize the rule being implemented. Sorting the claim levels gives a direct exact algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. But that algorithm is a consequence one can derive from the rule, not a result the paper states as a computational theorem.

So this candidate covers computation of CELmin allocations; it does not mirror either proposition’s axiomatic claim. Those propositions themselves concern axiomatic comparisons, which the programme places outside scope when the population remains finite. The weakest point is decisive: the paper’s contributions are axiomatic and applied, not computational. The proposed mirror is sensible as a new, likely easy computation problem, but it cannot support the requested anchor-based case for continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that the paper has no computational result to anchor a mirror. Proposition 1 is an axiomatic verification; Proposition 2 is a Lorenz comparison. The ERDF calculations apply explicit allocation rules to a fixed set of regional claims. None of these results studies the complexity of a computational problem.

The proposed high-multiplicity scenario is plausible: many standardized grant applicants could share claim types. But its formula changes the paper’s defining minimum guarantee. For \(N\) repeated claimants, per-capita claims \(c_i\), and per-capita endowment \(e\), the finite rule’s lower bound per claimant is
\[ \frac{\min\{Ne,c_{\min}\}}{N}, \]
which tends to zero as \(N\) grows. The faithful high-multiplicity limit therefore loses CELmin’s positive guarantee and reduces to CEL. If instead total endowment stays fixed as claimants multiply, each claimant’s award tends to zero.

One can rescue the guarantee by imposing a per-capita floor \(b=\min\{e,c_{\min}\}\), as the proponent does. That is a reasonable mass version, but it is a newly normalized rule rather than the limit of CELmin’s stated lower bound. Its allocation is then given by a weighted threshold over claim types, obtainable by sorting the support. That may be useful as an implementation exercise, but it supplies no paper result whose computational complexity or algorithmic structure ChoCo would be charting.

The negative case is strong for a paper-anchored mirror: the axiomatic results are outside the programme’s computational scope, and the fallback either loses CELmin’s distinctive guarantee or introduces a new rule with a direct evaluation procedure. It cannot honestly establish that no adjacent continuous allocation project could ever be worthwhile; its weakness is that broader claim, especially if ChoCo treats standalone rule-evaluation problems as sufficient.
