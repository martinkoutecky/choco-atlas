The continuous mirror question

Given a society \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) valuation types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\) and each type’s cumulative valuation \(F_t\) given by an arithmetic circuit, decide whether the resource \([0,1]\) can be divided using at most \(\tau-1\) ordered cuts into alternately labeled intervals \(A_+\) and \(A_-\), so that \(F_t(A_+) = F_t(A_-)\) for every type \(t\) with \(\mu_t > 0\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of valuation types, each type carrying mass \(\mu_t\) and a circuit-described valuation \(F_t\); the decision variables are at most \(\tau-1\) cut locations and alternating interval labels, and the feasibility constraints require exact balance for every positive-mass type.

The objection that survived

The mirror is computationally identical to the paper’s finite consensus-halving problem with one representative agent per type, so population shares themselves have no effect and the reductions do not establish hardness specifically for societies with \(N \gg \tau\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers exact fewer-cuts feasibility in Theorem 16 and exact search in Theorem 11; leaves the approximate consensus-halving results and the paper’s complexity-class results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of exact type-consensus-halving feasibility by support size \(\tau\) and valuation-circuit description length?

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible regime is a very large membership dividing a divisible common resource into two labeled bundles. Members’ valuation schedules over the resource fall into a relatively small number \(\tau\) of exact classes, even though the population \(N\) is enormous. A type \(t\) is its cumulative valuation function \(F_t\), represented by the arithmetic circuit used in the paper; its value for an interval \([a,b]\) is \(F_t(b)-F_t(a)\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with positive binary-rational masses summing to one.

The mirror keeps the paper’s fairness requirement: every member must value the two bundles equally. For a type of mass \(\mu_t>0\), this can be written \(\mu_t[V_t(A_+)-V_t(A_-)]=0\), which requires that type’s members to be individually balanced. The mass records the type’s population share; it does not turn consensus into a single average-utility constraint. There is no optimization objective, as in the paper: the task is exact search or feasibility. Since duplicate valuation types impose the same fairness constraint, the natural cut benchmark is \(\tau\), the number of distinct types.

My lead mirror is Type-Consensus-Halving Fewer-Cuts. Its instance is \((\mu_t,F_t)_{t=1}^{\tau}\). It asks whether there are at most \(\tau-1\) ordered cuts \(0\le x_1\le\cdots\le x_{\tau-1}\le1\) and signs for the induced intervals, such that every type values the union \(A_+\) of the positively signed intervals exactly as much as the union \(A_-\) of the negatively signed intervals. A solution is a yes/no answer; the witness for “yes” is the exact real cut vector and interval labeling.

This mirrors Theorem 16, printed as “\((n,n-1)\)-CH is \(\mathrm{ETR}\)-complete.” In full, the paper’s problem gives \(n\) agents whose cumulative valuation functions are arithmetic circuits and asks whether an exact consensus-halving partition exists with at most \(n-1\) cuts. The paper proves ETR membership and ETR hardness, reducing from bounded polynomial feasibility; it uses a cited result for the hardness of bounded feasibility. In the mirror, \(n\) becomes \(\tau\): the number of distinct fairness constraints sets the cut threshold. Every discrete instance embeds by giving each agent its own type, so ETR hardness transfers; membership follows from the same existential-real encoding. I expect this mirror to be ETR-complete, a Class B case.

A second, related question is Type-Consensus-Halving Search: on the same input, find a labeled partition with at most \(\tau\) cuts that balances every type exactly. This mirrors Theorem 11, “\((n,n)\)-CH is \(\mathrm{FIXP}\)-hard.” The underlying paper problem is to find an exact consensus-halving partition for \(n\) circuit-described agents using at most \(n\) cuts. The paper proves the hardness by reducing from the \(\mathrm{FIXP}\)-complete problem of finding a Nash equilibrium in a game with at least three players, using the cited Nash-equilibrium result of Etessami and Yannakakis. Giving each original agent its own type embeds those instances in the mirror, so I expect \(\mathrm{FIXP}\)-hardness to transfer. The paper’s Borsuk–Ulam containment construction also applies to the type-support instance. This is a second Class B mirror, though the paper does not establish an exact completeness classification for it.

I think the authors would recognize both as their problem: the valuations, bundles, and exact fairness condition are preserved, while the population is represented by the shares of members with each valuation. The \(\tau\)-cut benchmark follows the number of distinct constraints; repeated copies add no new fairness condition. This case covers these two exact consensus-halving results. It does not claim a population-continuous version of the paper’s approximate results or of its complexity-class contributions.

The soft spot is that the masses disappear from the feasibility constraints once their types are known. The hardness reductions also use instances where the number of types can equal the number of agents, so they do not show that a society with \(N\gg\tau\) remains hard. That leaves a useful follow-up: determine the complexity when the population is highly repetitive, and whether the \(\tau\)-type structure permits faster exact algorithms. The mirror remains a faithful fair-division question, but the paper’s hardness results alone do not answer that high-multiplicity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection applies to both anchors: in consensus halving, population mass has no effect on the fairness constraints. For a type \(t\), let
\[ \Delta_t=F_t(A_+)-F_t(A_-). \]
The faithful type-level condition is \(\mu_t\Delta_t=0\) for every type. Whenever \(\mu_t>0\), this is equivalent to \(\Delta_t=0\). Changing a type’s share from \(1\%\) to \(99\%\) changes nothing; only whether that type is present matters.

So the proposed fewer-cuts mirror is exactly the paper’s \((\tau,\tau-1)\)-CH problem after keeping one representative agent for each distinct valuation function. The search mirror is likewise \((\tau,\tau)\)-CH. Theorem 16 and Theorem 11 already concern those finite-support problems. The masses add no computational variables, and the cut thresholds \(\tau-1\) and \(\tau\) count independent fairness constraints, not population mass. In a large society with few valuation types, the paper’s finite-agent model can already be reduced to those representatives.

A more ambitious mirror could require balance for a continuum of valuation types. But then finite support is gone: the input must describe an infinite family of constraints, and the paper’s finite-agent cut guarantee does not specify the resulting problem. Alternatively, balancing only the population’s average imbalance, \(\sum_t\mu_t\Delta_t=0\), makes the shares matter but allows types to be unfair in opposite directions. That is a different fairness objective, not a continuization the authors would recognize as consensus halving.

This is a strong case against these two anchors as worthwhile *new population-continuous questions*: their faithful versions discard the population weights and coincide with the paper’s support-compressed problems. It does not establish the universal claim that no useful mirror exists. A plausible large membership with repeated valuation schedules is easy to imagine, and a separately motivated continuous-type model might be worth studying. The honest negative case is therefore strong on the proposed exact mirrors, but weak on ruling out every scenario.
