The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a credible case for a continuous mirror of the paper’s bulk freight problem, but there are no qualifying named result anchors in the paper. It gives models in Eqs. (19)–(30) and (39)–(49), and “Proof 1” justifies the proposed blocking-pair constraint. It does not state a numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result or a computed formulation. The CPLEX experiments—including the 500-by-200 instances—are evidence of computational performance on those instances, not a complexity classification. So I cannot honestly restate an anchor or claim that the paper establishes polynomial time. The paper’s related work cites Huang [54] on a fictitious continuum market for stable matching; that supports the modelling plausibility, but is not a result of this paper.

The lead candidate I would defend is continuous high-multiplicity 4PL bulk stable matching. Take a market with many recurring truck trips and bulk shipment requests on standardized freight lanes. A logistics type records every feature that affects its feasibility or preferences—route, weight and volume capacity, cost, charge, speed, and service score. A client type records its route, shipment weight and volume, and bid or market price. Types must be complete in the paper’s sense: agents of a type agree on all such attributes. In a plausible regime, the number \(N\) of trips and requests is large while the supports \(T_L,T_C\) of logistics and client types are much smaller: for example, repeated dispatches using the same vehicle specifications on the same lanes, and recurring orders with the same shipment profile. The paper’s own 4PL assignment setting is still the problem being solved; only counts become masses.

Formally, give the logistics types rational masses \(p_\ell\) summing to \(1\), and client types rational masses \(q_c\) summing to a market-size ratio \(\rho\). The input includes their finite type descriptions and the pairwise preference values \(a_{\ell c},b_{c\ell}\), calculated as in the paper. Choose a matching flow \(x_{\ell c}\ge0\), where \(x_{\ell c}\) is the mass of type-\(\ell\) truck trips assigned to type-\(c\) requests. Require \(\sum_c x_{\ell c}\le p_\ell\), and, for the bulk load cap in Eq. (43), \(\sum_\ell W_\ell x_{\ell c}\le W'_c q_c\). As in the paper, disallow assignments that violate the empty-loaded-rate bound or the provider-profit and client-savings conditions. Stability means there is no positive-mass blocking deviation: some truck mass prefers a client type to its current assignment, while a positive mass of that client type can accept it within its shipment cap, either using available demand or replacing less-preferred assigned capacity. The question is to find a stable feasible flow maximizing the paper’s scalarized preference objective, \(\sum_{\ell,c}x_{\ell c}a_{\ell c}b_{c\ell}\), corresponding to Eq. (49). An optimal stable flow is the solution.

I would expect this candidate to be tractable (Class A) if stability for the variable-capacity client types admits a polynomial-size type-level LP or an efficient separation procedure. There is one variable per supported type pair, so the support sizes—not the number of trips—would govern the model. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of the paper’s “linear model” claim: its matching variables are binary in Eq. (48), and extending its blocking constraint to mass flows needs its own proof. This mirror covers the paper’s bulk many-to-one assignment objective and stability conditions, not its entire one-to-one model or empirical claims. It also raises questions about existence of stable flows, finite-market rounding and stability loss, and when the discrete model’s solution can be recovered from the continuous one.

The soft spot is the stability definition under fractional mass. A type can be split across assignments, and a client’s capacity is tied to shipment mass; simply replacing the paper’s binary \(x_{ij}\) by \(x_{\ell c}\ge0\) does not ensure the resulting allocation is genuinely stable. That makes the algorithmic claim open, but the underlying mirror remains recognizable: it assigns recurring freight trips to recurring shipment requests using the same preferences, capacity limits, and no-blocking-pair idea as the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to continuize. The paper gives binary optimization models in Eqs. (19)–(30) and (39)–(49), proves that its proposed many-to-one blocking constraint works, and reports CPLEX runs. It does not state a complexity theorem or establish an algorithmic guarantee. So the proposed bulk-market question is a mirror of a formulation, not an anchor to a result as requested.

The paper’s own instances also give little evidence for a high-multiplicity regime. A type would need to preserve the features that determine feasibility and preferences: routes, capacities, shipment quantities, costs, prices, speed and service score. Exact routes matter because they determine the pair-specific distances used in the model. In the case study, the providers and orders have varied routes and quantities; in the experiments, parameters are generated per entity. Grouping them exactly would tend to leave support sizes close to the number of agents. Grouping by lanes, regions or attribute bands could create repeated types, but it would change inputs that drive the paper’s preferences and stability. The 500-by-200 formulation is already indexed by agent pairs, so the paper does not show that aggregation unlocks a new tractable formulation.

The proponent’s best version is a market of recurring, standardized trips and shipments. That is plausible, but it turns the paper’s snapshot of distinct routes and orders into a clone market. Its flow constraints can aggregate total load across a client type; stability must still specify whether each client in that cohort has a blocking provider given its own assigned load and capacity. Replacing binary pairings with mass flows does not establish that equivalence. Deriving it could be worthwhile work, but it would be a new model and a new result rather than a continuization of a computational result in this paper.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, but it does not support the universal claim. A repeated-lane market with genuinely identical agents is a credible high-multiplicity scenario, and a mass-flow version could preserve the paper’s assignment objective and no-blocking idea. The case against is strongest against the paper as a result-anchored candidate and against the specific mirror as currently formulated; it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario.
