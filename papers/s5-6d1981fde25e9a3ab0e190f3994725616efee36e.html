The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Section III reports simulation findings, including the persistence of \(1-R \propto 1/\sqrt{F}\); the paper attributes that relation to the earlier rule and does not prove a complexity or algorithmic claim about it. The proposed question—whether a threshold \(\Theta\) can achieve given bounds on committee size, representativeness, and integrity—is therefore a new optimization problem suggested by the plots, not a continuous version of a result in this paper. That defeats the closest thing the case for raises as an anchor.

The proposed online-community setting does not by itself fix the modeling gap. For the rule to be determined, a type would need to capture a person’s opinions, integrity assessments of their contacts, and the relevant interaction structure. In the paper’s sparse networks, particular ties determine who each person selects, which cycles form, and how votes accumulate along attached trees. Grouping people by opinions, degree, or distributions of neighbor types loses that information; including their actual network positions tends to make the types individual-specific. The obstacle is not that people cannot repeat opinions. It is that the rule uses the arrangement of their ties.

A stronger version could add a type-level interaction kernel and ask for the best threshold, or the least mass of strategic misreporters needed to lower committee integrity. That could be a worthwhile project, but it would require choosing a new network model and specifying how individual cycles and vote counts become mass flows. A dense interaction kernel changes the paper’s local contact mechanism; a sparse limit still depends on global features such as cycles and accumulated trees. Either way, the resulting computational question is an extension of the paper’s algorithm, not a mirror of a named result. In particular, an attacker’s effect can depend on *where* they sit in the network, so the fraction of affected people alone is not enough to specify the continuous problem.

This is a strong case against a direct, result-anchored mirror, but a weaker case for the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A carefully chosen block model or repeated network motifs, paired with an explicit optimization or robustness question, might produce a continuous problem the authors recognize. The case against is strongest if “mirror” means continuizing a computational result the paper actually establishes; it is less decisive if a new computational extension of the voting rule counts.
