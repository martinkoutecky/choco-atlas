The continuous mirror question

Given a finite preference DAG \(G=(V,A)\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\in[0,1]\) for each interchangeable item class \(v\in V\), and a rational threshold \(D\), decide whether there are real masses \(x_B\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq V\) such that \(\sum_B x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni v}x_B\le q_v\) for every \(v\), and \(\sum_B x_B\delta_G(B)\le D\), where \(\delta_G(B)=|\{v\in V:B\cap\operatorname{pred}[v]=\varnothing\}|\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass homogeneous population shares the preference DAG \(G=(V,A)\). Each \(v\in V\) is an interchangeable item class with rational per-capita supply \(q_v\); each unit of population receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq V\). Choose bundle masses \(x_B\ge0\) summing to \(1\), subject to \(\sum_{B\ni v}x_B\le q_v\), to minimize \(\sum_B x_B\delta_G(B)\).

The objection that survived

The mirror counts dissatisfaction by item class, so receiving one copy satisfies a class even though the paper counts distinct items; the paper does not itself establish that authors would accept this semantics.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10’s hardness result for one-way directed bipartite preference graphs of maximum in-degree \(2\). It leaves the paper’s polynomial-time results, including Theorems 8, 11, and 13–15, untreated.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead, and only anchor, is Theorem 10, proved in this paper as a shorter proof of a result previously given in [20]. It says that for every fixed \(k\ge 3\), deciding whether CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction has an allocation with total dissatisfaction at most \(d\) is NP-complete, even when the common preference graph is one-way directed bipartite and has maximum in-degree \(2\). Here “one-way directed bipartite” means every arc goes from \(X\) to \(Y\), so there is no directed path of length two. The paper’s reduction subdivides the edges of a graph \(H\); its resulting allocation instance has a good allocation exactly when \(H\) is \(k\)-colorable.

A continuous counterpart I find credible is Continuous Common-Graph Min-Sum Dissatisfaction. Its instance is a finite DAG \(G=(V,A)\) over item classes and a rational per-capita supply \(q_v\in[0,1]\) for each class \(v\). The population has mass \(1\) and one agent type: everyone has the same preference graph. A solution assigns mass \(x_B\ge 0\) to each integral bundle \(B\subseteq V\), with
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq V}x_B=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{B\ni v}x_B\le q_v\quad(v\in V). \]
For a bundle \(B\), define its dissatisfaction by
\[ \delta_G(B)=\bigl|\{v\in V:B\cap\operatorname{pred}[v]=\varnothing\}\bigr|. \]
The objective is to minimize \(\sum_B x_B\delta_G(B)\), or, in the decision version, ask whether this is at most a given threshold. Each individual still gets an integral bundle; the continuous variable describes how much of the population gets each bundle.

For the Theorem 10 reduction, set \(k=3\) and \(q_v=1/3\) for every item class. A discrete allocation to three agents maps directly to a continuous allocation by giving each agent mass \(1/3\); the continuous cost is the discrete total dissatisfaction divided by \(3\). The continuous question allows bundle masses beyond those three equal shares, so it is a genuine relaxation. The relevant regime is a large, homogeneous population receiving repeated copies of a standardized catalog: the item-class supply scales with population, while the shared preference DAG stays fixed. The paper itself motivates common preferences by group consensus or objective quality, so a single agent type is a natural fit.

The reduction makes the relaxation’s effect concrete. For each \(x\in X\), the lower bound on dissatisfaction is \(2/3\), and for each \(y\in Y\) with two parents it is \(0\). Attaining the sum of these bounds requires each \(x\) to be assigned to mass \(1/3\), with the recipient sets of adjacent \(x,x'\) disjoint; the remaining mass can receive the item \(y\) corresponding to their edge. Equivalently, the population can be distributed over independent sets of \(H\) so that each vertex appears with mass \(1/3\). This is a fractional-coloring condition, rather than ordinary \(3\)-colorability. That distinction is why the paper’s NP-hardness does not automatically transfer. My forecast is continuum-specific hardness: the fractional formulation has independent sets as its natural columns and maximum-weight-independent-set pricing as an apparent obstacle. That is a reason to expect hardness, not a proof; whether this threshold problem has a polynomial algorithm is one of the questions the mirror generates.

I think the authors could recognize this as their allocation problem with a high-multiplicity population and scaled supplies: the common preference graph and per-agent dissatisfaction remain central, while the individual bundles remain indivisible. The scope is deliberately limited to Theorem 10’s hardness result; I am not claiming a mirror for the paper’s algorithmic results.

The weak point is the item-class model. The paper counts individual items, whereas this mirror treats repeated copies as one class for each person’s dissatisfaction. That is plausible when copies are interchangeable and receiving one copy satisfies the relevant class, but it is a real modeling choice. If the authors require every physical copy to remain a separate vertex whose missing copies each count separately, this particular mirror will need revision or may fail their recognition test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 10 is a real computational anchor: it states NP-completeness for a named allocation problem, and its coloring reduction is explicit. So the negative case cannot rest on there being no result to mirror.

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes what counts as an item. In the paper, every vertex is a distinct indivisible object, and dissatisfaction counts each object the agent neither receives nor has dominated. Keeping those objects distinct while scaling the population does not give the proposed model: finitely many items serve a vanishing fraction of an atomless population. Scaling supplies requires repeated copies. But the preference DAG cannot express copies as equivalent objects: its strict partial order either leaves them incomparable or imposes an artificial order. Treating copies as one class, where receiving any copy satisfies the class, avoids that problem only by changing dissatisfaction from a count of items to a count of classes.

That change is not merely a technicality. It is what lets the coloring reduction become a fractional-coloring condition: the continuous model asks for a distribution of integral bundles in which each \(x\)-class reaches mass \(1/3\), with adjacent classes assigned to disjoint parts of the population. This is a coherent and potentially interesting problem. The question for the negative case is whether the paper’s authors would recognize it as their problem, rather than as a nearby high-multiplicity allocation model with a different satisfaction unit. The paper motivates shared preferences, but its objective is defined over distinct items; it does not supply a semantics for interchangeable copies.

That objection is narrow. A stocked catalog with many interchangeable copies and class-level dissatisfaction is a plausible scenario, and the programme explicitly permits supply and averaging changes forced by population scaling. If the authors accept that interpretation, the mirror survives: the fractional-coloring condition gives a clear computational question. The honest case against is therefore weak. The only substantial basis for rejecting this anchor is uncertainty about whether class-level satisfaction preserves the authors’ problem identity; I cannot make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
