The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a named computational-complexity result in this paper that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. Theorem 1, proved here, says GNSP cannot occur for Condorcet rules on single-crossing profiles. Propositions 2 and 3, also proved here, give sufficient conditions for GNSP under particular continuous-voter models. These are structural results, not results classifying a computational problem as hard or tractable. Section 4’s ILP and experiments are computational, but no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition states their complexity result. The introduction’s NP-completeness statement cites [17]; it is not a numbered result of this paper, and the text does not pin it to the exact one-dimensional STV problem. So I can’t honestly present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest plausible follow-up mirror is Minimum-Mass STV No-Show. Consider a large electorate whose voters share complete rankings over candidates arranged on a policy axis: each ranking is a type \(R\), and its rational mass \(\mu_R\) is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. Here \(n\gg\tau\): many voters, but relatively few distinct single-peaked rankings. Let \(a\) be the lexicographically tie-broken STV winner. Choose a possible beneficiary \(b\ne a\), and for each type choose abstention mass \(x_R\), with \(0\le x_R\le\mu_R\) and \(x_R=0\) unless \(b\succ_R a\). The residual society has masses \(\mu'_R=\mu_R-x_R\). Find \(b\) and \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_R x_R\), subject to STV selecting \(b\) from the residual society; report infeasibility if no such choice exists. This asks about the underlying abstention problem, not its ILP encoding. It tracks the paper’s GNSP definition and the objective in its Section 4 formulation, while making the abstaining group’s size a share of the population.

That is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem, but it is an extension rather than a mirror anchored by a named complexity result. The paper’s continuum voter model in Section 3 is also nearby: Proposition 2, for example, gives geometric conditions under which abstaining voters in \([0,\ell]\) changes the STV winner from \(a_3\) to \(a_2\). A mirror focused only on that interval condition would risk restating an analysis the paper already performs. The distinct question is exact optimization over rational masses and types beyond those particular geometric constructions.

For the unrestricted ranking version of Minimum-Mass STV No-Show, my tentative expectation is hardness from the combinatorics of the candidate-elimination order (Class B if the relevant discrete hardness carries over). I would leave the single-peaked restriction open: the propositions identify cases where abstention succeeds, but they do not classify that optimization problem. The main weakness is therefore also clear: the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and the class prediction is an informed conjecture, not a result established by its numbered statements.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an impossibility result for Condorcet rules; Propositions 2 and 3 give sufficient conditions for STV to exhibit GNSP. Section 4 supplies an ILP and Section 5 reports experiments, but neither classifies a computational problem’s complexity. The NP-completeness claim is attributed to prior work and is not pinned to this paper’s one-dimensional setting. On the programme’s strict anchor standard, the proponent has no qualifying theorem.

The geometric versions of Propositions 2 and 3 are also already continuous-population analyses: they model voters on \([0,1]\), reason about interval masses, and exhibit abstention intervals that change the winner. A mirror that merely restated those conditions would add little.

But the proposed Minimum-Mass STV No-Show problem is harder to dismiss. It closely tracks Section 4’s objective of minimizing abstentions, replacing counts with type masses. In a one-dimensional election, many voters can share each ranking, and a group’s size as a share of the electorate is meaningful. With fixed candidate positions, even arbitrary abstention sets reduce to choosing how much mass to remove from each ranking type. That is a coherent high-multiplicity problem, and the paper does not solve it.

So the strongest case against is that this would be a follow-up to an ILP and a set of structural results, not a mirror of a named complexity result; for the paper’s geometric constructions, the continuous population is already present. That is a real scope objection, but it does not establish that the follow-up is unworthy. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every scenario: the minimum-mass problem survives as a plausible one.
