The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind the brief requires: it proves no complexity classification or algorithmic bound. Its Theorems 1–3 are semantic correspondence results. The closest foothold is Theorem 2, proved here: when no attack targets an argument that is strictly preferred to its attacker, the extensions defined by the paper’s Deﬁnition 6 are exactly Dung preferred extensions, in both directions. That is a useful basis for a mirror, but it is not a qualifying computational anchor.

The strongest candidate is Mass Preferred-Extension Membership. Imagine a large deliberation system where many submissions instantiate a small set \(T\) of argument templates. A type records the complete argument role: its content, attack relations to other types, and preference status. The input is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), the type-level attack relation \(R\), an all-indifferent preference preorder, and a target type \(t^\star\). The question is whether some preferred extension contains \(t^\star\). A solution is an accepted-mass vector \(x\), with \(x_t>0\) exactly for the types in that extension. The extension must be conflict-free, defend each of its members, and be inclusion-maximal among admissible sets. At maximality, \(x_t=\mu_t\) for every accepted type and \(x_t=0\) otherwise.

A plausible regime is a large, structured online consultation that routes repeated submissions into a small catalogue of argument templates, each with the same attack and preference relationships. Then the number of submissions can be large while \(\tau=|T|\) stays small. Theorem 2 gives the semantic connection: in the all-indifferent case, the mass model’s type supports correspond to Dung preferred extensions. Authors working on preference-based extensions could recognize the underlying question, though the mass representation adds little to their semantics.

I would expect hardness to transfer when \(\tau\) is unrestricted: any finite argumentation framework can be represented by singleton types with rational masses, so the continuous encoding does not remove its graph structure. A useful further question is whether target acceptance is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\); direct enumeration over type subsets gives an immediate route to investigate that.

The weak point is substantial: exact argument types must share the same attack neighborhood, and maximal extensions accept all copies of a chosen type or none. Thus the masses do not soften the semantics; they mainly compress repeated vertices. Free-form debates often have nearly as many distinct attack roles as arguments, making the high-multiplicity regime less convincing. More fundamentally, because Theorem 2 is not a computational result, this is only a candidate positive mirror—not a case anchored as the brief requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The main difficulty is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–3 are semantic characterizations: they say when the proposed extensions coincide with stable, preferred, or grounded extensions. They do not analyze an algorithm or the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent acknowledges this about Theorem 2, but then proposes Mass Preferred-Extension Membership as a new problem rather than a continuous version of a result in the paper.

That membership question also loses the mass as soon as argument types are defined completely. Suppose several arguments have the same attacks and preferences. Under the paper’s no-self-attacks assumption, copies of one type do not attack each other. If a preferred extension contains one copy, it can include every copy: they have the same conflicts and attackers, so adding an omitted copy preserves admissibility, contradicting maximality otherwise. Thus each extension contains either all copies of a type or none. Its support is just an extension of the finite quotient framework. The target-type membership question depends on that quotient, not on the rational mass values. Theorem 2 supplies the all-indifferent semantic correspondence, but the distribution adds no continuous content.

The proposed FPT angle has the same limitation: once \(\tau\) is the number of distinct argument roles, enumerating subsets is parameterized analysis of the quotient graph. Singleton types likewise encode an ordinary finite framework; they show that the encoding can retain the original problem, not that population mass creates a new regime. Neither is a computational result of this paper.

A stronger proposal might weight extensions by the mass of the arguments they include, or ask what fraction of a population’s argument types is accepted across extensions. That could be an interesting adjacent problem for a large consultation. But the paper defines extensions through conflict-freeness, defense, and maximality; it never says that an extension should represent the largest share of participants. Selecting extensions by mass would add a popularity objective and change the semantics. Allowing fractional acceptance would require new rules for conflict and defense; treating fractions as a lottery over extensions would make the continuity probabilistic. Those may be worthwhile directions, but they do not provide a continuous mirror of Theorem 2.

The negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational anchor and the irrelevance of mass to extension membership. It is not airtight: a separate paper could give population weights a principled role in collective argumentation. The material here does not supply that role, and the proponent’s consultation scenario alone does not make argument multiplicity behave like voter mass.
