The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no positive anchor, and that is the strongest point in their case. This article has no theorem about an algorithm, complexity class, or hardness. Its Questions 1 and 2 are answered with finite coalition counts and voting-power calculations; Hypothesis 1 is assessed against historical data. Those are computational and empirical findings, but they do not give a prover a complexity result to continue. A continuous version of “compute the same indices” would introduce a new problem.

Question 1 is the closest candidate. The paper compares NBI, PPI, and PII for the \(27\) named Member States under two models. Its inputs and outputs remain state-specific: the model restricts which countries may oppose a Commission initiative, and the reported change in power is especially large for Germany and other populous states. Treating residents as the population would change the agents: citizens do not cast the Council votes. Treating states as the population leaves a small set of distinct members, not a high-multiplicity regime.

Question 2 has the same problem in sharper form. The paper reports counts of minimal blocking coalitions by number of states, including the treaty’s minimum of four members, and tracks which named states occur in those coalitions. In an atomless society, an individual’s pivotal influence disappears; “at least four members” also has no direct mass analogue. One could define type-level influence or blocking mass, but the paper’s coalition counts do not determine a canonical probability law or measure for those quantities. That would be a new model with new observables.

The verified hypothesis about Commission initiatives is also tied to distinct states and a finite Council: it concerns support from at least \(55\%\) of Member States and opposition by no more than two large states. Recasting those conditions as shares of a continuous population would change the hypothesis and its evidence. Taking many proposals as the population would continuize issues or time, while leaving the Council finite.

The strongest alternative is a large synthetic federation with many interchangeable voting members, perhaps retaining a few major states as atoms and representing small members by types. That could support worthwhile work on asymptotic or type-level voting influence. Its honest weakness is that it studies a different institution: the paper evaluates the power and blocking options of particular EU Member States under EU rules. A large-council extension is a possible new research lead, not a continuous mirror anchored by this article’s results.
