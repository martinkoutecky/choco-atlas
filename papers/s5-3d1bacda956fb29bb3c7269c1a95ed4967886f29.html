These are mechanism and axiom characterizations; neither establishes a complexity classification for a computational problem.

The most plausible population analogue would be to ask whether the same characterization holds for school allocation over a distribution of student types. The strongest repair is a district with many students in recurring cohorts, where a type records a preference order, merit tier, and minimal-access rights, and capacities are measured as population shares. One could ask whether deferred acceptance is the unique mass-allocation mechanism satisfying population versions of the paper’s properties. That is a coherent population model, but the question remains an axiomatic characterization—the kind of continuization ChoCo puts out of scope. Turning it into a question about computing the allocation would be a new algorithmic project, not a computational mirror of Theorem 1.

There is also a deeper mismatch in carrying the theorem’s axioms over. Its strategy-proofness and monotonicity are about an individual student changing her report or rights. In a nonatomic society, one person’s report changes no aggregate mass, so the distribution alone does not capture that person’s counterfactual assignment. Treating an entire type cohort as the deviator restores a nonzero change, but replaces individual incentives with group incentives. That changes a central property of the theorem; it does not preserve its anchor.

The exact model makes cohorting harder still: schools have strict priorities over individual students, so priority position distinguishes students who might otherwise share a type. Replacing those rankings with merit tiers and splitting mass at cutoffs may be a sensible repair, but it does not turn the characterization into a complexity result.
