What the mirror covers

The proposed limit concerns Theorem 13's large-population production and transfer outcomes; it leaves the finite-population mechanism characterizations, transfer restrictions, and participation-constrained results unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is a large-population public-project mechanism with two repeated preference types. A large city might have many residents with one of two standard valuations for an infrastructure project, with each type sharing the same endowment and other relevant characteristics. Then \(\tau=2\) even as the population grows, and the project’s cost should scale with the population: \(K(N)/N\to\kappa\). This is a natural population continuization; the project itself remains fixed and indivisible.

The closest anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper. It considers a sequence of economies with fixed low-type probability \(\pi\in(0,1)\) and \(K(N)/N\to\kappa\in(0,1)\). It proves that the feasible reduced-form production probabilities for the two types converge to the diagonal \(Q_1=Q_2\): for every \(\varepsilon>0\), eventually \(0\le Q_1-Q_2\le\varepsilon\). In the limit, both types have the same expected production probability. If
\[ \lambda\pi+(1-\pi)<\kappa\bigl[\lambda\pi v_1+(1-\pi)v_2\bigr], \]
the efficient mechanism has zero production; if the inequality is reversed, it has full production. At equality, every production probability is optimal. The paper also concludes that limiting transfers are equal across types: zero with no production, or \(\kappa\) per person with full production. This theorem is proved here, not cited.

A precise continuous problem suggested by that result is Continuum Public-Project Mechanism Optimization. The input is rational \(\pi,\lambda,\kappa,v_1,v_2\), with \(0<\pi<1\), \(\lambda>0\), \(0<v_1<v_2\), and \(0<\kappa<1\). The society has mass \(\pi\) of low types and \(1-\pi\) of high types. Choose a production probability \(q\in[0,1]\) and expected per-capita taxes \(a_1,a_2\). Maximize
\[ \lambda\pi(q-v_1a_1)+(1-\pi)(q-v_2a_2) \]
subject to budget feasibility
\[ \pi a_1+(1-\pi)a_2\ge\kappa q \]
and truth-telling constraints
\[ q-v_i a_i\ge q-v_i a_{3-i}\qquad(i=1,2). \]
Since an individual’s report cannot change the public project’s probability in the continuum, those constraints force \(a_1=a_2\). Feasibility and welfare then give \(a_1=a_2=\kappa q\), so the remaining problem is a one-variable linear optimization. It is tractable by an exact rational comparison, with the same threshold as Theorem 13.

This is a plausible object for the authors to recognize: their own large-economy section takes precisely this kind of limit, and the continuous problem captures its limiting reduced form. The proposed instance regime is the point: a large population with a small set of genuinely shared types, not every public-goods setting.

But there is a strict limitation under your anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 13 is a proved asymptotic economic characterization, not a theorem that an algorithmic problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. So it is the strongest positive anchor available in substance, but it is not a qualifying computational anchor. The resulting continuous optimization is also very simple; it does not expose the continuous-optimization toolbox or a complexity landscape.

That is the weakest point, and it is substantial. The continuum removes the finite population’s aggregate uncertainty and makes an individual’s report powerless to change production; the incentive constraints collapse to equal taxes. The authors may accept this as their large-population limit, but could reasonably say it no longer captures the computational problem of designing the finite Bayesian mechanism. Theorem 13 already supplies much of the limiting answer, which supports plausibility while leaving little new complexity question to ask. The natural follow-ups are the finite-\(N\) error near the threshold and whether the equal-tax collapse persists with more than two types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its theorems characterize interim-efficient mechanisms; none states the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent’s only substantive anchor, Theorem 13, is an asymptotic economic result about a sequence of finite economies. The repeated two-type population is a credible high-multiplicity regime, so I would not object to the mirror on population grounds. The problem is that the proposed continuous optimization does not uncover a computational question.

In that mirror, an individual report cannot change the production probability. Incentive compatibility therefore forces the two types to face the same expected tax. Budget feasibility then sets that tax to \(\kappa q\), leaving only the choice of \(q\): maximize
\[ q\bigl[\lambda\pi(1-\kappa v_1)+(1-\pi)(1-\kappa v_2)\bigr]. \]
That is a one-variable comparison. More fundamentally, the continuum removes the finite population’s aggregate uncertainty and the pivotal effect of an individual report—the forces that make incentive-compatible mechanism design the subject of the paper. The resulting problem is a public-project choice with known type masses, not a continuous computational version of its mechanism-design analysis.

The stronger candidate mirror would be to ask for an algorithm that computes an optimal finite-\(N\) mechanism from rational parameters, perhaps with \(N\) given compactly. But the paper’s two-type results already reduce production to a cutoff rule. Computing that rule is a direct threshold calculation, with a compact representation of the mechanism; it does not open a complexity landscape or call for continuous-optimization machinery. Generalizing to more types could create a computational project, but the authors identify that as an extension, not a result of this paper. In the atomless setting, individual reports still cannot change aggregate production, so that extension would need a new source of strategic interaction to avoid the same collapse.

The proponent is right that Theorem 13 makes this population limit natural and gives a precise continuous question. The negative case is weaker on the universal claim: one can imagine adjacent computational mechanism-design problems worth studying. But they would require changing the paper’s central problem or adding structure it does not analyze. As a mirror of this paper’s results, even the best anchor leads to a limit in which the computational object has disappeared.
