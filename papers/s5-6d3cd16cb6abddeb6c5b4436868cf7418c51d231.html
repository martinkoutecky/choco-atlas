The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational result: there is no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, proposition, or formulation theorem asserting a complexity classification. It defines and computes several optimization models, including Ordinal Aggregation (OA), but its reported results are experimental comparisons. The specialized formulations are attributed to cited work. So I cannot honestly supply a numbered anchor from this paper.

The strongest candidate for a continuous mirror is the paper’s underlying OA problem. Let \(V\) be a fixed set of images. A type \(t\) is a worker’s strict ranking \(a_t\) of a subset \(V_t\subseteq V\). The instance gives \(\tau\) such types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). The task is to find a complete ranking \(u\) of \(V\) minimizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\,d_{\mathrm{NP\ KS}}(a_t,u)\), using the paper’s normalized projected Kemeny–Snell distance. A solution is any minimizing ranking. This keeps the paper’s OA objective intact; only the worker list becomes a distribution over response types.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a platform repeatedly asking large crowds to rank the same small batches of images. With a fixed batch of \(k\) images, there are at most \(k!\) strict-ranking types, while the number \(N\) of workers can grow much larger. The paper’s randomized assignments across many subsets make this regime less evident in its own experiment, but the recurring-batch scenario is credible. The authors should recognize the mirror: it is their OA problem with each response weighted by its share of the crowd.

For this candidate, I would expect hardness to transfer when the number of images varies: complete rankings reduce the objective to Kemeny aggregation, and a discrete profile can be represented by rational type masses. That is an inference about the underlying problem, not a result established in this paper. The resulting questions include how complexity depends on \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length; whether small support enables useful algorithms; and whether incomplete rankings change the boundary.

The weak point is decisive for the requested anchor-based case: OA is a named model in the paper, but it is not a numbered computational result of the required kind. Also, in less controlled crowdsourcing settings, distinct assignments and responses may make \(\tau\) close to \(N\). So this is a plausible mirror of one computational object the paper studies, but it cannot be presented as a positive case anchored in a qualifying result from the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result of the kind the continuization programme is meant to mirror. Its Ordinal Aggregation (OA), Cardinal Aggregation (CA), COA, and SD models are optimization formulations; the paper’s own findings are experimental comparisons of accuracy and efficiency. The specialized formulations are attributed to cited work. There is no theorem or algorithmic classification here whose discrete-to-continuous counterpart the authors establish.

OA is still a plausible computational object. In the recurring-batch scenario, a type can include the worker’s evaluated subset and response, and the society can be a distribution over those types. But the resulting objective is population-weighted aggregation: for \(N\) workers with type counts \(n_t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) makes the continuous objective exactly \(1/N\) times the finite crowd’s summed loss. That identity makes a weighted OA problem well-defined; it does not supply a result from this paper to mirror.

The stronger candidate, COA, does not fix the gap. A type can include both the worker’s ranking and ratings, and the continuous objective can weight their joint losses by type mass. Yet this again turns the paper’s per-worker optimization into a weighted formulation. Its reported claim that multimodal aggregation improves accuracy, especially with fewer workers, is an empirical claim about finite crowds and their estimates. Studying the complexity of the weighted optimization problem could be worthwhile, but it would be a new computational study, not a continuous counterpart to a computational result this paper proved.

This is not a convincing universal case against a mirror. Crowds repeatedly working on the same tasks are a credible high-multiplicity setting, and the optimization problems are recognizable parts of the paper. The negative case is strong only under the requested paper-anchored test: the article offers no qualifying computational result to anchor it to. It does not honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous question could be asked.
