The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\) of partner types, and rational costs \(c_t(u)\), find a minimum-cost stable matching or report that none exists. A matching is a symmetric nonnegative matrix \(q\) with \(\sum_u q_{tu}=\mu_t\); define \(W_t(u;q)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}q_{tv}\), and require \(W_t(u;q)=0\) or \(W_u(t;q)=0\) for every pair \(t,u\). Determine the exact computational complexity in the support size \(\tau=|T|\) and input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

Use types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\) over types, and costs \(c_t(u)\). Choose a symmetric matrix \(q\ge 0\) with row sums \(\mu_t\), subject to \(W_t(u;q)=0\) or \(W_u(t;q)=0\) for every \(t,u\), where \(W_t(u;q)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}q_{tv}\). Minimize \(\sum_{t,u}q_{tu}c_t(u)\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population mirror I believe is the paper’s optimal stable matching problem. My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for a solvable Stable Roommates instance \(I\) with \(2n\) agents and minimum crossing distance \(k\), an optimal stable matching can be found in time \(2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}\); hence the problem is FPT parameterized by \(k\).

Consider a citywide roommate-matching service with many residents but relatively few preference profiles. A type records a resident’s complete preferences over partner types and their per-match costs. For example, profiles might reflect work schedules, quiet hours, pets, and location. There could be hundreds of thousands of residents but only dozens of supported types. Each \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). This is a single population with mutual compatibility, as in Stable Roommates.

Here is the continuous question: Type-Stable-Roommates-Opt. The input is a finite type set \(T\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\) of partner types, and rational costs \(c_t(u)\) consistent with those rankings. Find a minimum-cost stable matching of the population, or report that none exists. A solution is a symmetric nonnegative matrix \(q\), where \(q_{tu}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) residents matched to type \(u\). It must satisfy \(q_{tu}=q_{ut}\) and \(\sum_u q_{tu}=\mu_t\). Thus \(q_{tt}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) residents matched within their type; those matches use \(q_{tt}/2\) pairs. Minimize \(\sum_{t,u}q_{tu}c_t(u)\).

Stability has a direct mass-based definition. Let \(W_t(u;q)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}q_{tv}\), the mass of type \(t\) currently matched to someone it likes less than type \(u\). A positive mass of type \(t\) and a positive mass of type \(u\) can block each other exactly when both \(W_t(u;q)>0\) and \(W_u(t;q)>0\). So require \(W_t(u;q)=0\) or \(W_u(t;q)=0\) for every pair \(t,u\). This includes \(t=u\), requiring no residents to be matched below their own type if they would prefer a same-type partner. The fractional entries of \(q\) describe how cohorts divide among partner types; every individual still gets one whole roommate.

The authors should recognize this as their minimum-cost stable-matching question applied to exchangeable cohorts: the matching remains mutual and stable, and the objective remains aggregate partner cost. The discrete bridge is also concrete: a finite population with \(n_t\) residents of type \(t\) has \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). The continuous model allows cohort mass to split across partner types, so translating its solutions back to finite headcounts, and studying rounding and stability, are part of the research rather than automatic consequences.

I expect Class A parameterized by support size \(\tau=|T|\) as a first result. For each distinct pair \(t,u\), choose which of the two stability equalities \(W_t(u;q)=0\) or \(W_u(t;q)=0\) to impose. Every stable \(q\) is covered by at least one of the \(2^{\binom{\tau}{2}}\) choices, and each choice gives a linear program. This yields an exact \(2^{\binom{\tau}{2}}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\)-time algorithm, where \(L\) is the input bit length. Whether the dependence on \(\tau\) can be made polynomial, or whether hardness persists as \(\tau\) grows, is an immediate next question. Theorem 1’s minimum-crossing parameter \(k\) does not automatically transfer: this type-level model has no established analogue of the paper’s rotation poset.

The weakest point is that the paper uses strict rankings of named individuals, while this mirror ranks partner types and treats members of a type as interchangeable. That can change which outcomes are stable, and the paper’s rotation-based proof does not simply carry over. I think the mirror survives because type is a complete description of the preferences and costs used in the model, and the mass formulation preserves the paper’s central objects: one population, stable pairs, and minimum aggregate cost. I would not use the paper’s hardness result for Minimum Crossing Orientation as an anchor: its population would be rotations in an auxiliary poset, which continuizes the outcome structure rather than the society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1’s optimal stable matching result; the paper’s MCO theorem concerns an auxiliary poset, not the population. The theorem’s faithful population mirror runs into a real mismatch. In SR, each agent ranks named individuals, and blocking depends on which two people prefer one another. Under the programme’s definition, a type must include those preferences and costs. Grouping many residents is meaningful only when they are interchangeable across that whole description. Ranking partner *types* instead drops identity-specific preferences and introduces ties among people in a category. The proposed \(W_t(u;q)\) condition rules out positive-mass blocking groups; it does not preserve the paper’s individual-pair stability axiom in a nonatomic limit. Nor does the paper’s rotation-based parameter \(k\) automatically carry over.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror, though. The authors could study roommates with preferences over partner categories, weak stability, and an aggregate cost objective. Then \(q\) describes genuine cohort assignments, and the blocker condition has a clear interpretation. This is a changed preference model, but it remains a recognizable stable-matching question; the finite clone model gives it a concrete high-multiplicity bridge. Its complexity may be quite different from Theorem 1’s, but that is not grounds to dismiss it.

So the negative case is narrow: the proponent has not shown that Theorem 1’s result or its crossing-distance parameter survives aggregation. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The category-preference version looks like a plausible mirror, and the universal claim against every scenario is weak.
