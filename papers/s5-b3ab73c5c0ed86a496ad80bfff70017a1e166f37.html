The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror in large bargaining or insolvency settings: many standardized claimants, divided into a small number of classes with identical preferences and legal treatment. Represent the population by a finite-support mass distribution over claim levels, \(\nu=\sum_j \mu_j\delta_{q_j}\), where each \(\mu_j\) is a fraction of society. A continuum bankruptcy rule \(R\) assigns each class a per-person award \(a_R(q_j;\nu,E)\), with total awards \(E\). Universal Kantian deviation scales every claim by \(\alpha>0\), producing the distribution \(\alpha_{\#}\nu\).

One possible problem would be to characterize the distributions \(\nu\) that are Kantian equilibria for an explicitly represented rule \(R\): for every \(\alpha>0\), awards must satisfy
\[ a_R(\alpha q;\alpha_{\#}\nu,E)=a_R(q;\nu,E) \]
for \(\nu\)-almost every \(q\). This is a population continuization; the estate is already divisible, but the claimants become mass. The main modeling cost is that the paper allows each player to choose a separate claim, while a finite-support representation groups agents by claim level and would require an anonymous rule.

The nearest results in the paper are Proposition 4.2, proved here: equal treatment of equals guarantees that every equal-claims profile producing a bankruptcy problem is a Kantian equilibrium, with equal division. Proposition 4.5, also proved here: if the bankruptcy rule satisfies equal treatment of equals and weak no proportionality for unequal claims, there are no other Kantian equilibria. Proposition 4.1, proved here: under the proportional rule, every claims profile producing a bankruptcy problem is a Kantian equilibrium. These are equilibrium existence and characterization results, not computational results.

So the paper contains no eligible named computational anchor under the stated standard: no result asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithmic result of the required kind. The mass-distribution problem is a credible research question, but it is unanchored to a qualifying result in this paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive for ChoCo: this paper contains no computational result to continuize. Propositions 4.1, 4.2, and 4.5 are named equilibrium results, but they give no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem. They quantify over bankruptcy rules as mathematical functions; the paper does not specify an input representation for a rule or a running-time question. Turning one of these propositions into a computational task would require choosing both, so it would start a new project rather than mirror a result in the paper.

The proponent’s population model is nevertheless sensible. Take a probability distribution \(\nu\) over claim levels, define awards per claimant, and scale the estate per unit of population so awards do not collapse as the population grows. That handles the naturalness objection. Under this model, Proposition 4.2 concerns the point mass \(\nu=\delta_q\): equal treatment of equals and efficiency give every claimant the same per-person award, so scaling all claims leaves the outcome equal. The proposition carries over, but its continuous version adds no population-level phenomenon.

Proposition 4.1 carries over just as directly. Under the proportional rule, a claimant’s award is proportional to their claim relative to aggregate claims. Scaling every claim by \(\alpha\) preserves that ratio, so every distribution remains an equilibrium. Proposition 4.5 has the same shape: a continuous version of weak no proportionality, applied to positive-mass types, supplies a scaled claim distribution that rules out an unequal-division equilibrium. These are analogues of the paper’s existing scaling arguments. With standard rules and finite support, the distribution is a compact way to record claimant classes; it creates no new algorithmic question.

A stronger population model could give each claimant a complete type such as claim level and Kantian or Nashian behavior, then study equilibrium as the proportions of types vary. The paper itself suggests mixed Kantian and Nashian populations as future work. That could be a worthwhile economic game-theory question. It changes the game and asks for new results, though, so it does not rescue a continuous mirror of this paper’s results or supply the computational target ChoCo is looking for.

My negative case is strongest on programme fit, not on the claim that population models of bargaining could never be useful. The proposed finite-support mirror is coherent; the paper simply gives it no computational anchor, and its three closest results transfer through the same one-dimensional scaling argument. The mixed-population avenue is the honest weakness in a universal “no worthwhile research” claim, but it is a new research direction rather than a mirror.
