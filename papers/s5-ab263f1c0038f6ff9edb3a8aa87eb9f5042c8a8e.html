  • Theorem 2 — survived: The ordered arrival process can remain in discrete stages while each arriving cohort is a mass of interchangeable tenants.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite resource set \(C\), a finite set of complete tenant types \(T\), and a finite ordered sequence of \(K\) arrival stages, stage \(k\) brings a rational mass \(\rho_k\) of type \(t_k\). Type \(t\) has per-unit-mass resource demand \(d_c(t)\) and revenue \(v(t)\); resource capacities are one and costs are \(f_c(y)=q_c y\), under Theorem 2’s earning-density assumptions. At each stage, a deterministic causal policy chooses an accepted mass \(x_k\in[0,\rho_k]\), subject to \(\sum_{i\le k}d_c(t_i)x_i\le1\) for every resource \(c\) and prefix \(k\). What is the optimal worst-case competitive ratio against an offline policy that knows the full stage sequence, and does fractional cohort acceptance improve on Theorem 2’s ratio?

The model it lives in

A finite ordered stream of discrete stages carries rational masses of tenants drawn from a finite support of complete types. The decision at each stage is how much arriving mass to accept; welfare is accepted revenue minus linear resource costs, subject to capacity at every prefix. Arrival order remains discrete and known only as it unfolds, while population mass is continuous.

The objection that survived

The proponent indexes causal decisions by a continuum-valued arrival position, so that formulation continuizes arrival order as well as the population; retain a finite ordered stage sequence instead.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s online social-welfare guarantee for linear resource costs; it leaves aside the privacy guarantees, decentralized implementation, topology and VNF placement, and experimental results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a primal-dual posted-price policy attain the optimal ratio with running time polynomial in \(C\), \(|T|\), \(K\), and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I see is for the paper’s online social-welfare problem in a large market of small tenants using standardized slice packages. Imagine an MVNO serving many thousands of small IoT or enterprise service providers. Providers in the same type have the same complete relevant profile: resource demand, estimated revenue, service template, and any price or budget attributes used by the model. A market might have \(N\) tenants and only \(\tau\) such profiles, with \(\tau\ll N\). Each tenant still requests one slice; the continuum represents the fraction of tenants served from each profile.

I would name the mirror the Continuous-Tenant Online Slicing Competitive-Ratio Problem. Its instance has a finite resource set \(C\), a finite type catalogue \(T\), and linear resource costs \(f_c(y)=q_c y\). A type \(t\in T\) specifies its resource bundle \(d(t)\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{C}\) and estimated revenue \(v(t)\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), along with any other attributes that affect the paper’s decisions. Use per-capita units: mass is a fraction of the tenant population, and \(d(t)\) and \(v(t)\) are the resource use and revenue per unit of that mass. Resource capacity is one in these units. This is the natural high-multiplicity scaling in which each tenant’s individual slice becomes small as the market grows.

An instance also has a piecewise-constant arrival path \(\sigma:[0,1]\to T\), with rational segment masses. Its induced society is \(\mu_t=\lambda(\{u:\sigma(u)=t\})\), where \(\lambda\) is mass on the unit interval. The path records the original online order: at cumulative mass position \(u\), the algorithm sees the arriving type and the history, but not the future path. Thus the static population is a distribution over \(\tau\) types, while the online order remains part of the instance.

The policy chooses an acceptance density \(a(u)\in[0,1]\). This means selecting that fraction of the infinitesimal arriving cohort; each actual tenant still gets either one slice or none. Feasibility requires \(\int_0^u d_c(\sigma(r))a(r)\,dr\le1\) for every resource \(c\) and every prefix \(u\). The welfare is
\[ W(a;\sigma)=\int_0^1 v(\sigma(u))a(u)\,du -\sum_{c\in C}q_c\int_0^1d_c(\sigma(u))a(u)\,du. \]
The offline benchmark knows the full path and chooses a feasible measurable acceptance density. The problem is to find the optimal deterministic causal policy and its worst-case ratio \(\alpha^\star=\inf_A\sup_{\sigma:\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)>0}\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)/W_A(\sigma)\), or to prove matching bounds on that ratio. This asks the same welfare question as the paper’s P1, with the online arrival order retained.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that with linear costs and the paper’s positive cost-slope and earning-density-bound assumptions, DPoS attains the optimal competitive ratio among online algorithms. In the paper’s notation, its value is \(\alpha=\max_{c\in C}1/w_c\), where
\[ w_c=\left(1+\ln\frac{\sum_{c'\in C}(\bar p_{c'}-q_{c'})}{\underline p_c-q_c}\right)^{-1}. \]
Here \(\underline p_c\) and \(\bar p_c\) denote the lower and upper earning-density bounds; for linear costs, the initial cost slope is \(q_c\), with \(0<q_c<\underline p_c\). The continuous question is whether this same ratio remains optimal for a nonatomic stream, or whether the ability to select arbitrarily fine portions of arriving mass improves it. A candidate policy follows the paper’s primal-dual approach: maintain resource use, post resource prices as functions of that use, and accept the arriving mass when its value covers the bundle’s posted price. The expected complexity class is A: a finite type support and a price trajectory over \(C\) resources suggest a direct continuous online algorithm. The exact optimal ratio still needs proof.

The paper’s unnumbered introduction says resource allocation is algorithmically NP-hard, citing [9]. I do not use that as an anchor: it is not a numbered result proved here. Theorem 2 is the named computational result carrying this case, and the paper provides no comparable named hardness result to prefer.

This mirror covers the online social-welfare allocation problem and Theorem 2’s linear-cost regime. It does not cover VNF placement or topology constraints, nor does it claim that the paper’s privacy guarantees or decentralized implementation are themselves continuized. Two natural next questions are whether the continuum’s optimal ratio equals Theorem 2’s ratio, and whether the result extends to convex resource costs while retaining an efficient policy.

The weakest point is the scenario. The paper’s motivating tenants are business actors, and many such providers may be too large or too idiosyncratic to form a convincing high-multiplicity population. Standardized small enterprise or IoT tenants make the regime plausible, but it is a more specific market than the paper’s general narrative. There is also a mathematical wrinkle: nonatomic arrivals can be split exactly, so the discrete theorem’s optimality need not survive unchanged. That is the main question this mirror should put to a prover, not a reason to replace the underlying welfare problem with an easier one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Theorem 2 is about an ordered online stream, not a society specified just by its type distribution. Its guarantee is worst-case over arrival sequences, and the same masses of tenant types can produce different online outcomes in different orders. A static \(\mu_t\) therefore does not define the problem the theorem solves.

The proponent’s path \(\sigma(u)\) restores that missing order, but it adds a time-like coordinate and makes the policy a causal control over a continuum. An arbitrary piecewise-constant path also needs its number of segments as an input parameter; that number can grow with the number of tenants, leaving the online complexity intact. This is a real scope objection to that formulation: it does not get the theorem’s problem from a distribution over types alone.

A better mirror could keep time discrete and continuize only the population: at each of \(K\) arrival stages, a mass of tenants of each of \(\tau\) complete profiles arrives, and the online algorithm accepts some of that mass subject to prefix capacities. Its complexity could be stated in \(C,\tau,K,L\). That is well-defined, and the type masses and aggregate welfare are natural for a market with many small providers buying standardized slices.

That stronger version is hard to dismiss. P1 depends on aggregate revenue and resource use, not on tenant identities, and the paper’s tenant profile can be summarized by the attributes its optimization uses, such as \(d\) and \(v\). The stated NP-hardness in the introduction is only a citation, not another result to mirror; Theorem 2 is the sole anchor raised, and it is a named algorithmic result.

So the strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror blurs a population distribution with an online arrival process. But once the process is represented as discrete cohorts, a plausible high-multiplicity mirror remains. I cannot honestly defeat that anchor in every scenario; the case against is weak precisely there.
