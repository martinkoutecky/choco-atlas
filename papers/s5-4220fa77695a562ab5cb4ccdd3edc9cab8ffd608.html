The continuous mirror question

Given a finite school set \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of types \(t=(g_t,\succ_t,(\pi_{it})_{i\in C})\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_i\) with \(\sum_i\kappa_i\ge 1\), and a parameter \(\alpha\) with \(\alpha\sum_i\kappa_i\ge\sum_{t:g_t=D}\mu_t\), compute the mass assignment \(x_{ti}\) from type-aggregated student-proposing deferred acceptance: first assign \(D\)-mass against reserved capacities \(\alpha\kappa_i\), then assign \(F\)-mass against residual capacities. Determine whether the resulting assignment is fully allocated, group-stable, and \(\alpha\)-fair, allowing fractional allocation at priority cutoffs.

The model it lives in

Use complete types containing group membership, school rankings, and each school's priority tier; rational type masses \(\mu_t\); and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_i\). The decision variables are assigned masses \(x_{ti}\), with reserved capacity \(\alpha\kappa_i\) for \(D\)-types and residual capacity for \(F\)-types. There is no optimization objective: the task is to compute the two-stage mass deferred-acceptance outcome, splitting mass at tied priority cutoffs when needed.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its theorems establish properties of allocation mechanisms, but do not classify an underlying problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized tractable.

The nearest results are Theorem 8, proved in the paper: the \(\alpha\)-fair deferred-acceptance procedure yields a \(G\)-stable, fully assigned, \(\alpha\)-fair matching and is strategy-proof. Theorem 15, also proved there, says policy \(P1\)’s top-trading-cycles step is \(G\)-efficient, preserves each school’s numbers of \(F\) and \(D\) students, and weakly improves every student’s assignment. Theorem 16 says policy \(P2\)’s outcome is globally efficient. These are substantive mechanism results, but none asserts a computational complexity bound. Calling one an “in P” anchor would add a claim the authors do not make.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the paper: a large municipal school intake with relatively few supported student types. A type could specify a group, a complete school ranking, and priority tiers at each school; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s population share, and school quotas would scale with the intake. A natural new problem would take rational type masses, per-capita quotas, and \(\alpha\), then compute the aggregate assignment \(x_{ti}\) produced by student-proposing deferred acceptance: first assign \(D\)-mass against reserved capacity \(\alpha q_i\), then assign \(F\)-mass against the remaining capacity. Its solution is the type-to-school mass assignment. Aggregating proposals by type looks tractable in the number of schools, supported types, and input bit length.

That is a reasonable *new* computational question, not a mirror anchored by a complexity result in this paper. It also does not preserve Theorem 8’s incentive claim as stated: an individual in an atomless population has zero mass, so individual strategy-proofness risks becoming vacuous. The strongest honest positive case is therefore that the school-allocation setting has a credible continuous-population regime; this paper itself supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a mismatch with the programme’s target: the paper has no numbered result about computational complexity. Theorems 8, 15, and 16 establish properties of mechanisms—stability, fairness, strategy-proofness, and efficiency. They do not classify a computational problem as tractable or hard. So the proposed mass deferred-acceptance problem would be a new computational question suggested by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a complexity result it proves.

Theorem 8 is the best candidate. A large school intake with a small support of types—group, school ranking, and priority profile—is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Rational type masses and per-capita quotas give a coherent mass-assignment question. But the theorem’s strategy-proofness claim concerns an individual’s report; for a zero-mass individual that claim has no force. Replacing it with incentives for a positive-mass cohort or group would pose a different strategic problem. One can retain stability and ask for the aggregate assignment, but that is the new computation the proponent acknowledges, not a computational result of Theorem 8.

The other named results do not supply a stronger anchor. Theorem 15’s TTC step uses students’ preferences for exchanging places with other students. Preserving those preferences in types risks making the identities of the exchange partners part of each type; replacing them with preferences over type classes gives a new mass-exchange model. Either way, the theorem’s claims about individual Pareto improvements and school-by-school group counts are mechanism properties, not complexity claims. Theorem 16 has the same gap: a positive-mass notion of Pareto efficiency can be defined, but the paper’s global-efficiency theorem neither defines nor analyzes that problem.

This is a strong reason to reject the paper as a direct ChoCo complexity anchor. It is not a strong reason to claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists. The high-multiplicity school-allocation scenario is plausible, and an aggregate deferred-acceptance problem may be worth studying on its own. Under the programme’s stated standard, however, that possibility does not turn this paper’s mechanism theorems into computational results. My negative case is therefore strong on eligibility and weak on the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror.
