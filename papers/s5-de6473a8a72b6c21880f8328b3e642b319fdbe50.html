The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(G\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over bidder types with integer-valued monotone strong-gross-substitutes valuations, rational per-capita supply \(b\), and an integral starting price \(p^\circ \le p^*\), does the ascending process that repeatedly chooses an inclusion-minimal minimizer \(X\) of \(L_\mu(p+\chi_X)\) find the minimal clearing price \(p^*\) in exactly \(\lVert p^*-p^\circ\rVert_\infty\) updates, and yield a type-level clearing allocation?

The model it lives in

Types are complete bidder valuation schedules \(f_t\), each person receives an integral bundle \(x\), and \(\lambda_{t,x}\) is the fraction of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(x\). Require \(\sum_x\lambda_{t,x}=1\), \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_x\lambda_{t,x}x=b\), and positive \(\lambda_{t,x}\) only on bundles demanded at price \(p\). The price process minimizes the mass Lyapunov function \(L_\mu(p)=\sum_t\mu_t\max_x(f_t(x)-p\cdot x)+b\cdot p\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the minimal ascending-price bound in Theorem 4.7 and the two-phase minimum-price bound in Theorem 4.12; it leaves the other price-selection variants and the unit-demand comparisons aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the minimizing-set steps and a clearing allocation be computed in time polynomial in the support size and binary input length, without dependence on the numeric price gap, under a specified valuation or demand-oracle model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Type-Mass Ascending Auction. Take a finite set of goods \(G\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) bidder types, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type is a complete valuation schedule: every bidder of type \(t\) has the same integer-valued, monotone strong-gross-substitutes valuation \(f_t\) over integral bundles \(B_t\). Let \(b\in\mathbb Q_+^{G}\) be the supply per capita. For each type, \(\lambda_{t,x}\) is the fraction of that type assigned bundle \(x\in B_t\). A market clears at prices \(p\) if
\[ \sum_{x\in B_t}\lambda_{t,x}=1,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{x\in B_t}\lambda_{t,x}x=b, \]
and \(\lambda_{t,x}>0\) only when \(x\) maximizes \(f_t(x)-p\cdot x\). Thus each person receives an integral bundle; only the type-level allocation can be fractional. The price objective is the mass Lyapunov function
\[ L_\mu(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max_{x\in B_t}\bigl(f_t(x)-p\cdot x\bigr)+b\cdot p. \]
The input includes exact demand access for each type and promises a feasible market satisfying the paper’s assumptions.

The scenario I find convincing is a large platform allocating standardized bundles of capacity—such as compute or energy products—to many small organizations. Many buyers may share a complete substitution schedule because they face the same contract and service options, while differing schedules form a much smaller set of types. A population of hundreds of thousands of buyers with hundreds or thousands of types is plausible there. Supply scales with population, and the mass allocation records how each type is split among integral bundles. This is a continuization of the buyer population; it does not make individual bundles divisible.

The anchor is Theorem 4.7, proved in this paper. It says that, under the paper’s assumptions, if AscendMinimal starts at an integral price vector \(p^\circ\le p^*\), where \(p^*\) is the minimal integral equilibrium price, it outputs \(p^*\) after exactly \(\lVert p^*-p^\circ\rVert_\infty\) price updates. The continuous question is: given the type masses, per-capita supply, valuations, and a promised lower-bound initial price, does the analogous process—at each step taking the inclusion-minimal set \(X\subseteq G\) minimizing \(L_\mu(p+\chi_X)\), then updating \(p\leftarrow p+\chi_X\)—find the minimal clearing price in exactly \(\lVert p^*-p^\circ\rVert_\infty\) updates? A solution is \(p^*\) and a type-level clearing allocation \(\lambda\). I expect the exact update bound to carry over: rational masses and supplies can be scaled to a finite replicated market, and the weighted Lyapunov function preserves the relevant discrete-convex structure. For computing each update, the step objective is submodular in \(X\); with exact rational arithmetic and type-demand access, this points toward support-sensitive oracle algorithms. The result would be output-sensitive, however: the number of updates depends on the numeric price gap, which can be large relative to its binary encoding.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 4.12, also proved here. It says that TwoPhaseMinMin, starting from any integral price vector \(p^\circ\), terminates at \(p^*\); its ascending phase uses at most \(\eta(p^\circ,p^*)\) updates and its descending phase at most \(2\eta(p^\circ,p^*)\), where
\[ \eta(p,q)=\lVert p-q\rVert_\infty^+ + \lVert p-q\rVert_\infty^-, \]
with the two terms the largest positive and negative coordinate differences. Its continuous mirror asks whether the same mass market admits that two-phase process and bounds: ascend using inclusion-minimal minimizers of \(L_\mu(p+\chi_X)\), then descend using maximal minimizers of \(L_\mu(p-\chi_X)\), returning the minimal clearing price from any integral start. I expect the same bounds, again with the qualification that they count auction updates rather than guaranteeing polynomial total time in the bit length. A natural follow-up is whether a scaling or ellipsoid method can find the desired price with a bit-polynomial bound, and what demand-oracle assumptions that requires.

The authors should recognize both as their problems: the goods, gross-substitutes valuations, anonymous linear prices, demand sets, equilibrium condition, and Lyapunov objective remain intact. What changes is that identical bidders are represented by type mass, with supply expressed per capita. The paper’s rational, integer-valued setting makes this especially credible: rational masses can be scaled to high-multiplicity finite markets, while the normalized description exposes dependence on \(\tau\) instead of the replicated population count.

My weakest point is that the paper proves iteration bounds, not bit-polynomial running times for the full auction. Its valuations are also unrestricted enough that the cost of obtaining each type’s demand set is not analyzed. So I would not present this mirror as showing that the paper’s whole auction becomes polynomial in the continuous model. The positive case is narrower: this is a well-posed, support-compressed high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s central price-adjustment results, with a precise output-sensitive bound and a clear next question about total computational complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors reduce to one result. For rational type masses and rational per-capita supply, choose a common denominator \(D\) and make a finite market with \(D\mu_t\) copies of each type and \(Db\) units of supply. Its Lyapunov function is

\[ L_D(p)=\sum_t D\mu_t V_t(p)+Db\cdot p=D L_\mu(p). \]

Multiplying by \(D\) changes none of the minimizing price sets or update choices. Theorem 4.7 therefore gives the stated ascending bound for the mass market, and the same replication carries Theorem 4.12’s two-phase bound over. Both mirror claims are exact consequences of the paper’s finite-market theorems; neither requires a new continuous argument.

That leaves a problem with the proponent’s case: these theorems count price updates, not time in the binary input length. The number of updates can grow with the numeric price gap, while the rational masses may encode a replica count \(D\) that is enormous. The proposed bounds do not establish that a compressed type list makes the full auction efficient. A sharper mirror would ask for support-sensitive bit complexity, with explicit valuation or demand-oracle access, and perhaps an algorithm that avoids dependence on the price gap. That is a more substantial question, but its input model and algorithmic target go beyond the results these anchors state.

This is the strongest case against prioritizing these particular mirrors: their advertised exact bounds are inherited by clearing denominators, and they do not settle the more consequential complexity question. But it does not defeat the better version. Repeated bidder types in large markets are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and support-sensitive computation could be worth studying. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror; the negative case is weak precisely where the paper’s price adjustment structure aggregates cleanly by type.
