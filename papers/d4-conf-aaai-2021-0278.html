The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named anchor in this paper: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing that a computational problem is in a complexity class or has an algorithmic guarantee. MPES, introduced in Section 4, is the closest object: it defines a mutual-information acquisition function and evaluates it experimentally. That is relevant to a mirror, but it is not a named complexity result. So I can offer a concrete follow-up question, but not an anchor-backed case that this paper’s results warrant a continuous mirror.

The most plausible scenario is a large consumer study: many people compare product designs, recipes, or other options, and their preferences fall into a small number \(\tau\) of stable segments. Each segment is a type: its members share the same latent utility function \(f_t\) over designs and the same response model, including its noise and tie behavior. The segment masses \(\mu_t\) are rational fractions summing to one. The regime is many respondents \(n\) with \(\tau \ll n\); each person’s noisy top-\(k\) response remains individual, while the continuum observation is the mass of respondents in each response category. The paper’s SUSHI experiment, whose target uses average ratings across 5,000 users, offers a foothold for this population-level interpretation.

A precise candidate is Population-MPES Query Selection. An instance gives a finite design set \(X\), a known type distribution \(\mu\), a joint posterior over the type utilities \(f_t(x)\), and query size \(r\) and ranking depth \(k\). Define population utility by \(F_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t f_t(x)\), and let \(x^\star\) be its maximizer, with ties broken by a fixed rule. For each proposed query \(C\subseteq X\), \(|C|=r\), members of type \(t\) rank the designs in \(C\) according to the paper’s Plackett–Luce model. In the continuum, the observation is the vector of type-level response masses \(\rho_{t,o}=\mu_t p(o\mid f_t,C)\), over types \(t\) and possible top-\(k\) rankings \(o\). The problem is to choose \(C\) maximizing \(I(x^\star;\rho_C\mid D)\), the information the population response gives about the best design, and return an optimal query—or, for a specified \(\varepsilon>0\), one within \(\varepsilon\) of optimal. Its computational parameters include \(|X|,\tau,r,k\), the posterior’s encoding length, and \(\varepsilon\).

This mirrors the paper’s central query-selection objective while making the society, rather than the design space, the continuous object. The natural initial complexity conjecture is that the query-selection combinatorics may retain hardness from finite batch selection as \(|X|\) and \(r\) grow; any tractability or continuum-specific hardness would need to be established. The question covers MPES-style joint query selection, not the paper’s variational-inference procedure or its empirical performance claims.

The weak point is substantial: the proposed population readout changes the paper’s single noisy ranking observation into exact type-level response shares, and the target becomes population utility. The paper does not analyze this version. Its authors might recognize it as a natural population extension of preference-based BO, but its experimental results do not establish that the computational question is tractable, hard, or even equivalent to their original one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no theorem, lemma, or corollary here to mirror as a computational result. The paper names MPES and reports its empirical performance, but gives no complexity classification or exact, approximation, or parameterized guarantee for a computational problem. Even if MPES itself is treated as the anchor, its central object is choosing inputs to query an unknown function—not computing an outcome from a society’s preferences.

The consumer-panel scenario is plausible, and SUSHI gives it a foothold. But the paper’s 5,000 ratings are used to construct a benchmark objective; they are not a population whose ballots the BO algorithm aggregates. In the paper, one oracle supplies a noisy ranking, and the algorithm uses it to learn a single objective \(f\). Treating many respondents as repeated noisy oracles makes population size a feature of the observation protocol: with infinitely many responses, the response frequencies reveal choice probabilities. If respondents have different utility functions and the target is \(F_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t f_t(x)\), the target itself has changed to a social-welfare objective. That could be a worthwhile follow-up, but its computational problem comes from the new panel model and aggregation objective, not from a result this paper established.

The proposed \(\rho_{t,o}=\mu_t p(o\mid f_t,C)\) makes the gap especially clear. With type labels and known positive \(\mu_t\), dividing by \(\mu_t\) recovers each type’s response probabilities. The continuum therefore supplies exact per-type response distributions; it does not preserve the finite panel’s sampling problem. An anonymous aggregate \(\rho_o=\sum_t\mu_t p(o\mid f_t,C)\) would make the mixture matter, but then the mirror requires a new heterogeneous BO model and a new acquisition-complexity question. The hard part would be those additions, not continuizing the society underlying MPES.

So the strongest case against is a mismatch of objects: this paper has no society-dependent computational problem whose complexity its results chart. A large preference panel could motivate new research, perhaps even research adjacent to continuous social choice, but calling it a continuous mirror of this paper overstates the connection. The negative case is strongest on that scope and provenance point; it cannot honestly establish that no panel-based question could ever be valuable.
