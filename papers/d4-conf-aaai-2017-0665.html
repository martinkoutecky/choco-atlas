What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror extends the iterative quality and ability updates in Algorithms 1 and 2 to type masses; it does not mirror the experimental performance claims, and there is no named theorem to cover.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the fixed-round updates be computed to additive precision in time polynomial in the artifact count, type support sizes, input bit length, number of rounds, and precision, under explicit degeneracy and conditioning assumptions?
  • Do the mass-weighted updates converge, and under what conditions do their limits approximate estimates from finite worker populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no admissible named anchors in this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. Algorithms 1 and 2 give the Pairwise HITS and Two-Stage Pairwise HITS procedures; Table 3 reports experimental performance. Those are relevant to a mirror, but they do not meet the stated anchor requirement. So there is no per-anchor problem to give, and I can’t honestly claim that this paper establishes a hardness result that might dissolve under continuization.

The strongest positive case I see is a new, unanchored mirror of the paper’s quality-estimation task. Consider a large peer-assessment or content-production platform with many creators and evaluators working on recurring, standardized task families. A possible regime has thousands of workers but tens or hundreds of stable creator and evaluator profiles. A type would include the task and assignment profile and the creator’s production pattern or evaluator’s pairwise response model. The input would give each type’s population mass as a binary rational. This is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario for the paper’s model, though it depends on the profiles genuinely being shared.

One precise candidate is Continuous Two-Stage Pairwise HITS-\(H\), where \(H\) is a specified number of update rounds. The instance has a finite task set and artifact set \(A_d\) for each task \(d\); creator types \(k\) with masses \(\nu_k\) and artifact-production shares \(g_{ka}\); and evaluator types \(i\) with masses \(\mu_i\). For each compared pair \(e=(a,b)\), \(\rho_{ie}\) is the share of type \(i\) assigned to that pair, and \(p_{ie}\) is that type’s probability of preferring \(a\). Each artifact has a creator type \(\kappa(a)\). The masses, shares, probabilities, and \(\lambda\) are rational input; the type supports are \(\tau_C\) and \(\tau_E\).

At round \(h\), set

\[ b_e^{(h)}=\sum_i\mu_i\rho_{ie}r_i^{(h)}(2p_{ie}-1), \]

and find the minimum-norm least-squares score vector \(q^{*,h}\), centered separately for each task, fitting

\[ q_a^{*,h}-q_b^{*,h}\approx b_e^{(h)} \]

over compared pairs. Then update artifact scores by

\[ q_a^{(h+1)}=(1-\lambda)q_a^{*,h}+\lambda c_{\kappa(a)}^{(h)}. \]

Update each evaluator type’s ability from its expected fraction of correct comparisons under \(q^{(h+1)}\), and each creator type’s ability from its production-share-weighted mean artifact score, \(\sum_a g_{ka}q_a^{(h+1)}\). Normalize the ability vectors using the population-weighted \(L^2\) norms, as the paper normalizes its individual-worker vectors. The solution is the score vector after \(H\) rounds, to additive precision \(\varepsilon\), and its induced ranking within each task. Ties, zero norms, and initialization would need explicit conventions; they are not settled by the paper.

I would expect this fixed-round version to be Class A: each round uses sums over type supports and least-squares linear algebra, so its runtime should be polynomial in the artifact count, \(\tau_C\), \(\tau_E\), \(H\), the input bit length \(L\), and the precision requested, subject to ordinary numerical-conditioning bounds. It covers the estimation procedures in Sections 3–4; it does not continuize artifacts or outcomes. Further questions include whether these updates converge, whether their limits are unique, and when estimates from a discrete worker population approximate the mass-based estimates.

The paper gives some reason to care about this mirror: Table 3 reports that Two-Stage Pairwise HITS significantly outperformed the compared methods across its three datasets, particularly with few evaluators per pair. But that is experimental evidence, not a complexity anchor.

My weakest point is that the fixed-round question is largely the paper’s estimator with worker sums replaced by population-weighted sums. Its tractability does not show that continuization resolves a hard computational problem. Also, the paper’s complete behavioral types could have large support if each type must encode responses to every artifact pair. Finally, the printed algorithms initialize abilities at zero and normalize after updates; in degenerate cases that normalization is undefined. A continuous version needs a well-defined initialization and tie convention, which makes the proposal recognizable as the authors’ quality-estimation problem but not a theorem-backed mirror of a named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. The paper introduces two quality estimation algorithms and reports experiments; it does not state a theorem about their complexity, prove an algorithmic guarantee, or establish hardness. The proponent is right not to present the experimental results as complexity anchors.

The strongest candidate is therefore a new population version of the estimator. Keep the artifacts finite and give evaluator profiles and creator profiles rational masses, with each profile specifying its comparison behavior or production pattern. That can be a coherent model of a large platform, and fractional production shares or mass-weighted normalization are reasonable adaptations. Zero norms and tie conventions are specification details, not fundamental objections.

The case against is about what that model would add. The paper’s updates use workers only through their contributions to a finite comparison system: weighted sums, least-squares solutions, and averages over artifacts. Once profile masses and response rates are supplied, the continuous version performs those same operations on an aggregated input. The growing population is not the combinatorial object the algorithms search over; the finite artifact and comparison structures are. So the mirror offers no evident new separation problem, optimization method, or complexity landscape. Its running time would be the cost of weighted finite linear algebra, rather than a tractability result unlocked by treating the population as continuous.

That is a reason to regard the mirror as a modest extension, not proof that it is meaningless. A fixed-round estimator is still a computational question, and a low-support workforce profile could be a sensible regime. One could also ask about convergence or the error from replacing individual workers with a type distribution, but those questions move toward algorithm dynamics or statistical estimation rather than the programme’s central complexity questions. The paper does not supply a named result to anchor them.

So the negative case is strong against an anchor-based claim: there are no eligible anchors. It is weaker against the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The evaluator-population version is coherent; the honest objection is that its computational payoff is unclear and appears to come from aggregation of an existing estimator, not from a new complexity phenomenon.
