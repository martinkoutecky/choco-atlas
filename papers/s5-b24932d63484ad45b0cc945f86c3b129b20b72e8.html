The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s persuasion problem, but it does not meet your named-anchor requirement: the paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity classification, algorithmic running time, approximation guarantee, or parameterized result. Its propositions characterize optimal experiments and welfare; they do not establish computational complexity. So there is no qualifying anchor to list.

The closest named result is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. For a finite electorate under a \(k\)-voting rule, assuming \(p\notin\operatorname{co}(W_k)\) and \(W_k\ne\varnothing\), an optimal experiment can be composed of two stages: a binary *collective* experiment whose posteriors \(q^-\in R_k\) and \(q^+\in\operatorname{co}(W_k)\) lie with \(p\) on one line and maximize \(d(p,q^-)/d(p,q^+)\); then, after the approval-favoring signal, a *targeted* experiment whose every resulting posterior lies in \(W_k\). The latter can persuade different winning coalitions on different realizations. This is a useful structural characterization, but it says nothing about the time needed to find that experiment.

A natural continuous version would use a large referendum population with a small number of recurring preference types. Let \(\Theta\) be the finite state set; let the input give a full-support rational prior \(p\), a rational quota \(\alpha\), and types \(\delta^1,\ldots,\delta^\tau\in\mathbb Q^\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_j\ge0\) summing to one. Each type is a complete state-contingent net-utility vector, and mass \(\mu_j\) is the fraction of voters with that vector. At posterior \(q\), type \(j\) approves exactly when \(\langle q,\delta^j\rangle\ge0\), so the proposal passes when
\[ A_\mu(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\mathbf 1[\langle q,\delta^j\rangle\ge0]\ge\alpha. \]
The problem is: given this instance, compute the maximum probability of passage and an optimal finite public experiment, represented by posterior beliefs \(q_s\) and probabilities \(\lambda_s\), subject to \(\sum_s\lambda_s q_s=p\). The objective is \(\sum_s\lambda_s\mathbf 1[A_\mu(q_s)\ge\alpha]\).

This is author-recognizable: it preserves the paper’s politician, common posterior, public experiment, voting decision, and passage objective, replacing voter counts with type masses. For rational inputs, clearing denominators recovers an exact finite electorate with the same quota and outcomes. A plausible regime is millions of voters but \(\tau\) preference profiles, where preferences are induced by a small set of recurring views about the policy’s possible states. The proposed mirror covers the optimal-experiment question, including collective and targeted persuasion; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s welfare propositions as computational results.

If studied, I would tentatively expect tractability when \(|\Theta|\) is fixed: the type approval hyperplanes partition the posterior simplex into polynomially many cells in \(\tau\), suggesting an arrangement-and-optimization approach. Tie faces and exact finite-support output would need proof; with variable \(|\Theta|\), I would leave the complexity open. Further questions include the exact dependence on \(|\Theta|,\tau,L\) and the support size needed for an optimal experiment.

The weak point is decisive for this assignment: even if that is a sensible mirror, the paper supplies no computational anchor for it. Proposition 3 motivates the optimization problem but cannot be presented as an in-\(\mathrm P\), hardness, or other complexity result. So this is a promising unanchored follow-on question, not a compliant positive case built on the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the missing computational anchor. This paper states no complexity classification, algorithm, running time, or approximation result. Proposition 3 is the nearest candidate, but it characterizes an optimal experiment through collective and targeted persuasion; it does not establish a computational result. So the proposed mass-voter problem is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. It is a new algorithmic question built around one of the paper’s economic characterizations.

That distinction is decisive under the stated anchor requirement, but it does not defeat the proposed model itself. A large referendum with recurring preference types is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Treating each type as a complete utility vector and its mass as a population fraction preserves what the voting model uses, and the result remains recognizable as public persuasion under a voting quota.

There is also close computational prior art: algorithmic work on public Bayesian persuasion studies \(k\)-voting with explicitly represented receivers and gives approximation algorithms and hardness results. That makes the proposed question less isolated, but it is not an “already done continuously” collision: those papers take a finite receiver list as input, rather than a rational distribution over voter types.
