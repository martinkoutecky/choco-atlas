The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(\Theta\) with rational arrival masses \(\mu_{\theta,t}\), where each type fixes its timing constraints, rate limit, maximum integral quantity, and valuation \(v_\theta(k)\), does there exist a causal allocation policy and a constant \(p>0\) such that \(p\,SW_{\mathrm{online}}\ge SW_{\mathrm{offline}}\) on every instance? The policy may split each type's mass among schedules, but each individual receives an integral number of units per period and past allocations cannot be revoked. At time \(t\), it observes the actual nondecreasing marginal procurement cost curve \(c_t(q)\); future curves are unknown but subject to the instance's stated forecasts and upper bounds. Procurement of mass \(q\) costs \(\int_0^q c_t(z)\,dz\), and the offline benchmark has the same feasibility constraints and knows all future arrivals and costs.

The model it lives in

A finite set of complete consumer types carries rational mass over arrival times. A type fixes each individual's timing constraints, rate limit, integral bundle options, and valuation; the policy assigns portions of that mass to feasible integral schedules. Aggregate procurement has a nondecreasing marginal cost curve, revealed as time advances, and welfare is mass-weighted value minus total procurement cost. Compare the best causal policy with the offline optimum under the same constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5's welfare guarantee only; it leaves the incentive and cancellation characterizations, the proposed mechanisms, and the simulation results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the impossibility persist for randomized policies evaluated by expected welfare?
  • Which restrictions on future-cost uncertainty or complementary valuations permit a positive competitive factor?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s online welfare guarantee problem, anchored only in Theorem 5, proved in Appendix C. In the paper’s setting, the offline benchmark knows all future arrivals and procurement costs. The theorem says that no online allocation mechanism has a bounded competitive ratio: there is no constant \(p>0\) such that, for every reported type profile and allowed cost sequence, \(p\,SW_Y \ge SW_{\mathrm{OPT}}\). Its proof uses one agent who values receiving two units but not one. The mechanism must decide whether to allocate the first unit before learning the second period’s cost: skip it, and a low second-period cost leaves the mechanism unable to complete the pair; allocate it, and a high second-period cost makes the allocation welfare-negative while the offline optimum allocates nothing.

A continuous mirror is Mass-Online Procurement Competitive Ratio. An instance has a finite set of complete consumer types \(\Theta\), with a rational mass \(\mu_{\theta,t}\) of type \(\theta\) arriving at each time \(t\). A type includes the consumer’s arrival and deadline, availability window, per-period rate limit, maximum quantity, and valuation \(v_\theta(k)\) for each integral quantity \(k\). At each time, actual procurement costs are revealed just before allocation; future costs are unknown, though their estimates and upper bounds are known. Extend procurement costs to nondecreasing marginal cost curves \(c_t(q)\) for aggregate quantity \(q\), so procuring mass \(q\) costs \(\int_0^q c_t(z)\,dz\).

A feasible continuous allocation assigns portions of each type’s mass to schedules in which each consumer still receives an integral number of units per period and past allocations cannot be revoked. Thus a type’s mass can be split among schedules, and aggregate quantities can be fractional, without giving any individual a fractional unit. Social welfare is the mass-weighted sum of consumers’ final values minus procurement costs. The question is whether there is a causal allocation policy and a constant \(p>0\), independent of the instance, such that \(p\,SW_{\mathrm{online}}\ge SW_{\mathrm{offline}}\) on every instance, where the offline benchmark faces the same allocation constraints but knows all future arrivals and actual costs.

The theorem’s obstruction survives even when the policy can split mass freely. Take mass \(1\) of one type, arriving at time \(1\), with deadline \(2\), availability \([1,2]\), rate limit \(1\), and values \(v(0)=v(1)=0\), \(v(2)=V\). Let the first-period unit cost be \(a\), where \(0<a<V\), and let the second-period cost \(b\) be revealed only at time \(2\), with known upper bound \(V\). If the policy allocates a first unit to mass \(x>0\), choose \(b>V-a\). Completing those bundles has negative welfare, and leaving them incomplete also loses the first-period cost; the offline optimum allocates nothing and gets zero. If \(x=0\), choose \(b<V-a\). The offline optimum serves the whole mass and gets positive welfare \(V-a-b\), while the online policy cannot deliver two units to any consumer by the deadline. The two cost continuations are indistinguishable when the first decision is made. This gives the continuous mirror a direct no-go result, including against policies that choose any fractional \(x\).

The regime is a large, standardized fleet charging through a demand-response aggregator: for example, many fleet EVs with the same charging window, rate, required charge, and value for completing the charge by a shared deadline. The number of vehicles can be large while the number of distinct types remains one or a small number. This is an idealized cohort, but a plausible one; the paper already allows multi-unit demand and complementarities. The mirror preserves its online timing, uncertain procurement costs, and welfare objective. It also gives the authors a natural interpretation of the mass split: the aggregator may serve some vehicles in the cohort first, while every individual still receives an integral schedule.

I expect the no-positive-competitive-factor result to transfer; in the programme’s trichotomy, that is closest to a hardness-transfers result, though Theorem 5 is an online competitive-analysis impossibility, not an NP-hardness result. This mirror covers Theorem 5’s welfare guarantee only. It does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s DSIC characterization, its specific mechanisms, or its simulation results. Further questions include whether the impossibility persists for randomized policies under an expected-welfare guarantee, and what cost or valuation promises would permit a positive guarantee.

The weakest point is also the anchor’s boundary: the impossibility needs an adversarially revealed future cost and a complementary two-unit value, and it is stated using a signed welfare comparison against an offline benchmark that can be zero. So this is a credible continuous counterpart to a specific online limitation, not evidence that continuization makes a computationally hard allocation problem tractable. The case rests on the model’s genuine large-cohort regime and on the lower bound surviving aggregate fractional allocation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about fit with ChoCo’s stated target. The paper’s one clear computational anchor, Theorem 5, is an online competitive-ratio impossibility. It does not classify the complexity of solving an allocation instance or expose a pricing problem that a continuous-optimization method might unlock. The other theory is a characterization of incentive and cancellation properties; the remaining evidence is simulation. So this mirror would extend online mechanism analysis, but it offers little of the complexity landscape ChoCo set out to chart. That objection is strongest if the programme’s scope is read narrowly; it is not a flaw in the proposed model.

I cannot make the stronger claim that the mirror itself fails. A large cohort of identical fleet vehicles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the type can include the complete charging window, rate, and value for a completed charge. Splitting that cohort into submasses assigned to different schedules preserves each vehicle’s integral bundle. The lower-bound argument also survives that split: if positive mass receives a first unit, a high second-period cost makes any completion or abandonment of that mass welfare-negative; if no mass receives a first unit, a low second-period cost leaves the online policy unable to complete bundles that the offline policy serves. The zero-welfare benchmark makes the stated guarantee especially brittle, but that weakness does not erase the underlying question.

So my negative case is limited: this is a weaker fit for ChoCo’s complexity agenda than the programme’s core examples, not a case where no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The fleet version is coherent, and I do not see an honest way to defeat this anchor on the supplied grounds.
