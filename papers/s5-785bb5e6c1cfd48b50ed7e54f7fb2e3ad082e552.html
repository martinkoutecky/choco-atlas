The continuous mirror question

Given firm types \(I\) and worker types \(J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), capacities, pair costs \(c_{ij}\), and succinct path-independent choice rules on integral vectors of partner-type counts, minimize total pair cost over stable mass matchings. A feasible plan assigns each type’s mass to integral bundle states, with compatible pair-incidence masses realizing those bundles; it is stable when no positively represented firm and worker states both demand an additional partner of the other type.

The model it lives in

A two-sided continuum of firm and worker types, each with rational mass and a path-independent choice rule over integral partner-count bundles. Decision variables are masses \(p_i(b)\), \(q_j(a)\) assigned to bundle states and compatible pair-incidence masses; minimize \(\sum_{i,b}p_i(b)\sum_j c_{ij}b_j\), subject to individual rationality and absence of mutually acceptable blocking type-state pairs.

The objection that survived

The antimatroid gadgets may require a distinct type for each named agent, so the paper’s reduction supplies no hardness encoding for the intended moderate-\(\tau\) regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s minimum-cost stable-matching objective and its hardness question; it leaves Theorem 1’s lattice-universality construction and the representation machinery aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s antimatroid reduction be encoded with a moderate number of types, or does the required type count grow with the number of constructed agents?
  • Does every feasible stable mass plan decompose into discrete stable matchings, or can fractional splitting produce new stable plans with lower cost?
  • What is the complexity as a function of the number of types, capacities, and choice-rule input size?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high multiplicity version of the paper’s minimum cost stable matching problem. Picture a large labor market with many repeated job units and workers: units of the same type have the same substitutable choice rule, and workers of the same type have the same choice rule, costs, and other relevant attributes. There might be hundreds of thousands of agents but only hundreds of distinct types. A type is that complete description; mass is the fraction of its side’s population with that description.

I would define the problem as follows. The input gives firm types \(i\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\), worker types \(j\) with rational masses \(\nu_j\), pair costs \(c_{ij}\), and a maximum number of partners per agent. Each type has a succinct, polynomial-time choice rule on bundles of counterpart types. A bundle is an integral vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{\tau_W}\): one agent’s number of partners of each worker type. The rules must satisfy the paper’s substitutability and consistency conditions, expressed on these type counts.

A solution assigns each type’s mass across bundles. For instance, \(p_i(b)\) is the mass of firm type \(i\) receiving bundle \(b\), with \(\sum_b p_i(b)=\mu_i\); define \(q_j(a)\) similarly for workers. The solution must include a mass pairing that realizes those bundles on both sides. It is individually rational when every bundle assigned positive mass is chosen by its type’s choice rule. It is stable when there are no positively represented firm and worker bundle states that both would accept an additional partner of the other type. The objective is
\[ \min\ \sum_{i,b}p_i(b)\sum_j c_{ij}b_j. \]
Thus each agent still receives an integral bundle; it is the population mass assigned to different bundles that can be fractional. That is the co-modification needed to make a population continuum meaningful while preserving the paper’s many-to-many matching question.

The anchor is Theorem 2, “Linear optimization over matching markets is NP-Hard,” proved in this paper. In full, it says that given a standard matching market \(I\), with firms \(F\), workers \(W\), path-independent choice functions supplied by polynomial-time algorithms, and an integer cost \(c:F\times W\to\mathbb Z\), finding a stable matching \(\mu\) minimizing \(c(\mu)\) is NP-hard. The authors prove it by reducing minimum cost feasible set in an antimatroid to minimum cost stable matching.

I think the authors would recognize the proposed problem as a continuous version of that optimization task: it keeps the same agents’ decision rules, stability requirement, pair costs, and objective. It asks how those objects behave when many agents are indistinguishable in every respect the market uses. The mirror covers Theorem 2’s hardness result; it need not reproduce the paper’s lattice universality theorem or all of its other contributions.

My provisional bet is hardness transfers (Class B). The reduction’s apparent source of difficulty is the antimatroid encoded by the choice rules and the resulting stable-matching structure, rather than the ability to target one particular person. That combinatorial structure can live in the types and their choice rules even when each type represents a large cohort. This is a reason to pursue the transfer, not a proof of it.

The key follow-up is whether a discrete instance’s optimum is preserved when its agents are replicated into type populations and mass can be split among bundle states. If every stable mass plan decomposes into a distribution over discrete stable matchings, then the objective is preserved and the hardness transfer has a clear route. If hybrid mass plans exist, the theorem’s reduction may fail, and one must determine whether the resulting problem is tractable or hard for a new reason. Further questions include the complexity as a function of the number of types and capacities, and whether configuration-LP or pricing methods apply.

The weakest point is exactly that missing decomposition or embedding result. The paper’s standard choice functions act on sets of named partners; they do not automatically define anonymous choice rules on type-count bundles. The mirror requires those rules to be specified, and fractional mixing may create stable plans with no discrete counterpart. That is a serious gap, but it is also the central computational question this mirror would put in reach.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is only one anchor, Theorem 2, and it is a real computational result: minimum-cost stable matching for succinct path-independent choice functions is NP-hard. So the strongest negative case cannot be “there is nothing to mirror.” It is that the theorem gives little evidence that its hardness belongs to a population regime.

The reduction encodes an antimatroid in a stable-matching lattice using agent-specific market structure and join-constraint gadgets. Its input describes choices over sets of named partners; it does not describe a small set of types with large multiplicities. If each agent’s full choice rule and cost profile defines its type, preserving the reduction may require \(\tau\) to grow with the number of agents. Grouping agents more aggressively requires a new choice rule on type-count bundles; the paper’s rules do not automatically induce one. Thus a high-multiplicity version of the reduction is not established by simply replicating agents.

The best version of the mirror would define those type-count choice rules directly, as the proponent proposes. That is a coherent market model, but it changes the object being optimized. In an atomless population, agents of one type can split across integral bundle states. The reduction’s discrete rotations and join constraints need not survive that splitting: partial movement along a rotation may create stable mass allocations absent from the finite market. Requiring a whole type cohort to move together would preserve more of the discrete structure, but makes the population indivisible at precisely the point where mass is supposed to matter. Restricting outcomes to mixtures of discrete stable matchings would preserve the finite optimum by construction, while making the continuous part largely objective-neutral.

This is a serious gap in the proposed bridge, not a reason to dismiss the question based on what its answer might be. The atomless type-count model could itself be a worthwhile computational problem, and repeated cohorts in labor markets are plausible. I therefore cannot honestly defeat the anchor universally. My negative case is that the paper’s NP-hardness theorem does not yet anchor the proposed continuous problem: it leaves a new model and a substantive stability-and-decomposition question to justify. That weakens the proponent’s hardness-transfer story considerably, but it does not show that the mirror is not worth studying.
