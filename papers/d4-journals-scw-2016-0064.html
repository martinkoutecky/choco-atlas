The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right to name no anchor. Proposition 2 is the closest candidate, but it characterizes equilibrium payoff ranges from the Gallai–Edmonds partition. Proposition 1 does the same for another bargaining game; Results 1–2 characterize solution allocations. None formulates a computational problem or asserts a complexity result. The paper gives no algorithmic question about its bargaining models whose continuous version could be compared with the discrete one.

The best proposed mirror would use a large market with repeated buyer and seller types, a type-level compatibility network, and masses for each type. One could then ask for a mass-valued Gallai–Edmonds analogue and characterize bargaining payoffs in the continuum. That may be a sensible research question, but it requires specifying continuum matching and bargaining dynamics. The paper’s results do not tell us how to do that, because they concern individual vertices and their roles in maximum matchings. Computing the finite graph’s Gallai–Edmonds partition is a prerequisite for applying those results, not a computational contribution of the paper.

The issue is not that continuous payoffs are already a continuous population model: they are outcome values, while the paper’s agents and network remain finite. Nor does the possibility of repeated types prove that a continuous extension would be worthless. It shows that a related project could be constructed. But its central question and computational target would come from that new model, not from a named computational result here.

So the strongest defensible negative verdict is that this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor for the continuization programme. I cannot honestly establish the broader claim that no continuum bargaining-network project could be worthwhile; the case is weak at that universal level. What it supports is that such a project would be a new line of work, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
