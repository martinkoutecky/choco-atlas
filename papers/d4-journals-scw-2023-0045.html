What the mirror covers

The closest candidates are Theorem 1's implementability characterization and Theorem 3's full-support classification; neither is computational, and Theorem 2 is cited background.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most I can honestly say for a continuous mirror is that the paper’s setting could arise in a large electorate: take a finite set \(X\) of alternatives, let each complete ranking be a voter type, and let \(\nu(P)\) be the fraction of voters with ranking \(P\). The designer’s belief remains a support of possible alignments. A large public consultation over policy alternatives is a more plausible regime for this than the paper’s example of a small committee.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Its closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: implementability implies strategy-proofness for each alignment and shared-monotonicity across alignments that share a preference profile; conversely, those conditions plus no veto power are sufficient for implementability. Theorem 3, also proved here, classifies implementable rules under full support over alignments: with three alternatives they are exactly the symmetric order-statistic mechanisms; with more than three alternatives there are none when \(n\) is even, while for odd \(n\) they are exactly the true median mechanism. Theorem 2 states Moulin’s characterization of anonymous, unanimous, strategy-proof rules as generalized median rules, but cites Moulin (1980).

These are implementation and rule characterizations, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper gives no input encoding, algorithm, or running-time bound. In particular, the “easy-to-check conditions” in Theorem 1 do not establish a complexity result for checking implementability from a finite representation.

A tempting follow-on question would take a distribution \(\nu\) over rankings for each possible alignment and ask whether a rule \(F(\alpha,\nu)\in X\) is implementable under the paper’s mixed-information requirements. That is not yet a sound continuous mirror: in a nonatomic population, one individual’s report does not change \(\nu\), so ordinary individual incentive constraints can become vacuous. Making positive-mass cohorts strategic could address that, but it changes the game and the paper’s implementation concept. Likewise, carrying Theorem 3 over as a characterization of distributional social choice would continuize an axiomatic question, which the programme places out of scope. Its even-versus-odd distinction also depends on finite \(n\), so it has no automatic mass analogue.

So my positive case has a plausible population regime, but zero qualifying anchor-based continuous problems. The weak point is decisive for this task: constructing one would require new strategic foundations and a new computational question, rather than continuizing a computational result established by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the paper’s own results states a computational claim. Theorem 1 characterizes implementability through strategyproofness, shared monotonicity, and no veto power. Theorem 3 classifies the rules that survive those conditions under full support. Neither gives an algorithm, a hardness result, a running time, or even an input representation for a rule. Theorem 2 is Moulin’s cited characterization. These can be important mechanism-design results, but there is no complexity landscape here for ChoCo to chart.

Theorem 1 has the clearest possible population mirror: represent voters by a distribution over rankings and ask which distributional social choice rules are implementable. A large electorate with repeated ballot types is a plausible regime, so multiplicity itself is no objection. The trouble is preserving the paper’s incentive question. In an atomless population, one voter’s report does not change the ranking distribution or an outcome determined by it, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Giving a positive-mass cohort the ability to deviate restores strategic bite, but changes the players and the implementation concept. Keeping finite voters preserves the paper’s incentives, but leaves the proposed task as high-multiplicity discrete mechanism design. Either route still needs a representation of the rule before checking its properties has a defined computational complexity; the paper supplies none.

For Theorem 3, a natural improvement over the proposed mirror is to replace its \(n-1\) phantom voters with phantom mass and its median with a mass quantile. That avoids leaning on the finite odd/even distinction and gives a coherent continuum analogue. Its question is still which rules satisfy an axiomatic implementation characterization under alignment uncertainty. Theorem 2 has the same limitation: a continuous median-rule characterization remains an axiomatic characterization. The programme explicitly sets that kind of continuization aside.

One could add a succinct encoding for rules and ask for the complexity of recognizing implementability, or define a cohort game and study its algorithms. Those could become worthwhile projects, but their complexity would depend on modeling choices and strategic assumptions the paper does not make. They would supply a new computational problem rather than continue a computational result established here.

So the negative case is strongest against these anchors: none yields a qualifying computational mirror, even under a sensible mass-median formulation. Its weakness is the breadth of the universal claim. A new representation and a new population-level incentive model might produce a useful ChoCo problem that the authors would recognize as related. The paper itself gives no basis for ruling out that future project; it gives no computational anchor for it either.
