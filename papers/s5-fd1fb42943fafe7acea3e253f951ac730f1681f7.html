The continuous mirror question

Given a finite hypothesis set \(\Theta\), a nonatomic unit-mass society with finitely many types \(t\) of masses \(\mu_t\), type-specific signal likelihoods \(L_t(\cdot\mid\theta)\), and a strongly connected type-interaction kernel, determine conditions under which the partial-sharing update (2)–(4), extended using type-level interaction averages, makes almost every agent's belief converge to the transmitted hypothesis \(\theta_{\mathrm{TX}}\), or instead yields the theorem's alternative collapse regime.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population distributed over complete signal and interaction types, with each agent retaining a private belief trajectory and the update aggregating neighbors' modified beliefs through a type-interaction kernel.

The objection that survived

The theorem relies on a strongly connected finite network and its Perron weights; a continuum interaction kernel needs a precise definition, and the proposed type grouping does not establish that the convergence result carries over.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an honest qualifying anchor: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Its Theorems 1 and 2 and Lemmas 1–3 concern almost-sure convergence of belief dynamics, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, or parameterized. The nearest result, Theorem 1, is proved in this paper: under its stated assumptions, the partial-sharing beliefs converge to the true hypothesis when it is transmitted and the specified KL divergence is positive; when a false hypothesis is transmitted, the beliefs either concentrate on it or split uniformly among the other hypotheses, according to the KL-divergence condition. That is a convergence classification, not a complexity result.

There is a plausible population analogue: many users of a platform could be grouped into finitely many types by their observation likelihoods and their proportions of interactions with each group. Mass would be the fraction of users of each type; the type-level belief trajectory would replace the finite network’s agent-level beliefs. One could ask whether the transmitted hypothesis gains belief mass in the limit. The authors might recognize that as a continuum-agent version of their learning model.

But that question would extend an analytic convergence result. The paper supplies no computational anchor for it, and turning the limit classification into a complexity problem would add a new question rather than mirror a named result. So the positive case is limited to a plausible continuous-population learning model; it does not establish a continuous computational mirror under ChoCo’s criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 classifies almost-sure limits of beliefs under KL-divergence conditions; Theorem 2 and Lemmas 1–3 do the same for related update rules. None defines a search, decision, or optimization problem, or studies its complexity. So the proposed question—whether a transmitted hypothesis gains belief mass in a population limit—could be a legitimate extension of the paper, but it mirrors an analytic convergence result, not a computational one.

The many-user platform scenario does not by itself make the mirror computational. Agents are distinguished by their likelihood models and by their positions in the network: the combination matrix and its Perron vector affect how their observations shape beliefs. In a general network, a complete type may need to encode a distinct network role, leaving little multiplicity to aggregate. A carefully chosen cohort or graphon model could address that and make a sensible continuous-agent learning model. But it would still ask about convergence of the belief dynamics.

The strongest repair would be to add a computational task, such as choosing the transmitted hypothesis or mixing weights to guarantee learning, then study the task over a society of likelihood and network-role types. That might be worthwhile research, but it adds a design problem the paper does not formulate or analyze; its stated results take the transmitted hypothesis and mixing rule as given. The adaptive “most relevant hypothesis” approach is only introduced, without a theorem to anchor such a task.

So the negative case is strong against a *ChoCo computational mirror*, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous analogue exists. A continuous-population version of this learning model may be valuable on its own. The case against is that its value would lie in analytic social learning, not in the computational complexity programme described here.
