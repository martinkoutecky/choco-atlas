The continuous mirror question

Given an ordered party set \(P\), coalition \(C\), favored party \(p_F\in C\cap(P\setminus S)\), spoiler set \(S\), addition limit \(k\), rational thresholds \(\phi,\rho\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the paper's symmetric single-peaked preference types, does there exist \(K\subseteq S\cap C\), \(|K|\le k\), such that for \(R(K)=(P\setminus S)\cup K\), \(q_C(K)=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}_t(R(K))\in C}\mu_t\ge\phi\) and \(q_F(K)=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}_t(R(K))=p_F}\mu_t\ge\rho q_C(K)\)? The type masses sum to one and are given as binary rationals.

The model it lives in

A society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete symmetric single-peaked ranking types, with support size \(\tau\). The chair chooses a discrete set of at most \(k\) coalition spoiler parties to add; each type's mass votes for its top running party, and the two vote-share constraints determine success.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 12's compact-representation NP-hardness for CCFP-ACP with symmetric single-peaked preferences; leaves the other control variants and their results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does parameterization by \(k\) or by support size \(\tau\) yield tractability for the rational-mass problem?
  • What approximation guarantees are possible for the attainable coalition and favored-party vote shares?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a single, high multiplicity mirror anchored by Theorem 12, the lead result. Theorem 12 is proved in this paper: for symmetric single-peaked preferences, Control for Coalition and Favored Party by Adding Coalition Parties (CCFP-ACP) is NP-hard under the compact representation. The proof reduces from \(k\)-Subset Sum.

Call the mirror SSP-Mass-CCFP-ACP. An instance gives an ordered finite set of parties \(P\), a coalition \(C\), a favored party \(p_F\in C\), spoiler parties \(S\), a limit \(k\), and rational targets \(\phi,\rho\). It also gives a distribution \(\mu\) over symmetric single-peaked voter types: each type is a complete ranking induced by a peak band, and its binary-rational mass is the fraction of society with that ranking. The distribution sums to one and has support size \(\tau\). For a proposed addition \(K\subseteq S\cap C\), \(|K|\le k\), the running parties are \(R(K)=(P\setminus S)\cup K\). Let
\[ q_C(K)=\sum_{t:\,\operatorname{top}_t(R(K))\in C}\mu_t,\qquad q_F(K)=\sum_{t:\,\operatorname{top}_t(R(K))=p_F}\mu_t. \]
The question is whether some such \(K\) satisfies \(q_C(K)\ge\phi\) and \(q_F(K)\ge\rho q_C(K)\). A solution is the set \(K\). The parties and the chair’s action remain discrete; the population whose votes determine the two shares is the continuous object.

A plausible regime is a large electorate voting over a fixed, ordered party landscape. Many people may have different individual peak locations but fall into the same band and therefore have the same complete ranking. With \(m\) parties, the paper’s SSP model has only \(\mathcal O(m^2)\) possible voter types, so millions of voters can be represented by a distribution supported on comparatively few types. Adding potential coalition parties preserves the paper’s actual control question, rather than replacing it with a different kind of intervention. I would include disconnected coalitions: restricting the mirror to contiguous coalitions would discard the setting needed for the hardness anchor.

The reduction gives a concrete bridge. For item weights \(a_i\) and target \(b\), the construction places \(a_i\) voters at the type associated with item \(i\), and \(b\) voters at the favored party’s type. In mass form these become \(a_i/N\) and \(b/N\), where \(N=b+\sum_i a_i\). Adding the corresponding coalition spoiler party moves that item’s mass to the coalition. The thresholds \(\phi=2b/N\) and \(\rho=1/2\) force the selected weights to sum to \(b\), while allowing at most \(k\) additions. Thus the mass model preserves the arithmetic that makes the theorem hard.

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). Clearing the denominators of any rational \(\mu\) gives integer voter counts for a compact election, and normalizing any compact count vector gives such a \(\mu\). These transformations preserve every vote share, quota, and successful addition, with binary encoding lengths that remain polynomial. So the continuous question is computationally equivalent to the compact discrete one and inherits its NP-hardness. The paper’s compact type-count representation is strong evidence that this high multiplicity regime is sensible to study.

The weakest point is that this mirror offers little prospect of a new complexity phenomenon: for these rational, scale-invariant vote-share objectives, it is nearly an exact reparameterization of the paper’s compact problem. Its strongest value is the explicit population-distribution formulation and the clean discrete-to-continuous correspondence. Natural follow-up questions are whether parameterizing by \(k\) or by the support size \(\tau\) yields tractability, and how well one can approximate the best attainable coalition and favored-party shares when quota targets have slack. This case covers Theorem 12’s SSP compact-representation hardness; it does not claim a mirror for the paper’s other control variants.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 12 is the strongest anchor, but its proposed mirror is already the paper’s compact problem in normalized notation. The paper groups voters by symmetric single-peaked type and measures complexity on that compact representation. For any rational mass vector \(\mu\), take a common denominator \(D\) and set \(n_t=D\mu_t\). The bit length of \(D\) and the counts is polynomial in the mass input length. Conversely, compact counts \(n_t\) give masses \(n_t/\sum_t n_t\). Since both objectives depend only on vote shares, these translations preserve every successful party addition and both thresholds.

This is not a general objection to high-multiplicity work. The point is that this paper’s own compact SSP results already study the same type masses as integer counts. A continuous distribution over real peak locations does not create a richer case: with finitely many parties, peaks inducing the same ranking can be combined into one of the paper’s \(O(m^2)\) types. So the proposed mass formulation gives no distinct computational question for Theorem 12. Its hardness, and the paper’s pseudo-polynomial result for the related favored-party problem, already apply through the direct normalization.

That makes the anchor a poor use of ChoCo’s research time unless the programme values this particular dictionary even when the source paper has already solved both sides. This is a case against the value of this mirror, not a proof that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror of any result in the paper; I do not see an honest basis for that universal claim.
