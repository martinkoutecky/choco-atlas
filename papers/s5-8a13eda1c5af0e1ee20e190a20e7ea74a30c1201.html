The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is conditional: this paper admits a plausible continuous-population extension, but it contains no eligible computational anchor under your rule.

The named results are structural rather than computational. For example, Theorem 3, proved in the paper, says that for every \(P\in\mathcal P^{-}\), the optimistic anti-core \(\mathcal A(v^o(\cdot,P))\) is non-empty. Corollary 2 strengthens this to
\[ \emptyset\neq\mathcal A(v^o(\cdot,P))\subseteq\mathcal C(v^p(\cdot,P)). \]
Theorem 4 gives core and anti-core membership for queueing transfer rules; Theorem 5, cited from elsewhere, gives duality for irreducible minimum-cost spanning-tree games; and Theorem 6 gives river-sharing allocation properties. None asserts membership in P, NP-hardness, parameterized tractability, or even a named algorithmic formulation. The informal statement that exhaustive calculation may require \(2^{|S|}n!\) computations is not a numbered computational result.

So I would not pretend that any of these is a computational anchor. That omission is decisive for the requested grading format.

The best positive research question suggested by the paper is nevertheless quite strong:

Typed Continuous Bankruptcy Anti-Core. Consider a large population of creditors divided into \(\tau\) claim types. Type \(j\) has population mass \(\mu_j\), with \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\), and each creditor of that type has claim \(c_j\). The estate is \(e\) per unit population, with
\[ 0<e<\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_jc_j. \]
A coalition is represented by a mass vector \(x\in[0,\mu]\). Its optimistic value is
\[ v^o_\infty(x)=\min\left\{e,\sum_j c_jx_j\right\}, \]
because it arrives first and collects either its claims or the whole estate. Its pessimistic value is
\[ v^p_\infty(x)=\max\left\{0,e-\sum_jc_j(\mu_j-x_j)\right\}, \]
because the complementary mass arrives first.

The decision variable is a payment density \(y\in\mathbb R^\tau\), where \(y_j\) is the payment per creditor of type \(j\). The task is to output \(y\) satisfying
\[ \sum_j\mu_jy_j=e \]
and, for every coalition mass vector \(x\in[0,\mu]\),
\[ \sum_jx_jy_j\le v^o_\infty(x). \]
That is the continuous anti-core construction problem. Equivalently, by duality, one may require the pessimistic core inequalities
\[ \sum_jx_jy_j\ge v^p_\infty(x) \]
for every \(x\).

This is a genuine population continuization, not merely divisible-resource continuity: the agents are a continuum of creditors, while the number of claim types remains finite. A convincing high-multiplicity regime is a large bondholder population grouped into a small number of instruments or legally identical claim classes. The paper explicitly discusses bankruptcy as a negative-externality application, identifies the first-arrival game with the optimistic value and the last-arrival game with the pessimistic value, and explains their duality. The authors would therefore recognise this as their problem rather than an unrelated relaxation.

The expected answer is Class A. The infinite family of coalition constraints collapses to
\[ 0\le y_j\le c_j\quad\text{for every }j, \qquad \sum_j\mu_jy_j=e. \]
A feasible payment vector can be constructed by filling the estate across the type capacities \(\mu_jc_j\). Thus the continuous problem is polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input encoding length, and its finite high-multiplicity version has the same interpretation.

The weakness is fundamental: this mirrors Theorem 3 and the bankruptcy discussion, but those are existence and duality results, not computational results. Hence it is a promising continuous extension of the paper, not a compliant mirror of a named computational theorem. Under the stated anchor requirement, the paper provides no proper positive case to grade.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strong on the programme’s actual gate, though not on the literal universal claim that no continuous model could ever be interesting.

The paper has no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 3 and Corollary 2 prove non-emptiness and inclusion of finite-agent anti-cores and cores; they do not state a computational problem, complexity bound, algorithm, separation oracle, or approximation guarantee. The same is true of Theorem 4’s transfer-rule membership, Theorem 5’s cited duality result, and Theorem 6’s allocation identities. The paper’s remark that evaluating all possibilities may require \(2^{|S|}n!\) calculations is not a numbered computational result.

Consequently, the proposed Typed Continuous Bankruptcy Anti-Core is a new problem, not a continuous mirror of one in the paper. The paper proves that an anti-core allocation exists. The proponent adds a particular output format—payment densities \(y_j\)—and asks for an algorithm constructing one. That is a perfectly legitimate research question, but it changes an existential cooperative-game theorem into a computational construction problem. No choice of type space can repair that missing source result.

The proposed model does pass the population test: typed creditors are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and one should not object merely because the estate is divisible. But the continuum is doing no work that is present in the paper’s theorem. The paper does not formulate “compute an anti-core allocation,” nor does it identify coalition separation or representation as a difficulty. Asking for those things is precisely the new contribution one would have to invent.

The stronger variants do not solve this. Asking for separation over all measurable coalitions is still a newly chosen computational formulation. Adding fairness, minimum payments, approximation, or type-dependent side constraints creates a new optimization problem. Allowing creditor-specific claims or eligibility constraints abandons the proposed finite-type high-multiplicity model rather than supplying a better mirror.

The other applications offer no fallback anchor. A continuous queueing model with cohorts of identical waiting costs may be sensible, but Theorem 4 concerns a finite-player Shapley-value allocation; a continuum requires a separately chosen Aumann–Shapley or finite-population limit, and the paper supplies neither. Minimum-cost spanning trees are even less suitable: agent identity and network incidence are essential, while fixed edge costs do not scale naturally with mass. A river model can be continuized spatially, but then position becomes the relevant type and the result becomes a new continuum flow problem, not a computational mirror of Theorem 6.

Thus the best negative verdict is: this paper may inspire worthwhile continuous cooperative-game projects, especially typed queueing or bankruptcy, but it contains no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Under the stated grading rule, the proponent’s own concession is decisive. An absolute claim that no future continuum model could be interesting would be overstated; the defensible claim is that no worthwhile *mirror of this paper’s computational content* exists, because that computational content is absent.
