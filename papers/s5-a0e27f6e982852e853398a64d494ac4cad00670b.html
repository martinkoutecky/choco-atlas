The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational position-class supplies \(q_j\) summing to \(1\), utilities \(u_{tj}\), and a per-capita threshold \(W\), consider exact HZ equilibria with a unit bundle \(x_t\) for each type, prices \(p_j\), market clearing \(\sum_t \mu_t x_{tj}=q_j\), and each type’s bundle maximizing utility subject to size \(1\) and cost at most \(1\). Under the promise that maximum per-capita welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}\) is at least \(W\) or at most \((175/176+\epsilon)W\), distinguish the two cases for fixed \(0<\epsilon<1/176\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity HZ market: each type records the applicant’s utility vector and unit budget, has mass \(\mu_t\), and receives a type-level per-person bundle \(x_t\); position classes have per-capita supplies \(q_j\). Prices \(p_j\) clear those supplies, and the objective is maximum per-capita welfare over exact equilibria.

The objection that survived

The opponent points to prior continuum-agent HZ work, so the population model itself is not novel; this narrows the contribution to computational complexity, which that work’s continuum-market mechanism results do not settle ([Hashimoto, 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022053118304423)).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity HZ market. HZ already has fractional allocations, so fractional outcomes alone would not make this a population continuization. The change is to represent applicants by masses of utility types and positions by classes with per-capita supplies.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of applicant types and \(J\) a finite set of position classes. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational supplies \(q_j\ge 0\) with \(\sum_jq_j=1\), and utilities \(u_{tj}\in[0,1]\). A type \(t\) describes everything relevant about an applicant—in this model, their utility vector and unit budget. The variable \(x_{tj}\) is the share of a type-\(t\) applicant’s unit bundle allocated to class \(j\); \(p_j\) is that class’s price.

An exact continuous HZ equilibrium has \(\sum_jx_{tj}=1\) for each \(t\), clears each class so that \(\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}=q_j\), and gives every type a bundle of cost at most \(1\) that maximizes its utility among unit bundles of cost at most \(1\). The population’s per-capita welfare is \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}\). These are the paper’s HZ market conditions expressed per member of each type, with supplies scaled alongside the population.

A plausible setting is a large applicant pool and a large set of positions, such as a recurring statewide placement system. Many applicants share the same cardinal utility vector, and positions fall into classes with the same utility profile across applicant types. The paper’s welfare reduction makes this especially concrete: its gadgets already use repeated groups of agents and goods. In Section 4, \(K=m^3\) agents or goods appear in many cohorts, while the number of distinct agent and good classes is \(O(n+m)\). The few clause-specific agent types can remain rare types with small positive mass; they do not prevent the bulk of the population from being high-multiplicity.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1.2, proved in this paper by a reduction from MAX 3SAT, using the cited gap hardness of Håstad [Hås01]. It says that, given a one-sided matching instance and \(w\), it is NP-hard to distinguish whether the maximum social welfare among exact HZ equilibria is at least \(w\), or at most \((175/176+\epsilon)w\), for any constant \(\epsilon>0\).

The corresponding question is Continuous HZ Welfare Gap: given the type masses, class supplies, utility matrix, and a per-capita threshold \(W\), under the promise that the maximum welfare over exact type-level HZ equilibria is either at least \(W\) or at most \((175/176+\epsilon)W\), distinguish the two cases. A solution is the correct classification. From a finite market with \(N\) agents, aggregate identical utility rows into types of mass equal to their cohort size divided by \(N\), and identical goods columns into classes with supply equal to their class size divided by \(N\). The threshold becomes \(W=w/N\), preserving the gap ratio. In the paper’s construction, the number of agents and goods is polynomial in the SAT instance size, while this aggregation leaves only \(O(n+m)\) supported types and classes. The exact equilibrium constraints and the welfare objective survive aggregation: identical agents’ bundles can be averaged, and the construction’s identical goods within a class have the same equilibrium price.

I expect this problem to be Class B: the hardness transfers through the high-multiplicity representation. This is not an objection to the mirror that rational masses can be expanded back into a finite market; that is precisely the high-multiplicity relationship. The result would still leave open whether the gap is tractable or fixed-parameter tractable when the number of supported types and classes is small, and whether the \(175/176\) factor can be improved.

A second, related anchor is Theorem 1.1 (Main). Its hardness is proved here—Theorem 3.1 gives PPAD-hardness at accuracy \(1/n^5\), followed by padding—while the PPAD-membership result is cited from Vazirani and Yannakakis [VY21]. The theorem says that computing an \(\epsilon\)-approximate HZ equilibrium is PPAD-complete when \(\epsilon=1/n^c\) for any constant \(c>0\); the hardness also holds when every agent has at most four distinct utility values.

That gives a second precise question, Continuous HZ Approximation: given the rational type distribution, class supplies, utilities, and tolerance \(\eta\), return normalized prices \(p\) and a bundle \(x_t\) for each type such that supplies clear, each bundle has total size \(1\) and cost at most \(1+\eta\), and its utility is within \(\eta\) of the type’s best feasible utility at those prices. In the paper’s sparse threshold-game reduction, the number of supported types is proportional to the market size up to fixed constants, since its gadget parameter is fixed. Thus its inverse-polynomial accuracy becomes inverse-polynomial in the typed instance’s support size. I expect Class B hardness here too: the reduction encodes threshold-game variables in market prices, and uses agent and good cohorts to impose the equilibrium conditions. A direct proof for the type-level formulation should still check the approximate-price and allocation bounds. In particular, the paper’s PPAD-membership citation for the explicit finite market does not by itself establish membership for every rational-mass typed input.

These mirrors cover the paper’s two main computational questions: finding an approximate HZ equilibrium and selecting a maximum-welfare exact HZ equilibrium. They do not claim to continuize matching generally, or to study fractional outcomes as the central object. The authors should recognize them as their problems: the HZ equilibrium definition, utilities, budgets, and welfare criterion are retained; population masses and scaled supplies express those same conditions at the cohort level.

The weakest point is that the welfare reduction’s support still grows with the SAT instance. It establishes hardness for a high-multiplicity regime, not hardness at fixed \(\tau\), and it does not show that population continuization makes the problem easier. The approximate-equilibrium mirror also needs its own membership analysis for rational-mass inputs. Those limits narrow what these anchors establish, but they do not undermine the lead mirror: the welfare construction itself is already organized into repeated agent and good cohorts, and its hardness survives their aggregation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case I can make is that both proposed mirrors may amount to the same HZ market written in per-capita units, with no demonstrated algorithmic gain from changing the population representation. But under the programme’s own standard, that is not enough to reject a mirror: the finite and continuous versions are distinct models, and their equivalence or different complexity is exactly a question to investigate.

For Theorem 1.2, the welfare-gap mirror survives. Group identical agent rows into types and identical good columns into classes, divide their counts by the market size, and divide total welfare by that size. Exact equilibria and the welfare gap are preserved: identical agents’ optimal bundles can be averaged, and identical goods have the same equilibrium price. The paper’s repeated cohorts make this a concrete encoding; the clause-specific agents can remain rare types. The reduction does not establish hardness at fixed support size \(\tau\), but the programme asks for complexity in \(\tau\), so that limitation does not defeat the question.

For Theorem 1.1, the typed approximation question also survives. The threshold-game reduction is built from agent and good groups. Give each group mass or supply equal to its size divided by the total market size, and the market-clearing and per-person budget conditions rescale back to the paper’s construction. The support grows with the threshold game, but that is still a valid continuous-population instance. The proponent is right to flag a real technical gap: the finite-market PPAD-membership result does not automatically establish membership for rational-mass inputs, since expanding a binary-encoded denominator can create a much larger finite market. That limits the claim to a hardness mirror until membership is proved; it does not remove the computational question.

Nor can I use prior continuum work as a collision. A continuum-agent extension of HZ equilibrium has studied existence and mechanisms for approximating outcomes in large finite markets, but that is not a computational-complexity classification of the typed problem. It shows that the continuum model is established, not that these computational questions are answered. [The continuum-agent HZ extension](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022053118304423)

So the honest negative case is weak. The typed models may be a high-multiplicity reformulation of HZ rather than a striking new application, and these reductions do not show a tractable fixed-\(\tau\) regime. But neither point defeats either anchor under the stated criteria. I cannot make the requested case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The paper’s computational results are explicit, its objectives do not depend on named identities, and both have recognizable population mirrors.
