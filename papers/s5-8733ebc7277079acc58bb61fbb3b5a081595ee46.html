The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. The paper’s named results are mathematical claims about finite-agent allocations: Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that for each chosen bipartition of the agents and of the pies there is a unique \(2\times2\) matrix of group allocation ratios making the two groups’ expected dividends and standard deviations proportional to their NBS shares. Theorem 2, also proved here, counts the possible fair allocation matrices: their number is \(f(n)g(m)\), from the paper’s recurrences for agent and pie partitions. Neither classifies the complexity of a problem. The seven-step computation algorithm is not a numbered complexity result, and uses Monte Carlo simulation. So I can’t honestly present either theorem as an “in P,” hardness, or parameterized-complexity anchor.

The strongest positive case I can make is therefore a proposed, unanchored mirror of the paper’s central allocation task. Call it *Continuous Nash-Fair Pie Allocation*. It would fit a large water-sharing scheme among many similar irrigation users: the “agents” are farmers or water-right holders, the stochastic pies are supplies from rivers, reservoirs, and other sources, and the population is large while the number of distinct entitlement and bargaining types is modest. For example, there might be millions of users but tens of types. A type \(t\) is a complete description of a user for this problem, including their disagreement payoff \(c_t\), bargaining weight \(\beta_t\), and relevant utility parameters. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is that fraction of users.

An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), the fixed disagreement payoffs \(c_t\), positive rational weights \(\beta_t\), and \(m\) random pie returns specified by a rational mean vector \(a\) and rational covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). Supplying the joint covariance makes the standard deviations in the paper’s fairness condition well-defined. Let \(S=\sum_j a_j-\sum_t\mu_t c_t>0\). Define the per-user weighted NBS gains \(u_t^*\) by maximizing \(\prod_t u_t^{\mu_t\beta_t}\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t u_t=S\) and \(u_t>0\).

The decision is whether there are per-type allocation ratios \(p_j(t)\in[0,1]\) and a common \(\rho\ge0\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t p_j(t)=1\) for every pie \(j\), and, for every type \(t\), \(X_t=\sum_j p_j(t)P_j-c_t\) satisfies \(\mathbb E[X_t]=u_t^*\) and \(\operatorname{sd}(X_t)=\rho u_t^*\). A solution is the ratios and \(\rho\), or a certificate that none exist. This preserves the paper’s task—compute each pie’s allocation ratio so that expected dividends and risk are proportional to an NBS—while making the *population* the continuous object. With rational input, one can ask for an exact algebraic witness and measure complexity in \(m\), \(|T|\), and input bit length \(L\).

For this proposed question, I would tentatively expect continuum-specific difficulty: exact standard-deviation proportionality gives coupled quadratic constraints, so the allocation problem is not simply the LP one might hope for. That is only a conjecture; the paper proves neither hardness nor tractability for it, and I would not claim a Class C result without a reduction. Natural follow-ups are whether fair allocations always exist under the paper’s assumptions, whether approximate fairness is tractable, and how the answer changes with correlated pies or different risk preferences.

This mirror covers the paper’s core pie-ratio computation, not its full cooperative-game discussion. Its weakest point is also substantial: the paper’s agents are finite organizational actors, while the proposed scenario treats many individual users as agents and adds a weighted population NBS. The user types and mass preserve the central allocation question, but those extensions would need the authors’ acceptance—and there is no named computational result here for the mirror to inherit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1 gives a uniqueness claim for a chosen pair of partitions; Theorem 2 counts fair allocation matrices. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem. The seven-step procedure is a Monte Carlo computation method, with no complexity bound. So the proponent’s “Continuous Nash-Fair Pie Allocation” is an interesting new formalization, rather than a continuous version of a result this paper establishes.

That distinction matters because the proposed version adds substantial modeling choices: a mass-weighted Nash product, type-level bargaining weights, a joint covariance matrix, and an exact feasibility question for proportional standard deviations. These choices make a coherent model, and each may be defensible, but the paper does not analyze them. Its central algorithm searches for one among many fair matrices using random partitions and simulation; it gives no canonical objective for choosing among them or complexity question that the continuous version inherits. A complexity study would therefore need to motivate and fix those choices before it could claim to chart this paper’s computational landscape.

The case weakens sharply at the universal claim. A large irrigation cooperative with many users sharing a small number of entitlement, payoff, and utility types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Mass allocation and per-capita disagreement payoffs make sense there, and the proposed constraints preserve the paper’s central fairness condition. Neither finite organizational examples nor identity-sensitive payoffs rule that scenario out once complete types are allowed.

So I can make a strong case that this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor, and a credible concern that the proposed mirror introduces much of its own problem. I cannot honestly show that the resulting population allocation question is unworthy of the programme. The negative case is weakest precisely at the proponent’s improved irrigation scenario: it looks like a sensible continuous-population question, even though it is not anchored in a complexity result from this paper.
