The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) binary proposals and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference vectors \(v\in\{0,1\}^m\), with proposals revealed online through the distributions of prefixes, does there exist a polynomial-time online rule choosing \(A_j\in\{0,1\}\) such that every supported type \(v\) receives utility \(\sum_{j=1}^m\mathbf{1}[A_j=v_j]\ge \min_{w\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^m\mathbf{1}[v_j=w_j]\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete binary preference vectors \(v\in\{0,1\}^m\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_v\); outcomes are binary sequences \(A\in\{0,1\}^m\), and each type’s per-person utility is \(u_v(A)=\sum_j\mathbf{1}[A_j=v_j]\). When every supported type has at least \(m\) representatives, its cloned-agent adaptive share is \(\operatorname{HMMS}_{\mu}(v)=\min_{w\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\sum_j\mathbf{1}[v_j=w_j]\). At proposal \(j\), the online rule sees only positive-mass preference prefixes through \(j\), and chooses a bit that keeps a positive-mass extension; the final outcome is a supported type’s vector.

The objection that survived

The benchmark gives even an arbitrarily small positive-mass type standing equal to every other type, so the result says little about mass-sensitive fairness; exact cloning explains this behavior but does not establish its broader substantive value.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.3’s online adaptive-MMS guarantee question. It leaves Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 and the MNW approximation result, Theorem 4.1, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite populations where some type has fewer than \(m\) representatives, how do online adaptive-MMS guarantees change as the multiplicities \(N\mu_v\) grow toward the support-based limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would build a narrow case around Theorem 3.3, proved in Section 3.2. It says that for \(n\ge 7\), no online algorithm can guarantee every agent their \(\mathrm{MMSadapt}\) value on every preference matrix; “online” means the decision at step \(j\) depends only on the first \(j\) decision columns. This is an algorithmic impossibility result, not an NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result. The continuous mirror asks whether that impossibility survives when the population has high multiplicity.

Take a large electorate or DAO in which many members follow one of a small number \(\tau\) of complete voting programs over \(m\) binary proposals. A type is a full preference vector \(v\in\{0,1\}^m\); \(\mu_v\) is the fraction of the population with that vector. Each member of a type has the same per-person utility, \(u_v(A)=\sum_{j=1}^m \mathbf{1}[A_j=v_j]\). For example, there might be millions of token holders following seven recurring mandates, with many members per mandate. Assume each occupied type has at least \(m\) representatives. This is a genuine population continuation: decisions stay binary, and the types are complete preference descriptions.

My proposed problem is High-Multiplicity Online Adaptive-MMS Feasibility. The input is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses. Proposals arrive one at a time; at step \(j\), the rule sees the distribution of preference prefixes of length \(j\), but not future preferences. It must output a bit \(A_j\). The question is whether there is an online rule whose final outcome gives every occupied type at least its high-multiplicity adaptive maximin share.

For type \(v\), that share is

\[ \operatorname{HMMS}_{\mu}(v) = \min_{w\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)} \sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}[v_j=w_j]. \]

This is the stable value of the paper’s \(\mathrm{MMSadapt}\) under cloning: once every type has at least \(m\) representatives, the two values are exactly equal. For the upper bound, whatever partition of the decisions the type chooses, the adversary can assign every nonempty bundle to a distinct copy of a type \(w\) with minimum total agreement. For the matching lower bound, put all decisions in one bundle; the adversary’s worst assignment then gives precisely the minimum agreement above. The type masses determine which preferences occur, while the per-person guarantee remains unweighted, as in the paper.

There is an online solution: follow a positive-mass preference prefix. At each step, extend the current prefix by a bit whose resulting prefix still has positive mass, and output that bit. The final outcome is some supported type \(v^\star\). Every type \(v\) then gets \(u_v(A)=\sum_j\mathbf{1}[v_j=v^\star_j]\), which is at least \(\operatorname{HMMS}_{\mu}(v)\), since \(v^\star\) is one of the types in the minimum. Thus this mirror is tractable for every \(\tau\). It gives a concrete contrast with Theorem 3.3: the finite-agent online impossibility disappears in the high-multiplicity version.

The main further question is how the exact result changes before each type has \(m\) copies. In that intermediate regime, the adversary’s capacity to assign multiple bundles to the same type is limited by its population mass. Characterizing the share and online guarantees as \(N\mu_v\) grows relative to \(m\) would show where the finite impossibility gives way to the high-multiplicity result.

My weakest point is the same feature that makes the mirror tractable: in the limit, the share depends on the support of \(\mu\), not on the precise positive masses. A referee might see this as too weak a version of the paper’s fairness question. I think it is defensible because the collapse follows from the paper’s own share definition under exact duplication; it is not an outcome-space relaxation or a substitute fairness axiom. Still, this mirror covers Theorem 3.3’s online guarantee question only. I would not claim that the paper’s other results, including its MNW approximation bound, automatically carry over under this changed benchmark.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.3 is the strongest anchor, and its continuous mirror is real. If each positive-mass preference type has at least \(m\) copies, the paper’s share for type \(v\) is exactly

\[ \operatorname{HMMS}_{\mu}(v)=\min_{w\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}[v_j=w_j]. \]

The upper bound follows because the adversary can assign each nonempty bundle to a different copy of the least-agreeing type; the lower bound follows by putting every decision in one bundle. An online rule can maintain a positive-mass prefix and choose a bit that keeps it positive. Its final outcome is a supported type’s preference vector, so every type gets at least this share. This is a valid population continuation of the paper’s per-person guarantee, and it directly answers the online-impossibility question.

The negative case is that, for fixed \(m\), the continuation discards the masses. Once \(N\mu_w\ge m\) for each supported type, even a very rare type can supply enough distinct copies to receive every nonempty bundle. The benchmark is therefore controlled by the worst pair of supported types, not by how the society is distributed among them. That is a sharp change in the object being measured: the continuous question becomes whether one supported preference vector satisfies a worst-pairwise-agreement guarantee. It may be less compelling as a study of fairness across a population distribution.

That objection is not decisive. The per-capita guarantee gives every positive-mass type standing, and the collapse to support is a consequence of the original definition, not an invented benchmark. Nor does the easy online rule invalidate the question: the result could establish that the finite-population impossibility depends on scarcity of agents available to receive bundles.

A stronger attempt to retain the masses would let the number of decisions grow with the population, so a type’s share limits how many bundles the adversary can assign it. That could yield a mass-sensitive benchmark. But it is a joint scaling of electorate and agenda, with a new choice of their relative growth; it does not follow from the fixed-horizon population limit. Assigning positive mass to each bundle instead would require deciding how a binary proposal is resolved when different agents favor different outcomes, changing the paper’s model.

So I would argue against treating this anchor as evidence of a rich complexity landscape: the direct mirror is support-driven, and the mass-sensitive repair needs a separately motivated model. But I cannot honestly defeat the anchor outright. Theorem 3.3 has a coherent continuous mirror, and a universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists is weaker than the evidence allows.
