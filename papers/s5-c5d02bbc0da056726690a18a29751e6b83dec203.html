The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive candidate is the paper’s problem of computing the IRV margin. But there is an anchor problem: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its numbered items are definitions, algorithms, and tables. In particular, Algorithm 1 computes a lower bound by searching valid elimination sets, and Algorithm 2 constructs a greedy set of ballot changes giving an upper bound. Those are useful computational contributions, but they do not establish a complexity classification of the kind the requested anchors require. The paper says exact margin computation can be expensive and credits that calculation to work by Magrino et al.; it gives no eligible numbered anchor for it. So I cannot present a fully compliant anchored case.

As an unanchored candidate, I would mirror the exact IRV margin from Section 6. Call it IRV Continuous Error-Mass Margin. Let \(C\) be the candidates, with the election’s deterministic tie rule fixed. A type \(S\in\Pi(C)\) is a ballot signature: an ordered subset of candidates, including the blank ballot. The input is a distribution \(\mu\) over reported signatures, given by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and binary-rational masses; its winner is \(w\).

For each pair of distinct signatures \(S,S'\), choose a mass \(x_{S,S'}\ge 0\) to change from reported type \(S\) to actual type \(S'\), subject to \(\sum_{S'\ne S}x_{S,S'}\le\mu_S\). This produces a distribution \(\nu\) by subtracting each type’s outgoing mass and adding its incoming mass. Charge each transfer the paper’s IRV error cost: \(\varepsilon(S,S')=1\) if exactly one signature is blank, and \(2\) if both are nonblank. The problem is to compute the infimum of \(\sum_{S\ne S'}\varepsilon(S,S')x_{S,S'}\) over transfers for which IRV on \(\nu\) elects someone other than \(w\). Equivalently, given a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether such a transfer of cost at most \(q\) exists, and return one if it does. The objective is the minimum normalized ballot-error mass needed to change the winner.

A plausible regime is a large ranked-choice election with many ballots but far fewer distinct ballot signatures—for example, a large electorate using ballots that permit only a few rankings from a much larger candidate field. Each ballot signature is a complete type for this problem, since the error cost depends only on the reported and corrected signatures. This mirrors the paper’s margin question directly; it does not claim to continuize the audit’s sampling probability. The paper’s reported ballot-count examples are often small, so this case depends on a different, genuinely large-election scenario.

I expect the main difficulty to remain in the candidate-elimination structure: fixing an elimination sequence makes the winner condition expressible through linear inequalities, but the number of sequences grows with the candidate set. That suggests hardness driven by the number of candidates—the Class B direction—though the paper establishes no hardness result for its margin problem, and a discrete hardness result would not automatically transfer through fractional mass transfers. With a fixed number of candidates, enumerating elimination sequences and solving the resulting continuous optimization problems looks plausible. The natural follow-up questions are the complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), whether the continuous margin approximates the finite-ballot margin after rounding, and whether its computation helps produce risk-limiting audits.

The weakest point is also decisive for this assignment: this is a credible mirror of a central problem in the paper, but the paper supplies no qualifying numbered complexity result to anchor it. The continuous model also allows fractional ballot mass, so it does not automatically recover the paper’s indivisible ballot-error margin.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s sole anchor fails the paper’s stated-result test. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Its IRV algorithms give a lower bound and a greedy upper bound on the margin; the exact calculation is described as costly and attributed to Magrino et al. The proposed continuous margin is therefore a new problem suggested by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its formal computational results.

The best version of that problem is still coherent: in a large ranked-choice election, ballot signatures are natural types, and the minimum mass of ballot changes needed to alter the winner is a normalized robustness measure. High multiplicity is plausible in that setting, and nothing in the paper makes voter identity essential. I can’t defeat this mirror by objecting to fractional mass or to a change in the margin’s units.

The stronger objection is that this mirror leaves the paper’s central task behind. The paper develops statistical procedures that sample ballots and compare each reported record with its paper ballot. A reported-signature distribution \(\mu\) defines the proposed robustness margin, but it does not describe the joint distribution of reported and true ballots that generates audit evidence. Adding paired signatures could support a continuous audit model, but that would be a different, broader research question; the proposed margin alone does not carry over the paper’s risk-limiting guarantee.

So the strongest case against is that the proposed question is an adjacent robustness project, not a mirror of a named computational result or of the paper’s main audit contribution. That is enough to reject it under a strict “anchor on a result of this paper” standard. It is not enough to establish the universal claim that no continuous mirror would be worthwhile: under the programme’s broader standard of a sensible high-multiplicity computational question, the IRV margin mirror remains plausible. My negative case is weakest exactly there.
