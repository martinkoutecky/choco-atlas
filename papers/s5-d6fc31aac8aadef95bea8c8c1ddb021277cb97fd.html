The anchor — hardness

Proposition A.1

  • Proposition A.1 — survived: The proposed mass version preserves the one-good witness-extension task for recipient and good types, and the opponent concedes that this is a coherent high-multiplicity regime.

The continuous mirror question

Given recipient types \(r\) with incumbent bundles \(B_r\) and rational masses \(\mu_r\), and indivisible good types \(s\) with rational supplies \(\lambda_s\), where \(\sum_r\mu_r=\sum_s\lambda_s=1\), a common additive valuation accessible by comparison queries, and a highest-valued incumbent bundle \(B_{\max}\): determine whether there are masses \(x_{rs}\ge 0\) with row sums \(\mu_r\), column sums \(\lambda_s\), and \(x_{rs}=0\) unless the query certifies \(u(B_r\cup\{g_s\})\ge u(B_{\max})\). If so, output such an assignment; otherwise, certify infeasibility in the graph of query-certified pairs.

The model it lives in

A capacitated bipartite matching model: recipient types carry masses and incumbent bundles, good types carry equal-total supply, and variables \(x_{rs}\) assign recipient mass to indivisible gifts, using only query-certified pairs.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition A.1's one-good witness-extension subproblem in a support-compressed, mass-scaled regime; leaves the paper's EF1 algorithms, PROP1 and TPS guarantees, and \(k\)-witness structural results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the comparison-query and arithmetic complexity bounds in the number of recipient types, good types, and mass bit length?
  • How does the problem change when each recipient needs multiple fresh goods or recipient types have different valuations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is a narrow one: the paper’s witness-extension problem, where each recipient already has a bundle and must receive one fresh good that certifies the repair. The population version keeps every recipient’s bundle and every new good indivisible; only the assignment of goods to groups of recipients becomes fractional.

The anchor is Proposition A.1, proved in this paper. It says that finding a feasible bijection between \(n\) existing bundles \(B_1,\ldots,B_n\) and \(n\) fresh goods requires \(\Omega(n\log n)\) comparison queries in the worst case, even when all agents have the same additive valuation. The same lower bound holds for randomized algorithms with constant success probability. A bijection \(\sigma\) is feasible when \(A_i=B_i+\{g_{\sigma(i)}\}\) satisfies \(u(A_i)\ge u(B_j)\) for every \(i,j\), so each added good is a designated EF1 witness. The proof gives the algorithm the values \(u(B_i)=H-i\) and the *set* of fresh-good values \(\{\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon,\ldots,n-1+\varepsilon\}\), hiding only their assignment to the goods. Each hidden permutation has a unique feasible output, forcing the \(\Omega(n\log n)\) query bound.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mass Witness Extension. There is a population of recipients with a common additive valuation \(u\). A recipient’s complete task type \(r\) includes their incumbent bundle \(B_r\); type \(r\) has mass \(\mu_r\), with \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). There are fresh-good types \(s\), represented by goods \(g_s\), with supply masses \(\lambda_s\), where \(\sum_s\lambda_s=1\). Let \(B_{\max}\) be a highest-valued incumbent bundle. The input gives the rational masses and comparison access to \(u\). A recipient–good pair \((r,s)\) is *certifiable* if \(\Comp(B_r\cup\{g_s\},B_{\max})=\false\); that answer guarantees \(u(B_r\cup\{g_s\})\ge u(B_{\max})\).

The question is whether there is a mass assignment \(x_{rs}\ge0\) such that
\[ \sum_s x_{rs}=\mu_r,\qquad \sum_r x_{rs}=\lambda_s, \]
and \(x_{rs}=0\) for every uncertifiable pair. There is no welfare objective: the goal is a full certified extension, or a certificate that none exists under the comparison answers. A full assignment means every recipient gets one whole fresh good, and every resulting bundle is at least as valuable as every incumbent bundle. Thus each added good serves as the same kind of explicit witness as in Proposition A.1. The mass \(x_{rs}\) simply records what fraction of type \(r\) receives a good of type \(s\).

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large agency restocking one top-up item per beneficiary, where recipients share a standard valuation schedule and both incumbent bundles and top-up goods come in a few template classes. The number of recipients \(N\) can be much larger than the number of recipient and good types, \(\tau+\sigma\). An atomless cohort can be split exactly according to \(x\); finite rational approximations can realize it when their population size clears the mass denominators. The authors would, I think, recognize this as their witness-repair question in a population-scaled regime: the repair criterion and each recipient’s indivisible good are preserved, while the assignment of mass is the continuous decision variable.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. The comparisons build a bipartite compatibility graph on the \(\tau\) recipient types and \(\sigma\) good types. A capacitated max-flow finds a full assignment if one exists, in polynomial time in \(\tau,\sigma\) and the mass bit length; the comparison cost is at most \(O(\tau\sigma)\). The discrete lower bound does not rule this out: its construction has \(n\) distinct incumbent values and \(n\) distinct gift values, so its type support grows with the population. A useful next question is how the query bound changes as support grows, and whether the mass feasibility condition has a simple Hall-type characterization. Extending repair to multiple fresh goods per recipient may also replace ordinary flow with a more difficult configuration problem.

The weakest point is the scope: Proposition A.1 is a lower bound for a technical repair subproblem, not a hardness result for computing EF1 allocations end to end. The mirror also needs the plausible but specific regime of shared valuations and a single top-up good per recipient. Its case rests on those being recognizable parts of the paper’s own witness machinery; it does not claim to continue every result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition A.1 is the strongest anchor: it is a named lower bound for a computational problem, so there is no “nothing to mirror” objection. The best case against is that its \(\Omega(n\log n)\) bound comes from discovering an arbitrary permutation among \(n\) individually named bundles and \(n\) individually named goods. In the construction, every bundle value and every gift value is distinct. A support-small population regime collapses those identities into repeated task and good types, removing the hidden permutation that the proposition measures. Keeping that information requires support growing with \(n\), so the lower-bound instance offers no multiplicity compression.

That objection does not defeat the stronger version of the mirror. Suppose incumbent bundles and fresh goods come in a small number of known templates, and the population and per-capita supply scale together. A type can include the incumbent template and all relevant valuation data; a mass assignment then records how much of each recipient type gets each good type. Each unit of mass still receives one indivisible good, and the witness condition remains the paper’s own condition. This is a coherent high-multiplicity regime under the programme’s rules. It asks whether the witness-extension task changes when many recipients and goods share types. Its relation to A.1 is limited, but that is not enough to disqualify it.

The strongest remaining criticism is scope. The proposed mirror isolates the paper’s auxiliary one-good repair step and needs repeated templates; it does not continuize the full EF1 allocation problem. The restocking scenario makes those assumptions conceivable, though narrow. Since one recognized mirror is sufficient, neither the narrowness nor the fact that A.1’s hardest instances have growing support defeats the anchor.

So the negative case is weak at its decisive point. I can argue that the proposed mirror does not capture A.1’s lower-bound regime, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror of the type-based witness-extension problem. The proponent’s anchor likely survives.
