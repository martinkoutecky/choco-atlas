The continuous mirror question

Given channels \(C\), a weighted diversity graph \((D,W)\), viewer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\) and preference sets \(P_t\), a block social graph \(H\), a hop bound \(h\), and a population-share threshold \(\rho\), choose a nonempty channel set \(K\) and selected masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Require \(\sum_{t:q\in P_t}x_t\ge\rho\) for every \(q\in K\), require \(P_t\cap K\ne\varnothing\) whenever \(x_t>0\), and require every pair of types with positive selected mass to be at distance at most \(h\) in \(H\). Find a feasible pair \((x,K)\) maximizing \(\sum_{[q,r]\in D(K)}W[q,r]/|K|\), or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

Types encode exact channel preferences and social cohorts, with mass \(\mu_t\); \(H\) represents the paper’s social graph in a block-structured regime, and \(x_t\) is the selected population mass of type \(t\). The objective and the coverage, minimum-interest, and social-tightness constraints retain SDSQ’s defining choices, with its count threshold expressed as a population share.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s hardness result for SDSQ; it leaves the SDSSel approximation analysis and the user-study and dataset experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Mass-SDSQ become tractable for fixed \(\tau\) or under useful restrictions on the type social graph?
  • Can SDSSel’s two-approximation guarantee and social-error bound be adapted to rational type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a Mass-SDSQ for large event audiences organized into socially connected cohorts. Picture a major tournament with many viewers, a short list of simultaneous camera feeds, and large fan communities. A type records both a viewer’s exact preferences over those feeds and their social cohort. There may be millions of viewers but only hundreds of preference-and-cohort types, each with rational population mass \(\mu_t\). For this mirror, cohorts form a block social graph: viewers within a cohort are mutually connected, and an edge between two cohorts means every viewer in one is connected to every viewer in the other. That is a restrictive social-network regime, but a concrete one—for example, a platform where watch-room membership supplies the social connection.

Here is the lead question. Given channels \(C\), their weighted diversity graph, types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t\) and preference sets \(P_t\), a block social graph \(H\), a hop bound \(h\ge1\), and an interest floor \(\rho\), choose a nonempty channel set \(K\) and selected masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Require that each selected channel \(q\in K\) is preferred by at least \(\rho\) selected population mass, so \(\sum_{t:q\in P_t}x_t\ge\rho\); every type with \(x_t>0\) prefers some channel in \(K\); and every pair of types with positive selected mass is at social distance at most \(h\) in \(H\). Maximize the paper’s objective, \(\sum_{[q,r]\in D(K)}W[q,r]/|K|\). The solution is an optimal pair \((x,K)\), or a report that no feasible pair exists. The population is the continuous object here; channels and the diversity objective remain as the paper defines them.

The co-modification is to express the paper’s minimum interest count \(p\) as a share of the whole population: for a finite audience of \(N\) viewers, set \(\rho=p/N\). That preserves a meaningful per-capita interest requirement as the audience grows. It also makes the answer directly interpretable as “at least this fraction of the audience,” which suits a large watch-room service better than a fixed count of a few friends. The type masses describe the audience; \(x_t\) describes how much of each type joins the soiree.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: SDSQ is NP-hard and, unless \(P=NP\), is inapproximable within any finite factor. The paper proves this by reducing from the NP-hard Maximum Diameter-Bounded Subgraph problem, cited to Asahiro, Miyano, and Samizo (2010). That problem asks, given a graph and integers \(k,d\), whether it contains a subgraph of at least \(k\) vertices whose vertices are pairwise at distance at most \(d\). In the paper’s reduction, each graph vertex becomes a viewer, all viewers prefer both of two channels, those channels have one diversity edge of weight \(1\), \(p=k\), and \(h=d\). Thus an SDSQ solution exists exactly when the source graph has such a diameter-bounded subgraph.

The same reduction gives a continuous-population hardness result for Mass-SDSQ. For a source graph on \(n\) vertices, make one type per vertex, each with mass \(1/n\), use the source graph as the type social graph, set \(h=d\) and \(\rho=k/n\), and again let every type prefer both channels. A feasible selected mass must have support on at least \(k\) types, since each type contributes at most \(1/n\); that support must have pairwise distance at most \(d\). Conversely, a qualifying \(k\)-vertex subgraph supplies feasible mass. In a yes-instance, selecting both channels gives positive objective \(1/2\); in a no-instance there is no feasible solution. So this is hardness that survives the mass formulation, rather than a claim that any continuous relaxation must become easy.

The construction also admits genuine high multiplicity: replace each type by \(r\) socially identical viewers, with complete connections within and between adjacent cohorts. Then \(N=rn\) while the support still has \(n\) types, and \(N/\tau=r\) can be large. The exact social-distance condition survives this blow-up. For the general Mass-SDSQ problem, I therefore expect hardness to transfer. The reduction locates the difficulty in choosing a socially tight support over types, not in assigning an integer identity to every viewer.

The weakest point is that the reduction’s combinatorics still grow with the number of types, \(\tau\), and its social graph is block-structured. It does not show hardness when \(\tau\) is fixed and only the mass per type grows. Also, because the objective rewards channel diversity but neither penalizes nor caps the selected group, any feasible \(x_t>0\) can be increased to \(\mu_t\) without harming feasibility. Fractional selected masses may therefore be redundant at an optimum; the hard choice remains which types and channels to include. These limits matter, but they do not erase the mirror: it asks the same joint question about social tightness, preference coverage, and channel diversity for a large audience represented by population mass. They leave sharp follow-up questions: does the problem become tractable for fixed \(\tau\), or with restrictions on the type social graph, and can SDSSel’s approximation guarantee be adapted to weighted type masses?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that SDSQ’s social constraint resists aggregation. Two viewers with identical channel preferences can still have different friends, and exact \(h\)-hop distance depends on those individual connections. In a typical friendship graph, preserving that information may require a separate type for nearly every viewer, leaving little population compression. A graphon-style model could aggregate interactions, but the paper’s constraint that *every* selected pair be within \(h\) hops does not pass directly to an almost-everywhere relation: that would ignore exceptional viewers, and measure-zero bridges can change finite-graph distances.

Even in the proposed block-cohort model, selected masses do not seem to need fractional values. Increasing \(x_t\) for an already selected type preserves coverage and social tightness and can only help the minimum-interest constraints. So an optimum can set \(x_t=\mu_t\) for every selected type. In the equal-mass reduction, feasibility then amounts to selecting at least \(k\) socially compatible types—the original diameter-bounded-subgraph choice. The mirror’s mass variables do no optimization work; making them matter would require adding a cap, cost, or other constraint absent from SDSQ.

That makes the mirror look like a weighted, block-structured version of a discrete graph problem, with limited access to the continuous-optimization machinery that motivates ChoCo. But I cannot honestly turn that concern into a decisive objection. Theorem 1 is a named computational result, and the cohort construction gives it a clear mass-based question: with \(\rho=k/n\), the feasible support must contain at least \(k\) types, while repeating each type \(r\) times yields \(N/\tau=r\). A large audience organized into socially cohesive cohorts is at least a plausible regime, and the programme explicitly treats high multiplicity as supporting evidence. The negative case is therefore weak: it challenges how much this mirror gains from continuity, but it does not defeat the anchor or show that no worthwhile scenario exists.
