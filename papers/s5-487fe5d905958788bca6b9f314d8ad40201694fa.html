The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that emissions-permit allocation has a natural population-scale formulation. A plausible regime is a large population represented by a small number \(\tau\) of recurring per-capita profiles. Let type \(t\) have historical emissions \(h_t\) and business-as-usual emissions \(c_t\) per person, with population share \(\mu_t\). Write \(H=\sum_t\mu_t h_t\). Given a global target \(E\), a follow-up problem could ask for the permit mass \(x_t\) assigned to each type. The paper’s equal-per-capita and historical equal-per-capita formulas become \(x_t=\mu_t(E-H)\) and \(x_t=\mu_t(E-h_t)\), respectively. This is a concrete population-mass model, and the small-support regime is plausible where many people share a few regional or community profiles.

But this is a prospective reformulation of the allocation rules, not a computational result anchored in the paper. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes the historical equal-per-capita rule by historical accountability, equal treatment of per-capita equals, irrelevance of excessive per-capita emissions, and resource additivity. Theorem 2, also proved here, replaces accountability with history independence to characterize the equal-per-capita rule. Theorem 3 characterizes the historical equal-per-emissions rule using historical accountability, claim upper bound, history lower bound, and resource additivity; Theorem 4 characterizes the equal-per-emissions rule using history independence, claim upper bound, and resource additivity. These are normative axiomatic characterizations, not results that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

So there is no eligible named computational anchor, and I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem *per anchor* as requested. The rule-evaluation problem above would be computable by direct arithmetic in \(O(\tau)\), but the paper does not study it as a computational problem; making the axioms themselves continuous would also fall outside the stated programme. The positive case is therefore a plausible setting for a future computational question, not a demonstrated continuous mirror of this paper’s results. The main weakness is that the paper allocates permits to a finite set of communities, rather than formulating a population of agents with computationally difficult decisions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the one the proponent nearly concedes: the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 characterize allocation rules by axioms; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm. The regional and country tables apply explicit formulas. Treating those theorems as anchors would turn an axiomatic question into a computational one, which the programme explicitly excludes.

The proposed population model is plausible, but it does not repair that gap. If types are communities with similar per-capita profiles, their masses can represent population shares. If types are people, the model changes the allocation unit: the paper assigns permits to communities and uses population to determine their shares. In either version, the equal-per-capita rules become formulas over type masses. Computing those allocations is a new evaluation task, not one of the paper’s results; the paper has no search over mass transfers, candidate outcomes, or other computational choices.

A stronger follow-up might add transfer costs, constraints, or strategic behavior and ask for an optimal allocation. That could produce a worthwhile computational social-choice problem. But the article supplies no objective or constraints that select such a problem: adding them would create a new research question rather than mirror a named result. The same is true of robustness questions about how allocations change when population shares or emissions change.

I would not argue that a high-multiplicity regime is implausible here. A world with many people represented by a small number of recurring per-capita profiles is a sensible setting. The negative case is narrower: that setting does not reveal a computational result in this paper to continuize. It is therefore strong against a continuous *computational mirror of this paper*, but weak as a universal claim that no valuable computational extension could ever be built from its subject.
