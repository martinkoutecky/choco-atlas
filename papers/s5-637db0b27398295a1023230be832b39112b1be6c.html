The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational influence rates \(\kappa_{tu}\), and a per-capita message budget \(b\), consider populations of size \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) voters of each type and independent directed edges from type \(t\) to type \(u\) live with probability \(\kappa_{tu}/N\). Each type includes its initial ranking; use the paper’s three-candidate pessimistic revision rule and MI-IC diffusion. Choose fractions \(x_{t,I}\) of each type to seed with each nonempty message vector \(I\in\{-,\cdot,+\}^3\), subject to \(\sum_I x_{t,I}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,I}|I|x_{t,I}\leq b\). Is there a polynomial-time algorithm that finds a feasible allocation maximizing the limit, as \(N\to\infty\), of expected margin increase divided by \(N\), or achieves a stated approximation ratio?

The model it lives in

A sparse typed random network with a fixed finite set of voter types, type masses \(\mu_t\), influence rates \(\kappa_{tu}\), and seed-message fractions \(x_{t,I}\); maximize the limiting expected margin increase per voter under a per-capita message budget.

The objection that survived

The type-level influence rates replace the paper’s fixed graph and its ability to target particular locations, so the mirror covers typed sparse random networks rather than the paper’s general network instances.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers expected margin control under pessimistic ranking revision with three candidates in sparse typed random networks. It leaves the paper’s results for other ranking rules, network regimes, objectives, and extensions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the limiting expected margin objective be computed efficiently from \((\mu,\kappa,x)\)?
  • Does fractional seeding admit an approximation algorithm, or can this typed sparse-network problem be shown hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would anchor the mirror in Proposition 3, proved in this paper as a consequence of Theorem 1. It says that with three candidates and pessimistic ranking revision, for every \(\rho>0\)—even one depending on the instance size—no polynomial-time algorithm can return a \(\rho\)-approximation to the expected increase in \(c_0\)’s margin of victory, unless \(\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}\). The paper’s proof ultimately reduces from Set Cover.

A credible high-multiplicity setting is a large city or platform electorate with recurring preference and social-network cohorts. There may be millions of voters but relatively few types. A type records a voter’s complete initial ranking and social-network role; members of a type have the same influence profile toward every other type. This is a natural regime when many voters share both preferences and patterns of interaction. The network remains directed and heterogeneous: influence from type \(t\) to type \(u\) can differ from influence in the reverse direction.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Continuous Pessimistic Three-Candidate MI-IC Margin Control. Fix \(C=\{c_0,c_1,c_2\}\) and the paper’s pessimistic ranking-revision rule. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a rational influence-rate matrix \(\kappa_{tu}\), and a per-capita message budget \(b\). Interpret its network as the sparse typed limit of \(N\) voters: there are \(N\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), and each directed type-\(t\)-to-type-\(u\) edge is live with probability \(\kappa_{tu}/N\). The same live edge forwards every message its source carries, as in the paper’s MI-IC model.

For each nonempty message vector \(I\in\{-,\cdot,+\}^3\), let \(x_{t,I}\) be the fraction of the whole population consisting of type-\(t\) voters chosen as seeds to send \(I\). Require \(\sum_I x_{t,I}\leq\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,I}|I|x_{t,I}\leq b\). Let \(F(x)\) be the limit, as \(N\) grows, of the expected increase in \(c_0\)’s margin of victory divided by \(N\), using seed counts converging to \(Nx_{t,I}\). The question is to find a feasible allocation \(x\) maximizing \(F(x)\); a \(\rho\)-approximate solution must satisfy \(F(x)\geq\rho\max_y F(y)\). The empty allocation is feasible, so the optimum is nonnegative.

This keeps the authors’ central computational choice intact: who sends which positive or negative candidate messages, subject to a budget, to change the election through a spreading process. The mass variables make the electorate continuous; the typed influence kernel supplies the network structure needed for this social-influence problem. The paper’s Proposition 3 is my strongest anchor for this mirror. Its scope here is specifically the three-candidate pessimistic-revision case and the expected margin objective, not the paper’s other ranking rules or extensions.

I would tentatively expect this continuous problem to be hard for continuum-specific reasons (Class C). The shared cascade, message interactions, and maximum over opposing candidates leave a nonlinear optimization over type-level mass allocations, with no evident LP or submodular structure. But I would not claim that Proposition 3’s inapproximability transfers: its Set Cover reduction uses indivisible seed choices, while \(x_{t,I}\) allows fractional seeding. A fresh reduction would need to preserve a gap under that fractional relaxation. The natural follow-up questions are whether the limiting objective can be computed efficiently from \((\mu,\kappa,x)\), and whether the fractional allocation admits any constant-factor approximation.

That fractional relaxation is the weakest point in the case, alongside the restriction to typed sparse networks rather than arbitrary finite network topologies. Still, a large electorate with repeated preference and influence types is a plausible instance regime, and the proposed question preserves the paper’s actual campaign, diffusion, and election objective rather than replacing them with outcome-space continuity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is a real computational result, and voters plainly can occur in large, repeated cohorts. The strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed mirror makes the network, rather than the population, do too much of the modelling work.

In the paper, the manipulator sees a fixed graph and chooses seed locations in it. The Set Cover reduction encodes incidence in that graph: a seed at a set-node reaches the element-nodes corresponding to that set. The proposed type-level model supplies only edge rates \(\kappa_{tu}/N\) and chooses seed *fractions* \(x_{t,I}\). If the network is sampled from those rates, this is a different information and control problem: the allocation cannot target favorable locations in the realized graph. If the realized graph is supplied and targeting is allowed, type-level fractions do not capture the relevant choices.

Putting network position into the type repairs that mismatch, but on the paper’s general graphs nearly every vertex can have a distinct role, so the support can grow with the electorate. Keeping few types instead imposes exchangeability. In sparse networks, members of a type still have different realized neighborhoods; in dense block networks, a seed can reach an entire cohort, so a single seed of vanishing population mass may change a positive fraction of votes. A smooth mass-allocation model then requires a campaign budget that scales with the population, changing the scale of the intervention.

That is a substantial reason to doubt that Proposition 3 has a faithful, low-support continuous mirror. It does not establish that no useful mirror exists: a computational study of campaign allocation in typed random networks could still be worthwhile. The negative case is strongest as a warning that this would be a new network model, with different assumptions about topology and targeting, rather than a direct continuization of the paper’s result. The universal claim that no scenario is worth the programme’s time is weak.
