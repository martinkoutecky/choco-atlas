The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) roles with rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational exposure matrix \(W\), and rational \(q\in(1/2,1]\), decide whether there are colour masses \(x_{i,b},x_{i,r}\ge0\) with \(x_{i,b}+x_{i,r}=\mu_i\) such that at least mass \(q\) belongs to roles whose strict local majority opposes the strict global majority. Here \(B=\sum_i x_{i,b}\), \(R=\sum_i x_{i,r}\), and \(\ell_{i,c}=\sum_j W_{ij}x_{j,c}\); a tie at either level counts as no winner.

The model it lives in

A dense block-structured society, represented by roles of mass \(\mu_i\) and symmetric link densities \(W_{ij}\). The decision variable is each role’s division into blue and red mass, and the objective is to reach illusion mass at least \(q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and the mass-rewiring analogue of Theorem 2. It leaves Theorem 3’s addition-only and removal-only results, and the plurality-illusion example, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the complexity, as \(\tau\) varies, of deciding whether rational \(\mu_i\), \(W_{ij}\), and \(q\) admit colour masses with illusion mass at least \(q\), accounting for strict-majority ties.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a mass version of \(q\)-MAJORITY ILLUSION, with mass-rewiring illusion elimination as a second, related problem. Both use a society whose members fall into a small number of repeated exposure roles. A role records how its members connect to each other role; a mass \(\mu_i\) is the share of the population in role \(i\). The number of people can be enormous while the number of distinct roles, \(\tau\), stays moderate.

A plausible setting is a large platform where many users share an opinion and a similar exposure profile—for example, the same proportions of contacts across communities, regions, or account cohorts. The profile is described by a symmetric matrix \(W\), where \(W_{ij}\) is the connection density between roles \(i\) and \(j\). This preserves the paper’s central comparison: which colour is the strict majority in the whole society, and which colour is the strict majority among an agent’s neighbours. Within a role, members have the same exposure measure, so they share that local perception.

My lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper by a reduction from 3-SAT. It states that, for every rational \(q\in(1/2,1]\), \(q\)-MAJORITY ILLUSION is NP-complete: given an unlabelled social network, decide whether there is a blue-red colouring under which at least a \(q\) fraction of nodes are under majority illusion.

The continuous mirror is Mass \(q\)-Majority Illusion. An instance consists of rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational symmetric exposure matrix \(W\), and rational \(q\in(1/2,1]\). The decision is whether the population can be assigned colours so that at least mass \(q\) is under illusion. A solution is a set of colour masses \(x_{i,b},x_{i,r}\ge 0\), with \(x_{i,b}+x_{i,r}=\mu_i\). The global colour masses are \(B=\sum_i x_{i,b}\) and \(R=\sum_i x_{i,r}\); role \(i\)’s local exposure to colour \(c\) is \(\ell_{i,c}=\sum_j W_{ij}x_{j,c}\). If \(B>R\), every member of role \(i\) is under illusion exactly when \(\ell_{i,r}>\ell_{i,b}\); if \(R>B\), the condition is reversed. Ties at either level produce no strict winner, as in the paper. The question is whether the masses of the illusionary roles sum to at least \(q\).

This is a reasonable analogue of the paper’s question about whether a network *admits* a colouring with a specified level of illusion: it asks whether a large society with a given exposure structure admits a population-level assignment with that property. It also gives the prover a concrete finite input and a concrete solution. For a fixed set of roles designated as illusionary, the constraints are linear in the colour masses. The search over which roles have which local majority is the hard part. I would tentatively expect continuum-specific hardness as \(\tau\) grows, because the combinatorics move to selecting roles with the required exposure signs. For fixed \(\tau\), enumerating those patterns and solving the resulting linear programs gives an exact algorithm; that does not settle whether the unrestricted problem is polynomial in \(\tau\).

As a second anchor, Theorem 2, also proved in this paper by reduction from 2P2N-SAT, states that \(q\)-ILLUSION ELIMINATION is NP-complete for every rational \(q\in(0,1)\). Its input is a labelled network that already induces a \(q\)-majority illusion, plus an edge-change budget \(k\); it asks whether at most \(k\) edge additions or deletions suffice to make the network cease to induce a \(q\)-majority illusion.

The mirror is Minimum Mass Rewiring for \(q\)-Illusion Elimination. An instance consists of rational role masses \(\mu_i\), a symmetric rational connection-density matrix \(W\), fixed colours \(\chi_i\) for the roles, and rational \(q\in(0,1)\), with the initial society inducing at least \(q\) illusion mass. Choose a symmetric matrix \(W'\in[0,1]^{\tau\times\tau}\) that makes the illusion mass fall below \(q\), minimizing the changed dyad mass
\[ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\mu_i\mu_j\lvert W'_{ij}-W_{ij}\rvert. \]
Its decision version asks whether such a \(W'\) exists with cost at most a supplied budget \(\beta\). This makes the action and objective explicit: the decision variable is the density of links between role pairs, and the cost is the mass of potential dyads whose connection status changes. It is a population-scale version of the paper’s edge-editing intervention, not a model of editing a named individual’s friendships.

I expect this second problem also to be continuum-specifically hard when the number of roles is unrestricted, while admitting an exact fixed-\(\tau\) approach by enumerating local-majority patterns and solving linear programs. The paper’s discrete hardness does not automatically transfer: its reductions use individual-level gadgets and discrete edge edits, while this mirror allows mass-level edits within role pairs.

The proposed case covers Theorem 1 and the unrestricted edge-modification problem in Theorem 2. Theorem 3’s separate addition-only and removal-only results suggest directional variants, but I would leave them outside this case rather than claim their continuous versions without analyzing those constraints separately.

The weakest point is the network representation. The paper’s agents have actual neighbours, and its hardness reductions rely on detailed graph topology. Here, the matrix \(W\) gives members of a role a shared exposure measure; it does not preserve each person’s realized neighbour list. That is a genuine change in the network model. The case stands if the authors would recognize block-structured exposure as a sensible large-population version of their majority-illusion problem. It does not depend on claiming that their discrete hardness survives this change.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does have named computational results: Theorems 1–3 give NP-completeness results, so “no result to mirror” is not available. My strongest objection is that the hard part of these problems is the placement of edges around particular vertices. A type that captures everything relevant to an agent’s perception must preserve that agent’s neighbourhood. In a typical network, those neighbourhoods differ from person to person, leaving little repeated mass. If a type records only average exposure to other roles, as in the proposed matrix \(W\), it replaces realized neighbours with an aggregate exposure. Two networks with the same role masses and average exposure can give different local majorities. That changes the object whose illusion is being measured.

The best version of Theorem 1’s mirror would use a graphon \(W(x,y)\) and a measurable colouring \(f(x)\), so each point has its own exposure profile. But a finite input for this model needs a succinct representation. If it is a finite step graphon, the mirror is a dense, exchangeable network model; if it uses a separate step for each vertex to preserve an arbitrary graph, the number of types grows with the population, and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. The matrix model is a reasonable model of groups receiving curated exposure quotas, but that is a special mean-field regime. It does not carry over the paper’s arbitrary-neighbourhood colouring problem.

Theorem 2’s rewiring mirror has the same structural gap, and an additional scaling problem. The proposed cost counts changed dyads as a fraction of all possible dyads. That fits dense networks, where changing a role-pair density can affect a positive share of the population’s exposure. The paper’s reductions instead use sparse gadget networks and budgets on the order of the number of vertices; normalized by all possible dyads, those edits have vanishing cost as the network grows. A sparse-network limit could retain their importance, but it must represent individual neighbourhood effects that the role-pair density matrix averages away. The addition-only and removal-only variants inherit this problem.

These objections do not show that a block-structured or curated-feed version would be uninteresting. With roles and a matrix \(W\), the colouring and rewiring questions are well-defined; their complexity in the number of roles could itself be worth studying. The weakness in the negative case is that such a regime is plausible and the authors might recognize it as a useful variant. I would argue against treating these theorems as strong mirrors of arbitrary social-network problems, but I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
