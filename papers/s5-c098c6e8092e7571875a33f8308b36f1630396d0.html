The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete tenant types \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(\sigma\) room classes with rational proportions \(q_r\) summing to \(1\), a rational room-supply ratio \(\kappa\in[1/2,1]\), and utility tables \(u_t(s,r)\) and \(u_t(\bot,r)\), find a welfare-maximizing feasible mass assignment. Its variables are nonnegative masses \(x^S_{t,r}\) of type-\(t\) singles and \(x^P_{\{t,s\},r}\) of pairs in room class \(r\); each type’s assigned mass must equal \(\mu_t\), counting two units for a same-type pair, and each class must use room mass \(\kappa q_r\). Maximize the total utility of singles and pairs, where a pair of distinct types \(t,s\) contributes \(u_t(s,r)+u_s(t,r)\) and a same-type pair contributes \(2u_t(t,r)\).

The model it lives in

A rational linear program over type-level single and pair masses, with room-class supply scaling as \(\kappa q_r\). Its objective is summed roommate-and-room utility; its constraints assign all tenant mass and use all room mass. It has \(O(\sigma\tau^2)\) variables and is solvable in polynomial time in the input bit length.

The objection that survived

Exact complete compatibility profiles may be uncommon when roommate preferences are highly individualized, so the finite-catalogue regime needs application-specific support.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the welfare-assignment guarantees in Theorem 2 for \(\kappa=1/2\) and Corollary 3 for \(1/2<\kappa\le1\), under finite tenant types and room classes. It leaves the greedy algorithms, the REF and \(\varepsilon\)-EF pricing results, and the MWIS conjecture unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • How closely does the continuous optimum approximate a fixed-size finite instance as a function of the number of types and room classes?
  • When can a fractional type-level optimum be rounded to an integer roommate assignment with a controlled welfare loss?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchors are the welfare approximation results. Theorem 2, proved in this paper using Cygan’s MWIS approximation, says that when \(m=2n\), there is a polynomial-time \((4/3+\varepsilon)\)-approximation for the maximum social-welfare assignment: partition the \(2n\) tenants into \(n\) pairs, assign one pair to each room, and maximize the sum of the roommates’ valuations across their assigned rooms, minus the fixed total rent. Corollary 3, also proved here, extends that guarantee to \(m<2n\) in the paper’s range \(m\in[n,2n]\), using placeholders to reduce to the pair-only case. The paper has no numbered in-paper hardness result for this welfare problem. Its introduction cites NP-hardness for Chan et al.’s related roommate-market model, which is not the same model, so these algorithmic anchors are the appropriate ones to use.

I would mirror the welfare-assignment problem as follows. Let \(\mathcal T\) be a finite set of complete tenant types, with support size \(\tau\). A type specifies a tenant’s utility \(u_t(s,r)\) for sharing room class \(r\) with type \(s\), and \(u_t(\bot,r)\) for living alone in that class. Let tenant mass be \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let there be \(\sigma\) room classes, with proportions \(q_r\), where \(\sum_r q_r=1\). Set \(\kappa\in[1/2,1]\) as the room supply per unit of tenant mass, so class \(r\) has supply \(\kappa q_r\).

Call the problem Continuous Roommate Welfare Assignment. Choose nonnegative masses \(x^S_{t,r}\) of type-\(t\) singles in room class \(r\), and \(x^P_{\{t,s\},r}\) of pairs with types \(t,s\) in that class, to maximize total utility. The instance consists of the rational masses, room-class supplies, utility tables, and \(\kappa\). The solution is a feasible mass assignment attaining the maximum of

\[ \sum_{t,r} u_t(\bot,r)x^S_{t,r} \;+\; \sum_{\{t,s\},r} \bigl(u_t(s,r)+u_s(t,r)\bigr)x^P_{\{t,s\},r}, \]

where a same-type pair contributes \(2u_t(t,r)\). Feasibility requires that each tenant type be fully assigned,

\[ \sum_r x^S_{t,r} +\sum_{s\ne t,r}x^P_{\{t,s\},r} +2\sum_r x^P_{\{t,t\},r} =\mu_t, \]

and that each room class be fully used,

\[ \sum_t x^S_{t,r}+\sum_{\{t,s\}}x^P_{\{t,s\},r} =\kappa q_r. \]

This preserves the paper’s central choice—who rooms with whom and in which room—along with its joint roommate-and-room utilities. The fixed rent \(R\) can be omitted from the objective because it does not change which assignment maximizes welfare.

The regime I find convincing is a large housing provider assigning many students or employees, where tenants’ full utility profiles come from a finite catalogue and rooms fall into a few standardized classes. For example, tens of thousands of tenants might share a few dozen or a few hundred exact utility types, while the housing stock has only a handful of room classes. A type here means identical utility data for every possible partner type and room class, not merely similar demographics. That is a demanding condition, but plausible where preferences are collected through standardized profiles and utilities follow a common scoring scheme. The model also preserves singles and pairs: \(\kappa=1/2\) forces everyone into pairs, while larger \(\kappa\) permits singles.

Theorem 2’s regime is the \(\kappa=1/2\) case; Corollary 3’s is \(1/2<\kappa\le1\). In both, the continuous problem is a polynomial-size linear program with \(O(\sigma\tau^2)\) variables, so I expect it to be exactly tractable in time polynomial in \(\sigma,\tau\), and the input bit length. Each finite allocation maps to a feasible mass assignment by recording how much of each type-pair uses each room class. Conversely, a rational mass assignment can be realized by taking a sufficiently large replicated population, scaling the masses to integers, and pairing the required agents. That replication connection is what makes this a high-multiplicity mirror rather than a different rent or fairness question.

The scope is the paper’s maximum-welfare assignment results, Theorem 2 and Corollary 3. It does not cover the price-fairness results. The weakest point is that the LP permits fractional splitting of each type across assignments: it solves the aggregate problem, but does not show that the paper’s finite instances have an exact polynomial-time algorithm or prove its MWIS conjecture. I still consider it a faithful mirror because type-level fractional assignments are the intended continuous object, and the LP’s solution can be implemented exactly in a suitable replicated society. The next questions are how well its optimum approximates a *fixed-size* finite instance, with bounds in \(\tau\) and \(\sigma\), and when the continuous optimum can be rounded with a controlled welfare loss.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 and Corollary 3 are genuine computational anchors, so the negative case has to rest on whether their high-multiplicity version is a sensible model of the paper’s assignment problem.

The strongest concern is that the paper’s utilities are identity-specific: \(v_{ijr}\) can differ for every ordered pair of tenants. In its iid simulation model, exact duplicate utility profiles are unlikely. A type model with \(\tau\ll m\) therefore needs an additional assumption: tenants’ valuations must be constant across counterpart types, so the utility matrix is block-constant. Without that assumption, \(\tau\) may be as large as \(m\); the continuous LP then lets each uniquely described tenant’s mass split across assignments, losing the discrete roommate choices that Theorem 2 approximates.

That concern also applies to the proponent’s stronger version. A large housing provider could have repeated tenant and room classes, but exact utility profiles over all potential roommate types are a demanding condition for a problem whose defining feature is personal compatibility. Coarse survey categories could make the regime plausible, though they would need justification as a faithful description of the utilities the assignment uses. This makes the mirror less securely grounded in the paper’s examples than its simple LP formulation suggests.

Still, the better version survives that objection in a plausible setting: a large pool with a finite catalogue of complete, type-level compatibility profiles and standardized room classes. Its mass assignment is a well-posed continuous problem, and its type-pair variables give an LP of polynomial size. That is a meaningful Class A result, even if it is structurally simpler than the paper’s MWIS approximation.

So the negative case is weak. It can challenge whether the required type symmetry is realistic for this paper’s interpersonal preferences; it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario. I would not claim that these anchors have been defeated.
