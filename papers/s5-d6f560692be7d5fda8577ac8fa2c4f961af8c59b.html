The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_s\), and finitely many types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type has a path-independent choice function \(C_t\), an entering or continuing status, a previous bundle \(B_t^-\) if continuing, and a priority tier at each school. Priority tiers are strictly ordered, with mass within a tier divisible at a capacity cutoff. Find \(x_{t,B}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(B\subseteq S\), such that \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), school capacities are respected, every positively used bundle is individually and dynamically rational, and the allocation is non-wasteful and has no justified claims. A justified claim occurs when positive mass assigned bundle \(B\) would choose to add school \(s\), yet positive mass assigned to \(s\) from a lower-priority type had no tenure at \(s\) in the previous period.

The model it lives in

A fractional matching over finite types, with \(x_{t,B}\) assigning type mass to school bundles and capacities measured per capita. Types carry their choice behavior, tenure state, and school priority tiers; the objective is to return any dynamically stable allocation, using a mass version of deferred acceptance.

The objection that survived

The paper assumes strict priorities among individuals, while the mass model must allow ties within priority tiers and split mass at a capacity cutoff; the proponent explains why this is plausible but cannot establish that the authors would accept it as their model.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and could support mass analogues of Theorems 2 and 3. It leaves TREADA’s consent-based efficiency result and individual manipulation results aside: a zero-mass teacher cannot change a continuum outcome, and coordinated positive-mass misreports would change the strategic question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a mass version of TRDA compute a dynamically stable allocation in time polynomial in the number of schools, types, and the explicit input size of the choice functions and masses?
  • Which tier and cutoff conventions best preserve the paper’s justified-claim definition when mass is split within a priority tier?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or gives a runtime bound. The closest possible lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, when teachers have path-independent choice functions, the TRDA mechanism produces a dynamically stable matching. TRDA gives each school a derived priority that puts its previous-period teachers first, preserving the original order within the previous-period and other groups, then runs teacher-proposing deferred acceptance under those priorities. The theorem establishes correctness, not computational complexity. I would use it as a lead only if a constructive correctness theorem counts as an algorithmic anchor under the requested standard.

The regime I find most plausible is a large public-school hiring system with many teachers but a modest number of recurring profiles: subject and location constraints, choice behavior over sets of schools, priority category, and—among continuing teachers—their previous assignment. A profile’s mass is its share of the current teacher population; school capacities scale with that population. Periods remain discrete. The object being continuized is the population in one period, including its inherited tenure state.

The candidate problem is Dynamic Stable Mass Matching. An instance has schools \(S\), per-capita capacities \(q_s\), and finitely many types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type specifies a path-independent choice function \(C_t\), its school priority category, whether it is entering or continuing, and, for a continuing type, its previous-period bundle \(B_t^-\subseteq S\). The decision variables are \(x_{t,B}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned exactly bundle \(B\subseteq S\). They must satisfy
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq S}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t\sum_{B\ni s}x_{t,B}\leq q_s\quad\text{for each }s\in S. \]
The question is to find such an allocation that is dynamically stable; the solution is the vector \(x\), with no welfare objective.

For stability, every bundle used by positive mass must be individually rational: \(B\succeq_t^B\emptyset\), where \(A\succeq_t^B D\) means \(A=C_t(A\cup D)\). Continuing teachers must also satisfy \(B\succeq_t^B B_t^-\). The allocation must be non-wasteful: if a positive mass of type \(t\) would choose to add school \(s\) to its bundle, \(s\) is full. Finally, there must be no justified claim: no type with positive mass assigned a bundle excluding a school it would choose to add may outrank, at that school, positive mass assigned there whose previous bundle did not include it. The continuous model uses priority tiers over types, allowing a school’s capacity to divide a tier’s mass.

I would expect this problem to be tractable by a mass version of deferred acceptance over the finite type-and-bundle description, so it is a plausible Class A target. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result of the paper; a polynomial bound in \(m\) and \(\tau\) is not supplied by Theorem 1. This mirror covers the paper’s construction and its dynamic-stability claim. It does not mirror the paper’s strategic-manipulation results. It also suggests questions about whether mass TRDA is Blair-optimal among dynamically stable mass matchings, whether it minimizes unjustified claims, and whether the paper’s consent-based efficiency result for TREADA extends to mass allocations.

The weakest point is the priority model. The paper assumes strict priorities over individual teachers. If an individual tie-break can affect assignments, teachers with different ranks are different types, and the proposed small-support regime may disappear. The mirror therefore uses priority tiers and divisible mass at a tier cutoff. That is a natural continuum adaptation, but the authors might regard it as a material change to their model. This case is strongest as a mirror of Theorem 1 under that co-modification; under the strict anchor criterion, the paper offers no qualifying computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strict anchor criterion gives the clearest negative: the paper has no numbered result about computational complexity. Theorem 1 is the best candidate because it proves that TRDA produces a dynamically stable matching. But it proves existence and correctness, not a runtime bound or a complexity classification. Its proof reduces the dynamic problem to a static one and invokes deferred acceptance. The paper also leaves the input representation of teachers’ choice functions unspecified. So the proposed mirror begins with a constructive theorem, but it does not mirror a computational result in the programme’s stated sense.

Even granting Theorem 1 as an algorithmic anchor, the proposed finite-support society does not preserve the paper’s strict-priority model. Priority affects claims and who keeps a school’s positions. Under a strict order, teachers with different ranks are distinguishable for those purposes; treating them as one type loses information. Preserving their exact ranks can make the support grow with the number of teachers, removing the intended high-multiplicity regime.

The best repair is to use shared priority tiers and allow a school to divide capacity within a tier. That could describe a real hiring system, but it changes a central assumption of this paper: schools have strict priorities. Another repair is to give teachers a continuous priority score and describe its distribution. That preserves strict ordering almost everywhere, but it changes the finite-support input into a distribution over a continuous attribute. Either repair needs its own input model and a case that the authors would recognize as their problem. Without that, a claimed runtime polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length is not grounded: the paper permits arbitrary path-independent choice functions, whose explicit tables can already have size exponential in \(m\).

Theorem 1’s result on dynamically stable mass matching is still the strongest version of the mirror. Theorem 2’s constrained efficiency and Theorem 3’s claim minimization would need aggregate definitions, such as Blair dominance for almost every teacher and inclusion of positive-mass claim types. Those definitions are possible, but the paper gives no complexity result for them either. Theorem 4 raises a further issue: individual consent has no effect on an atomless population. A meaningful mass version would need positive-mass groups to consent, which adds a collective consent model to the paper’s mechanism.

The paper’s manipulation results are still less promising as mirrors. A single teacher has zero mass and cannot alter a continuum outcome; studying manipulation would require a model of coordinated positive-mass misreports. That changes the strategic object substantially.

This is a credible case against treating this paper as a clear continuization target: its named results concern existence, efficiency, and incentives, while the proposed finite-type model depends on replacing strict priorities and supplying missing computational input assumptions. It is not a strong universal “no.” A tiered-priority school system with many interchangeable teachers could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors might recognize its mass version. The paper alone does not settle that question.
