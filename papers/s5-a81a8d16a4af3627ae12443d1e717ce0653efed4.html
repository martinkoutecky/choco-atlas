The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\) and a finite support of admissible ballot types \(t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to one, choose a complete ranking \(\pi\) that maximizes \(\sigma_U^\mu(\pi)\), defined from the minimum over candidate pairs of their mass-weighted agreement with \(\pi\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over the paper’s complete or partial ballot types. The decision variable is a deterministic complete ranking \(\pi\in S(C)\); for each pair, each type contributes agreement \(1\), \(1/2\), or \(0\) as in the paper, and the objective is to maximize the resulting \(\sigma_U^\mu(\pi)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s optimization of \(\sigma_U\); it leaves the \(\sigma_{IIA}\) experiments, the empirical comparisons, and the quantitative Arrow statement outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is Mass-Optimal Majoritarian Ranking, a continuous-population version of the optimization in Theorem 3 (Greedy sort). The theorem is proved in this paper. It says the greedy-sort algorithm computes a voting rule \(f_U\) that maximizes \(\sigma_U\) on every profile—so \(\sigma_U(f_U,P)\geq \sigma_U(g,P)\) for every rule \(g\) and profile \(P\)—and that computing \(f_U(P)\) takes \(O(m^2n+m^3)\) time. I would use this as my lead and only anchor: the paper offers no numbered computational-hardness result, and its quantitative Arrow statement is an axiomatic impossibility result rather than a complexity result.

Here is the mirror. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\) and a finite support of \(\tau\) ballot types \(t\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type is one of the paper’s admissible ballots; it carries exactly the pairwise preference information used by the paper’s alignment definition. For a complete ranking \(\pi\) and an unordered pair \(\{A,B\}\), let \(a^\pi_t(A,B)\) be the agreement that type \(t\)’s ballot gives \(\pi\)’s ordering of that pair, with a tie or jointly unranked pair contributing one half as in the paper. Define

\[ M_\mu(\pi)=\min_{\{A,B\}\subseteq C}\sum_t\mu_t\,a^\pi_t(A,B), \qquad \sigma_U^\mu(\pi)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{M_\mu(\pi)}{1-M_\mu(\pi)}, & M_\mu(\pi)<\tfrac12,\\[4pt] 1, & M_\mu(\pi)\geq\tfrac12. \end{cases} \]

The question is to output a complete ranking \(\pi\) maximizing \(\sigma_U^\mu(\pi)\), with the exact optimum value if requested. The output and objective are the paper’s: a single ranking, assessed by the worst-aligned candidate pair. Only the population input changes from a list of individuals to a distribution over ballots. This is a direct mirror of what Theorem 3 computes, not an optimization over fractional rankings or over a new axiom.

A plausible regime is a large online membership vote on a fixed slate of, say, eight proposals. Millions of members submit rankings, while the number of distinct ballot types is bounded by \(8!\) and could be much smaller in practice. Members with the same ballot are indistinguishable for this problem. The authors should recognize the mirror: their theorem already takes a profile, chooses its \(\sigma_U\)-optimal ranking, and bases that choice on pairwise comparisons. This regime simply asks the same question when ballot frequencies are represented as masses.

The expected direction is tractable. For a discrete profile with \(n_t\) ballots of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); the objective is unchanged. Conversely, any rational mass vector can be converted to a finite profile by clearing denominators. So the continuous input is the compressed high-multiplicity representation of a discrete histogram. Pairwise margins can be computed from the \(\tau\) supported types, then the weak majority edges can be removed until the remaining graph is acyclic, and a topological sort gives a ranking. A sorted-threshold search over the rational edge weights yields a polynomial-time implementation in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the mass encoding length, without expanding the input into the potentially enormous electorate obtained by clearing denominators.

There is one technical qualification to the anchor’s stated runtime. Theorem 3 prints the bound \(O(m^2n+m^3)\), but its appendix justifies at most \(m\) removal rounds by saying that at least one edge is removed per round; there can be as many as \(\binom m2\) edges, so that reasoning alone does not establish the printed bound. The threshold-search implementation gives polynomiality for the mirror without relying on that bound. This is a limitation on how much runtime detail I would inherit from the theorem, not on the naturalness of the continuous question.

The weakest point is that this paper is not presenting discrete hardness that might dissolve at high multiplicity. Its \(\sigma_U\) score is already a real-valued statistic, and its named algorithmic result is polynomial on ordinary finite profiles. The continuous mirror is therefore a faithful, compressed-input version of an existing tractable problem, rather than a likely source of a new tractability boundary. I would keep its scope to Theorem 3’s \(\sigma_U\)-optimal ranking; it does not mirror the \(\sigma_{IIA}\) experiments or continuize Arrow’s theorem. Natural next questions include how the optimal ranking changes under small perturbations of \(\mu\), and what the Pareto frontier between \(\sigma_U\) and \(\sigma_{IIA}\) looks like for mass societies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised one anchor: Theorem 3’s optimization of \(\sigma_U\). It is a real computational result, so I cannot argue that the paper offers no named algorithmic result. Nor is there a convincing objection to its population model: a large membership vote with many repeated ballots is a natural high-multiplicity setting, and ballot types retain everything this objective uses.

The strongest case against is about how much computational substance the mirror adds. For any mass society, the objective depends on the type distribution only through its weighted pairwise agreement totals. The algorithm then works on the resulting weighted majority graph: it removes low-weight edges until the graph is acyclic and returns a topological sort. Thus the proposed mirror needs no new machinery for distributions over types; it aggregates the masses into pairwise weights and applies the paper’s own graph argument. A rational mass vector can also be represented by a finite electorate after clearing denominators. The continuous formulation makes the input more compact and gives a useful bound in \(\tau\) and \(L\), but here it does little to expose a new computational structure.

That is a limited reason to call the mirror low-yield for ChoCo, not a reason it is ill-posed or unrecognizable to the authors. The paper’s real-world elections already have many voters and comparatively few ballot types, and the mass interpretation of its objective is direct. I therefore cannot make a strong case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: this anchor survives the best objection I can support.
