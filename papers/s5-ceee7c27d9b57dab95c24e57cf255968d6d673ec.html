The continuous mirror question

Given integers \(s,\Delta s>0\), a finite type graph \(H=(T,F)\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and an initial count \(\alpha_t\in\{0,\ldots,s\}\) of A-supporters per district type \(t\), treat each type as an atomless mass of interchangeable districts, with every pair of districts in compatible types adjacent. Each dissolved district must transfer all \(s\) voters to compatible surviving districts; each survivor must receive exactly \(\Delta s\) voters, with integral transfers for each district but fractional type-level averages allowed. What is the maximum mass of surviving districts that can have a strict A-majority, and is that mass at least a given rational threshold \(\rho\)?

The model it lives in

Use a distribution \(\mu\) over district types, with dissolved, winning, and other-surviving masses \(d_t,w_t,\ell_t\), and A- and B-transfer masses \(x^{A,k}_{tu},x^{B,k}_{tu}\) for \(k\in\{W,L\}\). Maximize \(\sum_t w_t\) subject to type-mass conservation, \(s\)-voter supply per dissolved mass, \(\Delta s\)-voter demand per surviving mass, compatibility restrictions, and the strict-majority threshold for winning mass. This gives a polynomial-size LP candidate; exactness requires showing its aggregate flows can be realized by measurable assignments of integral local transfers.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of Biased Dissolution for a large family of exchangeable district units. Picture a redistricting system with many districts but few electoral and exchange-eligibility profiles: districts of the same type have the same number of A-supporters and the same relations to every type. A type records those features. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of districts of that type. The eligibility graph is a finite type graph: every district in one type is adjacent to every district in a compatible type. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for repeated administrative regions whose transfers are pooled by compatibility class.

My anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that Biased Dissolution is solvable in \(O(n(m+n\log n))\) time when \(s=\Delta s=1\), by reducing it to maximum-weight perfect matching, and is NP-complete for every other choice: when \(s\ne\Delta s\), and when \(s=\Delta s\ge2\). The hardness for unequal sizes follows from the paper’s Dissolution result; for equal sizes at least two, the paper gives separate reductions, using restricted two-factors for size two and Exact Cover by \(t\)-Sets for larger sizes. This is a useful anchor because it asks whether hardness in choosing which vertices to dissolve and which survivors to win persists when repeated district types can be represented by mass.

Call the mirror Mass-Biased Dissolution. An instance gives fixed positive integers \(s,\Delta s\), a finite type set \(T\), a symmetric compatibility relation \(F\) on types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and an integer \(\alpha_t\in\{0,\ldots,s\}\) for each type. Here \(\alpha_t\) is the number of A-supporters in each district of type \(t\). The question is whether a valid dissolution can make at least a rational fraction \(\rho\) of the original district mass win for A; the optimization version maximizes that winning mass. A solution dissolves mass \(d_t\), leaves mass \(w_t\) designated as winning and \(\ell_t\) as other surviving districts, and transfers A-supporter and total-voter mass only along compatible type pairs. Each infinitesimal district still receives an integral number of voters under its local transfer plan; the type-level transfer values are fractional averages over districts. The total dissolved mass is \(\Delta s/(s+\Delta s)\), as in the discrete size-balance condition.

For precision, let \(x^{A,k}_{tu}\) and \(x^{B,k}_{tu}\) be the mass of transferred A- and B-supporters from dissolved type \(t\) to type \(u\), where \(k\in\{W,L\}\) indicates the target group. These variables are zero unless \(\{t,u\}\in F\). Feasibility requires
\[ d_t+w_t+\ell_t=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{u,k}(x^{A,k}_{tu}+x^{B,k}_{tu})=s d_t,\qquad \sum_{u,k}x^{A,k}_{tu}=\alpha_t d_t, \]
and, for each \(u\),
\[ \sum_t(x^{A,W}_{tu}+x^{B,W}_{tu})=\Delta s\,w_u,\qquad \sum_t(x^{A,L}_{tu}+x^{B,L}_{tu})=\Delta s\,\ell_u. \]
Writing \(h=\lfloor(s+\Delta s)/2\rfloor+1\), the majority condition is
\[ \alpha_u w_u+\sum_t x^{A,W}_{tu}\ge h w_u. \]
The decision question is whether \(\sum_t w_t\ge\rho\). These are linear constraints with \(O(\tau^2)\) transfer variables, where \(\tau=|T|\). They suggest a Class A result: a polynomial-size LP, together with an integral-flow decomposition after scaling rational masses, should give an exact algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The prover’s first task would be to establish that the aggregate constraints capture realizability by integral local transfer plans.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s biased-dissolution decision problem in the block-homogeneous regime, including the question of whether its NP-hard cases become tractable when the number of distinct district types is small. Further questions include whether the LP decomposition is exact for all rational instances, and how much additional structure—such as type-pair edge capacities—is needed to extend the result to sparse spatial adjacency graphs.

The weakest point is the regime: real geographic district graphs rarely have complete adjacency between whole classes, so preserving their local topology may require nearly one type per district. That makes this a narrow mirror, and it treats the paper’s manipulable vertices—district units—as the high-multiplicity population. The case still seems worth making: the paper defines its core computational problem over those units, and the type-compatibility model preserves the central operation, the balanced redistribution, and the majority objective.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine anchor: it gives a named complexity result, including a polynomial case and NP-complete cases. So the negative case cannot rest on the absence of a computational theorem.

The hardest issue is what counts as the population. In Biased Dissolution, A-supporters are the load inside each vertex; the decision acts on the vertices themselves, and a vertex’s possible transfers depend on its incident edges. Under ChoCo’s definition of type, the type must capture that network role as well as the district’s A-support. The proposed type graph does this only by requiring every member of one type to be adjacent to every member of each compatible type. That is a strong restriction on the graph, not a compression that works for the paper’s general instances. For a typical graph, encoding enough neighborhood structure to make vertices interchangeable would give nearly every vertex its own type.

This mismatch is especially sharp for the paper’s redistricting motivation. Its planar district graphs are sparse. A complete adjacency relation between two large type classes would contain a large complete bipartite graph, which cannot occur in a planar graph. Small classes and hub-like exceptions are possible, but they do not supply the general many-to-many redistribution structure assumed by the proposed LP. If the model instead records actual sparse edges, aggregate masses no longer specify which transfers can jointly occur: the incidence and cycle structure that drives the paper’s matching and factor results matters. Encoding that structure in types risks bringing the support size back up to the number of districts.

The strongest rescue would preserve locality through a continuous spatial or graph limit—for example, a growing district grid with transfers only between nearby cells. That may lead to an interesting flow or variational problem. But adjacency then lives in local geometry and correlations, rather than in a finite list of type masses. A useful computational version would need a finite input representation and complexity parameters; if it keeps enough detail to represent the original network, it also keeps the combinatorial structure that made the finite problem hard. Repeated copies of a fixed network pattern offer another route, but with fixed-size components their behavior can often be summarized by enumerating component solutions. More elaborate wiring between copies reintroduces the topology the type masses omit.

I would not attack the proposed LP’s arithmetic: for genuinely complete type blocks, an exact aggregate formulation may well be provable. The objection is that this gives a tractable mirror for a narrow, dense compatibility model, while the paper’s meaningful redistricting regime relies on sparse spatial adjacency. The paper’s other applications might support a different cohort-based scenario, but they do not by themselves establish that the biased-dissolution objective and this dense graph model are a compelling high-multiplicity instance.

That leaves the universal negative case weak. The programme allows scenario-specific regimes and recognized co-modifications, so a paper author could accept a large system of districts with complete compatibility between cohorts. The proponent’s anchor is not defeated outright on the supplied evidence. The strongest honest case against it is that its plausibility and value are concentrated in this dense block-graph regime; the available material does not justify claiming that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
