What the mirror covers

The nearest distributional analogue concerns the DIC basis characterizations in Theorems 1 and 7; the paper's other analytic mechanism results, including Theorems 8 and 9, are not computational results either.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a plausible setting, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. A large electorate deciding a multidimensional budget could have a small support of types, where each type records a complete ideal budget vector and the norm used to judge decisions, and its mass is the fraction of residents with that type. A proposed set of issues is a basis; issue-by-issue voting produces coordinate-wise medians. That is a natural high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s subject.

The nearest named results are Theorem 1, proved here, and Theorem 7, also proved here. Theorem 1 says that a marginal-median mechanism is dominant-strategy incentive compatible if and only if its basis makes the norm orthant-monotonic. Theorem 7 says that, for \(d\ge 2\) and \(p>1,\ p\ne2\), the DIC marginal-median mechanisms for \(\ell_p(d)\) are exactly those whose bases are formed by choosing an even-sized subset of standard basis vectors, pairing them, replacing each pair \(e_j,e_k\) with \(e_j+e_k,e_j-e_k\), and retaining the unpaired vectors. These are substantial characterizations, but neither asserts a complexity result or analyzes an algorithm for an input society. The paper’s other main results have the same analytic or mechanism-classification character.

A possible population-level question would be to find all bases for which coordinate-wise medians over a rational type distribution are DIC. But in a nonatomic continuum, one person has zero mass and cannot change the outcome, making individual DIC vacuous. Treating each type’s whole mass as a strategic bloc would restore meaningful deviations, but changes the paper’s agents and incentive question. Keeping one-person deviations at finite mass preserves the paper’s model, but then the mirror is the high-multiplicity representation of a finite electorate, not a new continuum mechanism result.

So the honest positive case is that this paper has a compelling high-multiplicity *scenario* and unusually clear candidate geometric structure. Its weakest point is decisive for the requested assignment: it contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous complexity question. I would not offer a prover-ready mirror as though Theorem 1 or Theorem 7 established one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes when a marginal-median mechanism is DIC; Theorem 7 lists the bases that work for \(\ell_p(d)\). Both quantify over preference profiles or describe a class of mechanisms. Neither takes a society as input and solves a computational problem. The paper’s semi-inner-product method even points toward a different computational question—solving equations to find suitable bases—but its input is a norm, not a population distribution.

The budget-electorate scenario is plausible: ideal budgets and preference norms can form complete types, and many residents may share each type. But the masses do not affect the DIC condition. If a basis is DIC for each supported preference type, making one type’s mass tiny rather than large changes nothing; dominant-strategy truthfulness is an individual condition. Given a basis, computing coordinate-wise weighted medians from \(\tau\) types is routine. Theorem 1’s population-continuous counterpart therefore has no population-dependent computational question to classify.

The same problem defeats Theorem 7 as an anchor. Its classification is a geometric description in dimension \(d\), independent of the society’s type masses. For \(p\ne2\), it gives the allowed basis forms directly. Asking which of those mechanisms maximizes welfare for a given population might be a worthwhile follow-on, but it adds an objective the theorem does not study; it is a new optimization problem, not a continuous mirror of the characterization.

A nonatomic population also removes the original strategic force: one person has zero mass and cannot change the median. Letting a positive mass coordinate its reports would restore an effect, but would replace individual DIC with a coalition-manipulation or robustness question. Rescaling small individual effects or studying pivotal probabilities could produce other projects, but they do not mirror these theorems.

So the strongest case against is that the paper’s results concern which mechanisms are truthful, not computation over a society. High multiplicity is sensible for the budget example, but it does not make these results population-dependent. I cannot rule out valuable adjacent projects, especially welfare-based mechanism selection; I do not see a worthwhile continuous mirror of the named results themselves.
