The continuous mirror question

Given worker types \(R\) and firm types \(S\), each with rational mass, and a finite set \(E\) of contract categories linking types in a block-complete market, find a mass allocation \(y_e\geq 0\). Require \(\sum_{e\ni a}y_e\leq\mu_a\) for each type \(a\), and permit positive \(y_e\) only if both endpoint types choose \(e\) from \(\{e\}\). For each endpoint type \(a\) of \(e\), let \(q_{a,e}(y)\) be its unmatched mass plus the mass assigned to categories \(e'\ne e\) for which \(C_a(\{e,e'\})=\{e\}\). Is there an algorithm to find an allocation such that \(q_{r,e}(y)q_{s,e}(y)=0\) for every category \(e\) linking worker type \(r\) and firm type \(s\)?

The model it lives in

A block-complete continuum placement market with rational masses of worker and firm types, shared type-level choice correspondences satisfying SUB and BA, and decision variables \(y_e\) for the mass assigned to each contract category. Each unit receives at most one whole contract; stability means no category has positive mass on both sides that would strictly choose it over their current assignment. There is no welfare objective.

The objection that survived

The mass-flow formulation requires block-complete availability: any units of the linked types must be pairable under a category. The proposed exchangeable placement regime points toward this assumption but does not spell it out fully.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's one-to-one R-stability result. It leaves the many-to-many and many-to-one existence theorems, the axiom comparisons, and the incentive questions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can stable mass allocations be computed in time polynomial in the number of types and categories, the rational-mass encoding length, and the representation size of the choice data?
  • How do exact stable allocations relate to stable matchings in finite replications of the same types?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would anchor the positive case on Theorem 2, proved in the paper’s Section 4.1 and Appendix C.1. It is a constructive existence result, not a theorem that classifies the problem as being in P or proves hardness. It says that when every agent’s choice correspondence satisfies substitutability (SUB) and binary acyclicity (BA), the paper’s matching algorithm produces an R-stable one-to-one matching. R-stability means every agent chooses their assigned contract, and there is no unmatched contract \(x\) such that both endpoints choose \(x\) as a replacement: \(C_i(\nu_i\cup\{x\})=\{x\}\). The proof is given here; it uses BA to extend strict pairwise choices to orders, then runs deferred acceptance and uses SUB to rule out blocks under the original correspondences.

A plausible regime is a large, centralized placement market with many applicants and single-seat posts. Applicants might be grouped by qualification and contract-choice behavior; posts by role, employer category, and their contract-choice behavior. A type is the complete description of an agent’s relevant options and choice correspondence, including the terms they face. If there are \(n\) applicants and posts but only \(\tau\) such types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than \(n\), the population distribution is the natural description. This assumes agents within a type are interchangeable over contract categories, which is a real regime assumption, not a claim about all matching markets.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous R-Stable Matching for Choice Types. The input is finite sets of worker types \(R\), firm types \(S\), and contract categories \(E\). A category \(e\) links a worker type \(r\) to a firm type \(s\) under specified terms. Each type has a rational mass, \(\mu_r\) or \(\mu_s\), and a complete choice correspondence over its contract categories satisfying SUB and BA. The decision variables are \(y_e\geq 0\), the mass matched under each category. They must obey the type capacities: for every type \(a\), \(\sum_{e\ni a}y_e\leq\mu_a\). A category may carry positive mass only if its singleton contract is individually rational for both endpoint types.

The question is to find any feasible mass allocation with no positive-mass replacement block. Precisely, for type \(a\) and incident category \(e\), define \(q_{a,e}(y)\) as the unmatched mass of type \(a\), plus the mass assigned to categories \(e'\ne e\) for which \(C_a(\{e,e'\})=\{e\}\). These are the agents of type \(a\) who would strictly choose \(e\) over their current contract, including those currently unmatched. The allocation is stable exactly when \(q_{r,e}(y)q_{s,e}(y)=0\) for every \(e\) linking \(r\) and \(s\). If both quantities were positive, equal positive masses on the two sides could form a blocking replacement. The variables describe how a cohort splits among assignments; each individual still receives at most one whole contract. There is no welfare objective in the paper’s underlying problem: the task is to return a stable allocation.

I expect this question to be Class A. Theorem 2’s proof supplies the structural route: use each type’s acyclic strict pairwise choices, extend them to an order on individually rational contracts, and adapt deferred acceptance to move masses through finite contract lists. That expectation needs proving, especially with partial acceptance of a type’s mass and with the bit complexity of rational masses. The main follow-up questions are whether the bulk algorithm is polynomial in the number of types, contract categories, encoding length, and choice data; and whether its rational output gives an exact correspondence with stable matchings in finite replications of the same types.

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s one-to-one result only. That is a meaningful part of the paper’s problem: the instance still has its choice correspondences, SUB and BA conditions, individual rationality, and replacement blocking. The full correspondence remains part of each type’s description; pairwise choices suffice for the stability test because that is how R-stability is defined.

The soft spot is that the paper proves constructive existence, not a complexity bound, and its most distinctive IRP and symmetric-algorithm result concerns the many-to-many model. So this is a credible continuous mirror of a specific theorem, rather than evidence that the paper’s full agenda has already opened a new complexity landscape. The authors and a referee should still recognize the question as theirs: it changes how the one-to-one population is represented while retaining the same stability problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the paper’s strongest anchor, and it has a real limitation for this programme: it proves existence, but states no complexity bound for a computational problem. The choice correspondences have no specified encoding, and the result’s algorithm is deferred acceptance on the finite contract set. So the paper itself gives no complexity landscape for a continuous model to extend.

The proponent’s mass-flow formulation needs an exchangeable market: every worker in a type must face the same contract categories and choice behavior, and every firm in a type must face the corresponding categories. The paper allows an arbitrary set of named bilateral contracts, with each agent’s choices defined over those contracts. In a sparse or identity-sensitive market, category flows can add pairings that were never available. Preserving the exact contract structure may require treating agents with different counterpart sets as different types, leaving support size close to the original population.

A stronger version would start with a block-complete placement market and quotient by renaming interchangeable agents. That is a coherent regime. It also narrows the result being mirrored: one-to-one R-stability tests only pairwise replacement, so the relevant choice data reduce to strict pairwise choices, which BA extends to orders. The resulting cohort problem would study how to compute stable flows as a function of type count and rational mass encoding. That could be useful, though it is an added complexity project rather than a complexity question posed by the paper.

I can’t honestly turn these limitations into a universal objection. A large, homogeneous placement market is plausible, and its mass-flow stability question is recognizable as a continuous mirror of Theorem 2. The negative case is strongest as a claim that the anchor is less compelling than it first appears; it does not defeat the anchor outright.
