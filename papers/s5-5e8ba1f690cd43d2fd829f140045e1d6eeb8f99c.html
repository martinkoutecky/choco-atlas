The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational anchor in this paper under your rule. Theorem 1, proved here, says that a \(t\)-price equilibrium exists under Assumptions 1 and 2, and that suitable budgets make every equilibrium allocation greedy under Assumption 3. That is an existence and structural result, not an algorithm or complexity classification. The other named theorems concern efficiency, fairness, asymptotic mechanism properties, or Lindahl-equilibrium existence. Section 6’s simulations use a customized algorithm, but give no named result or runtime bound for computing equilibria. So I can’t honestly supply a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem *per anchor*.

The strongest positive lead is a new computational question suggested by the paper, not a valid anchor. Call it *Continuous Sequential Price-Equilibrium Computation*. At stage \(t\), give an instance with \(m\) objects, a finite support of \(\tau\) complete agent types—each specifying an ordinal preference and arrival period—binary-rational masses for the remaining arrivals, binary-rational remaining supplies, and budgets. Specify the price noise finitely; for example, let every object receive the same shock \(c\sim\mathrm{Unif}[-\delta,\delta]\), with rational \(\delta\) below the minimum budget. The task is to output base prices \(p\ge0\) and a lottery \(a_i\) for every type such that \(a_i\) is that type’s expected demand under the randomized prices and

\[ \sum_i \mu_i a_{i,x}\le S_x \quad\text{for every }x, \qquad p_x>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_i\mu_i a_{i,x}=S_x. \]

A solution is an equilibrium price-and-allocation pair; the sequential version repeats this computation after each period’s supply is consumed. This mirrors the equilibrium subproblem that SEM solves. The underlying computation is clear; the paper’s Theorem 1 supplies existence, while the type masses, supplies, and price shocks specify the input needed to ask about algorithms.

A plausible regime is a large hospital, shelter, or placement service handling large weekly cohorts. Many arrivals in a cohort share the same complete preference type, and facility capacity scales with cohort size: population size \(n\) is large while the number of distinct types \(\tau\) remains moderate. The paper’s own application and replica markets make this regime credible, and its authors would recognize equilibrium computation as part of their mechanism. With the specified common uniform shock, expected demand is piecewise linear; that suggests a polynomial route when \(m\) is fixed. For variable \(m\), the paper gives no basis to predict tractability, hardness transfer, or continuum-specific hardness. A useful follow-up is to classify runtime in \(m,\tau,T\), and input bit length, and then connect equilibrium computation to finite-market implementation.

The soft spot is decisive for this screening task: the paper already formulates its large-market model using type-level arrival distributions and equilibrium allocations. This candidate is computational analysis of that model, not a new continuization of a discrete problem—and, above all, no numbered result in the paper establishes a computational result to anchor it. The case is a credible research direction, but it does not pass the stated source-anchor gate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous *mirror* is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 6 establish equilibrium existence; Theorems 2–4 concern efficiency and fairness; Theorem 5 gives properties of SEM and its asymptotic behavior. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic runtime bound. The paper mentions a customized algorithm for its simulations, but does not state a computational result about it. Calling SEM a mechanism does not fill that gap: its theorems establish what the mechanism achieves, not how efficiently its equilibria can be computed.

The proposed equilibrium-computation problem can be sharpened. With rational type masses, supplies, and budgets, a specified uniform price shock, and a precise exact or approximate output requirement, finding an equilibrium price and allocation could be a legitimate algorithmic project. The authors would likely recognize it as relevant. But the population is already aggregated in the paper’s large-market model: demand is weighted by type-level arrival probabilities, and the replica construction explicitly scales those masses and supplies. Studying the complexity of equilibrium computation would analyze an existing continuous-population model; it would not construct a continuous mirror of a discrete computational result in this paper. A plausible application or a better type space cannot supply the missing source anchor.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here. Repeated cohorts can share complete preference and arrival-time types, with capacity scaling along with arrivals; the paper itself develops that regime. Nor would I claim that analytic existence results already answer the computational question. They do not. Those points make equilibrium computation a credible follow-up, but do not turn it into an anchored mirror under the requested standard.

So the negative case is strong for a no-pass as a continuization candidate: there is no named computational result to anchor, and the best proposed task is new algorithmic analysis of a model that is continuous already. The broader claim that no worthwhile computational project could arise from this paper is weaker. Equilibrium computation may be worth pursuing in ChoCo, but I would classify it as a new project, not a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s results.
