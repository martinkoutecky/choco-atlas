The case FOR (proponent)

On your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational result. Propositions 10–15 establish comparative or game-theoretic properties; Proposition 16 gives Gaussian formulas used in the empirical analysis. None asserts tractability, hardness, or a parameterized result for computing the risk measures or allocations. The Shapley and Banzhaf sums range over coalitions, but that suggests a computational question; it is not a complexity result. So there are no honest anchors from which to pose one continuous prover question per result.

The strongest positive foothold is the model’s broad risk-allocation setting. Imagine a federation of many small cooperative lenders with a few standardized risk profiles. A type would include every feature the game uses—exposures, return and dependence behavior, distress eligibility, and risk weights—and \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s share of the institutions. One could let \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) be the distressed mass of type \(t\), extend the paper’s \(ES-SCoES\) cost to those mass coalitions, and ask for a continuous risk allocation across types. That is a plausible population-level version of their cost game, and the paper’s discussion of Aumann–Shapley allocation gives some reason the authors might recognize the direction.

But that is a proposed extension, not a result anchored in this paper. In the simplest mass model, where aggregate distressed returns scale linearly with \(z\), Proposition 12’s homogeneity makes the cost constant along each positive ray: \(c(sz)=c(z)\) for \(s>0\), while \(c(0)=0\). The natural radial Aumann–Shapley extension can therefore be discontinuous at zero and fail to recover the total cost. Resolving that requires an additional model choice about how returns aggregate across many institutions. The paper itself studies eight countries and computes allocations for seven potentially distressed players; it makes no scalability claim. That missing computational result is the weakest point—and, under your criteria, it prevents this from being a positive case with a qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Propositions 10–15 state properties of the risk measure and cooperative game; Proposition 16 supplies Gaussian formulas. None claims a complexity bound or gives an algorithm for a computational problem. The Shapley and Banzhaf formulas sum over coalitions, but the paper neither studies that cost nor claims a computational result about it. So there is no named result from which to pose the requested continuous prover question.

The best rescue is a large population of similar banks, grouped by complete risk type, with distress represented by a vector of type masses. One could extend the paper’s cost to those masses and define a continuum allocation, perhaps using an Aumann–Shapley value—the paper itself mentions that solution concept. That is a plausible new model, but it needs a new continuum game and a new computational question. The paper’s Shapley and Banzhaf values allocate marginal contributions among a finite set of named institutions; a mass allocation does not follow from those results.

The proposed homogeneity objection is narrower than it first appears. Proposition 12 scales returns within a fixed coalition. It does not establish what happens when the mass of a cohort of institutions changes: adding institutions can change the aggregate return distribution and its dependence on safe institutions. A model with perfectly shared shocks may inherit the ray-constant cost and an Aumann–Shapley discontinuity at zero; a model with independent or partially correlated returns need not. So that argument does not rule out every sensible population model.

The negative case is therefore strong against an *anchored mirror of this paper*, but weak as a universal claim. A block-structured population of banks could support a worthwhile continuous systemic-risk study. On the evidence here, though, it would be a new computational project, not a continuization of one of this paper’s computational results.
