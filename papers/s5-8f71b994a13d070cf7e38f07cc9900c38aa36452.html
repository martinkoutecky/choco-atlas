The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(n\), \(k\), and support bound \(\tau\), let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over complete binary \(k\)-bounded acyclic CP-net types, with support size at most \(\tau\). Given an exact oracle that returns \(q_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[N_t\text{ entails }x.1\succ x.2]\) for any swap \(x\), determine the query and computational complexity, in \(n\), \(k\), \(\tau\), and the response bit length \(L\), of outputting a mixture of at most \(\tau\) such types whose aggregate response agrees with \(q_\mu\) on every swap.

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete binary \(k\)-bounded acyclic CP-net types. Each swap query asks what fraction of mass prefers its first outcome; the output is any support-\(\tau\) mixture with the same responses on all swaps, rather than the identities of the hidden users or their exact type distribution.

The objection that survived

The model assumes exact access to population-wide fractional responses, abstracting away the cost of surveying users or estimating those rates; the resulting query bounds would depend on that oracle.

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Theorem 7's exact membership-query learning for one complete binary \(k\)-bounded CP-net to a population with at most \(\tau\) CP-net types. It leaves the paper's VC dimension, teaching dimension, recursive teaching dimension, and corrupted-oracle results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an equivalent support-\(\tau\) mixture be recovered in time polynomial in \(n\), \(\tau\), and \(L\) from exact aggregate swap responses?
  • What query lower bounds or non-identifiability barriers apply when distinct mixtures induce the same response function?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a query-learning problem for a large population of users with repeated preference models. Take a platform with \(M\) users choosing among bundles described by the same \(n\) binary attributes. A type is a complete acyclic \(k\)-bounded CP-net: the user’s dependency graph and every conditional preference order. Users of the same type are indistinguishable for this problem. In a plausible high-multiplicity regime, \(M\) is very large and the society has only \(\tau\) distinct types; type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

For a swap \(x=(o,o')\), ask what fraction of the population prefers \(o\) to \(o'\). The oracle returns
\[ q_\mu(x)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[N_t\text{ entails }o\succ o']. \]
The continuous problem is: given \(n,k\), a support bound \(\tau\), and access to this exact aggregate-response oracle, return a list of at most \(\tau\) complete acyclic \(k\)-bounded CP-net types with rational masses summing to one, such that their aggregate answer agrees with \(q_\mu(x)\) on every swap. The goal is to compute a compact representation of the population’s aggregate local preferences with few queries. It deliberately asks for an equivalent representation, not the literal hidden type distribution: different mixtures can induce the same swap-response rates.

My lead anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper. It states that for \(n\geq 1\), binary attributes, and \(0\leq k\leq n-1\), Algorithm 2 learns every complete binary \(k\)-bounded acyclic CP-net \(N^*\) using
\[ O\!\left(n\mathcal U_k+e_{N^*}\log_2 n\right) \]
membership queries over swaps, where \(e_{N^*}\) is the number of edges in the target network and \(\mathcal U_k\) is the size of a smallest binary \((n-1,k)\)-universal set. This is a query-complexity result for learning one hidden preference model, not a theorem about population mixtures. For fixed \(k\), it gives a concrete low-query baseline for asking whether exact query-efficient learning survives when answers are population fractions.

I would initially aim for a Class A result for fixed \(k\) and moderate \(\tau\): a poly\((n,\tau,L)\) algorithm, where \(L\) accounts for the bit lengths of the masses and oracle answers. The paper’s result gives a reason to pursue that target: each type has a compact dependency structure, and universal-set queries expose conditional preferences without querying every context. Exact fractional answers also contain more information per query than a single yes/no response. But the theorem does not establish this extension. Recovering a sparse mixture from marginal swap rates is a new inverse problem, and whether those rates admit an efficient, general-purpose reconstruction method is the central open question. Other natural follow-ups are whether a representation can be recovered with fewer queries than the number of swap contexts, and how the result changes with incomplete CP-nets or sampled, noisy population responses.

The scenario has real support in the paper’s own framing: it motivates preference learning through recommender systems serving large user groups, and its related-work discussion notes work on learning an average CP-net from multiple users. That prior work supports the high-multiplicity regime; it is not a collision, since the mirror here uses a continuous distribution of types and aggregate swap-response rates.

The weak point is the distance between the anchor and the mirror. Theorem 7 learns one coherent CP-net from that target user’s answers; the mirror learns a population-level mixture from fractional answers, which may not preserve enough information to recover the types. The paper’s authors might see this as a new multiuser problem adjacent to their contribution, rather than a direct continuization of their theorem. I think the mirror remains worth asking because the population interpretation is natural and the output is a well-defined aggregate object—but its tractability is a research conjecture, not a consequence of their result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7 is a genuine query-complexity result, so “there is no computational result to mirror” would be the wrong objection. Its problem, though, is specifically to identify one fixed target CP-net: every swap query goes to the same user, and the answer is a yes/no label determined by that user’s network.

There are two ways to extend that setup to a population. If everyone has the same CP-net, the aggregate answer is still just that yes/no label. The mass distribution is a point mass, and Theorem 7 applies unchanged; this leaves no population-level learning problem. If users have different CP-nets, the proposed answer \(q_\mu(x)\) is a new kind of oracle. It must survey or otherwise aggregate responses across users and return an exact rational fraction. Theorem 7’s query bound says nothing about the cost of obtaining that fraction. A sampled version would instead need a noise model, an accuracy target, and assumptions that make the aggregate rates estimable.

The proponent’s strongest repair is to make the output only an equivalent representation of the aggregate swap rates, rather than the hidden type distribution. That makes the target well-defined, but it changes what is being learned. The rate vector loses which preferences co-occur in the same person: different populations can agree on every \(q_\mu(x)\) while having different type distributions. It can serve queries about aggregate swap rates, but it cannot recover users’ networks for personalization. If the intended output is instead a collective ranking or decision, the model also needs to specify how fractional comparisons become a social choice; the rates alone need not define a consistent preference order.

A large platform with repeated preference templates is a plausible setting for a multiuser learning problem. The paper’s discussion of learning an average CP-net makes that nearby setting credible, not a collision. But turning it into aggregate learning requires a new observation model and a new target; it does not preserve the question or query guarantee of Theorem 7. I would therefore reject this as a worthwhile *continuous mirror of that result*, while recognizing that the resulting multiuser problem could be worthwhile on its own. The negative case is weakest on the universal claim: no modeling argument can rule out a carefully specified population-learning project that shares the paper’s subject matter.
