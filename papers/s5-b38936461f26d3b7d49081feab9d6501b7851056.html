What the mirror covers

The candidate covers replicated evaluation of the Minimal Overlap rule; it does not mirror the axiomatic uniqueness result or the paper’s additivity and continuity characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a high-multiplicity version of computing the Minimal Overlap allocation. Imagine a large pool of pari passu creditors holding standardized claims: many creditors share each claim amount, and the number of claim amounts is small. Let type \(t\) be a claim amount \(c_t\), let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s fraction of creditors, and let \(e\) be the estate per creditor, with \(0\le e\le\sum_t\mu_t c_t\).

One precise candidate is: given rational \((\mu_t,c_t)\) and \(e\), for each admissible integer \(q\), form the finite bankruptcy problem with \(q\mu_t\) creditors of type \(t\) and estate \(qe\). Compute each type’s average recovery under the Minimal Overlap rule, then determine whether those averages converge as \(q\to\infty\) and, if they do, compute the limiting recovery per creditor type. The output is the vector of limiting recoveries; this is rule evaluation, not an optimization problem. A large standardized creditor pool makes the regime plausible, and the authors could recognize recovery allocation as their subject. I would expect a tractable limit calculation after deriving it from the rule’s formulas, though that is a conjecture and a new result.

This is only an unanchored lead, however. Theorem 1 says that a bankruptcy rule satisfies Anonymity, Continuity and \(\mu\)-Additivity if and only if it is the Minimal Overlap rule. It is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about a problem being in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Proposition 1 establishes claims-truncation invariance under \(\mu\)-Additivity and Continuity; it is also not a computational result. The other stated findings are axiomatic too. So the paper has no qualifying computational anchor, and the candidate above cannot honestly be presented as a continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper. That is the positive case’s weakest point, and it prevents a fully anchor-compliant case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

No qualifying anchor survives, because the proponent raised none: it explicitly concedes that Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization and Proposition 1 is a structural identity, not a computational result. Those are not computational questions the continuization programme is meant to classify.

The creditor-pool example is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, so lack of a plausible population story is not the objection. But its strongest computational version is rule evaluation, and the paper’s formula makes that unusually direct. Write the positive claim types as \(c_1,\dots,c_\tau\), with masses \(p_i\), and let the estate per creditor be \(e\). Replicating the pool \(q\) times gives estate \(qe\). For any \(e>0\), this eventually exceeds the largest individual claim. In the paper’s alternative definition of the Minimal Overlap rule, the initial Ibn Ezra allocation then has total estate at most \(c_{\max}\), so its average contribution within each positive-mass type vanishes as \(q\) grows. The remaining allocation converges to constrained equal losses:
\[ r_i=\max(c_i-\lambda,0), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i\max(c_i-\lambda,0)=e. \]
For rational input, sorting the \(\tau\) claim levels and solving this one-variable piecewise-linear equation computes the limit directly. That is a clean limit calculation, but it appears to be a short consequence of the rule formula, not a complexity landscape or a problem whose tractability turns on new optimization machinery. With fixed total estate instead, per-creditor recovery vanishes.

A continuum axiomatization of anonymity, continuity, and \(\mu\)-additivity would still be an axiomatic question, which the programme excludes. A richer optimization problem would need to add a new task—such as strategic choice over mergers—that this paper does not study. So the strongest case against this paper is that its natural population mirror is outside the programme’s computational target, while the natural computational task is essentially closed-form.

The negative case is strong as a reason not to select this paper as a ChoCo target. It is weaker as the literal claim that no worthwhile continuous bankruptcy computation could exist: the homogeneous creditor regime is credible, and a separate computational problem in that setting might be worthwhile. But the proponent has not supplied such a problem anchored in a computational result of this paper.
