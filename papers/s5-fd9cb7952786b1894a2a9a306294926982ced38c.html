The continuous mirror question

Given a finite panel \(\mathcal Q\) of binary-rational mass distributions over the three types \(\{x\text{-preferring},\text{indifferent},y\text{-preferring}\}\), how many distinct labelings \(\lambda:\mathcal Q\to\{-1,0,1\}\) extend to a map \(F\) on the full mass simplex that is neutral under swapping the \(x\)- and \(y\)-preferring masses and monotone under increases in \(x\)-support and decreases in \(y\)-support?

The model it lives in

Use two alternatives and three voter types, with each society represented by a mass vector \(\mu=(\mu_+,\mu_0,\mu_-)\) in the simplex. The global decision variable is a social welfare map \(F\) from that simplex to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), constrained by neutrality and monotonicity; the objective is to count distinct restrictions of valid maps to the input panel \(\mathcal Q\).

The objection that survived

For arbitrary panels, the task counts restrictions of a family of axiomatic maps, and agreement on the full finite-electorate grid does not establish that the authors would regard panel-extension complexity as their question.

fatal: False

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize which labelings on \(\mathcal Q\) extend to a neutral monotone map on the full simplex.
  • Determine the exact complexity of counting extendable labelings in the panel size and the bit length of the rational masses.
  • Identify whether the fixed three-type case admits a polynomial-time algorithm, for example by dynamic programming over the induced dominance order.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible anchor is Proposition 9, proved in this paper. It states that the number of anonymous, neutral, and monotonic social welfare functions for \(n\) voters and two alternatives is \(\binom{n+1}{\lfloor (n+1)/2\rfloor}\). The paper derives this by counting threshold sequences; it also says Young et al. [8] had obtained the same count earlier. Proposition 9 is an exact counting result, not a theorem stated in complexity-class language. Still, with \(n\) measured as the electorate size, its formula gives a polynomial-time exact computation: the answer has \(O(n)\) bits. The paper has no named hardness result to anchor a claim that discrete hardness might dissolve.

Here is the continuous problem I would put forward as the mirror of Proposition 9: Continuous-panel SWF Behaviour Count. There are two alternatives, \(x\) and \(y\), and three voter types: prefer \(x\), indifferent, and prefer \(y\). A society is a mass distribution \(\mu=(\mu_+,\mu_0,\mu_-)\) in the simplex, with nonnegative real masses summing to one. An anonymous social welfare function is a map \(F\) from that whole simplex to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Require neutrality, \(F(\rho\mu)=-F(\mu)\), where \(\rho\) swaps \(\mu_+\) and \(\mu_-\); and monotonicity, \(F(\mu)\le F(\nu)\) whenever \(\nu_+\ge\mu_+\) and \(\nu_-\le\mu_-\).

An instance is a finite list \(\mathcal Q\) of societies with binary-rational masses. The question is: how many distinct output patterns on \(\mathcal Q\) are restrictions of some such global \(F\)? Count each pattern \(\lambda:\mathcal Q\to\{-1,0,1\}\) once, regardless of how many global rules extend it. The answer is a finite integer even though there are uncountably many possible global rules.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: large electorates in a referendum or binary-choice setting, where voters fall into just those three ballot types. Each society is represented by its type proportions, so the number of people can be far larger than the support size, which is at most three. A catalogue of rational mixes could represent the population compositions on which a rule designer wants to compare possible aggregation rules. The decision variable is the global winner map, or equivalently its positive region in the mass simplex; the counted solutions are its distinct behaviors on the catalogue.

There is also a direct bridge to Proposition 9. Let \(\mathcal Q\) be the full grid of distributions whose masses are multiples of \(1/n\). Its points are exactly the anonymous profiles of an \(n\)-voter electorate. Every continuous rule restricts to a rule on this grid. Conversely, a finite-grid rule satisfying anonymity, neutrality, and monotonicity extends to the whole simplex: take its grid points where \(x\) wins, extend that set upward in the monotonicity order, assign \(y\) to the reflected region, and assign ties elsewhere. Monotonicity and neutrality ensure those two regions do not overlap. Thus the restrictions on this grid are exactly the finite-electorate rules counted by Proposition 9. For a general input panel \(\mathcal Q\), the same problem asks how many behaviors on those rational mass points admit a globally valid continuous rule.

I would expect the fixed two-alternative, three-type version to be Class A: comparisons between societies form a two-dimensional dominance order, and valid rules have monotone boundaries that appear amenable to dynamic programming. That is a research conjecture, not a result of this paper. The immediate questions are whether panel consistency admits a clean characterization and whether the count can be computed in time polynomial in the number of queried societies and their bit length. Allowing more voter types or alternatives may change the complexity.

This mirror covers Proposition 9’s rule count only. Its weakest point is that the paper counts all rules on one finite electorate, whereas the continuous problem counts distinct restrictions to an input panel. The unrestricted continuum has no finite cardinality analogous to Proposition 9, and the paper supplies no canonical measure on its rule space. A referee could therefore regard the panel problem as a useful extension rather than the authors’ own continuous question. The case survives because the extension is exact on every finite-electorate grid, while arbitrary rational panels make society composition itself a continuous-domain input.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Proposition 9: it counts the anonymous, neutral, monotonic SWFs for a finite electorate. That is a real combinatorial result, but the paper does not formulate or analyze the complexity of a computational problem. Its other named results characterize rules or count sequences. Proposition 9 supplies a closed form for a finite domain; it does not give an input model or study the resources needed to solve a problem on a society.

The most faithful continuous version would count all anonymous, neutral, monotonic rules on the simplex of masses over the three ballot types. That loses the finite count: even the family that declares \(x\) the winner when \(\mu_+>\mu_-+\alpha\mu_0\), declares \(y\) symmetrically, and otherwise ties gives distinct rules for every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). The paper offers no canonical way to encode or measure this space of rules. Classifying its boundaries would be a question about axioms and the geometry of voting, rather than a computational problem on a supplied society.

The proposed panel count is the strongest repair, but it changes the task. On the full \(n\)-voter grid, the restrictions are exactly the finite-electorate rules counted by Proposition 9; that bridge keeps the original finite profile domain. For an arbitrary finite panel, the input is instead a catalogue of test societies, and the output counts which patterns on that catalogue can be extended to a globally valid rule. That could be a coherent rule-extension problem, but its central object is a family of axiomatic maps, not computation over one continuous society. The mass distributions serve as query points; the task does not ask how to alter, control, or assess a society.

So the negative case is strongest on scope: making populations continuous does not turn this paper’s rule-counting theorem into the kind of computational problem ChoCo is designed to chart. It is not airtight. A researcher interested in counting rule extensions might find the panel problem worthwhile, and its rational mass inputs are genuine. But that would be a new axiomatic counting direction, not a convincing continuous mirror of this paper’s result.
