What the mirror covers

The proposed statistic relates to the disagreement term in equation (2), but the paper’s result is about the asymptotic NDI of anonymous rules; it gives no computational result over continuous societies.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a population interpretation of Theorem 1, which is proved in this paper: for fixed \(m\), every sequence \((f_n)\) of anonymous social choice functions on \(n\)-voter profiles satisfies
\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{NDI}(f_n)=\frac{m-1}{m}. \]
This is the paper’s closest anchor, but it is an asymptotic theorem, not a named computational result asserting tractability or hardness. Strictly applying your anchor requirement, the paper has no qualifying computational anchor.

A natural continuous companion to Theorem 1 is Continuous NDI Evaluation. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking of \(A\), and let a society be a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, given by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types. Given \((A,\mu,a)\), where \(a\) is the society’s selected alternative, return the fraction of voter mass whose top-ranked alternative differs from \(a\):
\[ \mathrm{CNDI}(\mu,a) =\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}(t)\ne a}\mu_t =1-\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}(t)=a}\mu_t. \]
Here mass is the share of society holding each ranking; the benchmark is the top choice of a voter drawn from that mass. For fixed \(m\), a plausible regime is a large electorate with \(n\gg\tau\), such as repeated elections over a fixed set of alternatives where each ranking type has many voters.

There is a close bridge to the theorem. For anonymous finite rules, the paper’s equation (2) expresses NDI as the average, over profiles, of the fraction of voters whose top choice differs from the winner. Under the paper’s uniform profile model, the empirical distribution of rankings approaches the uniform distribution \(\mu^\circ\). At \(\mu^\circ\), every alternative is top-ranked by mass \(1/m\), so \(\mathrm{CNDI}(\mu^\circ,a)=(m-1)/m\) for every \(a\). That gives Theorem 1 a clear continuous-population reading.

For this proposed evaluation problem, the expected direction is tractability: scan the \(\tau\) supported rankings and sum the relevant masses, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. But this is a proposed extension, not a complexity result in the paper. It covers only Theorem 1’s asymptotic NDI claim; it does not supply a computational mirror of a hard problem or a substantive algorithmic challenge.

The soft spot is that the paper averages disagreement over all finite profiles, while Continuous NDI Evaluation measures disagreement within one society. The bridge is meaningful, but the continuous evaluation itself is simple, and the paper asks an asymptotic question rather than a computational one. A nonuniform population raises further questions about how the index behaves under different type distributions, and allowing \(m\) to grow with \(n\) leaves the theorem’s fixed-\(m\) setting. These are plausible follow-ons, but they do not turn this paper into a strong ChoCo complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its Theorem 1 is an asymptotic statement about the value of an index: for every sequence of anonymous rules, the NDI converges to \((m-1)/m\). The proof uses concentration under a uniform random profile. It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic problem over societies. The proponent acknowledges this, so the paper does not supply a qualifying ChoCo anchor.

The proposed Continuous NDI Evaluation is a sensible population statistic, but it is not the paper’s NDI. It measures, for one society and one selected alternative, the share whose top choice differs from that alternative. The paper instead measures the distance between an entire social choice function and the closest voter-specific dictator, averaging over all finite profiles. Its equation (2) shows that a society’s mass disagreement is an *integrand* in that average; it does not make the integrand the index. At the uniform society, the proposed statistic returns \((m-1)/m\), but matching the theorem’s limit at that one point does not preserve its claim about every anonymous rule.

The stronger version would define a continuous rule \(F\) on distributions and average its mass disagreement over some distribution of societies. But the paper’s uniform random profiles converge to the single uniform society. In that limit the averaging distribution collapses, and the theorem reduces to evaluating the share disagreement there. Choosing a richer distribution over societies could produce a different, potentially useful question, but the paper supplies no such distribution; it would be a new model, and computing the proposed share statistic for a given society is just a sum over its \(\tau\) types.

There is also a structural mismatch behind the statistic. The paper’s benchmark is a dictator: a named voter whose top choice determines the outcome. An atomless society has no positive-mass individual for an anonymous rule to treat as dictator. Comparing the winner with the top choice of a random mass unit is a plausible replacement, but it changes the benchmark from distance to a dictatorial *rule* into within-society disagreement. Keeping a named voter or a finite sample could restore the original comparison, but then the individual identity the continuum was meant to absorb remains in the model.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated purpose: a continuous population reading of the theorem is easy to state, and its share-disagreement statistic may be useful descriptively, but neither yields a computational mirror of a result in this paper. The honest limit of the claim is that one could develop a worthwhile adjacent project around such population statistics; it would not be a continuization of this paper’s computational problem.
