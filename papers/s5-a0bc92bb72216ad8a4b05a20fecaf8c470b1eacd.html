The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an eligible named computational result to anchor a positive case on. The paper’s Lemma 1 says that a consistent aCP-net always yields a valid CP-net at each point in the search. That is a semantic guarantee, not a complexity result. Algorithm 1 gives a consistency test, and the text states an \(O(n|\hat C|)\) bound under a bounded-parent-size assumption, but that bound is not asserted by a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition. The dominance-testing discussion also gives no complexity classification.

A mass model for users of a product configurator is imaginable, but turning the paper’s individual preference and dominance questions into population-level questions would add a new problem rather than mirror a named computational result. Under the stated anchor requirement, there is no defensible lead anchor and no continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper does not establish a computational result about a population. Lemma 1 is a semantic guarantee: consistent changes preserve valid CP-nets. It gives no complexity classification to continuize.

Algorithm 1 is the closest candidate, and the paper does state an \(O(n|\hat C|)\) consistency-checking bound under a bounded-parent assumption. Even treating that as an anchor, a society of \(\tau\) complete aCP-net types would be checked type by type. Asking whether all types are consistent takes \(O(\tau n|\hat C|)\); asking what fraction of the society has consistent networks adds a weighted sum. The masses do not affect the consistency condition or create an optimization problem. That is a population statistic, but a thin mirror of the algorithm.

Dominance testing offers a more ambitious possibility: given two configurations, compute the fraction of users whose aCP-nets prefer one to the other. But that makes a collective query from a paper whose dominance questions are individual, and the paper supplies no complexity result for them to mirror. A mass-customization market can plausibly have repeated, fully specified preference types; the high-multiplicity regime itself is not the objection. The objection is that turning individual dominance into a population-level question supplies the main new problem.

So I see no worthwhile *mirror of this paper’s computational results*. I cannot honestly rule out a useful new project on collective dominance for a population of aCP-net users. That would be an extension built on the paper’s model, rather than a continuous counterpart of a computational result it proves.
