The continuous mirror question

Given a horizon \(T\), common cost functions satisfying the paper’s assumptions, and \(\tau\) retailer types with rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\) and rational per-unit-mass demand profiles \(d^r\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^T\), compute cost-share rates \(a_r\). For each fractional subpopulation \(\nu\) with \(0\le \nu_r\le\mu_r\), let \(D_t(\nu)=\sum_r \nu_r d_t^r\) and let \(F(\nu)\) be the minimum ELS cost of meeting \(D(\nu)\). Require \(\sum_r\mu_r a_r=F(\mu)\) and \(\sum_r\nu_r a_r\le F(\nu)\) for every such \(\nu\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete retailer demand-profile types. A type’s mass scales its demand, so a subpopulation \(\nu\le\mu\) has aggregate demand \(D_t(\nu)=\sum_r\nu_r d_t^r\); its coalition cost is the paper’s ELS cost \(F(\nu)\). The decision is to compute per-unit-mass cost shares \(a_r\) that are efficient for the grand society and stable against every fractional subpopulation. Cost functions need finite rational descriptions that support exact polynomial-time evaluation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s computation of a core allocation for the general concave-cost ELS game. It leaves aside the nonconcavity result, core membership testing, population-monotonic allocation schemes, and other cooperative solution concepts.

Open questions for a prover

  • Given rational rates \(a_r\), what is the complexity of deciding whether \(\sum_r\nu_r a_r\le F(\nu)\) for every \(\nu\in[0,\mu]\)?
  • Can core allocations be selected consistently across all fractional subpopulations to form a population-monotonic allocation scheme?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s core-allocation computation, not its existence result alone.

Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that for the economic lot-sizing game with backlogging, nondecreasing concave ordering costs, and linear inventory-holding and backlogging costs, the core is nonempty and a core allocation can be found in polynomial time. The paper gives no comparable named hardness result for this task; its question about the complexity of testing core membership is left open. Theorem 2 is therefore the lead anchor.

I would formulate its continuous mirror as Continuous ELS Core Allocation. Take a horizon \(T\), common cost functions \(c_t\) and \(h_t^+,h_t^-\) satisfying the paper’s assumptions, and a finite set \(R\) of \(\tau\) retailer types. A type \(r\) is a complete demand profile \(d^r\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^T\); \(\mu_r>0\) is its fraction of the population, with \(\sum_{r\in R}\mu_r=1\). For a standard finite input, the cost functions have rational descriptions that permit exact polynomial-time evaluation at rational quantities.

A coalition is any subpopulation \(\nu\) with \(0\le\nu_r\le\mu_r\). Its aggregate demand in period \(t\) is \(D_t(\nu)=\sum_r\nu_r d_t^r\). Let \(F(\nu)\) be the minimum ELS cost of meeting that demand: minimize \(\sum_{t=1}^T[c_t(z_t)+h_t^+I_t^++h_t^-I_t^-]\) over nonnegative orders, inventory, and backlog, subject to \(z_t+I_{t-1}^+-I_{t-1}^-=D_t(\nu)+I_t^+-I_t^-\) and \(I_0^+=I_0^-=0\).

The question is: compute a cost-share rate \(a_r\) for each type such that \(\sum_r\mu_r a_r=F(\mu)\) and, for every subpopulation \(\nu\le\mu\), \(\sum_r\nu_r a_r\le F(\nu)\). Rates are shares per unit of population mass, so integrating them over a coalition gives its assigned cost. They may be produced as \(a_r=\sum_t b_t d_t^r\), where \(b\) is a period-price vector. This is the underlying core-allocation problem, not merely a request to solve an encoding.

The expected classification is tractable. Theorem 2’s proof supplies a route: compute grand-coalition demand \(D(\mu)\), solve the paper’s price LP (19), and assign each type its price-weighted demand. That LP has \(T\) price variables and \(O(T^2)\) interval constraints. The paper’s monotonicity argument makes the resulting prices feasible for every coordinatewise smaller demand vector. Since every \(\nu\le\mu\) has \(D(\nu)\le D(\mu)\), the argument gives the required core inequality for every fractional coalition as well. The support size \(\tau\) affects aggregation and the output, while the LP’s structural dimension is \(T\).

A credible regime is a large network of small outlets buying the same product through a purchasing cooperative or central supplier. Many outlets can share a demand profile—say, a standardized weekly trajectory for a store format and region—so the number of outlets can be much larger than the number of types. Mass represents outlet share; demand vectors are per-unit-mass demand, making a coalition’s demand scale with its mass. Fractional aggregate demand is a natural consequence of that population model. Ordering, inventory, and backlog decisions remain the paper’s decisions, and the deviation test remains its core condition.

This is a plausible continuation of the authors’ problem: it preserves their ELS cost function, joint-ordering economies, and question of whether any coalition can do better. The mirror covers only Theorem 2’s task of computing a core allocation; it does not claim results about core membership, population-monotonic schemes, or other cooperative solution concepts. Those suggest follow-up questions: can a given continuous allocation be tested for core membership efficiently, and can one choose allocations consistently across all subpopulations to obtain a population-monotonic scheme?

The weakest point is the population regime. Retailers are firms, and an individual outlet cannot literally split across coalitions. The mirror is most convincing for very large networks in which each type has many outlets and fractional participation is a useful aggregate approximation. It also stays within the paper’s common-cost assumptions; type-specific ordering or holding costs would require an extension and a new proof. Finally, cooperative cost allocation sits at the edge of a narrow voting-centered view of computational social choice. This is a strong mirror of the authors’ computational problem, though its fit with ChoCo depends on treating coalition cost allocation as within the programme’s remit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that its population layer appears to add little computational structure. For a type \(r\) with demand profile \(d^r\), a coalition \(\nu\) matters only through its aggregate demand,
\[ D_t(\nu)=\sum_r \nu_r d_t^r. \]
Its cost is \(C(D(\nu))\). Theorem 2 already constructs period prices \(b_t\) that give each type a share rate \(a_r=\sum_t b_t d_t^r\). The paper’s monotonicity argument covers every smaller aggregate demand, so it also covers fractional subpopulations. The continuous problem therefore seems to reuse the paper’s price LP, with the support size \(\tau\) affecting aggregation and output but not the optimization.

That makes this a low-yield candidate for ChoCo’s complexity landscape: there is no new pricing problem or population-driven algorithmic obstacle to investigate. But that is not enough to defeat the mirror under the programme’s stated standard. An easy continuous answer is still an answer, and fractional coalitions are a distinct model even when the existing proof extends directly.

The obvious modeling concerns also seem repairable. A normalized population measure needs demand expressed per unit of mass, or a population-scale parameter, to represent the total load of a large retailer network. The cost functions need a finite input representation, such as rational piecewise-linear concave functions. And networks with many outlets sharing standardized demand profiles provide a credible high-multiplicity regime. None of those choices appears to rule out a version the authors would recognize.

So I cannot honestly make the universal negative case. Theorem 2 is a strong anchor: the mirror is well-posed and plausibly worthwhile as part of a systematic classification, even if its particular continuous result may be an immediate extension of the paper’s algorithm. The against case is weakest exactly where it must be strongest: I have no sound reason to rule out every meaningful high-multiplicity scenario.
