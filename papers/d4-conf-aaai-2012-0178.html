The claim that there is no eligible anchor is too quick. The paper has no numbered theorem stating a complexity result, and the cited NP-hardness is prior work. But Sections 3.5 and Table 2 do give this paper’s own worst-case resource bounds for RB-LBP: \(O(GA)\) memory and communication per agent, and \(O(GA^2)\) computation per agent per iteration. Those claims support a continuous question, even if they are not packaged as a theorem.

The difficulty for that mirror is that the bounds measure the structure of an individual trading network: its goods, degrees of competition, and local communication. A distribution over agent types does not preserve an arbitrary TDN’s partner graph. If type includes the full neighborhood and partner-specific terms, the support can grow to \(n\), leaving no meaningful multiplicity. If a continuous model instead assumes that suppliers and buyers of compatible types can trade anonymously, it has changed the network structure that RB-LBP was designed to handle. Its aggregate mass flows do not directly test the paper’s claims about local messages, privacy, or resource use on TDNs.

The stronger alternative is to mirror the underlying maximum-surplus SCF problem in a market with many interchangeable suppliers, consumers, and production types. Per-capita supplies and demands would scale with the population, and a mass-based allocation would be a coherent question. But it would study aggregate supply-chain optimization, rather than the paper’s decentralized message-passing contribution. That is a real distinction in what the result measures; the possibility that the resulting optimization is tractable would not itself count against the mirror.

So the strongest negative case is that the paper’s distinctive results have no faithful continuous-population counterpart for general TDNs. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is weak: an exchangeable-market version of the underlying SCF problem is plausible, and the paper does not rule out its value. The proponent is right about the missing numbered theorem, but that alone does not establish that there is no mirror to ask.
