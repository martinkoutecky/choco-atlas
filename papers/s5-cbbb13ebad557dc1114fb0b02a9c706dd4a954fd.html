The appended case raises no continuous mirror to rebut; it argues that the paper has no computational anchor. That screen is too narrow here. Algorithm 1 names TAAUDA, and Section 4.3 gives a finite-step termination argument and the runtime bound \(O(n^2m^3l^3)\) for unit adjustment steps, calling the method polynomial-time and inexact. The bound is not a classification of the underlying optimization problem, but it is a concrete computational result about the paper’s central algorithm.

A natural mirror is a large hiring or service market with recurring jobs and workforce cohorts sharing skill profiles. Let service type \(A\subseteq S\) have mass \(\mu_A\), and task type \(q=(B_q,u_q)\) have mass \(\nu_q\), required skills \(B_q\), and utility \(u_q\) per unit of completed task mass. An assignment \(x_{Aq}\) uses each unit of service mass at most once; \(z_q\) is the mass of type-\(q\) tasks completed. Feasibility requires \(0\le z_q\le\nu_q\) and, for every \(s\in B_q\), \(\sum_{A:s\in A}x_{Aq}\ge z_q\). This preserves the paper’s complementary-skill requirement, while allowing a service type with several skills to contribute them together. The welfare benchmark is \(\max\sum_q u_qz_q\).

The paper’s central design question carries over: can task types split their per-unit utility into skill shares \(p_{q,s}\), with \(\sum_{s\in B_q}p_{q,s}=u_q\), so that self-interested service mass chooses assignments whose completed-task welfare is near that benchmark? With an anonymous rule for allocating oversubscribed skill slots, this becomes a type-level equilibrium and algorithm question: does a TAAUDA-style adjustment converge, what approximation can it guarantee, and can it run in time polynomial in the numbers of service and task types, the skill-set size, and the input bit length? Population mass affects which task mass can actually be completed, so the continuum does substantive work. Individual jobs remain integral; assignments are fractional only when summarized over a type’s mass.

That regime is credible in repeated hiring and standardized service markets, and the paper itself motivates its model with recruitment. Types can include all relevant attributes, such as skill sets and outside options. The paper’s finite runtime bound does not settle the mass-based incentive problem, which is precisely where a computational mirror could contribute. The case is not airtight: the paper gives no theorem-style complexity classification, and the continuous incentive model needs a carefully specified rationing rule.
