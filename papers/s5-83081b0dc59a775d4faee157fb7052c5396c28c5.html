The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(A\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over partial orders \(\Pi_t\) on \(A\), with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), decide whether there exist a linear order \(>\) on \(A\) and a weak-order completion \(\succeq_t\) of each \(\Pi_t\) such that the completed support is top monotonic with respect to \(>\), as in Definition 1.

The model it lives in

The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over partial-order types on \(A\). Choose a common order \(>\) and one weak-order completion \(\succeq_t\) per positive-mass type; the objective is feasibility of top monotonicity on the completed support.

The objection that survived

Feasibility is invariant under reweighting positive-mass types, so this mirror yields no mass-sensitive complexity phenomenon; the proponent acknowledges this, and it limits the contribution’s novelty.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s NP-completeness result for completing partial orders to a top-monotone profile. It leaves the weak-order testing results and the dichotomous-preference example aside.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Partial-Order Top-Monotonicity Testing. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: testing whether a profile of partial orders can be extended to weak orders that are top monotonic is NP-complete. The paper proves this by reducing non-betweenness constraint consistency to the test; its Lemma 2 cites Guttman and Maucher [20] for the NP-completeness of that constraint problem.

The continuous instance has a finite alternative set \(A\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over partial orders on \(A\). Each positive-mass type \(t\) is one partial preference order \(\Pi_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that order. The question is whether there is a linear order \(>\) on \(A\) and, for each type \(t\), a weak-order completion \(\succeq_t\) of \(\Pi_t\), such that the resulting society is top monotonic with respect to \(>\). Apply the paper’s definition to the support of the completed distribution: its alternatives, top sets, and required inequalities are exactly those in Definition 1, with types in place of voters. A solution is the common order and the completions; there is no optimization objective.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a large consultation on a fixed set of policy alternatives. Respondents may provide partial rankings, and many may share the same pattern of comparisons; for instance, a survey could have a very large number of responses but only dozens of recurring partial-order types. The distribution records their prevalence. Giving every member of a type the same completion is faithful here: if a voter-by-voter completion works, retaining one representative of each partial-order type preserves top monotonicity, since deleting voters can only remove conditions. That representative’s completion can then be used for the whole type.

The expected classification is Class B: hardness transfers. Any finite discrete profile embeds by assigning each distinct partial order its count divided by the total number of voters. The continuous test accepts exactly when the original profile accepts, so Theorem 2’s NP-hardness carries over; an order and one completion per support type give a polynomial-size certificate. This is not a claim that continuity makes the test harder or easier. It makes the society a distribution over types, while preserving the paper’s actual question: can one common societal order support suitable completions?

The weakest point is also clear: top monotonicity is a support property. Once a type has positive mass, its prevalence does not affect feasibility—a tiny positive group constrains the test just as much as a large one. So the mirror is computationally faithful, but it does not produce a mass-sensitive LP or a new tractability gain from fractional population sizes. The hardness reduction’s ordering constraints remain. I think the authors would recognize the mirror because it preserves their partial-order completion test and common-order requirement; whether a referee sees much value in a continuous representation whose weights do not affect the answer is the real vulnerability.

This anchor does not settle the paper’s open question for weak orders: Theorem 1 reduces that test to non-betweenness constraint consistency, but the paper does not prove the weak-order test NP-hard. The continuous version also raises parameterized questions, especially whether the partial-order test becomes tractable for bounded support size.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor, and the proposed distribution over partial-order types is a faithful way to encode its instances. My objection is that the theorem’s predicate contains no population multiplicity. If \(P\) is the support of the distribution, then the test depends on \(P\) alone: replacing every positive mass while keeping \(P\) fixed leaves the answer unchanged. Repeated voters with the same partial order add no conditions, and a completion chosen for one representative can be used for the whole type.

That makes the consultation scenario plausible as a population, but its prevalences have no role in the question. A type with an arbitrarily small positive share constrains top monotonicity as much as the majority type. After zero-mass types are removed, the input is a set of partial orders—the finite profile with duplicate voters coalesced. This is a coherent support-of-a-measure query, but it offers no computational question about population scale or mass.

The strongest mass-sensitive repair would ask how much population must be removed to obtain a top-monotonic profile. Yet top monotonicity depends on support, so retaining any positive mass of a type preserves all its constraints; partial trimming never helps. The repair therefore becomes a weighted choice of which whole types to discard. That may be a worthwhile robustness problem, but it changes the task into weighted outlier removal. A violation-rate version would also need a new way to weight the definition’s many logical conditions. Neither supplies a continuous mirror of Theorem 2.

So the negative case is strong against spending ChoCo effort on this as a population continuization: the named theorem survives exactly as a finite support problem, with masses playing no part. The limit of the case is that someone may still value the support query itself or pursue the weighted robustness extension. I cannot rule out that research interest; it does not establish a mass-bearing continuous mirror of the paper’s result.
