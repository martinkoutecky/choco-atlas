The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and for each type a group label, a strict school ranking, and a priority tier at each school, take school priority tiers to order types, with ties only among identical types. Given rational capacities \(q_c\) and reserves \(r^m_c\), with \(0\le r^m_c\le q_c\) and \(\sum_c q_c\ge 1\), and a mass allocation \(x^Q\) stable under majority quotas \(q^M_c=q_c-r^m_c\), compute an allocation \(x^R\) stable under minority reserves. Require that the old and new assignments admit a coupling, type by type, in which every unit of mass weakly prefers its new school and a positive total mass strictly prefers it, unless \(x^Q\) is already stable under reserves. Stability uses the paper’s reserve rule on mass: a positive mass of a type blocks a school if it prefers that school and the rule would admit that mass by filling vacancies or displacing lower-priority mass.

The model it lives in

A normalized continuum of students with finitely many complete types \(t\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\); types encode group, preferences, and school priority tiers. Rational capacity and reserve masses scale with the population. The decision variables \(x^R_{t,c}\) allocate each type’s mass across schools, subject to capacity and reserve-aware stability, while requiring a typewise coupling to the given quota-stable allocation that weakly improves every unit and strictly improves some positive mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s comparison between majority quotas and minority reserves for deferred acceptance. It leaves the paper’s TTC results, strategic properties, other welfare theorems, and simulations outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the reserve-stable, weakly Pareto-improving allocation be computed in time polynomial in the number of schools, support size, and input bit length?
  • Does the finite improvement argument extend to fractional type allocations with priority tiers and the stated mass-level blocking condition?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is around Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says: given majority quotas \(q^M\) and minority reserves \(r^m\) with \(r^m=q-q^M\), every matching stable under the majority quotas is either already stable under the corresponding reserves, or is Pareto dominated by a matching stable under those reserves. The proof gives a constructive improvement procedure. The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard, so this is an algorithmic welfare anchor, not a discrete-hardness result.

A plausible regime is a large centralized school district with many students but a much smaller number of recurring preference and admission-priority profiles. A type \(t\) records a student’s group, complete school ranking, and priority category at each school; students of that type share all those attributes. Let \(\mu_t\) be the type’s fraction of the population, and let \(q_c\) and \(r^m_c\) be school \(c\)’s capacity and minority reserve as fractions of the population. Capacities scale with the district, while each unit of student mass still receives one school. A type’s mass can be split across schools, so the aggregate allocation is fractional even though each student’s assignment is not.

The corresponding problem, Continuous DA Reserve Improvement, is: given the rational masses \((\mu_t)\), school capacities and reserves, type preferences and school priorities, and a mass allocation \(x^Q\) stable under majority quotas \(q^M_c=q_c-r^m_c\), find an allocation \(x^R\) stable under minority reserves. Require that, for each type, the old and new assignments can be coupled so every unit of mass weakly prefers its new school, with a positive mass strictly preferring it somewhere. Stability uses the paper’s reserve rule at mass scale: a school fills reserve mass with its highest-priority minority applicants, then fills remaining capacity from the applicants left. A blocking pair is a positive mass of a type that prefers a school and would be accepted there under that rule.

I expect this mirror to be tractable when the support size \(\tau\) is moderate: a mass version of student-proposing deferred acceptance works over finitely many types and schools, with rational masses as input. The complexity question is whether the stable, Pareto-improving reserve allocation can be found in time polynomial in the number of schools, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. This is a direct continuation of Theorem 1’s question about replacing rigid majority caps with reserves while improving student welfare. It covers that DA comparison result; it does not cover the paper’s TTC results, simulations, or broader strategic properties. It also generates questions about multiple student groups and about how continuous reserve outcomes relate to finite high-multiplicity instances.

The weak point is that the regime needs genuinely shared priority profiles as well as shared rankings. If every student has an idiosyncratic strict priority at even one school, \(\tau\) can approach the population size. Priority tiers and repeated preference lists make the proposed regime plausible, but they coarsen the paper’s strict individual priorities. And because Theorem 1 is a welfare result rather than a hardness result, this mirror would extend the authors’ matching question into a computational one; it would not show that discrete hardness dissolves in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational-complexity result. It proves a welfare comparison between mechanisms and gives a constructive improvement procedure, but states no complexity bound for a computational problem. The paper specifies no search objective, input encoding, or complexity classification for that procedure. Under the programme’s stated screening rule, there is no computational result here to continuize: “Continuous DA Reserve Improvement” would be a new computational question inspired by the theorem, not a mirror of a computational contribution.

The proposed school-district regime is plausible, but its types expose a modeling cost. In the paper, each school has a strict priority order over individual students. If a type records each student’s priority position at every school, those positions distinguish the students, so the support need not stay small as the district grows. Aggregating them into priority tiers or ties restores repeated types, but changes the priority structure and thus the stability comparisons the paper studies. A distribution over priority ranks is another sensible repair, though it asks for a new continuous-priority model. In either case, the typewise coupling and mass-level blocking condition require their own formulation and result; they are not already supplied by Theorem 1.

That said, this is a weak negative, not a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Large districts with recurring preference lists and priority tiers are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and a continuous reserve comparison there could be well-posed and useful. The honest case against is that it would open a new computational line around this paper rather than continuize one of its computational results. I cannot support the universal claim that no scenario is worthwhile.
