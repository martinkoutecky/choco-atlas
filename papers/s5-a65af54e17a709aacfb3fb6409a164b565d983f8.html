The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a grounded positive case that meets the anchor requirement: the paper has no numbered result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar.

The closest result is Theorem 14, proved here. It says that for a positive target vector \(p\), a matrix \(K\) that respects the linear relations among the measures and has zero row sums, and \(0\le\delta\le B\), the matrix \(G^+(P+\delta K)\) is row-stochastic, \(GG^+(P+\delta K)=P+\delta K\), and \(P+\delta K\) is a sharing matrix. Thus there is a partition with \(\mu_i(X_j)=p_j+k_{ij}\delta\). This is a constructive existence result with an explicit bound, not a named complexity result. The decision method in Section 3 and the construction for piecewise-constant densities are presented in unnumbered prose.

The nearest plausible population mirror would give each valuation type \(t\) a population mass \(\lambda_t\), with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\), and ask whether a per-capita allocation rule \(a_t(x)\)—the fraction of type \(t\) receiving cake point \(x\)—can satisfy resource constraints \(\sum_t\lambda_t a_t(x)\le 1\) and type-level fairness relations such as \(\int_X a_s\,d\nu_t\;r_{ts}\;p_s\). This is a sensible research question about many agents and few valuation types, with per-capita resource supply. For piecewise-constant valuations it looks amenable to a finite feasibility formulation, but the paper does not prove a complexity result for it, so it cannot serve as an anchored continuous problem here.

The weak point is substantial: the paper makes the cake—the outcome—continuous while keeping a finite list of \(n\) players. Moving to a continuum of agents and type-average bundles changes what counts as envy-free. I would not attribute that population model, or a Class A classification for it, to the authors’ results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result. Theorem 14 proves an existence statement for \(n\) individually indexed players: under conditions on their measures and \(K\), a sharing matrix \(P+\delta K\) exists, with an explicit bound on \(\delta\). It gives no complexity classification or running-time result. The paper’s decision and construction methods appear later in prose, but neither is a named computational theorem. So there is no paper result here whose complexity landscape a population-continuous version could chart.

The better population model would give each valuation type \(t\) mass \(\lambda_t\), and assign its members a per-capita bundle \(a_t(x)\), with aggregate feasibility such as \(\sum_t\lambda_t a_t(x)\le 1\). That can be a sensible fair-division problem. But it is not Theorem 14 carried over: the theorem’s sharing matrix has columns for individual recipients and rows summing to one. With cohorts, the columns represent average bundles and resource use is weighted by \(\lambda_t\). Either the weights are ignored, in which case the population distribution adds nothing, or they are included, in which case the feasibility model and fairness question have changed. A finite LP for piecewise-constant valuations would extend the paper’s unnumbered methods, but would not supply the missing computational anchor.

There is also a specific mismatch with the theorem’s interesting cases. Its arbitrary hyper-envy patterns depend on linear independence among the measures. Repeating a valuation across a large cohort makes those measures linearly dependent; the paper’s properness conditions then rule out many such patterns. Aggregating to \(\tau\) types may make a mass model tractable, but that is a new type-level economy whose main computational input is \(\tau\), not a complexity result about the paper’s finite-player theorem.

That is a strong negative case for a *paper-anchored* mirror. It does not prove that continuous-population fair division is unworthy in general: the per-capita cohort model could be worth studying on its own. The honest weak point is precisely that the paper gives too little computational content to rule out that adjacent question universally.
