The continuous mirror question

Given a shared unknown \(S\)-state, \(A\)-action MDP with horizon \(H\), known reward functions for \(\tau\) stakeholder types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, characterize the minimax cumulative regret over \(K\) episodes against the policy maximizing \(\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}(V_t^\pi)^{\mu_t}\), and determine whether an efficient learner attains the resulting rate.

The model it lives in

A centralized learner chooses one policy per episode in a shared unknown MDP. Stakeholders are grouped by complete reward function, type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), and the policy is evaluated by weighted Nash welfare \(\prod_t(V_t^\pi)^{\mu_t}\); regret compares each chosen policy with the optimal policy for that objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Nash-welfare learning problem anchored by Theorem 10; it leaves the axiomatic characterization, welfare guarantee, regret upper bounds in Theorems 7–9, and the weaker minimum-welfare regret result in Theorem 11 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the minimax regret as a function of \(S,A,H,\tau,L,K\), including any dependence on the smallest positive mass?
  • Can an efficient online learner attain that rate, and which lower-bound dependencies are unavoidable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification theorem. My strongest computational anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper (Appendix F.1): if \(K\ge \Omega(\log H)\) and \(H\ge \Omega(\log S)\), then for every learning policy \(\pi\), there is an \(S\)-state, \(A\)-action MDP on which its expected Nash-welfare regret over \(K\) episodes is at least
\[ C n\left(\frac{H}{2}\right)^n\sqrt{SAK}, \]
for a universal constant \(C>0\). It is a regret lower bound, rather than a complexity-class result, but it gives a concrete learning problem whose dependence on the number of agents is striking.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a public-health system learning one shared policy for a large population. Each person’s reward function describes how they value the system’s outcomes; many people in the same clinical or geographic cohort may share that complete reward function. The types are the distinct reward functions, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with type \(t\). In this regime, the number of people can be very large while the support size \(\tau\) remains moderate. The paper already studies a common policy evaluated by multiple stakeholders with different rewards, so treating repeated reward functions as a mass of like agents is a recognizable extension of its model.

I would call the mirror Continuous Stakeholder Nash-Regret. An instance gives \(S,A,H\), an initial-state distribution \(\rho\), a support of \(\tau\) known reward functions \(r_t:S\times A\to[0,1]\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, encoded in \(L\) bits. The transition kernel \(P^\star\) is unknown. For policy \(\pi\), let \(V_t^\pi(P^\star)\) be its expected episodic value for type \(t\), and define per-capita Nash welfare by
\[ W_\mu^\pi(P^\star) =\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}\bigl(V_t^\pi(P^\star)\bigr)^{\mu_t}, \]
with welfare zero if any positive-mass type has value zero. In each episode the learner chooses a possibly nonstationary policy and observes the resulting trajectory. Its regret is
\[ \operatorname{Reg}_\mu(K) =\sum_{e=1}^{K}\left( W_\mu^{\pi^\star_\mu}(P^\star)-W_\mu^{\pi_e}(P^\star) \right), \qquad \pi^\star_\mu\in\arg\max_\pi W_\mu^\pi(P^\star). \]
The prover’s task is to characterize the minimax regret and give an efficient online learner, or establish a lower bound ruling out the desired rate. The tractability target is runtime polynomial in \(S,A,H,\tau,L\), with regret polynomial in the structural parameters and sublinear in \(K\). This is the population-level version of learning a policy that is fair across reward-bearing stakeholders.

The connection to the paper is exact at the level of the optimal policy: if a finite population has \(n_t\) people of type \(t\), with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), then
\[ \bigl(W_\mu^\pi\bigr)^n=\prod_{i=1}^{n}V^\pi(r_i). \]
Thus the weighted objective has the same optimizer as the paper’s Nash social welfare for that finite electorate. Taking the \(n\)-th root is the natural per-person normalization when mass sums to one. This is a population continuization: the policy remains the decision variable, while the distribution over stakeholders’ types becomes the continuous object.

I would provisionally expect the continuous problem to be tractable (Class A) when \(\tau\) is moderate. With a known transition kernel, maximizing log welfare amounts to maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\log V_t^\pi\) over the occupancy-measure polytope, a convex-optimization formulation. That gives a concrete route into the optimization machinery the programme is meant to expose. The unknown-kernel learning problem is harder: the paper’s optimistic-planning approach is a plausible starting point, but a regret analysis must handle types with very small mass and values near zero. Further questions include how the regret depends on \(\tau\), the smallest positive mass, and \(L\), and whether matching lower bounds depend on support size rather than total population.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 10’s lower-bound construction gives all \(n\) agents identical reward functions. It therefore has \(\tau=1\). Under the per-capita objective above, that case is simply single-agent value, so the theorem’s exponential dependence on \(n\) does not carry over. It comes from measuring regret in the raw product \( (V^\pi)^n \), whose scale changes exponentially with \(n\). The theorem is still a useful anchor because it makes the continuous question precise—does the apparent dependence on population size survive when welfare is normalized per person?—but it does not establish transferred hardness. My case is strongest as a plausible, author-recognizable mirror of the paper’s underlying online fairness problem, not as a claim that its exponential lower bound persists unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 10 is a weak anchor for a population continuation. Its lower bound uses \(n\) identical reward functions. After grouping them, the support has size \(\tau=1\); under the proposed per-capita objective, \(W_\mu^\pi=V^\pi\), so the bound’s exponential dependence on \(n\) disappears. Keeping the unnormalized product gives \((V^\pi)^n\), but its scale changes exponentially just because the same utility was repeated. That does not establish a population-driven learning difficulty. And the finite-instance identity \((W_\mu^\pi)^n=\prod_i V^\pi(r_i)\) preserves the optimizer, not regret: regret differences are changed by taking the \(n\)-th root.

That undercuts the theorem as evidence for this particular mirror, but it does not defeat the strongest version of the question. A public-health system with many stakeholders in a small number of reward-function cohorts is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The type is a complete reward function, the masses can be rational, and weighted Nash welfare is a coherent population objective. One could ask for its minimax regret and an efficient learner. The fact that the masses affect welfare weights rather than generate extra transition observations narrows the population interpretation, but does not make it incoherent: a shared policy can still be judged by the welfare of the people it serves.

So the case against is that the paper’s striking lower bound does not survive the natural normalization, and the proponent’s broader heterogeneous-type problem is a new weighted learning question rather than a consequence of that lower bound. That is a real limitation on the anchor. It is not a fundamental objection to the mirror: I cannot honestly rule out the heterogeneous-cohort scenario as a worthwhile continuous problem. The negative case is therefore weak, and a universal “no worthwhile mirror” verdict would overstate what this paper supports.
