The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is concrete, but it runs into your anchor rule: the paper makes a compelling case that a population model is natural, and even analyzes one, yet it contains no numbered result establishing a computational complexity classification.

The closest structural result is Theorem 2, proved here: every polarized ray mechanism is OSP, and, when preferences are strictly monotone, every OSP trading mechanism is effectively dominated by a polarized ray mechanism. This reduces the menu-design space; Corollary 2 further bounds a non-redundant menu by \(I+1\) options. But these are characterizations and a dimension bound, not a result that the underlying computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified.

The closest population bridge is Proposition 3, proved here: in the sequence of replica economies, the optimal mechanism for the large-market program is asymptotically optimal in the original finite economies as the market grows. The paper explicitly interprets its large-market relaxation as each agent being a unit-mass population with a distribution of preferences. That is unusually strong evidence that the population regime is sensible—and also means the paper has already formulated much of the continuous model a mirror might propose.

The most plausible regime is a large workforce of doctors, investigators, or other workers divided into a modest number of performance classes. Within each class, many people have the same task-cost vector and status-quo assignment. A type is the complete tuple of performance, task-cost vector, and status-quo assignment; its mass is the share of the class with that tuple. Individual task bundles remain integral, while a type’s average bundle can be fractional. Each type chooses a trading set from an OSP menu, and the designer minimizes mass-weighted social cost subject to per-capita task supplies.

A precise candidate problem is Finite-Support Large-Market OSP Design. The input consists of \(I\) task types; performance classes \(g\), each of unit mass with performance vector \(\pi_g\) and status quo \(\sigma_g\); supplies \(n^i\) satisfying \(\sum_g \sigma_g(i)=n^i\); and rational masses \(\mu_{g,t}\) on finitely many positive cost vectors \(c_{g,t}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_{g,t}=1\). Choose for each class an OSP trading menu—at most \(I\) polarized rays rooted at \(\sigma_g\), plus the status-quo option, or the remedial menu. Each supported cost type selects a myopically optimal option, and receives an average bundle \(x_{g,t}\) in its chosen trading set. Require mass market clearing,
\[ \sum_{g,t}\mu_{g,t}x_{g,t}(i)=n^i\quad\text{for every }i, \]
and minimize
\[ \sum_{g,t}\mu_{g,t}\,\pi_g\cdot x_{g,t}. \]
A solution is a menu, type choices, and feasible average bundles within \(\varepsilon\) of the minimum. For a finite-support version, the OSP strategy should still be valid over the full positive cost domain; finite support specifies which types carry mass. This restricts the paper’s maintained full-support preference-distribution assumption, so it is a deliberate computational regime choice.

I would weakly favor continuum-specific hardness for the full menu-design problem, with low confidence: the difficult part is choosing polarized menus and their type responses, not the number of named agents. But fixed-menu allocation is an LP, and the paper’s dual decomposition leaves open whether the menu optimization has a tractable convex formulation. The key follow-up questions are the complexity of computing each support function \(S_j\), whether a convex reformulation exists, and how the finite-replica integrality gap depends on the number of types and the market size.

The soft spot is decisive under the requested standard: neither Theorem 2 nor Proposition 3 is a computational-complexity anchor, and the paper already defines the large-market OSP program whose complexity one would investigate. The authors would recognize the candidate problem—the paper says its computational complexity remains open—but that makes it a computational follow-up to an existing continuous-population model, not a new continuous mirror of one of the paper’s computational results. So the honest anchor count is zero; there is no compliant one-problem-per-anchor case here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes when choice improves on the status quo; Theorem 2 characterizes OSP trading mechanisms; Proposition 2 establishes strong duality; and Proposition 3 proves asymptotic optimality. None classifies an algorithmic problem by running time, hardness, approximation, or parameterized complexity.

Theorem 2 is the best-looking anchor, but its result concerns the geometry of each agent’s menu. In a population model, replacing many identical agents by a mass changes feasibility and aggregate allocation; it does not, by itself, turn that characterization into a complexity result. A continuum OSP characterization could be an interesting new incentive-model question, especially if atomless agents change the strategic logic. But that would be a new model theorem, not a computational mirror of Theorem 2. The computational task of choosing among polarized-ray menus is a different question.

Proposition 3 is an even poorer anchor for a new continuization: it already studies replica economies and proves that the large-market optimum is asymptotically optimal. Asking for finite-market error bounds or a sharper finite-type version could be worthwhile, but those would extend an existing population-limit result rather than mirror a computational result in the paper.

The proponent’s proposed Finite-Support Large-Market OSP Design is the strongest candidate overall, but it is already the paper’s own large-market model. Its mass market-clearing constraint, expected social cost, OSP menus, and menu optimization over support functions \(S_j\) all appear in Sections 4.1–4.2. The paper even says the program’s computational complexity remains open. Encoding preferences as finitely many rational masses would make a concrete algorithmic input, but it would not create a new continuous mirror; it would study the complexity of a population model the paper has already formulated. The full-support assumption may need adjustment for that input regime, but that is a modeling choice, not the central objection.

So the proposed case succeeds as a computational follow-up to the paper’s existing large-market program, not as a mirror of a named computational result. That distinction makes the negative case strong under the requested anchor standard. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous work can come from this paper: the open menu-optimization problem is plainly a potentially valuable computational question, and the paper has already put it in a population setting. The honest limit of the case is that there is no compliant computational-result anchor for a new continuization.
