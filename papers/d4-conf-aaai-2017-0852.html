None classifies an underlying computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is that this paper gives no algorithmic complexity classification for a problem whose input is a society and whose output is an optimal intervention. Theorem 1 and Theorems 4–6 bound distortion; Theorems 8–9 concern participation and incentives. The paper does report sample and communication costs, so “no computational result” is a little too broad. But those are resource guarantees for running a randomized mechanism, not complexity results for solving an input problem of the kind ChoCo studies.

The best proposed mirror would give society a distribution \(\mu\) over complete voter types, including preferences and metric costs, then analyze 2-Agree on samples from \(\mu\). That is a sensible model of a large electorate. Yet the mechanism’s behavior is already determined by the top-choice masses \(p_a\): it samples voters with replacement until a top choice repeats. If \(q_a(p)\) is the probability it selects alternative \(a\), and \(S_a=\int d(i,a)\,d\mu(i)\) is that alternative’s per-capita social cost, its distortion is governed by
\[ \frac{\sum_a q_a(p)S_a}{\min_b S_b}. \]
So the population enters through a finite collection of aggregate quantities. The continuous formulation does not expose a new separation problem, algorithmic bottleneck, or dependence on support size and encoding length. The sample bound also remains a function of \(m\), independent of population size.

The large-electorate scenario is credible, and I would not use high multiplicity as an objection. The problem is fit: the continuous version restates the mechanism’s existing distributional analysis rather than opening a computational question about the population. Studying new sampling policies or learning an unknown distribution might be worthwhile, but that would launch a different research problem rather than mirror a named result here.

That leaves the negative case strong on ChoCo’s computational remit, but weak as a universal claim that no useful continuous-population study could come from the paper. Its natural mass-distribution model is easy to formulate; what is missing is a compelling new complexity question for it.
