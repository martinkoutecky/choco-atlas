The continuous mirror question

Given a bipartite multigraph \(H=(U,W;F)\) of agent types, rational masses \(\mu_v\) with total mass \(1\) on each side, and a partial order \(\succ_v\) over each type's incident contracts, choose \(x_f\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{f\ni v}x_f\leq\mu_v\). For each contract \(f\) incident to \(v\), let \(\beta_v(f)=\mu_v-\sum_{g\ni v:\, f\not\succ_v g}x_g\); require \(\beta_u(f)=0\) or \(\beta_w(f)=0\) for every \(f=uw\), and maximize \(\sum_{f\in F}x_f\). Is it UGC-hard to approximate this maximum stable mass within \(2-\delta\) for every fixed \(\delta>0\)?

The model it lives in

Each type represents interchangeable agents with the same side, incident contracts, and partial order. The decision variable \(x_f\) is the mass assigned along contract \(f\); each person still receives at most one whole contract. Feasibility requires \(\sum_{f\ni v}x_f\leq\mu_v\), and stability requires that for each \(f=uw\), at least one endpoint has zero mass unmatched or strictly willing to move to \(f\), as expressed by \(\beta_u(f)=0\) or \(\beta_w(f)=0\). The objective is \(\max \sum_f x_f\).

The proposed problem overlaps with prior fractional stable-assignment work, and the case does not establish how much that work already covers for type capacities and arbitrary partial orders.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does UGC imply that maximum stable mass with arbitrary partial orders has no \((2-\delta)\)-approximation for every fixed \(\delta>0\)?
  • What are the exact, approximation, or parameterized complexity bounds in the support size \(\tau\), number of contracts \(q\), and input bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of maximum stable matching with partial orders. It keeps the paper’s objective and blocking rule while making the population the object that is continuous. I think that is a question the authors would recognize: a market has many applicants and posts, but relatively few complete preference and eligibility profiles. Each type is a group whose members share a side of the market, acceptable contracts, and the same partial order over those contracts. That is a high-multiplicity regime when the number of people \(N\) is much larger than the number of types \(\tau\). Large placement markets with repeated applicant qualifications and post categories are a plausible example.

Here is the model I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite bipartite multigraph \(H=(U\cup W,F)\), with \(\tau=|U|+|W|\) agent types and \(q=|F|\) contract types. Each type \(v\) has a rational mass \(\mu_v\geq 0\), with total mass \(1\) on each side, and a strict partial order \(\succ_v\) on its incident contracts. The masses are given in binary, with bit length \(L\). A solution is a nonnegative flow \(x_f\) on contracts, subject to \(\sum_{f\ni v}x_f\leq\mu_v\) for every type. It represents the mass of type-\(v\) people assigned contract \(f\); each person gets at most one contract.

For an incident contract \(f\), define the mass at type \(v\) that is unmatched or would strictly prefer \(f\) to its current contract by
\[ \beta_v(f)=\mu_v-\sum_{\substack{g\ni v\\ f\not\succ_v g}}x_g. \]
The flow is stable exactly when, for every contract \(f=uv\), at least one of \(\beta_u(f)\) and \(\beta_v(f)\) is zero. If both are positive, a positive mass on each side could form a blocking pair. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{f\in F}x_f\), and the computational question is to find an optimal stable flow or an approximation to it in time polynomial in \(\tau,q,L\). The fractional quantities describe portions of a type assigned to different contracts; every individual in those portions still receives one whole contract or remains unmatched.

This is my lead mirror of Theorem 3. Theorem 3, proved in the paper, says that assuming UGC it is NP-hard to approximate max-smpo within \(2-\delta\) for any \(\delta>0\). Its proof uses a cited hardness result of Bansal and Khot about independent sets. The continuous question is whether the same approximation barrier holds for the maximum stable mass problem with arbitrary partial orders, or whether splitting a type’s mass across contracts improves the approximation. My expectation is continuum-specific hardness, Class C: each contract imposes a disjunction—one endpoint type must have zero willing mass—and those coupled choices can retain substantial combinatorial structure even when individual agents are atomless. I would not call this transferred hardness without an embedding proof. The further questions are whether the \(2-\delta\) barrier survives, whether exact optimization is NP-hard, and whether the continuous model admits a useful algorithm parameterized by \(\tau\).

For interval orders, the paper gives a second, sharper pair of anchors on the same mass-flow problem. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for two partially ordered matroids on a common ground set whose orders are interval orders, one can find a kernel of size at least \(\frac{2}{3}\) of a maximum kernel. The paper explicitly derives the corresponding \(1.5\)-approximation for stable matching. Its continuous mirror is: with interval orders at every type, find a stable flow of mass at least \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the maximum stable mass. I expect this to be tractable, Class A. The interval-order structure that makes the finite algorithm work is still present in the type preferences, and the matched mass remains the same substantive quantity.

Proposition 2, also proved in the paper, says that assuming UGC it is NP-hard to approximate max-kernel-po with interval orders within \(\frac{3}{2}-\delta\) for every \(\delta>0\); this holds even for maximum stable marriage with interval orders. The paper obtains it from the cited result of Askalidis et al. on socially stable matching. Its continuous mirror asks whether the interval-order mass-flow problem likewise rules out every \((\frac{3}{2}-\delta)\)-approximation under UGC. My expectation for the interval problem is Class A with a \(1.5\) guarantee, and I think the matching lower bound is plausible as a type-structure hardness result. But Proposition 2 does not establish that lower bound for mass flows: its finite instances do not themselves show that splitting mass preserves the reduction. Proving or refuting that survival is the question this anchor adds.

The interval-order case is especially credible as a mirror because the paper already treats interval orders as meaningful preference structures, not as a contrived algorithmic restriction. In the continuous market, they can describe agents comparing options whose quality ranges overlap: two contract categories are incomparable when neither uniformly outranks the other. The paper’s \(\frac{3}{2}\)-approximation and its UGC barrier then give concrete targets for the continuous problem rather than a vague invitation to “study matching with populations.”

The paper’s named results do not already make this a continuous-population problem. Its three-copy construction is an algorithmic gadget, and its LP integrality-gap discussion concerns a relaxation of the finite matching problem. Neither makes population mass the input or objective. The proposed model does: \(x_f\) is the mass of people receiving contract \(f\), while each person still gets an integral assignment. The paper’s broader matroid-kernel result also suggests extensions, but I would keep this mirror focused on its bipartite stable-matching problem rather than claim the whole matroid generalization.

My weakest point is that the finite hardness results may not survive this change. In the continuous model, members of a type can be split among contracts, and the willingness-to-block conditions are disjunctions over masses rather than indivisible matching choices. That can change the optimum and the complexity. The paper’s reductions are evidence for where to look, not proofs about the continuous problem. The case for the mirror survives because the proposed question retains the authors’ matching objective, their preference orders, and their stability definition in a natural large-cohort regime. Whether fractional population mass dissolves their barriers is precisely the computational question worth asking.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that they sit very close to an existing computational literature on fractional stable matching. Chen, Roy, and Sorge study ordinal and cardinal stability for fractional assignments and classify optimization of social welfare and fully matched agents; in the all-tied case, maximizing welfare is the same objective as maximizing matched mass, up to a factor of two ([IJCAI 2021 paper](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/13)). The proposed model can be read as that problem with type capacities \(\mu_v\): it aggregates interchangeable agents and allows those capacities to be rational.

That overlap weakens the claim that the paper opens a wholly new computational direction. It does not, though, settle the ChoCo question. The existing work uses individual agents with unit capacity. Expanding rational type masses into identical agents can take time polynomial in the numerical mass rather than its bit length; it also does not supply the claimed results for arbitrary partial orders or interval orders. Since population compression is central to this programme, the gap remains substantive.

Theorem 3’s UGC reduction gives no direct reason to expect its approximation barrier to survive type aggregation: it encodes combinatorics in an instance of individual agents, and the paper supplies no construction showing that the hard instances compress into a small set of repeated profiles. Theorem 1 and Proposition 2 pose the corresponding algorithmic and hardness questions for interval orders. They are two targets for the same maximum-stable-mass problem, rather than independent mirrors. But failure of the old reduction to transfer is not a reason to reject that problem; whether splitting mass changes either bound is precisely the open computational question.

I also cannot make the population model itself fail. Repeated applicant and post profiles give a credible high-multiplicity market, and the proposed blocking condition has a clear interpretation: a contract blocks when positive mass on both sides would strictly prefer it. The mass objective remains meaningful, and no named-person identity is needed once type records contain the full profile.

So the honest negative case is weak. The best objection is that this is a capacitated, type-compressed continuation of an established fractional-stability literature, with all three anchors concentrating on one underlying optimization problem. That is a real novelty and prioritization concern, but it does not defeat the best mirror. The arbitrary-partial-order mirror survives, and the interval-order results provide meaningful algorithmic and hardness targets for its restricted version.
