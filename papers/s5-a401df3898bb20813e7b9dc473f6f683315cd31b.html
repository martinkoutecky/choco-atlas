The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is conditional: this paper has a sensible continuous-population mirror, but it does not contain a qualifying anchor under your rules.

I can find no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the paper asserting NP-hardness, membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), fixed-parameter tractability, or another computational classification. Table 2 reports empirical comparisons between A-CEEI and the auction; Appendix H/Table A4 reports computations from an integer program for Pareto-improving trades; and the paper describes implementation algorithms. None is a named complexity result. I therefore cannot honestly invent an anchor or claim that the proposed problem mirrors one.

My unanchored lead would nevertheless be a natural ChoCo problem: Continuous A-CEEI Course Assignment and Reporting Robustness.

Let \(J\) be the available course sections and let \(\mathcal S\) be the family of feasible schedules satisfying exactly the paper’s constraints: at most five courses, no time conflicts, and at most one section of each course. Each section \(j\) has per-capita capacity \(q_j\). A type \(\theta\) consists of a student’s complete true utility function \(u_\theta(S)\), budget \(b_\theta\), and preference report in the paper’s language,
\[ \widehat u_\theta(S) = \sum_{j\in S}v_{\theta j} + \sum_{\{j,k\}\subseteq S}a_{\theta jk}. \]
The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types. The mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of students with exactly the same preferences, budget, and reporting characteristics.

The decision variables are prices \(p_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{\theta,S}\). Each unit of mass receives an integral schedule \(S\); fractional \(x_{\theta,S}\) only means that different members of a type receive different schedules. Define
\[ D_\theta(p) = \arg\max\left\{ \widehat u_\theta(S): S\in\mathcal S,\ \sum_{j\in S}p_j\le b_\theta \right\}. \]
The allocation must satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\in\mathcal S}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta \quad\text{and}\quad x_{\theta,S}>0\Longrightarrow S\in D_\theta(p). \]
Writing \(d_j=\sum_{\theta,S\ni j}x_{\theta,S}\), the continuous A-CEEI computation minimizes the approximate market-clearing error
\[ \Phi(x,p) = \left( \sum_j[d_j-q_j]_+^2 + \sum_{j:p_j>0}[q_j-d_j]_+^2 \right)^{1/2}. \]
The output is \(p\) and \(x\), together with the true aggregate welfare
\[ W(x)=\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}u_\theta(S) \]
and a mass analogue of envy, for example
\[ \operatorname{Env}(x) = \sum_{\theta,\theta',S,S'} x_{\theta,S}x_{\theta',S'} [u_\theta(S')-u_\theta(S)]_+. \]

The computational question is: given \(J,\mathcal S,q,\Theta,\mu\), the true utilities, the reports, and tolerance \(\varepsilon\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-clearing A-CEEI outcome and determine its welfare and envy; optionally compare these quantities with the corresponding continuous bidding-auction outcome.

This is recognisably the paper’s problem. It preserves the same schedules, capacities, equal-budget logic, item values, pairwise complementarities and substitutes, and welfare and envy criteria. Only named students are replaced by masses of students sharing complete types. A plausible regime is a large university with thousands of students but perhaps tens or hundreds of recurring preference types: students in the same programme, career track, timetable pattern, and budget class. Course offerings and capacities scale with cohort size, so \(q_j\) is a per-capita supply rather than an artificial fixed number of seats.

This is genuinely population continuization, not fractionalizing schedules or merely adding a noise model. Every individual still receives an indivisible schedule. The continuous object is the distribution of students across schedules.

The likely complexity boundary is informative. With structured timetables and additively separable utilities, the type-level demand problem may admit dynamic programming or column-generation methods, making the aggregate problem plausibly Class A. With the paper’s full pairwise-adjustment language and arbitrary conflict structure, one type’s demand problem may already encode hard combinatorial optimization. Then continuation would not remove the hardness: the combinatorics live in schedules and alternatives, not in the number of students. That would be a meaningful negative result about the mirror, not evidence that the mirror was misguided.

The regime is especially plausible because A-CEEI is already intended for large course-allocation markets, and the paper’s cited implementation literature supports the market-level formulation. This is supporting prior art, not a collision: the paper itself does not formulate or classify the continuous-population computational problem.

The weakest point is decisive: there is no qualifying named computational anchor in this paper. Thus the proposed problem is a credible new continuous research question, but it cannot satisfy the programme’s anchor requirement as a mirror of a theorem from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is decisive under the programme’s gate: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Its results are experimental comparisons, survey statistics, and implementation diagnostics. Appendix H’s integer program is a computation used to measure Pareto improvements, not a numbered theorem or lemma asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic result. There is therefore no named computational claim for a continuous mirror to mirror.

The proposed A-CEEI model is nevertheless a plausible new problem, but it does not rescue the paper as an anchor. It continuizes the allocation mechanism, whereas the paper’s central question is whether human subjects can translate complex, private preferences into a restricted reporting language accurately enough. Given \(\mu\), the proposed algorithm merely computes an outcome conditional on the supplied reports. It does not explain or test reporting accuracy.

To preserve that question, the type would have to include true preferences, reported preferences, budget perturbations, interface exposure, time pressure, incentives, question order, and a stochastic model of reporting errors such as
\[ \Pr(\widehat u,\text{binary answers}\mid u,\text{interface},\text{incentives}). \]
But the paper supplies no such behavioural law. If it is supplied externally, the resulting problem is a new aggregate mechanism-design model whose conclusions depend on an assumed empirical distribution, not a computational mirror of this experiment. If the law is left out, “accurately enough” is not defined.

There is also a representation dilemma. Preserving the paper’s central mismatch requires true utilities containing features that the pairwise reporting language cannot express, such as balanced or contiguous schedules. Representing those utilities compactly is a new modelling and computational problem. Restricting true utilities to the report language erases the paper’s main phenomenon; allowing arbitrary utilities makes the type space exponentially large or requires an unspecified oracle. A finite explicit set of complete types is a legitimate high-multiplicity model, but then it is simply a new continuum A-CEEI project, not a result extracted from this paper.

The same applies to the random budget perturbations essential to A-CEEI. A continuum model must retain their distribution or encode tie-breaking as additional type information. That can be done, but each such choice constructs missing theory rather than translating a result in the paper.

The honest negative conclusion is therefore narrower than universal impossibility: this paper gives ChoCo no qualifying computational result, and its strongest plausible continuum is a worthwhile standalone course-allocation problem rather than a continuous mirror of the paper. The stronger claim that no sensible high-multiplicity course-allocation mirror could ever be worthwhile would be overstated; the proposed market is genuinely capable of supporting one.
