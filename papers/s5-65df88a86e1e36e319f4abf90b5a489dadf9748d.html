The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is a continuum of negotiating jurisdictions or firms. A type \(\theta\) would specify everything relevant to an agent’s payoffs—such as its ideal consumption level, initial green-technology stock, and investment cost. The distribution \(\mu\) gives the population share of each type. The action variables would be participation, emissions, and green investment; coalition members would also choose agreement duration and any contractible policies. This fits a plausible regime of many similar firms coordinating on emissions or green investment, with many agents but relatively few payoff types. The paper itself mentions cartels as a possible application beyond environmental treaties.

That suggests a new problem: given a finite-support rational \(\mu\), the model’s payoff parameters, and a threshold \(\alpha\), determine whether a stationary Markov-perfect equilibrium exists in which at least an \(\alpha\) share joins, and return its type-contingent participation and policy strategies. This would mirror the paper’s questions about coalition formation, duration, and policy. Continuous emissions or investment alone would not make this a population mirror; the relevant move is representing the negotiating agents by type masses.

The weakest point is that the proposed continuum changes a strategically important feature: a country is an indivisible sovereign actor, and the paper’s results include small finite coalitions. The firm-cartel scenario makes high multiplicity more plausible, but it is a further modeling step. The positive case is therefore a credible population-continuum extension of the paper’s *economic model*, but not a mirror anchored in one of its computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent named no qualifying result to anchor. Its own decisive admission is right: the paper’s propositions characterize equilibria, coalition sizes, and policy choices, but none gives a complexity classification or an algorithm for a computational problem. So the proposed task—deciding whether a stationary Markov-perfect equilibrium has at least an \(\alpha\) share of participants and finding its strategies—is a possible new research question, not a continuous mirror of a named result in this paper.

The best version of that proposal, with a continuum of similar firms, is still a plausible model. But it changes the strategic mechanism the paper studies. The hold-up effect depends on a country’s unilateral exit changing the coalition and its future agreement. In an atomless population, one agent’s exit changes the coalition mass by zero, so that pivotal effect disappears. Treat each type’s entire mass as a strategic bloc and pivotality returns, but then the blocs—not the high-multiplicity agents—are the negotiating players.

That is a strong case against calling this paper an anchored continuization candidate. It is not proof that no worthwhile continuous-population research could grow from the model: the firms scenario remains plausible, and a new nonatomic coalition-formation problem could be valuable on its own. The honest negative case is therefore decisive on the requested anchor, but weaker on the broader claim that every continuous extension is unworthy.
