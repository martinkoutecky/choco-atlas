What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers the anonymous pseudo-market framework of Theorem 1 and the regional floor-and-ceiling characterization of Proposition 1, while leaving endowments, roommates, coalition formation, combinatorial allocation, and bads largely untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(|T|\) and explicitly represented anonymous constraints, can an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium be computed in polynomial time in the encoding length and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • When regional unions are part of the input, what is the complexity of pricing or separating the induced family of union-of-region constraints?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s anonymous large-market allocation model: many doctors or students, few complete preference and eligibility types, and aggregate hospital or school quotas. I would not lead with endowments or roommates, where identities and pairwise structure are more difficult to compress.

One qualification matters at the outset: this paper contains no named theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or a comparable complexity classification. Its named results are equilibrium-existence and polyhedral-characterization results. I therefore use those as formulation anchors, not as claims that the authors already proved an algorithm.

The population mirror is as follows. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete agent types. A type includes preferences, utility, eligibility, demographic category, and all constraint-relevant characteristics. A society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta^{T}\), where \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of agents of type \(\theta\). The intended regime is \(N\gg |T|\): for example, thousands of doctors drawn from a modest number of preference/category types, or thousands of students with repeated ranking and demographic profiles.

For unit-demand allocation, let \(O\) be the objects and let \(x_{\theta,l}\) be the average share of object \(l\) received by type \(\theta\). This is not the claim that an individual receives a fractional hospital position. Equivalently, introduce \(z_{\theta,b}\), the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving integral bundle \(b\), with

\[ \sum_b z_{\theta,b}=\mu_\theta. \]

Then \(x_{\theta,l}\) is the type-level average induced by \(z\). Aggregate supplies and quotas are expressed in mass units, for example

\[ \sum_{\theta\in T}\mu_\theta x_{\theta,l}\le q_l. \]

Thus the continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\); the fractional assignment is the aggregate description of integral allocations across that population.

My lead problem is:

\[ \mathsf{Continuum\text{-}Constrained\text{-}PseudoMarket}. \]

An instance consists of \(T,\mu,O\), rational object supplies and anonymous aggregate constraints

\[ A_c(x)=\sum_{\theta,l}\mu_\theta a_{c,\theta,l}x_{\theta,l}\le b_c, \]

and a utility \(u_\theta\) for each type. In the computational version, one can use rational linear expected utilities \(u_\theta(x)=v_\theta\cdot x\), which are already a standard special case of the paper’s model.

A solution is an allocation \(x\) and nonnegative constraint prices \(p_c\) such that, writing

\[ \pi_{\theta,l}=\sum_c a_{c,\theta,l}p_c, \]

each type optimizes at the common unit budget,

\[ x_\theta\in \arg\max\{u_\theta(y):y\in X_\theta,\ \pi_\theta\cdot y\le 1\}, \]

the aggregate allocation satisfies every constraint, and complementary slackness holds:

\[ A_c(x)<b_c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_c=0. \]

The objective is therefore to find an equilibrium certificate \((x,p)\). The welfare consequences are the paper’s constrained Pareto efficiency and equal-type envy-freeness. An algorithmic version would ask for an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium, with approximate best responses and approximate complementarity.

The principal anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper, not cited. It states in full:

  • if utilities are continuous, quasi-concave, and strictly increasing, there exists a pseudo-market equilibrium whose assignment is weakly \(C\)-constrained Pareto efficient;
  • if utilities are semi-strictly quasi-concave, there exists a pseudo-market equilibrium whose assignment is \(C\)-constrained Pareto efficient;
  • every pseudo-market equilibrium assignment is equal-type envy-free.

This is a direct population mirror. Replacing the finite sum over named agents by the type-weighted sum over \(\mu\) leaves the equilibrium question intact while removing irrelevant individual indices. In the intended regime, the equilibrium problem has dimension governed by \(|T|\), \(|O|\), and the constraint description rather than by \(N\). The authors should recognize it as their pseudo-market problem, not as an unrelated welfare LP.

I expect the fixed-type, anonymous, explicitly separated version to be a Class A candidate for approximate computation: it has a finite-dimensional convex/polyhedral aggregate description, and its natural tools are exactly price search, LP, convex programming, and separation. I would not claim this for the unrestricted model. The paper itself notes that the Hylland–Zeckhauser special case has nontrivial computational complexity, so hardness may remain when the number of objects or the constraint structure carries the combinatorics. That is a useful boundary question rather than a reason to reject the mirror.

A more concrete anchor is Proposition 1, also proved here. It concerns regional hospital floors and ceilings. In the paper’s finite model, if \(C\) is the feasible assignment polytope and \(\operatorname{lcs}(C)\) its lower contour set, then

\[ \operatorname{lcs}(C) = \left\{ x\ge 0: \begin{array}{l} \sum_{l\in O}x_{i,l}\le 1\quad\text{for every agent }i,\\ \sum_{i\in I}x_{i,l}\le q_l\quad\text{for every object }l,\\ \sum_{i\in I,\ l\in R}x_{i,l}\le q_R \quad\text{for every union }R\text{ of regions} \end{array} \right\}. \]

The ceiling \(q_R\) is derived recursively from the original regional floors and ceilings. For a single region \(R_k\),

\[ q_{R_k} = \min\left\{ \bar q_{R_k}, N-\sum_{R'\neq R_k}\underline q_{R'} \right\}, \]

and for a union \(R=\bigcup_{j\in S}R_j\),

\[ q_R = \min\left( \left\{ q_{R\setminus R_j}+q_{R_j}:j\in S \right\} \cup \left\{ N-\sum_{R'\notin S}\underline q_{R'} \right\} \right). \]

The continuous version is:

\[ \mathsf{Continuum\text{-}Regional\text{-}Floor\text{-}PM}. \]

Its input is a type distribution \(\mu\), hospitals \(O\), a partition into regions, per-capita hospital capacities, regional floors and ceilings, and utilities. It asks for a type-level assignment and prices for the derived union-of-regions constraints such that

\[ \sum_l x_{\theta,l}\le 1, \]

\[ \sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_{\theta,l}\le q_l, \]

\[ \underline q_{R_k} \le \sum_\theta\mu_\theta\sum_{l\in R_k}x_{\theta,l} \le \bar q_{R_k}, \]

and, for every union \(R\),

\[ \sum_\theta\mu_\theta\sum_{l\in R}x_{\theta,l}\le q_R, \]

with type-level optimality, nonnegative prices, and complementary slackness. The \(q_R\) formula is the same as above after normalizing \(N\) to \(1\).

For a fixed number of regions, this is a particularly credible Class A candidate: Proposition 1 supplies the exact polyhedral structure, and the number of allocation variables depends on types and hospitals, not on the number of doctors. If the number of regions is part of the input, the exponential family of unions creates a genuine separation problem; that is an appropriate further ChoCo question. It might still be tractable because of the special recursive quota structure, or it might yield a continuum-specific hard case.

The high-multiplicity interpretation is especially natural here. A national or regional residency system may have tens of thousands of doctors but relatively few recurring preference, specialty, and demographic profiles. The paper’s anonymity observation is precisely what makes the mirror plausible: regional constraints do not single out individual doctors, and equal-type agents face equal prices. School-choice instances with repeated ranking and demographic types give a second convincing scenario.

This does not collide with the paper’s existing fractional assignments. The paper makes assignments random or fractional while retaining a finite set of named agents. The mirror removes the named population itself and replaces it by \(\mu\). For rational \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\), the continuous instance is exactly the high-multiplicity representation of a finite instance with \(n_\theta\) copies of each type; conversely, a continuous allocation can be rounded at granularity \(1/N\). The population continuization is therefore the new object, while random assignment is merely the paper’s implementation language.

The scope is deliberately limited to Theorem 1 and the regional-floor machinery of Proposition 1. I am not claiming equally strong mirrors for the endowment results, time exchange, or arbitrary coalition formation. Those may require additional assumptions about repeated endowments or repeated coalition preferences.

The weakest point is that the paper proves existence, not an algorithm. Its fixed-point proof does not by itself establish polynomial-time computation, and the general utility functions do not even have a finite computational encoding. The positive case therefore rests on a legitimate but narrower computational formalization: finitely many complete types, rational linear or otherwise efficiently represented utilities, and explicit anonymous constraints. Within that regime, the proposed problems are recognizably the paper’s pseudo-market questions, and their complexity—especially the separation problem for regional and other aggregate constraints—is exactly the sort of question the continuization programme is meant to expose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with a more basic problem: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is an existence and welfare theorem; Proposition 1 is a polyhedral characterization. Neither specifies an input encoding, an output representation, an exact or approximate search problem, or a complexity claim. Thus the proposed problems are not continuous versions of computational results in the paper. They are new computational-economics problems inspired by its model. That may be worthwhile research, but it does not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper under ChoCo’s gate.

The strongest version of the Theorem 1 mirror is nevertheless coherent. Take many doctors or students, repeat complete preference and constraint types, scale supplies and quotas with population mass, and represent integral assignments by \(z_{\theta,b}\), with \(x_{\theta,l}\) as type-level averages. This avoids the invalid objection that individuals cannot receive fractional hospital positions.

But it also reveals how much has been added. The paper permits arbitrary agent-specific consumption spaces, utilities, and a primitive feasible polytope \(C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N L}\). Its “equal type” condition requires equality not merely of preferences or demographics, but of \(X_i\) and every constraint coefficient involving the agent. In the general model, two doctors with the same ranking can occupy different constraint roles. Encoding those roles into the type is legitimate, but then the number of types can be as large as the number of agents; omitting them means the aggregate problem no longer represents the paper’s equilibrium notion.

To recover a finite-dimensional mirror, one must therefore impose anonymous aggregate constraints, a finite repeated type set, and computationally represented utilities—typically rational linear expected utilities. That is a sensible new special case, but not a computational consequence of Theorem 1. The theorem’s Kakutani proof gives no algorithm, and arbitrary continuous utilities and arbitrary primitive polytopes have no finite computational representation. Even the proposed \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium needs choices absent from the paper: the error norm, price bounds, utility oracles, feasibility oracles, and the dependence on \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

The \(z_{\theta,b}\) repair does not remove this gap. It preserves integral bundles, but can introduce exponentially many bundles and requires a decomposition or separation procedure for the feasible assignment polytope. The computational content then comes from newly chosen encodings and oracles, not from continuizing the theorem. A high-multiplicity pseudomarket algorithm could be interesting, but it would be a new algorithmic study of a restricted model.

Proposition 1 is an even weaker anchor. It does not assert an algorithmic result; it identifies \(\operatorname{lcs}(C)\) for regional floors and ceilings. The quotas \(q_R\) already concern aggregate regional counts, and replacing \(N\) by \(1\) while replacing counts by masses is merely a change of units. The proposed pricing problem over unions of regions is an additional computational question, not a question posed or implied by Proposition 1. The exponential family of unions appears in the polyhedral description, but the paper neither claims that equilibrium computation requires explicitly generating them nor studies their separation complexity.

Nor does the population distribution add anything essential to this proposition: the regional characterization is determined by regions, supplies, and quotas. The type distribution affects demand and utility optimization, but not the stated polyhedral identity. Calling that identity a continuous-population result confuses an aggregate finite-market formulation with a computational continuization.

There is a genuine weakness in this negative case. Anonymous residency and school-choice markets are plausible high-multiplicity regimes, and it would be wrong to reject them merely because the paper already uses random assignments or because the continuous answer might be easy. A carefully specified high-multiplicity pseudomarket problem could be worthwhile. The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower: the proponent has not found a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results, because the paper contains no such results. They have proposed a new algorithmic programme built on a restricted special case of two non-computational theorems.
