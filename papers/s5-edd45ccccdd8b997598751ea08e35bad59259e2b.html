The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying anchor-based case: the paper has no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic bound. Theorems 4.1–4.4, all proved here, are analytic: Theorem 4.1 gives a unique equilibrium expressible from the agents’ rates; Theorem 4.2 proves convergence to it when \(m=2\); Theorem 4.3 proves that global utility gain increases with the number of agents; and Theorem 4.4 proves that, with uniform rates, utility gain increases as attachment rises or detachment falls. Those are meaningful results, but none is a result about an algorithm’s complexity. So there are no eligible anchors, and no per-anchor prover questions to give honestly.

The closest positive lead is a new computational question prompted by Theorem 4.1, rather than anchored by a computational result in the paper. Consider a large cohort of buyers purchasing the same textbook from sellers with the same discount schedule. Buyers share the same joining and leaving rates; their current coalition size determines their state. For input \(m\), total mass \(M\), positive rational rates \(J_1,\ldots,J_{m-1}\) and \(L_2,\ldots,L_m\), and precision \(\epsilon\), ask for the equilibrium fraction \(x_n=nr_n^*/M\) of buyers in coalitions of size \(n\), to additive error \(\epsilon\) in every coordinate. A solution is a vector of fractions summing to \(1\) within the requested precision. Theorem 4.1 characterizes the equilibrium by \(r_n^*=q_n y^n\), where \(q_1=1\), \(q_n=\frac{J_1\cdots J_{n-1}}{L_2\cdots L_n}\), and \(y\) is the unique positive root of \(\sum_{n=1}^m n q_n y^n=M\). That suggests a tractable computation problem via univariate root isolation; it does not establish a complexity result in this paper.

The regime is plausible: many buyers can share the same product, discount rules, and behavior, while the number of coalition-size states is \(m\). But the paper already writes aggregate coalition counts as real-valued variables in an ODE. The proposed question would add a computational study of that aggregate model, rather than introduce a clearly new continuous-population model. It covers equilibrium computation only; it does not extend the convergence proof beyond \(m=2\), or address the paper’s stated open issue of optimizing strategies for global utility gain.

That is the weakest point, and it is substantial: even the best candidate is a computation problem suggested by an analytic theorem, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. Theorems 4.1–4.4 concern equilibrium, convergence, and comparative statics of a system of differential equations; none gives an algorithm or complexity bound. The proponent is right that a continuous computation can be suggested by Theorem 4.1, but that is a new task prompted by an analytic result, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

That proposed task is to compute the equilibrium fractions. Theorem 4.1 already reduces them to \(r_n^*=q_n y^n\), where \(y\) is the unique positive root of \(\sum_{n=1}^m n q_n y^n=M\). For rational rates, this is a one-variable algebraic root problem with a uniquely specified answer. Approximating it and recovering the fractions is standard numerical or root-isolation work. It has no evident combinatorial bottleneck of the kind the programme targets, such as pricing over exponentially many types. The question is well-posed, but it looks like routine evaluation of the paper’s characterization rather than a worthwhile complexity study.

The population regime itself is plausible: a large group of buyers with the same product, discount schedule, and behavior is a sensible high-multiplicity cohort. Mass fractions across coalition sizes are meaningful. But the paper already models aggregate counts \(r_n(t)\) as continuous-valued variables in an ODE. Normalizing those counts gives the proposed distribution over coalition sizes; it does not create a new continuous population model. A more ambitious task based on Theorem 4.2—computing convergence time for \(m=2\)—reduces to a scalar differential equation. Extending global convergence to \(m\ge 3\) would be a dynamical-systems question, not a computational mirror of a result the paper proves.

Theorem 4.3 and 4.4 do not supply a stronger computational anchor. Evaluating the gain again requires the same equilibrium calculation. Optimizing the agents’ rates could become interesting, but the paper treats rates as exogenous and gives no feasible strategy set, rate costs, or resource constraints. Without those additions, the stated monotonicity points toward boundary choices; with them, the optimization problem would be created by a new mechanism-design model.

So the negative case is strongest on the narrow point that this paper offers no native, nontrivial computational question: its principal findings are analytic, and the closest computation is elementary postprocessing of its equilibrium formula. It is not airtight against the broader burden. A richer model with heterogeneous buyers or costly strategy choices might yield a worthwhile computational problem, and the cohort interpretation is credible. But that would make the extension, rather than this paper’s results, carry the research question.
