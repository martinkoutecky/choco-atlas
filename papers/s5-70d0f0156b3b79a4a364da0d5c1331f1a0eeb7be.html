The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of voter types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), a prior \(\pi\) over states \(\theta\), type-specific signal laws \(P_t(s\mid\theta)\), and fixed strategies \(\sigma_t\) mapping signals to vote, oppose, or abstain, compute the probability that the mass majority selects the true state in an atomless society, under a specified tie rule.

The model it lives in

An atomless society has type masses \(\mu_t\), where types encode expertise or signal quality. Conditional on state \(\theta\), each type's mass splits according to \(P_t(s\mid\theta)\); its fixed strategy \(\sigma_t\) assigns those masses to actions. Compute the probability, under \(\pi\), that the resulting aggregate vote masses select \(\theta\), applying the specified tie rule.

The objection that survived

Lemma A3 depends on finite sampling without replacement, while an atomless population with independent signals has deterministic vote shares conditional on the state; the limit therefore changes finite-group accuracy into a mass-majority calculation.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying anchor: the paper has no named result classifying the complexity of a computational problem as tractable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest formal results are Proposition 1, proved here, which identifies two pure Nash equilibria under the strategic uncertainty assumption—only the expert votes, or everyone votes—and a mixed equilibrium in which the expert votes while non-experts vote about 70% of the time and abstain about 30%; and Proposition 2, also proved here, which says the only-expert-votes equilibrium Pareto-dominates the all-vote and mixed equilibria by more than 30 percentage points. These are equilibrium and payoff claims, not computational results. Lemma A3 gives a formula for the probability of a correct majority, but does not establish a computational complexity result either.

There is an intuitive continuous-population extension: describe a large cohort by the mass of expert and imperfectly informed participants, their signals, and their voting or abstention behavior; ask which strategy patterns form equilibria and how likely they are to choose the correct state. But it is a weak mirror of this paper’s central game. In a nonatomic population, an individual cannot be pivotal, while pivotality drives the voting incentives in Proposition 1. And preserving the paper’s single expert as \(n\) grows makes the expert’s mass vanish. Keeping a positive expert mass instead changes the game.

So there is a possible follow-on model, but no honest continuous problem anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest obstacle is that it contains no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes equilibria in a particular 35-voter game; Proposition 2 compares their payoffs; Lemma A3 gives a probability formula; Results 1 and 2 report observed behavior. None poses or classifies an algorithmic problem. Turning one of these into “compute the equilibrium” or “find the best voting rule” would introduce a computational question the paper does not study.

Proposition 1 is the closest candidate, but its incentives depend on pivotality: a vote can change the outcome in a tie or a one-vote lead. In a standard nonatomic population, one voter has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate vote, so those incentives disappear. Keeping finite committees sampled from a continuous population preserves pivotality only inside the finite committees; the committee game remains the object being analyzed. Scaling the committee instead makes the paper’s single expert vanish in the limit. Giving experts positive mass preserves their influence, but replaces one rare expert with a cohort of experts. That may be a sensible new model, but it is not the game Proposition 1 characterizes.

Proposition 2 admits a stronger proposed mirror: given masses of voters with different signal qualities, compute which types should vote or abstain to maximize accuracy. That is a plausible optimization problem about information aggregation. But the paper compares equilibria and studies how people learn to abstain; it does not optimize a voting policy. The proposed problem adds a planner, a new objective, and a new choice set. It is an extension of the topic, not a computational version of the paper’s result.

Lemma A3 does not supply the missing anchor. Its formula evaluates accuracy for a finite group with a specified voting pattern. In a continuum, aggregate signal masses replace much of that finite sampling calculation; retaining the sampling requires retaining finite groups. Meanwhile, a continuum account of local learning would need to represent the seating and communication structure that Result 2 relies on. Encoding that structure richly enough risks making types as numerous and individualized as the participants; a mean-field or graphon treatment could be worthwhile, but it would address a different analytic problem.

So the negative case is strongest as a paper-specific judgment, not a proof that no imaginable adjacent project could be useful. A continuous optimization problem can be built from the paper’s theme. The manuscript itself, however, offers no named computational result to anchor it, and the most promising replacements either lose its pivotal-voting mechanism or add a new problem.
