The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has a credible high-multiplicity regime: its \(m\) independent replicas contain many agents drawn from a fixed set of roles and private-information types. One could imagine a continuum version in which mass records the share of each role/type and blocking plans allocate bundles or invite coalitions. That makes a population-continuum question plausible.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Its named results are economic statements about core membership and convergence, not claims about complexity or algorithms:

  • Proposition 1, proved here, gives an economy and allocation whose replica belongs to the virtual utility core for every \(m\), although the allocation cannot be supported by a price-taking equilibrium satisfying Property P.
  • Proposition 2, proved here, says every replica of an incentive-compatible ex-post Walrasian allocation belongs to the randomized mediated core.
  • Proposition 3, proved here, says that under the stated Debreu–Scarf conditions, a strictly incentive-compatible allocation whose replica belongs to the randomized mediated core for every \(m\) must be an ex-post Walrasian allocation.

A continuum core-membership or convergence problem could be built around these ideas, but it would not mirror a named computational result from this paper. Calling any of these propositions a tractability or hardness anchor would overstate what they establish.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The replication structure is genuine high multiplicity: finitely many roles and information types are repeated across independent copies. That makes a continuous economy plausible. It does not, though, give this paper a computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–3 concern core membership and convergence to price-taking equilibrium; none states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm. The paper’s central objects are state-contingent commodity allocations, incentive constraints, and blocking mechanisms—not a computational social choice problem with an input and an algorithmic task.

Proposition 1 is the strongest negative result to try to mirror. A generous version would keep the joint information structure of each replica and take a continuum of replica blocks, then ask whether a mass allocation can remain in the virtual utility core without being a price allocation. That is a coherent economic question. But the proposition already establishes its finite-replica counterpart for every \(m\); its content is an economic counterexample, not a computational task that mass makes tractable or hard. Proposition 2 likewise has a plausible mass version: an incentive-compatible ex-post Walrasian allocation remains immune to randomized mediated blocking. Its proof reduces to state-by-state core inequalities and aggregation. That may yield a useful continuum theorem, but it would still be a theorem about equilibrium and core membership, not an algorithmic mirror.

Proposition 3 offers the best candidate for a continuous mirror: characterize the randomized mediated core in an atomless limit and ask whether it coincides with incentive-compatible ex-post Walrasian allocations. But the paper already studies the large-replica limit. Taking the limit as the object changes the economic formulation, while leaving the task qualitative and analytic. The paper also flags a real modeling complication: extending to atomless economies can make the state space atomless too, making coalition information difficult to define. A continuum of finite-state replica blocks could preserve that information structure, but then the result remains a continuum version of the same core-convergence analysis.

One could build a separate computational agenda around these economies—for example, deciding core membership, finding a blocking mechanism, or computing a replica threshold. The paper does not formulate those problems, give them finite input encodings, or establish that their key subproblems fit a tractable optimization framework. Adding such machinery could produce worthwhile research, but it would be a new computational project, not a continuous mirror of Propositions 1–3. So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope, though it cannot honestly prove that no algorithmic question arising from these economies could ever be worthwhile.
