The continuous mirror question

Given a finite Boolean vocabulary \(\mathcal X\), consistent knowledge bases \(K_1,K_2\), and a consistent observed result \(R\), decide whether there are positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2\) with \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\) such that \(\operatorname{Mod}(R)=\arg\min_{I\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal X|}}\bigl(\mu_1d_H(I,K_1)+\mu_2d_H(I,K_2)\bigr)\), where \(d_H(I,K)=\min_{J\models K}|\{x\in\mathcal X:I(x)\ne J(x)\}|\).

The model it lives in

A society consists of source units in two complete types, distinguished by knowledge bases \(K_1,K_2\), with rational population shares \(\mu_1,\mu_2\). Each unit contributes its type’s Hamming distance to the merge objective; given \(K_1,K_2,R\), ask whether some positive type-mass distribution makes \(R\) exactly the set of minimizing interpretations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the two-base weighted-Hamming obtainability completeness result in §3.1; it leaves the other distance results, the tractable restriction, and priority-base merging results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does exact obtainability behave with more than two knowledge-base types when the population shares are unknown or constrained?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s weighted-Hamming result in §3.1. The supplied text prints its heading simply as “Theorem,” with no number, so I can’t quote a theorem number without inventing one. The result is proved in this paper: deciding whether a satisfiable formula is obtainable from two knowledge bases by a weighted sum of Hamming distances is \(\Pi_2^p\)-complete.

Here is a continuous version that keeps the question recognizable. Take a finite Boolean vocabulary \(\mathcal X\), two consistent knowledge-base types \(K_1,K_2\), and a consistent observed result \(R\). A type is a complete source description for the merge: its members hold the same knowledge base and contribute in the same way. A mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of the source population of type \(K_i\). Ask whether there is a positive rational population mix \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\) for which \(R\) is exactly the result of merging those sources by Hamming distance. Formally, with
\[ d_H(I,K)=\min_{J\models K}|\{x\in\mathcal X:I(x)\ne J(x)\}|, \]
the question is whether some \(\mu\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^2\) satisfies
\[ \operatorname{Mod}(R) = \arg\min_{I\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal X|}} \bigl(\mu_1 d_H(I,K_1)+\mu_2 d_H(I,K_2)\bigr). \]
A solution is such a mass vector. This is a feasibility question, not an optimization problem: the requirement is exact equality with the observed result.

A plausible regime is a large network of exchangeable monitoring devices or databases, with millions of source units but only two recurring knowledge-base patterns. The result \(R\) comes from a later audit or trusted observation; the question is whether some balance of the two source populations could have produced it under the merge rule. Here \(N\) is very large while the support size is \(\tau=2\). Each type must include all features relevant to the merge, so devices with different report bases or other relevant properties belong to different types.

This is close to the paper’s problem for a specific reason: the paper asks how source influence could explain an observed merge result, and its introduction explicitly notes that group size can affect perceived source strength. In this exchangeable-source regime, a group’s total influence comes from its population share. The translation is exact: positive integer weights \(w_1,w_2\) correspond to masses \(\mu_i=w_i/(w_1+w_2)\); conversely, rational masses can be scaled to integer weights. Scaling does not change the minimizers. Thus the continuous decision problem retains the theorem’s \(\Pi_2^p\)-completeness. I expect hardness transfers (Class B): the hard combinatorics are in the propositional models and distance calculations, not in the number of source units.

The mirror covers only the two-base, weighted-Hamming obtainability result. I would leave the paper’s priority-base results out: duplicating formula sources does not naturally make their priority semantics depend on mass, so forcing that connection would change the problem. The mirror also does not continuize the world or the output formula; the continuous object is the population of sources.

The weakest point is the identification of population share with reliability. It is credible when repeated reports are independent and exchangeable, but it does not capture the paper’s full setting, where two sources can have different reliability for reasons unrelated to their counts. There is another limitation: here the mix is the unknown to be inferred, whereas many population models take the society’s masses as input. Even so, this is a defensible regime-specific mirror, and the complexity result says that population continuity does not dissolve the anchored hardness.

It also raises natural follow-up questions: how does the problem change when there are more than two source types, when observed population shares constrain the feasible mixes, or when the target result is approximate rather than exact?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed two-type mirror has a credible population story: many reporting units could share one of two knowledge bases, and their proportions could represent their aggregate influence. That makes it sensible as an interpretation. It does not make the anchored computational question new.

For two positive rational masses with \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), choose a common denominator \(q\) and set \(w_i=q\mu_i\). Then
\[ \arg\min_I\bigl(\mu_1d_H(I,K_1)+\mu_2d_H(I,K_2)\bigr) = \arg\min_I\bigl(w_1d_H(I,K_1)+w_2d_H(I,K_2)\bigr). \]
Conversely, every pair of positive integer weights gives masses \(\mu_i=w_i/(w_1+w_2)\). Since multiplying all weights by the same positive constant preserves the minimizers, asking whether *some* population mix makes \(R\) the merge result is exactly the paper’s weighted-Hamming obtainability problem. The paper proves that problem \(\Pi_2^p\)-complete. This is not an objection that the continuous answer would be hard or unchanged: the two questions have the same instances and witnesses after normalization.

The same identity covers the natural count-based rescue. If \(n_i\) reporting units have knowledge base \(K_i\), summing their equal per-unit contributions gives \(n_1d_H(I,K_1)+n_2d_H(I,K_2)\); dividing by the population size gives the mass objective. Replicating the population only scales the objective. With the mix unknown, the paper already ranges over every rational ratio; with the mix fixed, the operation is simply weighted merging with those weights. Neither version creates a distinct two-type obtainability question.

Allowing \(\tau\) knowledge-base types is the strongest plausible extension. But then the support size is just the number of bases in the weighted-sum semantics, which the paper already defines for \(m\) bases; the complexity result under discussion restricts its analysis to two. A result for variable \(\tau\) could be worthwhile as a many-base complexity study, but its contribution would be that generalization, not continuization of the population.

So this anchor fails as a continuous mirror because the proposed population variable is already the paper’s reliability-weight variable in normalized form. The universal negative remains less secure: a different paper might contain source-level decisions or constraints that make population composition matter beyond aggregate weights. This paper gives little support for that route, but I cannot honestly rule it out in every possible scenario.
