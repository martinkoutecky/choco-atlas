The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\), good categories \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), rational additive values \(a_{tg}\), and rational \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]\), does there exist a finite-support rational plan \(y_{t,b}\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\) such that \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b} b_g y_{t,b}=q_g\) for every \(g\), and every pair of supported bundles satisfies \(\sum_g a_{tg}b_g\ge(1-\varepsilon)\sum_g a_{tg}(c_g-\mathbf{1}_{g=h})\) for every category \(h\) with \(c_h>0\)? If so, compute such a plan from the compressed input.

The model it lives in

A continuum of type masses \(\mu_t\) assigned to indivisible integral bundles \(b\), with mass variables \(y_{t,b}\), exact per-capita supply constraints \(q_g\), and pairwise \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX comparisons evaluated using each type’s additive values \(a_{tg}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror draws on Theorem 1’s approximate EFX allocation guarantee and randomized algorithm under scaled supplies. It leaves the rainbow cycle bound, rainbow path degree, recovery algorithm, and zero-sum cycle application outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational type-and-supply instance admit a complete \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX mass plan?
  • What is the complexity in \(|G|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length, and is there a finite support bound that permits a compressed algorithm?
  • Can finite replicated allocations be converted into a complete mass plan while preventing mass from escaping into unbounded bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]\), every additive instance with \(n\) agents has a partial \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX allocation leaving \(O(\sqrt{n/\varepsilon})\) goods unallocated, and a randomized algorithm finds such an allocation in expected time polynomial in the input size and \(1/\varepsilon\). I would use this result alone: Theorems 3 and 5 give extremal bounds, but no comparable computational result, and the paper supplies no hardness anchor.

A natural regime is a large recurring benefits or equipment programme. There may be millions of households but only \(\tau\) standardized valuation types, with each type specifying a complete additive valuation over a fixed catalogue of goods. The catalogue’s goods are replicated with supplies proportional to population size: if \(q_g\) is the per-household supply of category \(g\), a population of \(N\) has about \(Nq_g\) copies. Thus \(N\) is large relative to \(\tau\), while each household still receives an integral bundle of indivisible goods.

Call the mirror Continuous EFX with Charity. Its input is finite sets of types \(T\) and good categories \(G\); rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\); rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\ge0\); additive values \(a_{tg}\ge0\); and \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]\). A bundle is an integer vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\), valued by type \(t\) at \(v_t(b)=\sum_g a_{tg}b_g\). The decision variable \(y_{t,b}\ge0\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\). It must satisfy \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\); each good category’s use \(\sum_{t,b}b_g y_{t,b}\) cannot exceed \(q_g\).

The mass plan is \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX if, whenever \(y_{t,b}>0\) and \(y_{s,c}>0\), every category \(g\) with \(c_g>0\) satisfies
\[ v_t(b)\ge(1-\varepsilon)v_t(c-e_g), \]
where \(e_g\) removes one copy of \(g\). The question is to find a finite-support rational plan minimizing total unused supply \(\sum_g\bigl(q_g-\sum_{t,b}b_g y_{t,b}\bigr)\), and in particular determine whether the minimum is zero. Each unit of population mass receives an integral bundle; only the fraction of a type assigned to each bundle is continuous. The model keeps the paper’s fairness test and charity objective while scaling supplies with population.

I expect the fixed-\((|G|,\tau)\) version to fall in Class A, though that is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result of the paper. Theorem 1 gives a reason to ask: for \(N\) replicated households and goods, its leftover bound divided by \(N\) is \(O(1/\sqrt{N\varepsilon})\), suggesting that unused supply can vanish in the population limit. Finding a compact-input algorithm for the mass plan would still require controlling the compatible integral-bundle supports, perhaps through configuration optimization and pricing. Further questions include whether the optimum is always zero, how runtime depends on \(|G|,\tau\), and input bit length, and how well continuous plans round back to finite replicated instances.

The weak point is the limit argument. A vanishing number of leftover goods per household does not by itself guarantee that a limiting plan allocates all per-capita supply: a vanishingly small cohort might absorb many goods each and disappear in the limit. One must rule out this loss, or account for it, while preserving EFX. The paper does not do so, and its polynomial-time algorithm takes the explicit finite population size as input; it does not establish a polynomial algorithm for the compressed mass description. That gap is substantial, but the proposed question remains a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own approximate EFX-with-charity problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only strong anchor, and it is a genuine computational result: it gives a randomized polynomial-time algorithm for constructing approximate EFX allocations. I can’t dismiss it for lacking an algorithmic claim.

The strongest objection is that the proposed optimization is nearly answered already. For rational type masses and per-capita supplies, clear denominators with a population size \(N\), apply Theorem 1 to the replicated instance, and divide the resulting allocation by \(N\). This gives finite-support rational mass plans whose unused supply is \(O(1/\sqrt{N\varepsilon})\). As \(N\) grows, the infimum of unused supply is therefore zero. The paper does not show that this infimum is attained, so “find a plan minimizing unused supply” may not be well-posed as stated; exact full-supply feasibility is the more precise question.

That limitation does not transfer the algorithm. Its runtime is polynomial in the explicit instance, whose agents and replicated goods grow with \(N\). The compressed input records rational masses and supplies using only their bit lengths. The theorem gives no polynomial-time bound in that compressed representation, and its vanishing leftover bound does not itself produce a complete mass plan.

That is a real weakness in the proposed mirror, but it does not defeat a better one. A problem asking whether a complete mass plan exists, or how to compute one from a compressed type-and-supply description, is coherent: the model can preserve integral bundles and compare every supported bundle pair, as EFX requires. Standardized recipients and repeated supplies are also a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Existing work on few valuation types supports that regime rather than ruling it out.

So the negative case is weak. It exposes a gap between the paper’s explicit-instance algorithm and a compressed-population algorithm, and it shows that minimizing leftover supply may have infimum zero without an attained minimum. It does not establish that the exact-feasibility or compressed-computation questions are unworthy of study.
