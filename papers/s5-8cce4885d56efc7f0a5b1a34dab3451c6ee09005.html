The continuous mirror question

Input supported left types \(I\) and right types \(J\), with rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j>0\), strict rankings over partner types, and rational matching costs \(c_{ij}\). A feasible allocation is a flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\); unmatched mass ranks below every partner type. For each type, interpret its allocation as a lottery over partner types and being unmatched, with partner probabilities \(x_{ij}/\mu_i\) on the left and \(x_{ij}/\nu_j\) on the right. Compare \(x\) and any feasible rival \(y\) by independently drawing from those lotteries and summing the expected vote margins, weighted by type masses. Compute a popular feasible \(x\) of minimum cost \(\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}x_{ij}\), where popular means its expected margin against every feasible \(y\) is nonnegative.

The model it lives in

A bipartite market of interchangeable types with masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), type-level rankings and matching costs, and decision variable \(x_{ij}\), the mass matched between types \(i\) and \(j\). Minimize \(\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}x_{ij}\) subject to flow capacities and nonnegative expected-vote margin against every feasible rival flow under the paper’s fractional-matching semantics.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s minimum-cost-under-popularity question. It leaves aside the half-integral restriction, the construction of \(G^*\), and the structural results used to relate popular half-integral matchings to stable matchings.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a compact linear programming formulation and prove its polynomial bit complexity in the number of supported types and the input bit length.
  • Specify how solutions of the type-mass problem relate to discrete high-multiplicity markets with rational type proportions.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a mirror of Theorem 9, the paper’s main computational result. Proved here, it says that for a bipartite graph with strict preference lists and rational edge costs, a minimum-cost popular half-integral matching can be computed in polynomial time. Here “popular” means that, against every matching, the expected number of vertices preferring the proposed fractional matching is at least the number preferring the rival. The theorem’s half-integral solutions have edge values in \(\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\).

Lead mirror: Min-Cost Popular Type-Mass Matching. The instance has left-side types \(I\) and right-side types \(J\), with rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), strict rankings over opposite-side types, and rational costs \(c_{ij}\) for matching type \(i\) to type \(j\). Types include all preferences and costs relevant to the problem. A feasible allocation is a mass flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_j x_{ij}\le \mu_i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij}\le \nu_j. \]
Unmatched mass is allowed and is ranked below every acceptable partner type. The question is to find a feasible \(x\) of minimum total cost \(\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}x_{ij}\) that is popular.

Popularity here uses the paper’s fractional, expected-vote interpretation. For type \(i\), let \(p^x_i(j)=x_{ij}/\mu_i\), with the remaining probability assigned to being unmatched; define \(p^x_j(i)=x_{ij}/\nu_j\) on the other side. Against any feasible rival flow \(y\), draw the two allocations independently and sum the expected vote margins across the population. For example, the left-side contribution is
\[ \sum_{i\in I}\mu_i \sum_{r,s\in J\cup\{\perp\}} p^x_i(r)p^y_i(s)\,\operatorname{vote}_i(r,s), \]
with the analogous right-side contribution. The allocation \(x\) is popular when this total margin is nonnegative for every feasible \(y\). Thus the input is the society’s type distribution, the decision variable is mass assigned between types, and the objective is total matching cost. Each realized assignment can still match individual agents integrally; the flow records the mass of each type pair.

The regime is a large, repeated market such as a centralized placement of students and projects, or applicants and posts: many agents per preference-and-cost type, but far fewer types than agents. Preferences over partner types are strict; individuals within a type are interchangeable. The paper itself motivates matching students to projects and applicants to posts, so the application is close to its setting. It also explicitly defines popular fractional matchings by expected votes. That makes the expected-vote extension recognizable to the authors, even though the input society is now continuous.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). Given \(x\), the strongest rival is a maximum-weight type-mass matching, with edge weights equal to the expected vote swing against \(x\). That is a transportation linear program; its dual gives a compact certificate for popularity. The min-cost objective is linear as well, so the natural formulation has size polynomial in the number of supported types and the input bit length. The paper’s discussion of the popular fractional matching polytope \(P\) in Section 2 gives an internal reason to expect such a formulation. This would be a genuine high-multiplicity question: expanding rational masses into named agents can require a number of copies exponential in their bit length.

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s minimum-cost-under-popularity question, not the paper’s entire stable-matching construction or its other cited results. The paper notes that finite-graph min-cost popular fractional matching was already polynomially solvable via [13]. That is supporting evidence for tractability, not a collision: those instances still list individual vertices, while this problem is given by masses over types. Natural follow-up questions are whether the type-level dual certificate yields the claimed bit-complexity bound, and how its solutions relate to discrete high-multiplicity instances.

The soft spot is real: this is not a literal continuum of the paper’s per-instance half-integrality restriction. Half-integral edge values become aggregate masses, and rankings over interchangeable partner types replace strict rankings over named individuals. A referee could say the mirror captures min-cost popularity but drops a distinctive feature of Theorem 9. The case survives only for markets where type-level preferences and interchangeability are genuinely the right model; it should not be presented as preserving the paper’s exact individual-level solution space.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed type masses do not, by themselves, determine the vote between two matchings. In the paper, the same named vertices compare their assignments in \(M\) and \(M'\). A flow \(x_{ij}\) records how much type \(i\) is matched to type \(j\), but not which members of type \(i\) receive which partners in each allocation. With three partner types, for instance, the same two type-level marginals can be aligned so that everyone keeps the same partner, or coupled cyclically so that a majority prefers one allocation. The popularity margin can therefore depend on information absent from the proposed input.

The proponent’s independent-draw definition resolves that ambiguity, and it is not arbitrary: the paper itself defines popularity for fractional matchings using expected votes. But it changes the question into popularity between lotteries over assignments, rather than a vote between two assignments of the same continuous society. A more identity-faithful version would need a way to track how each agent’s assignment changes across alternatives. Encoding those identities or couplings risks losing the compact type representation that made the high-multiplicity mirror attractive.

That is a real objection to the match between the proposed model and Theorem 9. It is not a decisive objection to the best mirror: a repeated placement market with stable types and the paper’s expected-vote semantics is plausible, and the authors could recognize that as a population-level variant. The paper’s popular-fractional-matching formulation also makes this variant look close to an existing LP framework, which may limit its novelty, but outcome-space continuity alone is not a collision under ChoCo’s scope.

So the negative case is weak at its hardest point. The proponent’s model changes the semantics of the comparison, but the paper gives them a credible basis for that change. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 9.
