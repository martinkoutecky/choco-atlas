The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses μ over a finite set \(T\), type valuations \(v_t\), demands \(k_t\in\{1,2,3\}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q\) satisfying \(\sum_j q_j=\sum_t\mu_t k_t\), does there exist a causal price rule \(p(r,z)\) that can be updated as type mass arrives and supply is consumed, such that every measurable arrival order and every utility-maximizing bundle choice yields total welfare equal to the optimum for the initial population and supplies? Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle of product categories.

The model it lives in

A fluid multi-demand market with finitely many complete buyer types, mass \(\mu_t\) for each type, and per-capita supply \(q_j\) for each category. The decision is a state-dependent posted-price rule \(p(r,z)\); mass may be split across bundles in aggregate, while each unit of mass receives an integral bundle. The guarantee is optimal total welfare for every arrival path and every tie-breaking choice.

The objection that survived

The mirror does not specify a finite representation for a policy over all residual states, so its algorithmic complexity is not yet fixed.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9.25’s tri-demand dynamic-pricing result; it leaves the four-player result, the at-most-two-optimal-allocations result, and the rough-price lemmas aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a finite, efficiently evaluable representation of a policy be synthesized, with complexity measured in \(|J|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length?
  • Does an optimal policy exist for every fluid instance with \(k_t\le 3\) under adversarial arrivals and ties?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s tri-demand result. Theorem 9.25, proved in this paper using Algorithm 4, says that in a market where every buyer wants at most three items, a dynamic pricing exists and can be computed. Here “dynamic pricing” has the paper’s full guarantee: despite unknown arrival order and arbitrary tie-breaking, every bundle a buyer might choose at the posted prices can be extended to an allocation of maximum social welfare; prices may be updated after each buyer arrives. This is an existence and construction result, not a polynomial-time claim: the theorem gives no running-time bound.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large marketplace allocating copies of a fixed catalogue of products or service slots. Let \(J\) be the finite set of product categories and \(T\) the set of buyer types. A type \(t\) specifies a complete valuation vector \(v_t\) over \(J\) and a demand \(k_t\in\{1,2,3\}\). A rational mass \(\mu_t\) of the population has type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Category \(j\) has \(q_j\) copies per unit mass of population; take rational supplies with \(\sum_j q_j=\sum_t\mu_t k_t\). In a market of size \(N\), that means roughly \(N\mu_t\) buyers of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) individually indivisible copies of category \(j\). The regime is \(N\) much larger than the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct types. Such types are credible when many buyers share the same demand cap and valuation pattern.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Tri-Demand Fluid Dynamic Pricing. The instance is \(J,T,\mu,(v_t,k_t)_{t\in T}\), and \(q\) as above. Each individual still chooses an integral bundle of at most \(k_t\) copies; aggregate type mass can be split fractionally among bundles. At state \((r,z)\), where \(r_t\) is the remaining mass of each type and \(z_j\) the remaining supply, a causal pricing policy posts positive prices \(p_j(r,z)\). An arriving type \(t\) may choose any utility-maximizing available bundle, and ties are adversarial. Define \(V(r,z)\) as the maximum welfare of a feasible fractional allocation of the remaining type mass to integral bundles, subject to the remaining supplies. The question is whether there is a policy that works for every measurable arrival order and every measurable tie-breaking choice, so that the final allocation always has welfare \(V(\mu,q)\).

Equivalently, at every reachable state, the posted prices must make every demanded bundle safe for the optimum: assigning any infinitesimal mass \(\varepsilon\) of a possible next type \(t\) to any demanded bundle \(B\) must satisfy
\[ \varepsilon v_t(B)+V(r-\varepsilon e_t,\;z-\varepsilon a_B)=V(r,z), \]
for all sufficiently small feasible \(\varepsilon>0\). Here \(a_B\) is the vector of copies in \(B\). The policy may then update prices using the new state. A solution is a causal state-dependent price rule with this property; the objective remains total social welfare, not seller revenue.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The completion value is an LP over type-and-bundle masses, so state-dependent dual prices are a natural starting point; the paper’s legality and Hall-type arguments also suggest useful exchange structure. But those observations do not prove the result: the difficult requirement is that *every* tied demand choice preserves an optimal continuation, at every state, under adversarial arrival. A static equilibrium price alone would not settle it. The main follow-up questions are whether a price policy has a finite description and can be computed with running time polynomial in \(|J|,\tau\), and the input bit length, and whether the bounded demand of three can be removed.

This mirrors Theorem 9.25, not the paper’s four-buyer result or its at-most-two-optimal-allocations result. The mirror retains the paper’s multi-demand preferences, welfare objective, posted prices, adversarial order, and arbitrary tie-breaking. Its co-modification is that supplies scale with population and each type’s mass may receive a fractional aggregate allocation, while each individual still gets an integral bundle. That makes it a genuine population continuum rather than merely fractional goods.

My weakest point is that the paper’s construction uses a legality graph on individually indexed items and finite allocation cycles. Compressing many equivalent copies into per-capita supply may change the structure those arguments rely on. The proposed question is therefore plausible as the same dynamic-pricing problem in a high-multiplicity regime, but Theorem 9.25 does not itself establish the continuous result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The tri-demand setting is a credible high-multiplicity regime: many buyers can share a valuation vector and demand cap, while supplies scale with the population. I would not reject Theorem 9.25 on multiplicity or identity grounds. The sharper objection is that the proposed mirror has not yet landed on a computational problem of the kind this programme studies. Theorem 9.25 gives an existence result and a construction via Algorithm 4, but states no running-time or bit-complexity bound. The proposed fluid version asks whether a causal pricing policy exists across a continuum of residual states and every measurable arrival path. Its safety condition involving \(V(r,z)\) is a pointwise property, not an encoding of that policy or an algorithm for producing it. As posed, policy existence looks like a continuous control or viability question; the paper’s finite algorithm does not tell us how to make it a finite computational task.

The strongest repair would ask for an algorithm that synthesizes a succinct policy, with a specified representation and complexity measured in \(|J|\), \(\tau\), and input bit length. But that adds a consequential modelling choice: the theorem supplies prices for a finite market, not a representation for a policy over all fluid states. Different policy encodings could produce different complexity questions, and the arbitrary-arrival, adversarial-tie guarantee makes that synthesis question substantially different from simply replacing buyer counts with masses. A dual LP for \(V\) is a starting point; it does not establish that a compact policy preserves the guarantee.

That is enough to reject the proponent’s anchor as currently formulated: it has not yet specified the computational object whose complexity would make it a ChoCo mirror. It is not enough to support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A finite, natural policy representation might make the repaired question compelling, and the high-multiplicity scenario itself is sensible. The negative case is therefore weak exactly at its broadest point: I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror of this result.
