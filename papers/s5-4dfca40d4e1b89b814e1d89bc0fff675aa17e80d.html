What the mirror covers

Only Theorem 9 is anchored; all other algorithmic and hardness results are left unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a single Class A candidate anchored in Theorem 9.

The paper’s Theorem 9, proved in the paper, states that deciding whether an Additively Separable Hedonic Game has a Nash-stable partition is strongly NP-hard even when the underlying graph is a star. The reduction is from 3-Partition: for item vertices \(A\), bin vertices \(B\), a stalker \(s\), and helper \(s'\), the arcs satisfy \(w(x,s)=-1\), \(w(s,b)=2T\) for \(b\in B\), \(w(s,a)=-a\) for \(a\in A\), \(w(s,s')=T\), and \(w(s',s)=1\). A Nash-stable partition exists exactly when the items can be divided into \(n\) triples of sum \(T\). This is a genuine hardness anchor, not merely a literature summary. [Theorem 9 in the published paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol244-esa2022/LIPIcs.ESA.2022.64/LIPIcs.ESA.2022.64.pdf).

My mirror is Nash-Star\(_\infty\). A type is a complete star-game role: for example, an item type \(\theta_a\) whose contribution to the stalker is \(-a\), a bin type \(b\), the stalker type \(s\), and the helper type \(s'\). The input consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), a total population scale \(M\), and an additive interaction kernel \(w:T\times T\to\mathbb Q\), whose nonzero support is a star centred at \(s\). Thus \(M\mu_t\) is the corresponding high-multiplicity population of type \(t\).

A solution is a finite collection of coalition-mass vectors \(x^1,\ldots,x^q\), where \(x^j_t\ge 0\) and \(\sum_jx^j_t=\mu_t\). Each individual belongs to one coalition; \(x^j_t\) is merely the aggregate mass of type \(t\) in coalition \(j\). The utility of type \(t\) in coalition \(j\) is

\[ U_t(j)=\sum_{u\in T}w(t,u)\,M x^j_u. \]

The partition is Nash stable if, whenever \(x^j_t>0\),

\[ U_t(j)\ge 0 \quad\text{and}\quad U_t(j)\ge U_t(\ell) \]

for every coalition \(\ell\). The first inequality rules out becoming a singleton; the second rules out joining another existing coalition. The decision problem asks whether such a mass partition exists.

The natural regime is a large population of exchangeable workers, jobs, or participants assigned to repeated teams. Agents of the same load or role type have exactly the same preferences and interaction coefficients. We can have \(M\gg \tau=|T|\): many item agents of each of a moderate number of load classes, many identical bin or team-slot agents, and repeated coordinator roles. This is not a claim that every national or organisational coalition system has few types; it is a specific high-multiplicity regime in which the paper’s star interaction pattern is a sensible type-level template.

The authors should recognise this as their problem: the object remains an additive hedonic game, the action remains a partition into coalitions, and the stability notion remains unilateral Nash stability. Only the population is continuized. Fractional \(x^j_t\) does not mean divisible outcomes: it means that a large cohort of otherwise indistinguishable agents can be split across coalitions.

I would expect Nash-Star\(_\infty\) to be tractable, at least for the one-centre star regime, probably through linear programming or a finite coalition-template formulation. The reason is precisely what Theorem 9 exposes. Its reduction needs every item to be assigned indivisibly to one bin. In the mass version, item-type mass can be divided among several bin coalitions. The condition that each bin receives total load \(T\) becomes a fractional load-balancing constraint, and the 3-Partition obstruction disappears. For the reduction-shaped instances, one can distribute mass of each item type fractionally so that every unit of bin mass carries load \(T\), making the stalker indifferent between its helper coalition and the bin coalitions.

Thus the expected classification is Class A, with an important qualification: for unrestricted numbers of types, the number of possible coalition-support patterns may itself be exponential. A full theorem should determine whether those patterns admit a polynomial-size LP, or whether the unrestricted typed-star problem becomes continuum-specifically hard.

This mirror covers only Theorem 9’s general Nash-stability existence problem. I would not stretch it to Theorem 10, which uses HC-nets rather than additive preferences, or to Theorems 11–12, where connectedness introduces a separate and nontrivial notion for mass coalitions.

The weakest point is that the type-level star is a high-multiplicity completion of a star, not literally a simple graph with one named centre and many individually distinct leaves. If a referee insists that graph adjacency itself must remain individual rather than type-level, the mirror becomes much less convincing. The positive case survives because the paper’s additive utilities already depend only on role interactions in the proposed regime, and the continuum question is exactly whether the 3-Partition hardness was caused by population multiplicity and indivisibility.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 9 is a legitimate computational anchor, but the proposed mirror does not preserve the phenomenon that makes the theorem interesting.

The reduction’s combinatorics live in a finite inventory of individually assigned objects: each item \(a_i\) is a separate agent, each bin vertex is a separate slot, and stability forces an integral \(3\)-partition. If every distinct item value is made a separate type, then the relevant type count is \(\tau=3n\) and the theorem’s instances have no high multiplicity. If equal-valued items are grouped into types, the problem has already been changed; if many copies are introduced, their mass can be divided among coalitions and the integral assignment constraint disappears.

More seriously, the proposed mass model has a direct degeneracy. Let \(\beta_j\) be the mass of bin type in coalition \(j\), and let the centre’s helper coalition give it utility \(TM\mu_{s'}\). Since the number of coalitions is unrestricted, split the bin mass among sufficiently many coalitions so that

\[ 2TM\beta_j\le TM\mu_{s'} \]

for every \(j\). Put the item mass in separate coalitions. Every item and bin agent then has utility \(0\), while joining the centre’s coalition would give it negative utility. The centre cannot profitably join any bin coalition, and the helper remains attractive. This is Nash stable for every collection of item values, whether or not the underlying \(3\)-Partition instance is feasible.

That is not merely a different computational answer. The bin-slot object has vanished: atomless mass can be fragmented into arbitrarily many tiny coalitions. Fixing the number of coalitions, imposing one unit of bin mass per coalition, or forbidding splitting would restore the intended packing structure, but each is an additional finite-slot or indivisibility constraint absent from Nash stability and not forced by continuizing the population. With a fixed number of slots but divisible item mass, one instead obtains a fractional packing problem.

There is also a structural mismatch. A genuine star has one exceptional centre. Giving the centre positive mass creates many centres and replaces the star by a type-level interaction network; giving it zero mass requires a hybrid model with a finite exceptional agent and a nonatomic population. That hybrid could be worth studying, but it is no longer the paper’s ASHG population being continuized: it is a fractional assignment problem with external finite facilities.

Thus the proponent has shown that one can invent a related typed or mixed finite–nonatomic game. They have not shown a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 9. The negative case is not a proof that no such hybrid problem could ever be interesting, but the pure population mirror proposed here collapses, while every repair reintroduces the discrete bin or item structure that the mirror was supposed to remove.
