The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as hardness, polynomial time, or fixed parameter tractability. Equations (1)–(4) state choice conditions; the paper defines the Kemeny optimization and computes examples, but neither is a numbered complexity result. So I cannot honestly give the required anchor-based case.

The strongest positive candidate is a continuous version of the paper’s Kemeny aggregation of university rankings. Call it Continuous Kemeny Median for University Rankings. An instance consists of a finite set \(U\) of universities and a support of \(\tau\) complete rankings \(R\) of \(U\), each with a positive binary rational mass \(\mu_R\), where \(\sum_R \mu_R=1\). A type is one complete ranking, including its ties; its mass is the fraction of ranking-generators with that ranking. The task is to output a complete ranking \(Q\) minimizing \(\sum_R \mu_R d(R,Q)\), where \(d\) is the paper’s Kemeny distance: the number of entries on which the two binary relation matrices differ. A solution is a minimizing ranking and its objective value.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a national university assessment assembled from many evaluators or panels that rank the same universities under a shared protocol. There might be hundreds of thousands of evaluators but far fewer distinct rankings, so \(N\gg\tau\). This is not the paper’s actual data regime: it aggregates nine published ranking mechanisms. The positive case is that the paper explicitly casts ranking aggregation as collective choice and defines the Kemeny median objective, so replacing a finite electorate of ranking inputs with a distribution over ranking types preserves the central mathematical task. The authors could plausibly recognize that as their problem in a setting with many comparable ranking-generators.

I would expect this mirror to be Class B. A finite profile with \(n_R\) occurrences of each ranking embeds by setting \(\mu_R=n_R/N\), where \(N\) is the total number of rankings. The continuous objective is then the finite-profile objective scaled by \(1/N\), so the minimizing rankings are unchanged. Any hardness driven by the number of universities therefore survives the population continuization. The paper itself makes no complexity claim about this problem, so that expected classification is not an anchor or a result of the article.

This mirror covers the Kemeny-median branch of the paper’s proposed aggregation method. It does not cover the paper’s Borda instability examples, its comparison of Copeland with Borda, or its empirical conclusions as a whole. It also raises questions about exact versus approximate medians and how the answer depends on \(|U|\), \(\tau\), and the bit length of the masses.

The weak point is substantial: the proposed many-evaluator scenario extends the paper’s setup, which has only nine heterogeneous ranking mechanisms, and the article reports no numbered computational result to anchor the extension. The Kemeny formulation makes the mirror mathematically credible; it cannot supply the missing anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Equations (1)–(4) define choice conditions; the Kemeny section defines an optimization objective and works through examples. The article reports a small empirical comparison, using an approximation to the Kemeny median. That gives us no computational result to mirror.

The strongest candidate is a continuous Kemeny median for a population of evaluators or panels ranking the same universities under a shared protocol. Give each complete ranking type a rational mass \(\mu_R\), then minimize \(\sum_R \mu_R d(R,Q)\) over aggregate rankings \(Q\). This is a coherent high-multiplicity problem, and the article’s collective-choice framing makes it plausible that the authors would recognize it. The fact that the paper’s actual inputs are nine distinct ranking mechanisms does not defeat that better version.

It still does not supply an anchor from this paper: the candidate is a new complexity question built around the paper’s objective, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper proves. So the strongest negative case is narrow and firm on provenance: none of the proposed work can be presented as mirroring a named computational result here.

The broader claim—that no scenario could make this Kemeny question worthwhile—is weak. A population of comparable ranking-generators is plausible, and the continuous question may deserve study on its own. The paper supports that formulation; it does not establish it as a result-anchored mirror.
