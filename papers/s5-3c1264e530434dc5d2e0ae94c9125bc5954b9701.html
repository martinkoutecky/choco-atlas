For mass \(x_{q,b}\) of class \(q\) with belief \(b\) expressing opinion \(1\), let \(y_r=x_{r,0}+x_{r,1}\); the cost of opinion \(a\in\{0,1\}\) is \(c_{q,b}(a;x)=\alpha_q|a-b|+(1-\alpha_q)\sum_{r\in Q}K_{qr}(y_r\text{ if }a=0,\ \lambda_r-y_r\text{ if }a=1)\). The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s small-minority subversion problem. The relevant anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper (using an NP-hardness result for its source problem cited as [8]). It states that, for every fixed \(0<\epsilon<133/155\), it is NP-hard, given the social-network instance—its graph and stubbornness levels—to decide whether there is a belief assignment whose initial minority has at most \(\frac{n-1}{2}(1-\epsilon)\) vertices and from which a sequence of best-response moves reaches an equilibrium with opinion \(1\) in the majority. The hardness is about finding a subvertable assignment despite the initial minority being substantially smaller than half; the endpoint need only be a majority, not a supermajority.

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study, which I’ll call \(\epsilon\)-Mass Subversion in Finite-Type Networks. An instance has a finite set \(Q\) of structural classes, with positive rational masses \(\lambda_q\) summing to \(1\); stubbornness values \(\alpha_q\in(0,1)\); and a symmetric, nonnegative rational interaction kernel \(K_{qr}\). The kernel specifies how strongly a member of class \(q\) is influenced by the mass of class \(r\). All inputs are binary encoded, and \(\epsilon\) is a fixed constant in the range of Theorem 2.

A candidate belief assignment chooses \(\beta_q\in[0,\lambda_q]\), the mass in class \(q\) whose belief is \(1\); the rest believe \(0\). Thus agents are grouped by their complete relevant type: structural class, stubbornness, and belief. Let \(x_{q,b}\) be the mass of type \((q,b)\) currently expressing opinion \(1\), and let \(y_q=x_{q,0}+x_{q,1}\). For an agent in class \(q\) with belief \(b\), the cost of expressing \(a\in\{0,1\}\) at state \(x\) is
\[ c_{q,b}(a;x)=\alpha_q|a-b|+(1-\alpha_q)\sum_{r\in Q}K_{qr} \begin{cases} y_r,&a=0,\\ \lambda_r-y_r,&a=1. \end{cases} \]
The truthful initial state has all belief-\(0\) mass expressing \(0\) and all belief-\(1\) mass expressing \(1\). A move selects positive mass currently expressing an opinion that is strictly more costly for its type and switches that mass to the cheaper opinion. A solution consists of a belief assignment and a finite sequence of such mass moves ending at an equilibrium, where no positive mass can strictly lower its cost by switching, and with
\[ \sum_{q\in Q}\beta_q\le \frac{1-\epsilon}{2}, \qquad \sum_{q\in Q}y_q>\frac12 \]
at the initial and final states, respectively. In other words: can a minority of at most \((1-\epsilon)/2\) of the population start truthfully, then reach an equilibrium where opinion \(1\) holds a strict majority?

The regime I have in mind is a large regional or organizational network considering a shared issue. Many residents or members have the same initial leaning, stubbornness, and rates of contact with each cohort; there may be millions of people but far fewer distinct structural classes. The finite interaction matrix is the network description at class level, and masses make minority size and public opinion naturally expressible as population fractions. Allowing beliefs to divide a structural class is appropriate: once belief is specified, its two belief groups are distinct complete types.

I expect the problem to remain hard, probably with hardness driven by the interaction pattern over classes rather than by the number of named people. The paper’s reduction encodes its combinatorics in a network of gadgets, so a finite-type kernel gives a natural place for that structure to live even when each class represents a large cohort. The question preserves the paper’s central computational concern—whether best-response behavior can subvert the initial majority—while replacing counts by mass and the graph by type-level interaction rates. It asks for reachability and robustness, not just an analytic characterization of equilibria.

The main obstacle is real: Theorem 2 does not itself prove hardness for this mirror. A continuous class can split across opinions, and fractional mass moves might create subversion paths unavailable in the discrete gadget construction. Establishing hardness would require a reduction that rules out those extra paths; if that fails, the mirror may instead be tractable for some kernel classes or hard for continuum-specific reasons. Further questions include whether hardness persists for restricted interaction matrices, what parameterizations by class count or kernel structure help, and how much initial minority mass is necessary for subversion.

This case covers Theorem 2 alone. Its weakest point is the change from individual graph neighborhoods to class-level mixing, especially the resulting fractional dynamics. Still, the proposed problem retains the same agents’ incentives, truthful start, best-response reachability, and majority-subversion objective. That makes it a plausible continuous form of the paper’s problem even though the hardness result must be established anew.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a real computational anchor: it proves NP-hardness for a decision problem about choosing beliefs and reaching a majority through best-response moves. So the negative case cannot dismiss the paper as lacking a result to mirror. Its strongest objection is that this result depends on a kind of individual influence that an atomless population does not preserve.

In the paper, a vertex’s identity in the graph determines which neighbors affect its incentives. A faithful type must therefore include its network position, not just its stubbornness and belief. For arbitrary graphs, those positions can all be different; the gadget reduction in Theorem 2 relies on specific links between variable and clause vertices. Grouping agents into a fixed number of classes and replacing edges with an interaction kernel changes that local network game into a mean-field game. The class-level model may be worth studying, but its reachability question is not the high-multiplicity version of Theorem 2’s graph problem.

A graphon-style mirror faces a sharper dilemma. If a single agent has measure zero, that agent’s best-response move cannot change anyone else’s aggregate incentives. The paper’s dynamics depend on individual moves changing neighbors’ incentives, potentially triggering further moves. Allowing a positive mass of a class to move together restores aggregate influence, but makes the move a coordinated cohort update. That is a substantive change to the dynamics and to the role of the adversary who chooses the sequence. If the continuum is encoded as a step graphon with one interval per vertex, it can preserve the finite graph, but the type count grows with \(n\); it has not compressed the population into a high-multiplicity regime.

The best version of the proponent’s case remains a network of large, nearly homogeneous cohorts with shared contact rates. That is a plausible setting, and fractional belief assignments and mass updates could define a legitimate computational problem. The weakness in the negative case is that the paper’s authors might recognize that cohort model as a useful extension of their question. The strongest conclusion the material supports is narrower: Theorem 2 has no close, faithful atomless mirror that preserves its individual best-response dynamics. The universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is not established; that part of the case is weak.
