The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), target \(c^\star\), \(k\), a rational budget \(\beta\), and types \(i \in [\tau]\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\), initial rankings \(\sigma_i\), and nonnegative rational arrays \(a^i,b^i\), decide whether there are masses \(x_{i,\rho} \ge 0\) over complete rankings \(\rho\) such that \(\sum_\rho x_{i,\rho}=\mu_i\), \(\sum_{i,\rho}\pi_i(\rho)x_{i,\rho}\le\beta\), and \(\sum_{i,\rho}x_{i,\rho}(I_{c^\star,\rho}-I_{c,\rho})\ge 0\) for every \(c\ne c^\star\). Here \(I_{c,\rho}\) indicates whether \(c\) is in \(\rho\)'s top \(k\), and \(\pi_i(\rho)\) sums \(a^i_j+b^i_{j'}\) over pairs whose order is reversed from \(\sigma_i\), where \(j<j'\) are their original positions.

The model it lives in

Types \(i\) consist of a shared initial ranking \(\sigma_i\) and shared position-based cost arrays \(a^i,b^i\), each carrying mass \(\mu_i\). Decision variables \(x_{i,\rho}\) distribute each type's mass among complete rankings; minimize total swap cost subject to mass conservation and \(k\)-Approval co-winner constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's \(k\)-Approval swap attack with additively separable costs and its continuous counterpart in Corollary 3; it leaves other cost structures and voting rules aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that for every fixed \(k\ge 2\), constructive \(k\)-Approval-Swap Bribery with additively separable nonnegative rational costs is NP-complete, even when every demotion price \(a_i^v\) is in \(\{0,1\}\) and every promotion price \(b_i^v\) is in \(\{1,2\}\). Here, swapping candidates originally at positions \(j<j'\) costs \(a_j^v+b_{j'}^v\). The paper proves this by adapting a reduction from Multicolored Clique. Thus the anchor is discrete hardness under a particularly restricted cost model.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Continuous Additive-Cost \(k\)-Approval Swap Bribery. An instance has candidates \(C\), a target \(c^*\), \(k\), a rational budget \(\beta\), and types \(i=1,\ldots,\tau\). Type \(i\) has mass \(\mu_i\), initial ranking \(\sigma_i\), and rational arrays \(a^i,b^i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). If \(c\) precedes \(d\) in \(\sigma_i\), their swap cost is \(a^i_{\mathrm{rank}_{\sigma_i}(c)}+b^i_{\mathrm{rank}_{\sigma_i}(d)}\). Let \(\pi_i(\rho)\) be the sum of these costs over pairs whose order is reversed in destination ranking \(\rho\).

The question is whether there are nonnegative masses \(x_{i,\rho}\), for every type and full destination ranking, such that
\[ \sum_\rho x_{i,\rho}=\mu_i,\qquad \sum_{i,\rho}\pi_i(\rho)x_{i,\rho}\le\beta, \]
and, for every opponent \(c\ne c^*\),
\[ \sum_{i,\rho}x_{i,\rho} \bigl(I[c^*\text{ is in the top }k\text{ of }\rho] -I[c\text{ is in the top }k\text{ of }\rho]\bigr)\ge 0. \]
The last condition makes \(c^*\) a co-winner. A solution is this mass-transfer matrix; equivalently, one can minimize its cost and compare the optimum with \(\beta\). Each infinitesimal agent still receives a complete ranking: the variables divide a type’s mass among rankings.

A plausible regime is a large electorate with millions of voters but a modest number of ballot segments, each with a shared initial ranking and a shared persuasion tariff based on positions in that ranking. A type must include both the full ranking and the cost arrays, so voters with different prices are separate types. That is a sensible setting for segment-level campaigning, and the paper itself motivates its model with percentage-point changes to voter segments. It is a property of this regime, not of every election: a small committee or an electorate with mostly individual price schedules may have no useful gap between population size and type count.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, in Class A. Corollary 3 of the paper proves polynomial-time solvability for the continuous \(k\)-Approval Margin-Swap Bribery problem with additively separable costs; the question above is its zero-margin special case. The algorithm’s key step is the polynomial-time pricing algorithm of Theorem 3, which uses a dynamic program over top-\(k\) sets and then solves the configuration LP. In particular, this continuous question is tractable even for cost arrays drawn from the restricted values in Theorem 4. That does not make Theorem 4’s finite-voter hardness disappear as a result about finite elections; it shows that the mass-transfer version is a distinct computational question with a different feasible set. This is the paper’s own, unusually direct evidence that the proposed mirror is one its authors would recognize.

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s \(k\)-Approval swap attack and additive cost model, not the paper’s entire bribery landscape. It raises useful boundary questions: which broader cost structures retain efficient pricing, and what happens when costs are general? The paper’s Theorem 7 gives a sharp nearby warning: with general costs, continuous \(k\)-Approval-Swap Bribery is not fixed-parameter tractable in \(k\) unless \(FPT=W[1]\). One can also ask whether the continuous optimum yields additive approximations or preprocessing for large finite electorates.

My weakest point is that Theorem 4’s reduction need not have small type support: its constructed voters may require many distinct rankings or cost schedules. So this argument does not claim that the hard instances themselves sit in the high-multiplicity regime. The case is instead that the underlying attack has a credible large-population, small-support regime, and that the paper states and solves its continuous analogue without changing the attack or cost model. If real persuasion costs are highly individualized, that regime is a poor fit; the type definition makes that limitation explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is a novelty collision. The proposed question is already answered in the paper: Corollary 3 gives a polynomial-time algorithm for continuous \(k\)-Approval Margin-Swap Bribery with additively separable costs. It minimizes cost over mass transfers, so it also solves the proponent’s budget-decision question. It covers arbitrary nonnegative rational cost arrays and margins, not just Theorem 4’s restricted arrays in \(\{0,1\}\) and \(\{1,2\}\), or the zero-margin co-winner case.

That match holds even though the proponent writes variables for every destination ranking. The paper proves that each type’s mass can be moved to stable representatives of its possible top-\(k\) sets without changing any candidate’s score or increasing cost. It then solves the pricing problem by dynamic programming and uses the configuration LP to obtain the continuous algorithm. The stronger mirror has therefore been formulated and solved, not just suggested.

The population regime itself is credible: voters sharing an initial ranking and a position-based persuasion tariff form sensible types. The reduction behind Theorem 4 need not have small support, but that does not undermine the mirror; Corollary 3 applies for arbitrary \(\tau\) and runs in polynomial time in it. Narrowing the question to fixed \(k\), the theorem’s restricted tariffs, or a particular type support still leaves it inside the result already proved. Further work on discrete approximations or on broader cost models could be worthwhile, but it would be a different question. For this anchor, there is no unaddressed continuous mirror to pursue.
