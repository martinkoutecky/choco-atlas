The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a plausible high-multiplicity continuation, not for claiming that this paper already establishes a computational result.

There is no named complexity result here: the paper does not state that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a similar class. Theorem 1, proved here, gives a unique contest equilibrium and a share-function characterization of winning probabilities and payoffs for a given team profile; it does not analyze running time. The closest structural anchor is Proposition 3, proved here: under equal prize shares, an assignment is stable if and only if, after ordering teams by winning probability, every player in each team has ability at least as high as every player in the next team. This is a sharp characterization, but it is not itself a complexity theorem.

A natural continuous question suggested by Proposition 3 is Egalitarian Continuum Team Stability. Consider a large pool of players whose complete types are their ability levels \(a_1<\cdots<a_q\); players of the same type have the same effort cost and face the same contest rules. Let \(\mu_r\) be the population fraction of type \(r\). Teams have \(M\) positions and use equal shares. There is a continuum of teams, grouped into leagues of \(J\) teams; each league draws its teams from a distribution \(\lambda\) over team compositions. A composition is \(n\in\mathbb{N}^q\) with \(\sum_r n_r=M\), and \(\lambda_n\) is the mass of teams with that composition. Feasibility requires \(\sum_n\lambda_n=1\) and \(\mu_r=\frac{1}{M}\sum_n\lambda_n n_r\).

For each composition \(n\), define its expected winning probability and a type-\(r\) member’s expected payoff by averaging the paper’s contest equilibrium over the \(J-1\) opponent teams drawn from \(\lambda\). The question is: given the type masses and contest parameters, find a feasible \(\lambda\) with no successful head-hunting swap, or report that none exists. A swap exchanges a type-\(r\) player in one team with a type-\(s\) incumbent in another; it is successful when the recruiting team’s expected winning probability rises and the recruited player’s expected payoff rises. The solution is the stable team-composition distribution. There is no separate welfare objective, just as the paper’s object is stability rather than optimization. Restricting to the all-teams-active regime keeps the mirror aligned with Section 4.

The regime is plausible: many parallel contests, a small number of publicly understood ability categories, and many players in each category. The type definition preserves the model’s actual distinctions—ability, effort cost, and applicable sharing rule—while the mass distribution removes arbitrary one-person granularity. This is also unusually close to a setting the authors themselves contemplate: the conclusion discusses a continuum of players and teams with finite team size, and cites related work on finite player types and continuum populations. That prior work supports the regime’s plausibility; it does not settle the complexity of this paper’s head-hunting question.

For the equal-share case, I would provisionally expect a tractable result: Proposition 3’s sorting structure suggests that a sorted distribution can be constructed directly from the type masses, and atomless mass removes divisibility obstacles in forming teams. The key open question is whether the proposition’s exact “if and only if” characterization survives when payoffs are averaged across random leagues. Further questions include whether stable distributions are unique, and how the answer changes under hierarchical sharing rules.

The weakest point is also the central qualification: atomless players cannot change the outcome of one finite contest by themselves. Making the population continuous therefore requires the replicated-league market and expected-payoff formulation, which is a genuine model extension. The authors’ conclusion gives that extension a credible footing, but Proposition 3 does not prove its stability characterization. So this is a concrete, author-recognizable continuous research question—not a computational mirror already established by the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes contest equilibrium; Proposition 3 characterizes stable assignments under equal sharing; Propositions 4 and 5 characterize stability under other sharing rules. None states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm. The proposed task, “find a feasible \(\lambda\) with no successful swap,” adds a search problem that the paper itself does not formulate. Turning that search into a precise computational problem would require choosing its input, output, and encoding.

Proposition 3 is the best anchor, and its strongest continuation would follow the authors’ own large-market discussion: finitely many player types, a continuum of players and teams, and finite teams drawn into contests. That makes multiplicity plausible. But the resulting \(\lambda\)-problem concerns stable distributions in a randomized market, with expected payoffs and a new account of deviations across leagues. Proposition 3 concerns exact swaps in a fixed finite assignment and compares the resulting contest and player payoffs there. Moving to the market changes the stability question itself. It could be a worthwhile economic model, but the paper offers no reason to expect its analysis to produce a computational problem or an algorithmic landscape.

This objection has a clear limit. The authors explicitly contemplate a continuum of players and teams, and their related work on finite player types supports the regime’s plausibility. That is evidence in favor of a sensible high-multiplicity setting, not evidence that the computational question is already solved. So I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The negative case is narrower: this is a weak target for ChoCo’s computational programme unless someone can motivate a concrete complexity question in the market model.
