The continuous mirror question

Instance: a finite set of complete student types \(T\), schools \(S\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\), weak school priorities \(\succsim_s\), and a rational assignment \(p\). Each assignment \(x\) has variables \(x_{ta}\geq 0\) for \(a\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), with \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\). Call \(x\) weakly stable if, for every type \(t\) and school \(s\), positive mass \(\sum_{a:s\succ_t a}x_{ta}>0\) implies that \(s\) is full and no type \(u\) with \(t\succ_s u\) has \(x_{us}>0\). Let \(\mathcal W(\mu)\) be the weakly stable mass assignments and \(\mathcal X(\mu)=\operatorname{conv}\mathcal W(\mu)\). Given \(p\in\mathcal X(\mu)\), decide whether there is no \(q\in\mathcal X(\mu)\) such that \(\sum_{s':s'\succeq_t s}q_{ts'}\geq\sum_{s':s'\succeq_t s}p_{ts'}\) for every type \(t\) and acceptable school \(s\), with strict inequality for at least one such pair.

The model it lives in

A society of atomless mass grouped by complete preference-and-priority types, with school capacities given as per-capita rates. Stable assignments may split a type’s mass across schools; ex-post stable assignments are finite convex combinations of such stable mass assignments, and stochastic dominance compares each type’s cumulative assignment mass.

The objection that survived

The paper’s evaluated school-choice settings do not show that complete preference-and-priority types have moderate support; if most applicants have distinct types, the proposed compression and algorithmic opportunity may be limited.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s constrained-SD-efficiency test and Theorem 6’s stable Pareto-improvement problem; it leaves the tie-breaking-specific input of Theorem 3, the strategy-proofness impossibility, and the empirical welfare results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does optimization or separation over weakly stable mass assignments admit an efficient algorithm in the numbers of schools, types, and input bits?
  • How does the complexity change as the support size of the type distribution grows?
  • Which finite-market DA tie-breaking lotteries have a well-defined limit in this type-mass model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity school-choice market: many students, but relatively few complete student types. A type \(t\) includes a strict school ranking, acceptable schools, and the student’s priority category at every school. The society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). School capacity becomes a per-capita rate \(\kappa_s\). This scaling is part of the population mirror: each person still receives one school, while an atomless cohort can be divided across schools.

That regime is plausible for a large centralized admission system with thousands of applicants, coarse priorities based on a few attributes, and many repeated preference-and-priority profiles. It is the regime, not school choice in general, that matters: a small committee or a market where nearly every applicant has a distinct profile would be a poor fit. The paper’s random assignment probabilities do not by themselves make its population continuous; the mirror is that each entry \(p_{ts}/\mu_t\) is a per-member assignment probability for a cohort of mass \(\mu_t\).

Here is a common definition for the mirrors. Let \(x_{ts}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\), with \(\emptyset\) representing being unassigned. Require \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\). Call \(x\) weakly stable if there is no positive mass of a type that prefers a school to its assignment while that school has a vacancy or assigns positive mass to a lower-priority type. Write \(\mathcal W(\mu)\) for these stable mass assignments and \(\mathcal X(\mu)=\operatorname{conv}\mathcal W(\mu)\) for their lotteries. These are population assignments: a stable \(x\in\mathcal W(\mu)\) can be realized by assigning different members of an atomless type cohort to different schools. For \(p,q\in\mathcal X(\mu)\), \(q\) stochastically dominates \(p\) if, for each type \(t\) and each acceptable school \(s\),
\[ \sum_{s'\succeq_t s}q_{ts'}\geq\sum_{s'\succeq_t s}p_{ts'}, \]
with strict inequality for at least one type and threshold. Since each type has fixed mass, this is the paper’s student-side stochastic-dominance comparison.

Theorem 3 is my lead anchor. The paper proves that \({\sc IsConstrained\text{-}SD\text{-}Eff}\) is coNP-complete even when student preference lists have length at most three and the input lottery is a uniform combination of Deferred Acceptance outputs under different tie-breakings; the restricted instances also have unit capacities. This is a particularly good anchor because its input is the kind of DA lottery the paper actually seeks to improve.

I would call its mirror DA-Mass-Constrained-SD-Efficiency. Its instance is the type society and capacity rates above, together with a finite list of strict tie-breakings of the school priority orders over types. For each tie-breaking, run mass Deferred Acceptance: each type proposes its remaining mass down its ranking, and each school holds the highest-priority proposed mass up to capacity, splitting mass at a cutoff if needed. Let \(p\) be the uniform average of those outputs. The question is whether \(p\) is constrained-SD-efficient: is there any \(q\in\mathcal X(\mu)\) that stochastically dominates it? A witness against efficiency is such a \(q\), with a finite decomposition into stable mass assignments; otherwise the answer is yes.

Theorem 2 is the broader companion anchor. The paper proves that the same constrained-SD-efficiency test is coNP-complete even when student lists have length at most three and every school has capacity one. For its mirror, Mass-Constrained-SD-Efficiency, the instance is \((T,S,\mu,\kappa,\succ_t,\succsim_s,p)\), where \(p\in\mathcal X(\mu)\) is a rational type-mass assignment. The question is exactly whether any \(q\in\mathcal X(\mu)\) stochastically dominates \(p\). This asks about the paper’s underlying efficiency problem, not its integer-programming encoding. It covers the constrained-efficiency results, not every part of the smart-lottery mechanism.

For both constrained-efficiency questions, my working expectation is Class A in the intended moderate-support regime: dominance constraints are linear, and the optimization has the same master-LP/column-generation shape as the paper’s method. The decisive question is whether linear optimization over weakly stable mass assignments can be made efficient in \(m,\tau,L\). Cohort splitting may remove indivisibility that drives the discrete hardness, but it does not automatically make stability easy. If that pricing problem stays hard as the number of types grows, the mirror could instead expose a continuum-specific hardness boundary.

Theorem 6 gives a distinct, deterministic anchor. The paper proves that \({\sc Max\text{-}Constrained\text{-}Improve}\) is NP-hard: given a market, a stable matching \(M\), and a threshold \(R\), decide whether there is a stable matching \(M'\) that Pareto-improves \(M\) for the students and reduces average rank by at least \(R\).

Its mirror is Mass-Stable-Pareto-Improvement. The instance is a type society, a stable mass assignment \(x\in\mathcal W(\mu)\), and rational \(R\). Ask whether there is \(y\in\mathcal W(\mu)\) and, for each type, a coupling \(\pi_t(a,b)\) from old assignments \(a\) under \(x\) to new assignments \(b\) under \(y\). The coupling must have marginals \(x_{ta}\) and \(y_{tb}\), put mass only on \(b\succeq_t a\), and put positive mass on at least one strict improvement. Also require
\[ \sum_{t,a}x_{ta}\,\operatorname{rank}_t(a) -\sum_{t,b}y_{tb}\,\operatorname{rank}_t(b)\geq R. \]
This coupling expresses that the atomless members of each type can be reassigned so nobody is worse off. I expect Class A here as well: once type mass can split, the Pareto and rank conditions become linear flow constraints. The hard part again is representing stable mass assignments.

The weakest point is the stability limit. The paper defines ex-post stability by decomposition into finite, integral stable matchings. My mirror defines it as a lottery over stable assignments of an atomless population, whose individual members still receive integral schools. That is a natural population limit, but the equivalence between this object and limits of finite DA lotteries is a theorem to establish, not an assumption to hide. In particular, the mass-DA tie-breaking model should be compared with finite markets where individual tie-break lotteries are sampled.

The most useful next questions are whether \(\mathcal X(\mu)\) has a tractable optimization or separation oracle, whether finite high-multiplicity markets converge to this definition of ex-post stability, and whether hardness returns when \(\tau\) grows. Those questions preserve the paper’s focus on stable Pareto improvement while making the population, rather than the outcome space, the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirrors is about the regime they illuminate, not about a lack of computational results. Theorem 3’s particular input—an average of DA outcomes under finitely many strict tie-breakings—has no canonical continuum version. A uniform random ordering of infinitely many students is not available; in finite replicas, sampled individual tie-breakings may instead converge to a deterministic split at priority cutoffs. Ordering whole types would define a different tie-breaking process. So this anchor does not establish that the paper’s DA-lottery input has a straightforward limit.

That objection does not defeat Theorem 2’s broader mirror. With complete types and rational masses, a stable mass assignment can be represented exactly by a sufficiently large finite replica: assign the right number of identical students to each school, and the mass stability conditions correspond to the finite blocking conditions. Convex combinations then represent lotteries over those stable assignments. Stochastic dominance by type is also the natural comparison when members of a type are interchangeable. This is a well-posed high-multiplicity version of constrained-SD-efficiency, and the authors could reasonably recognize it as their problem. Calling it merely continuity of the outcome would be wrong; the population itself is being grouped into types.

Theorem 6 has the same obstacle for a negative case. Its Pareto-improvement question has a credible mass version: couple each type’s old and new assignments so that no member is worse off, then optimize average rank subject to stability. The reduction’s bespoke agents make it a poor demonstration that this regime occurs in the paper’s applications, but replicating each role as a cohort does not make the question incoherent. I cannot rule out a useful computational problem there.

The practical objection is narrower. The paper’s generated preferences include individual random variation, and its real kindergarten data has only 152 families. Neither establishes that the relevant applications have a small support of complete preference-and-priority types. In larger systems, rankings may be mostly unique; if support size grows nearly with population, the claimed compression and the route to new algorithms may offer little. But the proposed alternative—many applicants sharing rankings and coarse priority categories—is plausible, and this paper does not rule it out.

So the best case against is that the paper does not yet show that its evaluated school-choice settings have the moderate-support structure that would make these mirrors useful. That is a real limitation on the programme’s practical promise. It is not enough to defeat Theorem 2’s mirror, and Theorem 6 also survives as a sensible question. I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case: at least one anchor, and likely two, remains worthwhile to ask.
