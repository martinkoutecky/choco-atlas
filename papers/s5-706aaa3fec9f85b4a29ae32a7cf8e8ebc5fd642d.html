The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant case for a continuous mirror: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

Theorems 1–3 are proved in this paper, but they characterize which firm structures are \(\chi\)-stable. Theorem 1 treats symmetric abilities; Theorem 2 treats two ability classes under specified threshold conditions; Theorem 3 treats pairwise-distinct abilities. They are structural results, not complexity results about deciding or computing stable structures.

The closest positive direction would be a firm with many employees drawn from a small number of ability classes: type would be ability class, mass its population share, and the decision a mass allocation into team compositions. Stability would need a defined continuum version of \(\chi\) wages and of a blocking coalition. Theorem 2 is the most natural connection to that regime. But those definitions and the computational question would be new modeling work; the paper does not supply a named computational anchor for them. So this is a plausible follow-up, not an honest anchor-backed case that the paper’s problem has a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor, and that is the strongest case against a ChoCo mirror here. Theorems 1–3 characterize which finite firm structures are \(\chi\)-stable under different ability assumptions. They do not state the complexity of a decision or search problem, or give an algorithmic result whose continuous counterpart could be investigated. The closest candidate, Theorem 2, is still a structural characterization of stable partitions, not a computational theorem.

The strongest mirror one could construct is a large firm with many workers in a few ability classes. Represent the workforce by rational masses and team formation by a distribution over team compositions; then define continuum wages and blocking in a way that extends \(\chi\)-stability. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the authors might well recognize the resulting stability question. So the negative case should not rest on claiming that workers lack meaningful types or that mass cannot represent them.

The problem is that this would introduce the computational question rather than continuize one the paper asks. The paper studies how ability heterogeneity shapes the set of stable structures. To make it a ChoCo task, one must add a search or decision problem—such as computing a stable composition measure—and supply the continuum definitions for wages, teams, and blocking. That could be a worthwhile separate project, but the paper gives no computational result to anchor it. On the stated anchor standard, there is nothing here for a continuous mirror to extend.

That is a strong case against an anchor-backed mirror, but a weak case for the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The large-firm, finite-ability-class model remains plausible; what is missing is evidence from this paper that its continuous version belongs on ChoCo’s computational agenda.
