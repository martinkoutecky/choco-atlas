The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: it proves no \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or similar classification. So it has no anchor that strictly qualifies under ChoCo’s complexity-anchor rule. The closest named result—and the one I would use to motivate a positive mirror—is Theorem 6.4, proved here. It says that for independent \(\varepsilon\)-bounded tournaments, Randomized Death Match (RDM)’s worst-case gain from a two-team coalition is exactly
\[ \alpha_2^{\mathrm{RDM}}(\mathcal T^\varepsilon) =\alpha_2(\mathcal T^\varepsilon) =\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{2\varepsilon^2}{3}. \]
Thus RDM attains the best possible worst-case guarantee among Condorcet-consistent tournament rules. This is an incentive guarantee, not a computational-complexity result.

The best continuous-population case I can make is a high-multiplicity version of that manipulation problem. Picture a large e-sports league with many entrants but few complete team archetypes: each type specifies the team’s matchup probabilities against every other type, as well as whether it belongs to a common-owner cartel. The number \(N\) of teams is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of types. Match outcomes are independent, and the baseline probability that one team beats another lies in \([1/2-\varepsilon,1/2+\varepsilon]\). A cartel owning a positive fraction of the entrants can coordinate the outcomes of matches among its own teams to increase the chance that one of its teams wins the championship. This is a plausible mass version of the paper’s manipulation model, and the paper itself points to larger manipulating coalitions as an open direction.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Call it Mass-RDM Cartel Manipulation. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a cartel subset \(C\subseteq T\) of positive mass, and a rational baseline matchup matrix \(p\) satisfying the \(\varepsilon\)-bound and independence assumptions. For each sufficiently large \(N\) compatible with the masses, instantiate \(N\mu_t\) teams of type \(t\) and run RDM. The cartel may replace the matchup probabilities for pairs of cartel teams by type-pair probabilities \(q\in[0,1]\), leaving all other match probabilities unchanged; outcomes remain independent. Let \(V_N(q)\) be the probability the champion belongs to the cartel. The question is to compute, to a requested additive accuracy, the asymptotic optimal gain
\[ \Delta(\mu,p,C) =\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left(\max_q V_N(q)-V_N(p)\right), \]
and return a type-level policy, or a sequence of policies, attaining that value.

This is the closest honest mirror of Theorem 6.4’s underlying question: how much can a coalition improve its chance of producing the winner under RDM? The paper’s theorem motivates it but does not answer it. In particular, the theorem’s two-person coalition has vanishing mass as \(N\) grows, while this mirror gives the cartel positive mass. I would tentatively expect a tractable route for fixed \(\tau\), using a finite-dimensional limit of the RDM elimination process and optimization over type-pair actions. That is a conjectured direction, not a consequence of the theorem.

The scope is just the RDM guarantee in Theorem 6.4; this does not mirror the RSEB result or the paper’s whole recursive analysis. My weakest point is the change from two named manipulators to a positive-mass cartel. That makes the high-multiplicity action meaningful, but it is a larger-coalition extension rather than a direct limit of the theorem’s exact setting. I still think the authors could recognize it as their problem family, especially given their stated interest in \(k>2\) coalitions. The other important limitation is that even a convincing mirror here would not turn this paper into a complexity anchor: its named results do not classify an algorithmic problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6.4 is the closest anchor, but it is an exact incentive-analysis result, not a computational one. It gives the scalar guarantee \(\alpha_2^{\mathrm{RDM}}(\mathcal T^\varepsilon)=\alpha_2(\mathcal T^\varepsilon)=\varepsilon/3+2\varepsilon^2/3\); it does not classify the complexity of a problem or present an algorithmic task. Theorem 6.5 and the recursive framework have the same character. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, this paper has no named computational result to mirror.

The proposed cartel problem is a plausible extension, but it changes the object behind Theorem 6.4. The paper’s coalition has two teams and can alter their one mutual match. The positive-mass cartel can alter match probabilities for a number of pairs growing with \(N\), then seeks to maximize its chance of supplying the champion. That may be a worthwhile large-coalition question, and the paper’s open question about larger coalitions gives it a connection to the authors’ interests. But it is a new scaling regime and intervention, not the high-multiplicity version of the theorem’s two-team guarantee.

The more faithful high-multiplicity version would keep two marked manipulators and make the background population large. That preserves the one-match intervention, but the manipulators have zero mass, and RDM eliminates only one individual per step. A continuum mass process does not remove any mass at a step, and its aggregate state cannot name the eventual champion. Recovering the manipulators’ gain requires analyzing the stochastic endgame among the last few survivors. That could yield an asymptotic probability problem, but the paper supplies no computational question or algorithmic bottleneck for it.

There is a related problem for the theorem’s comparison with all Condorcet-consistent rules. In a population with many copies of each type, the probability that any individual is a Condorcet winner becomes negligible when \(\varepsilon<1/2\): a union bound gives at most \(N(1/2+\varepsilon)^{N-1}\). At the type level, a positive-mass type cannot beat copies of itself with certainty. So a continuum analogue of Condorcet consistency either applies only to exceptional cases or loses the force that underpins the theorem’s minimax statement.

RSEB offers a more natural round-based mass process, and so is the strongest alternate anchor in the paper. But it still selects one individual champion after a growing number of rounds; the limit must recover that outcome from the finite-population tail. Its theorem is also an incentive guarantee, not a computational classification.

The negative case is strongest against calling these results ChoCo mirrors: the paper asks and answers probabilistic incentive questions, not computational ones, and the closest population models either retain zero-mass marked agents or introduce a positive-mass coalition with a different intervention. It is not airtight against the broader claim that a continuum cartel model could become a useful new computational project. The paper gives some reason to explore that extension, but not a computational result for a continuous mirror to inherit.
