The continuous mirror question

Given finite type and good-class sets \(T\) and \(Q\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_q\), and rational nonnegative values \(w_t(q)\), find a finite-support distribution \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(B=(b_q)_{q\in Q}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{Q}\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}b_qx_{t,B}=\rho_q\), and, for every \(t\) and every bundle \(B\) with \(x_{t,B}>0\), \(v_t(B)+\sum_{t',B'}x_{t',B'}\ell_t(B')\ge V_t\), where \(v_t(B)=\sum_qb_qw_t(q)\), \(V_t=\sum_q\rho_qw_t(q)\), and \(\ell_t(B)=\min_{q:b_q>0}w_t(q)\) with \(\ell_t(\varnothing)=0\); output such an allocation or report infeasibility.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity configuration-allocation feasibility model: \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(B\), aggregate constraints enforce type masses and per-capita supplies, and every used bundle must satisfy the type-specific PROPavg inequality.

The case FOR (proponent)

I think there is a credible positive mirror here, though it is an algorithmic rather than hardness-based case.

My lead and only anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper rather than cited:

For every instance of indivisible goods with non-negative additive valuations, a PROPavg allocation exists, and one can be computed in polynomial time.

In the paper, an allocation \(X\) is PROPavg when, for every agent \(i\),

\[ v_i(X_i)+\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k\ne i}m_i(X_k)\ge \frac{v_i(M)}{n}, \]

where \(m_i(S)\) is the least value agent \(i\) assigns to a good in \(S\), with \(m_i(\varnothing)=0\). The paper normalizes \(v_i(M)=1\), giving the displayed threshold \(1/n\). Theorem 2 therefore asserts both universal existence and polynomial-time search for this stronger-than-PROPm fairness guarantee. Theorem 6 is only cited from earlier work, so I do not count it as a second anchor.

The natural high-multiplicity regime is a large public-benefits or relief allocation. There are \(N\) households and a recurring inventory of indivisible goods: for example, standardized appliances, school materials, medical supplies, or household packages. Households fall into finitely many complete valuation types \(T\), where a type records the entire additive valuation vector and any other relevant eligibility information. If type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), then approximately \(N\mu_t\) households have that type. Likewise, there are finitely many good classes \(Q\), with approximately \(N\rho_q\) indivisible copies of good class \(q\). The important regime is \(N\gg |T|+|Q|\): millions of households, but a relatively small number of repeated household and item profiles.

This scaling of the inventory is necessary. With a fixed finite set of goods and a continuum of households, almost everyone receives nothing and the fairness question becomes degenerate. Scaling the number of physical goods with the population is therefore the supply-side co-modification forced by making the population continuous; the goods themselves remain indivisible.

Here is the precise mirror I would ask the authors to study. Call it PROPavg\(_\infty\)-Allocation.

The input consists of rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_q\ge 0\); and non-negative rational values \(w_t(q)\), where one copy of good \(q\) is worth \(w_t(q)\) to type \(t\). An integral bundle is a vector \(B=(b_q)_{q\in Q}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{Q}\). Its value to type \(t\) is

\[ v_t(B)=\sum_{q\in Q}b_qw_t(q). \]

Define the least-valued-good term by

\[ \ell_t(B)= \begin{cases} \min\{w_t(q):b_q>0\},&B\ne\varnothing,\\ 0,&B=\varnothing. \end{cases} \]

The decision variable is \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\), the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(B\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t \]

for every type \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t,B} b_qx_{t,B}=\rho_q \]

for every good class \(q\). Thus every unit of population receives an integral bundle and every unit of inventory is allocated; only the masses of agents receiving the various bundles are fractional.

For type \(t\), let

\[ V_t=\sum_{q\in Q}\rho_qw_t(q) \]

be that type’s total value of the per-capita inventory, and let

\[ \overline{\ell}_t(x)=\sum_{t',B}x_{t',B}\ell_t(B) \]

be the population-average least-good value, evaluated using type \(t\)’s valuation. The required fairness condition is support-wise:

\[ x_{t,B}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_t(B)+\overline{\ell}_t(x)\ge V_t. \]

The question is to find such an \(x\), or report that none exists. The objective is feasibility and construction, exactly as in Theorem 2, not welfare maximization.

This is genuinely the limit of the paper’s condition. In a finite \(N\)-agent realization, type \(t\) has \(N\mu_t\) agents and there are \(N\rho_q\) copies of each good. The finite proportional threshold is

\[ \frac{v_t(M_N)}{N}=V_t, \]

while the discrete average of least-valued goods converges to \(\overline{\ell}_t(x)\). Conversely, any rational \(x\) can be implemented by clearing denominators and creating a sufficiently large finite replicated instance. This gives the desired two-way high-multiplicity dictionary.

The mirror is also faithful in a detail that matters. I would not replace the support-wise condition by a condition on the average utility of type \(t\). If one type receives a mixture of excellent and empty bundles, its average may look fair while the agents receiving empty bundles fail PROPavg individually. The continuous problem must retain the paper’s “every agent” quantifier through the requirement that every bundle in the support of a type satisfy the inequality.

I would expect the bounded finite-type version to be a Class A candidate. The paper’s proof already has the right ingredients: a PROPavg graph, Hall-type matching, recursive redistribution, and a cut-and-choose construction. In the high-multiplicity model, ordinary matching should become capacitated matching or flow between valuation types and integral bundle configurations. The explicit population size \(N\) disappears, leaving a problem parameterized by \(|T|\), \(|Q|\), and the encoding length of \(\mu,\rho,w\). A continuous algorithm would have to produce a finite-support configuration allocation, probably through a compressed matching or column-generation formulation.

That tractability claim needs to be stated cautiously. Theorem 2 is polynomial in the expanded numbers of agents and goods; it does not itself prove polynomial time in the compressed high-multiplicity encoding. The real technical question is whether the paper’s recursive and matching argument can be aggregated without enumerating exponentially many possible integral bundles. If it cannot, the obstruction would come from bundle configuration or pricing complexity, not from population multiplicity. This is precisely the kind of boundary the continuization programme is meant to expose.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s PROPavg existence-and-construction result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s discussion of EFX, Avg-EFX, PROPm, or the cited Theorem 6. Those may generate separate questions, but they are not needed for this case.

The weakest point is the supply scaling. The original paper considers a finite one-off inventory \(M\), whereas the mirror considers a recurring inventory whose number of copies grows with the population. If the authors insist that only the fixed-inventory interpretation counts, then the continuum becomes vacuous: with infinitely many agents and finitely many indivisible goods, nearly all agents receive empty bundles. The positive case therefore depends on accepting the programme’s stated co-modification: population continuization requires per-capita supplies. In the proposed relief-allocation scenario, that is not an artificial relaxation. It is exactly the setting in which millions of agents share a small number of valuation profiles while the physical goods remain indivisible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 2 is a fork, and it ultimately does not support a universal negative.

With the paper’s fixed finite set \(M\) of goods, continuization is degenerate. At most \(|M|\) agents receive nonempty bundles, while the proportional threshold is \(v_i(M)/n\to 0\). In the continuum, the per-capita inventory is zero and the PROPavg condition collapses to a vacuous nonnegativity condition for almost every agent. Thus the literal population-only mirror is not worthwhile.

The proponent’s repair—scaling supplies to \(\rho_q\) goods per capita—is therefore necessary, but it creates a genuinely new configuration-allocation problem. The paper’s polynomial algorithm is polynomial in the explicitly listed agents and goods; it does not imply an algorithm polynomial in the compressed data \((\mu,\rho,w)\). The continuous formulation has potentially infinitely many bundle columns \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{Q}\), and the paper’s recursive deletion of named agents and goods does not automatically aggregate into capacitated matching or column generation. Moreover, the claimed finite/infinite dictionary is exact only for finite-support rational allocations: finite replicated instances can contain bundles of size \(\Theta(N)\), so mass may escape to unbounded configurations in the limit.

That is a serious objection to calling the proposed Class A result already justified. It is not, however, an objection to asking the question. The repaired model retains integral bundles for each agent, makes type a complete valuation description, preserves the per-agent guarantee through the support condition, and has a direct finite-replication interpretation. Scaling supplies is also precisely the kind of forced co-modification the programme permits. No identity-dependent objective or prior continuous treatment eliminates it.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The negative case establishes that the literal fixed-inventory limit is useless and that the proponent has proposed an open configuration problem rather than derived a polynomial algorithm. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A universal negative verdict here would be manufactured; the proponent’s Theorem 2 anchor survives, albeit with its algorithmic claim still unproved.
