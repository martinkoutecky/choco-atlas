The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(\Theta\) of complete valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), explicit rational valuations \(u_\theta(B)\), shared type weights \(w_\theta(B)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\hat q_j\), compute a welfare-optimal allocation of mass \(x_{\theta B}\) to integral bundles \(B\in\mathcal B_k=\{B\in\mathbb Z_+^G:\sum_j B_j\le k\}\), together with additive good prices \(p_j\ge0\). Require \(\sum_B x_{\theta B}\le\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,B}B_jx_{\theta B}\le\hat q_j\), and \(\sum_{\theta,B}w_\theta(B)u_\theta(B)x_{\theta B}\) to be maximal; for \(\epsilon\) at least \(2\delta_w\max_\theta\max_B u_\theta(B)\), every bundle assigned positive mass to type \(\theta\) must be within \(\epsilon\) of that type’s best net utility at prices \(p\), and unassigned types must have no bundle with net utility above \(\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents with a finite support of complete valuation types, where \(x_{\theta B}\) is the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\), supplies are per capita, and good prices are additive across bundles. With explicit valuation tables and fixed \(k\), the allocation and dual price certificate come from an LP with \(O(|\Theta||G|^k)\) variables.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2.7’s approximate envy-free price guarantee. It does not preserve Theorem 2.2’s finite-agent lottery-rounding guarantee, or mirror the asymptotic strategy-proofness and empirical results.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do the mass allocation and prices compare with normalized finite POPT outcomes as each type count grows and supplies scale with population?
  • What guarantees remain without perturbed weights, or when \(k\) is variable or valuations are given succinctly?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered hardness result and no numbered theorem classifying its allocation problem in P. Its polynomial-time statement is in §3.1.1, for fixed \(k\). The strongest named anchors are the LP results the mechanism actually computes with. My lead is Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper in Appendix A. In the discrete model, starting from an optimal fractional solution \(x^*\) of LIP, the authors construct integral allocations \(F\). Every \(x\in F\) is optimal for a modified LP with the same weighted-welfare objective and demand constraints. For each good \(j\), its modified supply cap is \(\tilde s_j\) if \(x^*\) leaves that constraint slack, and otherwise is \(x^*\)’s use of good \(j\); the cap is at most \(s_j+k-1\). Every dual solution of LIP is also a dual solution of that modified LP.

A second, closely related anchor is Theorem 2.7, also proved here, in Appendix C. For any optimal fractional allocation \(x^*\) of LIP, prices \(P(B)=\sum_j B_jp_j^*\), formed from supply-constraint multipliers of LIP, satisfy the paper’s approximate envy-freeness condition. Together, these results connect the computed welfare allocation to prices that support it. The paper also gives the relevant high-multiplicity structure in its analysis: it groups agents by type and rewrites the allocation LP using type-level variables.

A plausible regime is a large population of small wireless or IoT providers in replicated local markets. Goods are license slots for standardized coverage areas and bands; bundles remain integer bundles of at most \(k\) slots. A type is the provider’s complete valuation table over those bundles, including its deployment template and interference profile. Repeated deployment templates in repeated local-market classes give, for example, \(10^5\) providers but only tens or hundreds of types. The number of providers grows while the type catalog and goods catalog stay fixed.

For this regime, let \(\Theta\) be the finite type set, \(\mu_\theta\) the fraction of the population of type \(\theta\), \(G\) the good types, and
\[ \mathcal B_k=\{B\in\mathbb Z_+^G:\sum_{j\in G}B_j\le k\}. \]
Each type has a valuation \(u_\theta(B)\) for every \(B\in\mathcal B_k\). Let \(\hat q_j\) be the available supply of good \(j\) per unit of population, and let \(w_\theta(B)\) be the small welfare perturbation used by the mechanism, shared by all agents of the same type.

The lead continuous problem, Mass-POPT Allocation, is: given these rational masses, supplies, valuations, and weights, compute a mass allocation \(x_{\theta B}\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_k} w_\theta(B)u_\theta(B)x_{\theta B} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{B\in\mathcal B_k}x_{\theta B}\le\mu_\theta \quad\text{for each }\theta,\qquad \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_k}B_jx_{\theta B}\le\hat q_j \quad\text{for each }j,\qquad x_{\theta B}\ge0. \]
A solution includes optimal dual values \(\alpha_\theta,p_j\ge0\) satisfying
\[ \alpha_\theta+\sum_jB_jp_j\ge w_\theta(B)u_\theta(B) \]
for every \(\theta,B\), with equal primal and dual objectives. Here \(x_{\theta B}\) is the mass of type \(\theta\) assigned the integral bundle \(B\). Since each type is atomless in the continuum, that mass can be implemented by assigning whole bundles to a subset of its agents; no individual receives a fractional bundle. This is a direct population mirror of the paper’s allocation question, not continuity in the bundle or outcome space.

I expect Mass-POPT Allocation to be Class A for fixed \(k\) and explicit valuation tables: it has \(\tau|\mathcal B_k|=O(\tau |G|^k)\) variables, where \(\tau=|\Theta|\), so it is a polynomial-size LP. Further questions are how the finite-instance approximation behaves as population grows, and what changes when \(k\) is variable or valuations are given succinctly.

The second question, Mass Supporting Prices, takes the same market and a welfare-optimal mass allocation \(x\), and asks for additive good prices \(p_j\ge0\) that make it approximately envy-free. A solution must make every assigned bundle nearly as good as any bundle available to that type at those prices:
\[ u_\theta(B)-\sum_jB_jp_j \ge u_\theta(B')-\sum_jB'_jp_j-\epsilon_u \]
whenever \(x_{\theta B}>0\), for every \(B'\in\mathcal B_k\). For any unassigned mass of type \(\theta\), no bundle may offer net utility above \(\epsilon_u\). With type-shared weights close to \(1\), complementary slackness gives these conditions when \(\epsilon_u\) is chosen large enough relative to the perturbation. This is also Class A: the prices and their certificate come from the LP dual. It raises questions about exact envy-freeness without perturbations, and about how finite-agent incentive effects behave around the mass solution.

The authors should recognize both questions: they preserve the paper’s welfare objective, integer bundles, per-agent demand limit, supply constraints, and additive bundle prices. The continuous change is in how identical agents are represented: \(x_{\theta B}\) records a fraction of a type assigned each bundle. For a finite population of size \(N\), type counts normalize to \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\), and supplies normalize to \(\hat q_j=s_j/N\). The paper’s \(k-1\) units of possible over-allocation per good then amount to at most \((k-1)/N\) per capita. This makes the mass model a sensible high-multiplicity limit and offers a concrete bridge back to the discrete mechanism.

The mirror covers the allocation and supporting-price results, not every claim in the paper. In particular, the paper’s asymptotic strategy-proofness does not automatically become a substantive continuum incentive guarantee: an infinitesimal agent has no effect on the aggregate. The weakest point is the regime itself. Major spectrum auctions often have few large bidders, and fixed physical spectrum supply does not grow with the bidder population. The proposed regime therefore needs replicated local markets whose agents and license slots scale together, with providers sharing complete valuation types. If those assumptions do not fit the intended application, the mirror weakens; within that regime, the authors’ own type-based LP makes it a credible analogue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is against Theorem 2.2, but it does not dispose of the whole mirror. The theorem’s distinctive object is a lottery over integral allocations to finitely many agents, with each lottery outcome allowed up to \(k-1\) extra units of each good. In an atomless population, a mass \(x_{\theta B}\) can be assigned bundle \(B\) by partitioning type \(\theta\)’s agents. Each person still receives an integral bundle, but there is no aggregate integrality problem to round: mass can be split exactly and supplies can be respected exactly. So the proposed Mass-POPT allocation is well-defined, but it does not preserve the rounding question Theorem 2.2 answers. Preserving that question requires finite indivisible cohorts, which brings back the high-multiplicity problem rather than its continuous limit.

The best version of that mirror would use complete valuation tables as types and scale per-capita supplies in a replicated market. That is coherent, but it makes Theorem 2.2’s lottery and additive-overallocation guarantee disappear. If the new question is instead how to allocate mass and compute supporting prices, it is a different target: the paper already sets up and solves the welfare LP that supplies those objects.

Theorem 2.7 is harder to defeat. In the same mass model, its best mirror is clear: find an optimal allocation of type mass to integral bundles and prices under which each bundle receiving positive mass is approximately utility-maximizing for that type. Duality gives precisely that kind of certificate. Calling this merely a standard dual calculation does not show the question is unworthy; it is a meaningful price-support question, and the paper’s aggregation by agent type makes the regime especially plausible. The type catalog must truly stay small: if complete valuation tables include each provider’s idiosyncratic geography and interference profile, nearly every provider could become its own type. A replicated-market construction can avoid that, though it needs a persuasive account of how goods and supplies scale.

The paper also writes an LP in type-level variables in its strategy-proofness analysis and studies the large-population limit. That does not itself answer the normalized continuous allocation question, but it weakens any claim that the proposed type aggregation is alien to the authors’ model.

So the negative case is real for the rounding anchor and for spectrum applications with few, heterogeneous bidders and fixed supply. It is weak as a case against every worthwhile mirror. I cannot honestly defeat the best version of Theorem 2.7’s price-support mirror: a finite set of complete types with scaled supplies gives a sensible continuous population model, and its question is recognizable as this paper’s problem.
