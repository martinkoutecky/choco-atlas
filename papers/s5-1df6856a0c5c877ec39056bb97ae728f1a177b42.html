What the mirror covers

The proposed evaluation task concerns posterior pooling and the commutation property in §3.2; it leaves the static characterization in §3.1 aside. Neither result provides a computational guarantee.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for a continuous-population version of the paper’s sequential pooling model. But the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor: the displayed theorem in §3.1 characterizes an aggregation rule as linear, and the displayed theorem in §3.2 establishes dynamic rationality under a common-ground restriction. Neither asserts a complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The supplied text also does not print theorem numbers. The cited Theorem 4a of Dietrich and List is an external axiomatic result, not a computational result of this paper.

The natural population model is clear. Let \(\Omega=\{0,1\}^{|A|}\) be the set of truth assignments. A type \(t\) is a complete rational prior \(p_t\) over \(\Omega\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society with that prior, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For a proposition \(\phi\), that type assigns \(J_t(\phi)=\sum_{v\models\phi}p_t(v)\). The collective belief is the population pool
\[ P_\mu(\phi)=\sum_t\mu_t J_t(\phi). \]
This has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large forecasting or policy-consultation population using a modest number \(\tau\) of shared probabilistic models, with each type including its full prior and update rule. The paper’s example of three people is not itself such a regime, but it is not the only plausible scenario for its mathematical model.

The closest candidate mirror is a follow-on computational problem, not a result this paper establishes:

Continuous Sequential Pooling Evaluation. An instance gives an explicitly represented finite world set \(\Omega\), an agenda \(X\) of events, a conjunction-closed common ground \(\Phi\subseteq X\), rational prior vectors \(p_t\) for \(\tau\) types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a sequence \(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_k\in\Phi\). Require that every type assigns the same positive probability to each proposition in \(\Phi\), so the common-ground condition is explicit. The task is to output the exact collective posterior on every \(\psi\in X\) after the sequence of observations, and to report whether updating each type and then pooling agrees at every step with pooling first and then conditioning. The solution is the posterior vector and that equality verdict.

For explicit rational input, this evaluation problem is in P by exact rational arithmetic over the \(\tau\) types and the represented worlds. Its model genuinely continuizes the society: masses may be fractional, and the input size depends on support \(\tau\), not an individual headcount. It mirrors the substance of the §3.2 theorem: weighted linear pooling commutes with Bayesian conditioning when the learned propositions have shared positive probabilities, and the common ground is preserved across the specified sequence.

That is my strongest positive case, and also its weakness. The evaluation problem is a reasonable computational continuation of the authors’ model, but it is easy and is not what either theorem proves. The paper’s central contribution is an axiomatic and analytical possibility result; turning that result into a complexity question would require a follow-on research choice, such as studying succinctly represented prior types or relaxing the common-ground condition. Under the brief’s anchor rule, I cannot present the candidate problem as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its static theorem characterizes a rule under axioms; its dynamic theorem proves that linear pooling commutes with Bayesian updating on a restricted domain. Neither studies the complexity of a computational problem. The cited external characterization is axiomatic too. That is a decisive mismatch with ChoCo’s target, regardless of whether the population can sensibly be made continuous.

The population model itself is plausible. A large forecasting or policy-consultation group could contain many people sharing the same complete prior, with their proportions represented as masses. I would not object that the paper’s example has only three people, that beliefs are probabilities, or that a continuum version would need a natural reformulation.

The proposed “Continuous Sequential Pooling Evaluation” is a reasonable follow-on task, but it is not a mirror of either theorem. It turns the pooling and update definitions into an input-output problem: give the model and evidence sequence, then compute the posterior. The paper’s result supplies the commutation identity; it does not pose or analyze that evaluation problem. Asking for posterior evaluation may be useful implementation work, but adding it does not create a computational result in this paper to continuize. Stronger variants—such as finding admissible weights, testing common-ground conditions from an implicit representation, or using succinctly represented priors—would introduce new computational questions. Any difficulty from succinct priors, in particular, would arise in evaluating an individual prior, not from the population’s mass distribution.

So I can make a strong case that this paper offers no worthwhile continuous mirror of its results under the programme’s computational brief. I cannot honestly prove the broader universal claim that no worthwhile computational problem could ever be built from its framework; that would be a new research direction, not a mirror of the paper’s contributions.
