The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s case is sound: there is no anchor here to defeat. The paper’s numbered examples refute claims about fairness procedures for finitely many players; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Examples 4–7 give counterexamples to Pareto-optimality claims, and Examples 8–10 challenge incentive-compatibility claims. None asks for an algorithm or analyzes its running time. The continuous cake and value measures are part of the object being divided, not a continuous population of players.

The strongest attempted repair would be to turn those properties into computational questions: find a Pareto improvement, decide whether a procedure is manipulable, or determine whether equalizing cuts exist, given a distribution over valuation types. Those could be worthwhile questions, but the paper supplies neither those problem definitions nor complexity results about them. Its examples show failures on particular instances; they do not classify what it takes to detect or repair those failures across instances. A type-level model would also need new choices about how cake is supplied and how bundles are assigned across each type. For manipulation, a unilateral agent has zero mass in the continuum, so preserving the paper’s notion of individual strategy-proofness would require a new mechanism; allowing a positive-mass group to deviate changes the question.

One could build a high-multiplicity fair-division project around those choices, perhaps with per-capita cake supply and finitely many valuation types. But that would be a new computational programme, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper. So the negative case is strong for this paper: no worthwhile mirror is anchored in its results. The broader claim that no scenario could make continuous fair division worthwhile is harder to defend; the paper alone cannot establish that.
