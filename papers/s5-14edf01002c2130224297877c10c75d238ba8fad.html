What the mirror covers

The proposed mass tally represents weighted-approval winner selection; it does not mirror the axiomatic characterization or the independence result as computational findings.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that a voting procedure satisfies consistency in alternatives, consistency in voters, weak anonymity, neutrality, faithfulness, and continuity if and only if it is Personalized Approval Voting: each type has a strictly positive, finite weight, and the procedure selects every feasible alternative maximizing the total weighted approvals. Proposition 1, also proved here, says those six properties are independent. Neither result asserts a complexity classification.

The nearest positive case is a continuous version of the winner-selection rule characterized by Theorem 1, but it does not meet your anchor requirement. Its instance would contain alternatives \(C\), voter classes \(H\) with positive rational weights \(p_h\), and a finite support of pairs \((h,B)\), where \(B\subseteq C\) is an approval ballot and \(\mu_{h,B}\) is that group’s rational fraction of the electorate. The question is to return the winner set
\[ \arg\max_{c\in C}\ \sum_{(h,B)} \mu_{h,B}p_h\,\mathbf{1}[c\in B]. \]
A type here is the pair \((h,B)\): agents in that group share both the weight class used by the rule and the ballot that determines their contribution. For example, a large university election could have many voters but only a few role or seniority classes and, for a fixed agenda, a moderate number of distinct ballots. Replacing counts by fractions preserves the winners, since each continuous score is the corresponding discrete weighted approval total divided by the population size.

This winner-selection question is exactly tallyable in polynomial time in the number of alternatives, support size, and input bit length. It is therefore a tractable continuous formulation, but it does not show discrete hardness dissolving: ordinary Personalized Approval winner determination is already a direct tallying problem. The authors would likely recognize the mass version of their weighted-approval rule, though the paper’s contribution is its axiomatic characterization, not the computation of winners. The mirror also needs rational weights for a finite computational input, while the paper allows arbitrary positive real weights.

So the defensible positive case is narrow: Theorem 1 supplies a recognizable rule with a natural high-multiplicity population model, but the paper contains no named computational result to anchor the requested complexity mirror. Continuous mass-redistribution or winner-robustness questions could be built on that model, but they would be new questions rather than results this paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not survive the paper’s own scope test. Theorem 1 characterizes voting procedures by six axioms; Proposition 1 establishes their independence. Neither states a computational problem or a complexity result. The paper’s “Continuity” is an Archimedean axiom about finite electorates, not a continuous-population model. Replacing counts by rational masses gives a natural representation of the rule, but it does not turn either theorem into a computational result.

Winner determination is the nearest mirror: for support over characteristic-and-ballot pairs \((h,B)\), compute
\[ \arg\max_{c\in C}\sum_{(h,B)}\mu_{h,B}p_h\,\mathbf{1}[c\in B]. \]
That is a recognizable mass version of Personalized Approval Voting, but the paper does not study this computation. It specifies the rule axiomatically. So the proponent’s formulation is a plausible way to evaluate the characterized rule, not a computational finding to mirror.

A stronger proposal would ask how much mass must change ballots to make a designated candidate win, perhaps at minimum approval-edit cost. That is a sensible follow-on question, but its intervention costs and permitted changes are absent from the paper; the theorem concerns a fixed profile and exogenous weights. The same applies to optimizing weights or inferring voter types. Those may be worthwhile projects, but their value would come from adding a computational model, not from continuizing a result established here.

That is the strongest negative case: this paper offers no computational anchor, and its direct mass translation adds no computational question of its own. The universal claim remains weak, though. A well-chosen mass-robustness or bribery model could be worthwhile and recognizable to the authors; I cannot honestly rule out every such scenario. My case is that it would be a new project adjacent to this paper, rather than a continuous mirror of its results.
