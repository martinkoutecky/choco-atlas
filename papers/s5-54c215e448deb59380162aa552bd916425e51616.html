The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of role types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and strict type rankings inducing the paper's \(\mathcal B\)-preferences, does there exist a finite-support family of rational frequencies \(q_v\ge 0\) over nonzero integer team profiles \(v\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{T}\) such that \(\sum_v q_vv_t=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), and no member of any used team profile can move to another used profile or form a singleton, strictly improve, and be accepted by every member of the receiving team?

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete ranking over role types, with unacceptable roles treated as in the paper's \(\mathcal B\)-model. Teams remain finite and contain integer numbers of members of each type; rational \(q_v\) gives the frequency of each team profile, subject to \(\sum_v q_vv_t=\mu_t\). The question is whether such a population allocation can satisfy individual stability; there is no objective to minimize.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors cover Theorem 2's \(\mathcal B\)-IS existence result and Theorem 3's \(\mathcal B/\mathcal W\)-NS existence result; they leave the other stability variants and the paper's remaining results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the SAT hardness for \(\mathcal B\)-IS persist when each role type has interchangeable copies and only the rational mass ratios are fixed?
  • Does an analogous hardness result hold for the typed \(\mathcal B/\mathcal W\)-NS question, and what happens when \(|T|\) is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is existence of an individually stable partition in a large population of interchangeable role types. Imagine a professional community or consortium platform that repeatedly forms finite teams. There may be many thousands of participants, but only \(\tau\) relevant types: each type gives a participant’s complete ranking of the other role types, including which roles they find unacceptable. Mass \(\mu_t\) means the fraction of the population of type \(t\). The high-multiplicity regime is \(N\gg\tau\), with \(\tau\) allowed to grow when studying complexity.

To state the question precisely, let the population have rational type masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A team profile is a nonzero vector \(v\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{T}\): \(v_t\) is the number of members of type \(t\) in that team. Let \(q_v\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) describe the normalized number of teams with profile \(v\). A feasible continuous team allocation has finite support and satisfies
\[ \sum_v q_v v_t=\mu_t \qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]
Thus people remain whole members of finite teams; it is the population and the number of teams of each profile that are continuous. Each type has a strict ranking over the type classes and a “being alone” benchmark. A type-\(t\) member evaluates a team from the types of its other members, using the paper’s best-player or worst-player extension. In the paper’s \(\mathcal B\)- and \(\mathcal W\)-hedonic variants, an unacceptable member makes the team unacceptable.

My lead is Continuous \(\mathcal B\)-IS Existence. Its instance is the type set, rational masses, and strict type rankings just described. It asks whether there is a feasible \(q\) such that no type-\(t\) member of any used profile \(v\) can move to a used team profile \(w\) (or form a singleton) and strictly improve while every member of the receiving team weakly prefers the entrant. More explicitly, for a profile \(v\) containing a type-\(t\) member and a destination profile \(w\), the entrant compares their current team \(v\) with \(w+e_t\); each type-\(u\) member already in \(w\) compares \(w\) with \(w+e_t\). A solution is the finite list of profiles and rational frequencies \(q_v\) satisfying the mass constraints and these stability conditions.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper by a reduction from SAT: checking whether an individually stable partition exists is NP-complete for the paper’s \(\mathcal B\)-hedonic games with strict preferences. This is the version where an unacceptable member poisons the coalition. It is the strongest anchor because individual stability preserves both parts of the paper’s problem: the would-be entrant must benefit, and the receiving team must consent. The mirror asks the same existence question for a population distribution over complete types.

I would tentatively expect this problem to remain hard as \(\tau\) grows, with hardness most plausibly coming from the interaction among the role types. The theorem itself does not establish that: its SAT construction uses distinct players, and the continuous typed model permits many interchangeable members of each type. The useful next questions are whether that construction survives type replication, and whether existence of a continuous allocation is equivalent to existence in some sufficiently large finite replication of the same type masses.

A second, separate anchor is Continuous \(\mathcal B/\mathcal W\)-NS Existence. It has the same input and team-profile solution, but asks whether there is a feasible \(q\) with no profitable unilateral move to another used profile or to a singleton; the receiving team need not consent. Its source is Theorem 3, proved in the paper: checking whether a Nash stable partition exists is NP-complete for the paper’s \(\mathcal B\)-hedonic or \(\mathcal W\)-hedonic games with strict preferences. The paper’s proof uses the SAT-based construction from the preceding result.

I would also expect this Nash mirror to be hard with variable \(\tau\), provisionally by transfer: the relevant combinatorics appear to lie in the network of role types and their rankings, rather than in the sheer number of people. That is a conjecture about the typed population problem, not a consequence of Theorem 3. Further questions are whether the conjecture holds for both best- and worst-member preferences, and whether fixing \(\tau\) yields an algorithm.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: these questions mirror the paper’s existence results for individual-based stability in the unacceptable-member variants. They do not cover core stability, Pareto optimality, or the paper’s distinct best-preferences model; in particular, Theorem 7’s guaranteed, linear-time IS result concerns that other model.

The weak point is the passage from named players to repeated types. The source theorems assume preferences over individual players; in the mirror, members of one type are interchangeable and can be split across teams. Strict rankings between type classes are natural in this regime, but they do not reproduce strict rankings between every pair of named individuals. That is why I would present the two continuous existence problems as credible mirrors and predict hardness, while leaving the transfer of the actual NP-completeness results open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that their hardness comes from individualized preference networks. Theorem 2’s SAT construction uses clause and literal players with carefully different rankings; Theorem 3 reuses that construction for Nash stability. Grouping players who share a complete preference type would collapse many of those distinctions. Keeping them all requires roughly one type per gadget player, so the reduction says little about the intended regime \(\tau \ll n\). That defeats the proponent’s tentative hardness forecast for both IS and NS.

The best repair is a role-based model: many workers or participants share one of a small number of roles, and each role has a strict ranking over the other roles. Teams then have integer role-count profiles, while \(q_v\) gives the frequency of each profile. This is coherent, but it changes the preference domain that drives the theorems. For a fixed support of team profiles, stability depends on the profiles and rankings; the continuous masses only need to satisfy \(\mu=\sum_v q_vv\). The resulting question is a new search over stable profile supports and their mass cones. It does not inherit the paper’s reductions, and without a team-size bound its computational input regime needs more specification.

The same concern applies to the Nash mirror. Dropping the receiving team’s consent changes the deviation rule, but it does not restore the paper’s identity-based gadgets after players are grouped into roles. In a strictly atomless formulation, a single agent has zero mass; retaining the paper’s individual deviation therefore requires finite members within teams and continuous frequencies of team profiles. That can be modeled, but it leaves a hybrid high-multiplicity problem whose complexity needs its own analysis.

This case against has a clear limit. Repeated role types in large teams or professional cohorts are plausible, and the proposed profile-frequency model is a reasonable population question. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every such scenario. The negative case is strongest against claiming that either theorem already motivates or predicts a continuous complexity result; it is weak against asking the typed IS or NS existence questions themselves.
