The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over party-ballot types, per-capita candidate supplies \(s_{it}\), and a house rate \(H\), let \(q_i(\mu)\) be the share of ballots for party \(i\). Decide whether there are selected candidate masses \(x_{it}\) with \(x_{i\mathrm f}+x_{i\mathrm m}=Hq_i(\mu)\) for every party, \(\sum_i x_{i\mathrm f}=\sum_i x_{i\mathrm m}=H/2\), and \(0\le x_{it}\le s_{it}\) for every party and type.

The model it lives in

The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over party-ballot types; party shares \(q_i(\mu)\) determine continuous party quotas at house rate \(H\). Candidate supply in party-type cell \((i,t)\) is \(s_{it}\) per unit population, and the decision variable \(x_{it}\) is selected candidate mass. The objective is feasibility subject to party quotas, equal type totals, and supply bounds.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s feasibility characterization for fixed party quotas. It leaves the mechanisms’ structural and axiomatic results and the fair-share comparisons aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for a fixed party allocation \(J\) allowed by divisor method \(\gamma\), a candidate assignment meeting party proportionality and type parity exists if and only if the following polytope is nonempty: for each party \(i\), the numbers \(y_{i\mathrm f},y_{i\mathrm m}\) sum to \(J_i\); each gender receives at least \(\lfloor h/2\rfloor\) seats; and \(0\le y_{it}\le |C_i^t|\). The proof uses total unimodularity to show that a feasible point yields an integral candidate assignment. The paper presents this as an LP method for deciding feasibility, so the underlying feasibility problem is in P. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result; the paper offers no comparable hardness result to anchor instead.

A credible mirror is Continuous Parity-Supply Feasibility. Consider a federation of many similar electoral districts, or a large representative convention, where the electorate and the number of seats grow together. Each voter has one of a small set \(T\) of ballot rankings over parties. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings, with \(\tau=|{\rm supp}(\mu)|\ll N\), where \(N\) is the voter count. Let \(q_i\) be the share of ballots going to party \(i\). Candidate supply is given by \(s_{it}\), the mass of available candidates of party \(i\) and gender \(t\) per unit of electorate. Candidates in the same party–gender cell are interchangeable for this feasibility question.

Let \(H\) be the number of seats per unit of electorate. In the continuous limit, party \(i\)’s proportional allocation is \(J_i=Hq_i/\sum_j q_j\). This is the divisor quota with rounding removed as the house grows. A solution is a matrix of selected candidate mass \(x_{it}\). The question is whether there exists such a matrix satisfying \(x_{i\mathrm f}+x_{i\mathrm m}=J_i\) for every party, \(\sum_i x_{i\mathrm f}=\sum_i x_{i\mathrm m}=H/2\), and \(0\le x_{it}\le s_{it}\). There is no optimization objective: this is a feasibility problem, as in Proposition 1. Each candidate still receives either a seat or no seat; \(x_{it}\) is the aggregate selected mass in that cell.

This scaling gives a direct finite-to-continuous dictionary. For rational input, clear denominators with a sufficiently large \(N\): there are \(N\mu_t\) voters of each ballot type, \(Ns_{it}\) candidates in each cell, and \(NH\) seats. Choose \(N\) so the house is even and the quotas and a rational feasible \(x\) scale to integers. Then every divisor method in the paper gives the exact party quotas \(NJ_i\), and the continuous allocation scales to a finite assignment. Conversely, normalized finite assignments have continuous feasible limits. The computational question is therefore a rational LP with \(2n\) variables, polynomial in the number of parties \(n\), voter support \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\). I expect this mirror to be tractable.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the feasibility result in Proposition 1, not the paper’s axiomatic characterizations or its comparison between biproportional allocations and fair share. The latter is continuity of the outcome benchmark while the society remains finite; it is not the mirror proposed here. The continuous instance keeps a distribution of voters as its society and scales candidate supply and seats with that society, so parity and party quotas become mass constraints.

The weak point is that the decision variables concern candidates, while the paper does not study voter-level interventions. The mirror is convincing only in a regime where representation and nominee supply grow with the electorate, such as an aggregate of similar districts. If the house and candidate slate stay fixed as voter numbers rise, normalizing votes to shares leaves the candidate assignment discrete and does little to continuize the paper’s problem. Also, Proposition 1 is already tractable in the finite setting: this is a clean high-multiplicity analogue, but it does not demonstrate discrete hardness disappearing in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is weaker than advertised, but I cannot honestly defeat its strongest version. Proposition 1 gives an exact LP characterization of feasibility for a finite candidate slate; it does not state a running-time bound or classify a computational problem in the way the programme’s central results do. That makes it a modest anchor, not no anchor.

The proposed voter distribution also does little work in that feasibility question. Its party shares determine the quotas \(J_i\), while candidate supplies \(s_{it}\) are supplied separately. The ballot support \(\tau\) does not otherwise enter the constraints. If the house and candidate slate stay fixed as the electorate grows, the output remains a finite assignment of named candidates; normalizing votes does not turn that assignment into a mass allocation.

The stronger version survives that objection: scale the house and candidate supplies with the electorate, and ask whether candidate mass can meet party quotas, parity, and supply bounds. That is a genuine continuous-population question, and the co-scaling is allowed by the programme’s scope. It does add a growth assumption about nominee supply that the paper does not study, but an aggregate of similar districts could make that assumption plausible. Calling the resulting question merely a change to the outcome space would miss that its quotas come from a continuous voter society.

So the best negative case is that this mirror is narrow and depends on an additional institutional model; it is not that the model is incoherent. Since the continuous-supply version remains plausible, I do not think the case against can reliably defeat this anchor.
