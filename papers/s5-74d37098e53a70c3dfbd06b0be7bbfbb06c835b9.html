Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 2 — survived: The theorem’s guarantee applies to any valid comparison matrix, including the geometric aggregate of a society of expert types.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of complete reciprocal \(n\times n\) positive rational comparison matrices \(M^t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a common nonempty reference set \(K\), and positive rational reference priorities \(r_k\), compute the HRE priority vector \(q\) and its ranking for \(\bar m_{ij}=\prod_{t\in T}(m^t_{ij})^{\mu_t}\), where \(q_k=r_k\) for \(k\in K\) and \(q_j^{n-1}=\prod_{i\ne j}\bar m_{ji}q_i\) for \(j\notin K\). When \(q_i<(n-1)\sum_{j\ne i}q_j\) for every \(i\), determine whether the returned vector minimizes \(e_{\bar M}(q)=\sum_{i,j}(\ln\bar m_{ij}-\ln(q_i/q_j))^2\).

The model it lives in

A large expert panel is represented by a distribution over types, each type carrying a complete pairwise-comparison matrix; masses determine the geometric aggregate, and the HRE equations determine priorities for unknown concepts given shared reference priorities.

The objection that survived

The paper takes one completed comparison matrix as input and does not analyze how a population of experts’ reports should be pooled; the proposed geometric aggregation is an added modeling choice.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers geometric HRE computation and Theorem 2’s conditional multiplicative-error optimality for the pooled matrix; it leaves the arithmetic HRE feasibility analysis, the imported \(M\)-matrix facts, and other pairwise-ranking methods untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the exact bit complexity and output representation for priorities computed from rational masses and rational comparison entries?
  • How efficiently can the resulting priorities be ranked exactly, including when two priorities tie?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible positive angle is a large expert panel doing repeated valuations. Let a type be a complete pairwise comparison matrix from a fixed finite menu of reports; let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of panelists of type \(t\). Given a common set \(K\) of concepts with known reference priorities \(r_k\), form the population’s geometric-mean comparison matrix
\[ \bar m_{ij}=\prod_t (m_{ij}^{(t)})^{\mu_t}. \]
The task is to compute positive priorities \(q\) for the unknown concepts, with \(q_k=r_k\) for \(k\in K\), satisfying
\[ q_j^{n-1}=\prod_{i\ne j}\bar m_{ji}q_i \quad\text{for each }j\notin K. \]
The output is the completed priority vector, ordered as a ranking. A recurring evaluation program with many panelists but relatively few standardized reports gives this a credible high-multiplicity scenario. It is also close to the paper’s expert-panel motivation. The displayed logarithmic equations suggest a Class A route through a linear system.

That is a reasonable extension, but it has no qualifying anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, cited from elsewhere, gives equivalent conditions for a matrix to be a nonsingular \(M\)-matrix, including inverse positivity and the existence of a positive diagonal scaling with positive row sums. It is a linear-algebra fact, not a complexity result about the paper’s problem.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here. It says that the geometric-averaging solution minimizes the sum of squared multiplicative log errors
\[ e(q)=\sum_{i,j}\left(\ln m_{ij}-\ln(q_i/q_j)\right)^2 \]
when \(q_i<(n-1)\sum_{j\ne i}q_j\) for every \(i\). This is an optimality guarantee for the estimator, not a result classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. The paper derives a logarithmic linear system and uses Gaussian elimination in its examples, but gives no named complexity result or encoded-input running-time analysis. Applying Theorem 2 to the pooled matrix would support the panel extension mathematically; it would not make that extension a result the paper established.

So I would not present the panel problem as an anchor-compliant mirror. Its weakest point is that it adds a population of reporters and an aggregation step that the paper takes as already completed in its input matrix. Under the requested anchor rule, the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror, and therefore no per-anchor continuous problem can be offered honestly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper does not establish a computational result of the kind the programme is meant to continuize. Theorem 1 is an imported linear algebra characterization. Theorem 2 proves a condition under which the proposed estimator minimizes a multiplicative-error objective. Section 3.2 gives an existence argument, and the examples solve the resulting equations by Gaussian elimination. None states a complexity result for a defined computational problem.

The best population version is coherent: a large panel could contain many experts drawn from a finite set of complete comparison-matrix types, with common concepts and reference priorities. Pooling their reports geometrically gives \(\bar m_{ij}=\prod_t(m_{ij}^{(t)})^{\mu_t}\), then HRE supplies the priorities. This is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, and the geometric pool even fits the paper’s log-error objective. But the paper takes a single matrix as input; it neither defines how individual experts’ reports become that matrix nor studies the aggregation. Supplying an already pooled matrix leaves the population outside the task. Making the population the input adds a new aggregation model and a new question.

That extension may be worthwhile in research on group judgment. It still does not mirror a computational result established here: the paper gives no encoded-input analysis, complexity classification, or algorithmic theorem about aggregating a society of experts. One could pose such questions for the extension, but that would be new work rather than a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s results.

The negative case has a real limit: the expert-panel scenario is plausible, and the population estimator is well posed. So I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be asked about this subject. I would claim that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for one.
