The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited: the article contains no named computational result eligible as an anchor under your rules. Proposition 1 proves that \(\tau\) is a distance; Proposition 2 characterizes its maximum. Neither says a computational problem is tractable or hard. The article does compute LLSM and eigenvector rankings, and cites an external result about optimal completion, but those computations are not stated as a numbered complexity result of this paper.

There is a plausible *unanchored* mirror in a league with many teams drawn from a small set of tournament-relevant types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of teams of type \(t\), and let \(q_{tu}\) be the observed comparison ratio between types on the edges \(E\) of an incomplete comparison graph. One could find positive type strengths \(w_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t=1\), that minimize
\[ \sum_{(t,u)\in E}\omega_{tu}\left(\log q_{tu}-\log(w_t/w_u)\right)^2, \]
then rank the types by \(w_t\). This preserves the paper’s incomplete-comparison and LLSM structure while making the population of teams continuous.

Its weakness is substantial: the paper ranks individual teams, whereas this mirror returns a ranking of types and cannot distinguish teams within a type. More decisively, it has no qualifying named result in the paper to anchor the requested case. I would not present it as satisfying the anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no eligible result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. Proposition 1 proves a distance property, and Proposition 2 characterizes its maximum; neither is a computational result. The optimal-completion uniqueness claim and algorithm are attributed to Bozóki, Fülöp, and Rónyai, while this paper reports applying LLSM and the eigenvector method to one tournament. The rankings and comparisons are empirical findings, not complexity theorems. That is the strongest negative point: the proposed mirror does not answer the assigned question about continuizing a named result of this paper.

The best version of the proponent’s idea would define a finite set \(T\) of tournament-relevant team types, a population mass \(\mu_t\) for each type, and an encounter measure \(\nu_{tu}\) recording comparisons between types. With \(q_{tu}\) denoting their observed comparison ratios, one could fit type strengths \(z_t=\log w_t\) by minimizing
\[ \sum_{(t,u)}\nu_{tu}\bigl(\log q_{tu}-(z_t-z_u)\bigr)^2 \]
and rank the types. That is a coherent population model for comparing cohorts. But it changes the paper’s output: the article ranks the particular teams in a Swiss tournament, while this model ranks cohorts and suppresses differences among teams of the same type. Retaining individual rankings would require keeping the team-level comparison graph; the population distribution alone does not encode who played whom or what happened in each match. Adding an encounter measure makes the model possible, but turns it into a new population-level strength-estimation problem, not a continuous version of a result proved here.

I would not claim this establishes that no scenario could make such a cohort-ranking problem useful. A large league with repeated team types is conceivable, and a continuous analysis there could be worth doing in its own right. The negative case is narrower and firmer: this paper supplies no named computational result for the programme to mirror, and its strongest plausible population reformulation changes the object being ranked. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is therefore stronger than the evidence supports.
