The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The closest is Theorem 1, proved here: when every agent has a non-conditional preference domain, no social choice rule with range size other than \(2\) is both strategy-proof and non-dictatorial. That is an axiomatic impossibility result; it makes no claim about computational complexity. Proposition 1 is the cited Gibbard–Satterthwaite result. The two-step procedure and the appendix’s rule counts are structural analysis and example-specific calculations, with no numbered complexity claim.

A plausible population regime would be a large electorate whose ballots come from a small set of ranking types, represented by masses \(\mu_t\). But a rule that depends only on those masses is unchanged when one atomless voter misreports: the voter has zero mass. Strategy-proofness therefore becomes vacuous. Requiring a positive mass of voters with the same ranking to misreport together could give a meaningful continuous question, but it changes the incentive concept to a form of group strategy-proofness, which the paper does not anchor with a named computational result.

So I cannot give a continuous problem per eligible anchor, or predict whether such a problem is tractable or hard, without inventing a result the paper does not contain. The paper’s two-step structure may inspire a separate algorithmic project, but it is not itself a computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only near-anchor is Theorem 1 (with Proposition 1 as its Gibbard–Satterthwaite baseline). It is a named impossibility result, but it is axiomatic: it classifies which ranges a strategy-proof, non-dictatorial rule can have. The paper gives no complexity claim or algorithmic problem for that theorem. Algorithm 1 and the appendix’s rule counts do not supply one either.

The most natural population mirror exposes a deeper problem. Let \(\mu\) record the fractions of voters with each ranking, and let the social choice rule depend on \(\mu\). A single atomless voter’s misreport leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, so every such rule is strategy-proof. The individual whose incentives Theorem 1 constrains has disappeared. And \(\mu\) records realized rankings, while the theorem’s manipulation tests depend on each agent’s full admissible preference domain. Enriching the type to include that domain preserves the missing-mass problem.

The strongest repair is to let a positive-mass group misreport together. That makes manipulation non-vacuous, but changes the target to group strategy-proofness. One must choose which groups count—any positive mass, a fixed minimum mass, or whole type cohorts—and analyze the resulting property. The paper mentions group strategy-proofness, but does not establish a computational result about it; its two-step procedure is only a proposed route to characterizing rules under stronger properties. A complexity project on one of these mass-coalition models might be worthwhile in its own right, but it would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of a result here.

So the negative case is strongest at the programme’s entry gate: there is no computational result to carry across, and the best attempt to make the central theorem population-based either makes individual incentives automatic or replaces them with a different incentive concept. The honest weak point is that this replacement could motivate useful research; the paper does not give grounds to count it as its continuous mirror.
