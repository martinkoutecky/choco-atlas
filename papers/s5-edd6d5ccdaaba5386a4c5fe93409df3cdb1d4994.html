The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is an axiomatic population version of the paper’s impossibility result. Let types be complete rankings of a fixed finite candidate set \(C\), and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of society with ranking \(t\). This fits a large electorate with many repeated ballots: the number of people can be enormous while the support size \(\tau\) is much smaller than the full set of \(m!\) rankings. Define the pairwise margin by
\[ \Delta_\mu(x,y)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\sigma_t(x,y), \]
where \(\sigma_t(x,y)=1\) if \(t\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\), and \(-1\) otherwise. A voting correspondence \(F\) maps each \(\mu\) to a nonempty subset of \(C\). Positive involvement has a direct mass version: if \(x\in F(\mu)\), then \(x\) remains selected after adding any positive mass of rankings with \(x\) uniquely first, so \(F((\mu+\lambda\nu)/(1+\lambda))\ni x\) for every \(\lambda>0\) and every distribution \(\nu\) supported on such rankings. The Condorcet criteria use the signs of \(\Delta_\mu\); ordinal margin invariance uses only the orientations and ordering of the margins. One reasonable continuous analogue of resolvability is that societies with multiple winners are not dense in the simplex of distributions.

The resulting question is precise: for fixed \(C\) and its ranking types, does there exist such an \(F\) satisfying positive involvement, the Condorcet winner and loser criteria, ordinal margin invariance, and that resolvability condition? A solution would be a rule satisfying the axioms, or an impossibility proof. I expect impossibility: the paper’s finite profiles become rational distributions over rankings, its added-voter steps become added mass, and ordinal margin invariance makes a tie on a strict margin-order chamber persist throughout that open chamber. Dense unique winners therefore force a unique choice on each such chamber, as resolvability does in the paper’s proof; the remaining contradiction can follow its same pattern. This mirrors Theorem 4, but it is an axiomatic existence question, not a computational one.

The closest named result is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: no ordinal voting method satisfies positive involvement, the Condorcet winner criterion, the Condorcet loser criterion, resolvability, and ordinal margin invariance. It is not a qualifying computational anchor: it asserts an axiomatic impossibility, not that a computational problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classified. Lemma 2’s refinement of the defensible set and Lemma 3’s uniqueness result for linearly edge-ordered tournaments are structural lemmas, not complexity results either. The proof’s use of integer linear programming to construct profiles does not change that. So this is the best population-level mirror I see, but the paper contains no named computational result to anchor the requested ChoCo case.

My weakest point is resolvability: the paper’s single-voter and asymptotic formulations do not dictate a unique axiom for real-valued masses. The dense-uniqueness formulation is plausible and supports the proof transfer, but authors or a referee could reasonably prefer another continuation. More fundamentally, even if they accept this mirror, it extends an axiomatic theorem; it does not open a computational complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor problem is decisive: Theorem 4 is an axiomatic impossibility theorem, not a computational result. Lemmas 2 and 3 are structural, and the paper’s use of integer linear programming constructs witness profiles; it does not analyze an algorithmic problem or its complexity. The proponent also concedes that this is not a qualifying computational anchor.

The proposed population model is sensible. Rankings can be complete types, and \(\mu_t\) can represent their shares in a large electorate. But that gives a continuous *axiomatic* question, which ChoCo’s scope excludes. In fact, the paper’s witness profiles are rational distributions, and its added voter blocks become positive mass additions. With dense uniqueness replacing resolvability, the same margin chambers and transitions can carry the impossibility proof over. That may be a coherent extension of the theorem, but the objection is its subject matter, not that it reaches the same answer.

The closest computational seed is the paper’s open question about the minimum number of voters needed for an impossibility witness. Continuizing that objective loses what makes it a voter-count question: normalized societies always have total mass \(1\), while unnormalized ordinal-margin constraints are scale-invariant, so a witness can be scaled toward zero. Minimizing added mass to cross between strict margin-order regions can likewise collapse to a boundary infimum unless an extra margin-gap condition is imposed. Such a condition, or a minimum-mass robustness question for a named rule, could define a worthwhile neighboring project. It would add a computational task and a metric that the paper does not supply; it would not mirror Theorem 4.

So the negative case is strong for this paper as a ChoCo mirror: its principal result has no computational problem to continuize. It is weaker as the literal claim that no adjacent population-computation project could be worthwhile; the paper’s robustness discussion could inspire one. That possibility does not rescue the proposed anchor.
