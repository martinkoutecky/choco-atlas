The continuous mirror question

Given a finite program set \(C\), student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), strict type rankings, strict program priorities over types, and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_i\), compute the student-optimal stable assignment-mass matrix \(x_{ti}\), including unmatched mass, subject to \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ti}\leq\kappa_i\). Does this allocation equal the sequence-independent limit of normalized finite student-optimal stable matchings—and thus of the finite SPE outcomes in Theorem 3.1—whenever type frequencies and capacities converge to \(\mu\) and \(\kappa\)?

The model it lives in

A large placement market represented by a finite support of complete student types, each carrying mass \(\mu_t\), with programs having per-capita capacities \(\kappa_i\) and priorities over types. The decision variables are assignment masses \(x_{ti}\) and unmatched masses; the desired output is the student-optimal stable mass allocation, with fractional mass allowed at a program’s priority cutoff.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named complexity result in this paper: it gives no theorem classifying a problem as tractable, NP-hard, or otherwise. The closest anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved constructively here. It states that, for the paper’s student-sequentially-propose-and-colleges-choose mechanism, every Subgame Perfect Equilibrium induces the student-optimal stable matching \(l^S(P)\). The assertion is conditional on an equilibrium being present; the theorem is an implementation result, not a running-time result.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large annual placement market, such as residency allocation, where many applicants share complete preference rankings and programs’ admissions priorities. Let \(N\) be the applicant population and \(T\) the supported set of complete applicant types; the regime of interest has \(|T|\ll N\). Program capacities scale with the population, so \(\kappa_i\) is program \(i\)’s capacity per applicant. This is a scenario-dependent claim: if nearly every applicant has a distinct ranking, it is not a useful mirror.

I would pose the Limit Student-Optimal Stable Allocation problem. An instance consists of a finite program set \(C\), a finite support \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_i\), each type’s strict ranking of \(C\cup\{\varnothing\}\), and each program’s strict priority over types. Program preferences are responsive to those priorities and capacities, hence substitutable. The decision variable is an assignment-mass matrix \(x_{ti}\), together with unmatched mass \(x_{t\varnothing}\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{i\in C\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{ti}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{ti}\leq \kappa_i. \]
The question is to compute the unique student-optimal stable mass allocation, if one exists, and determine whether it is the limit of normalized SPE outcomes along every sequence of finite markets whose type frequencies and capacities converge to \(\mu\) and \(\kappa\). If there is no sequence-independent limit, a solution should exhibit two approximating sequences with distinct limiting allocations. Here stability rules out a positive mass of a type preferring a program over its assignment when the program has capacity or would admit that type ahead of lower-priority mass; student-optimality compares allocations type by type in their ranking order.

I expect the responsive-priority version to be Class A: a mass version of student-proposing deferred acceptance, or an equivalent optimization formulation, looks like a natural route. Further questions include the running time in \(|C|\), \(|T|\), and input bit length; convergence rates; and whether the result extends from responsive preferences to the paper’s full substitutable-preference domain. The mirror covers the student-proposal outcome in Theorem 3.1, not the paper’s college-proposal example or its order-dependent failures.

The weak point is that this is a limit problem about the theorem’s equilibrium outcome, not a continuum SPE theorem. In an atomless population an individual student has zero mass, and the paper’s sequential strategic incentives may not survive unchanged. Restricting colleges to responsive priorities also narrows the paper’s domain. I think authors would recognize the proposed allocation as the high-multiplicity analogue of the outcome they implement, but they could reasonably say it does not continuize their sequential mechanism itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem about computational complexity. Theorem 3.1 proves an implementation property: every SPE of the finite, student-by-student mechanism yields the student-optimal stable matching. It gives no complexity classification of a problem the paper formulates. That is the strongest reason to reject this anchor as a *computational* mirror.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is still a sensible high-multiplicity problem. Large placement markets can plausibly have many students with the same complete preferences, and the responsive-priority model gives a well-defined mass allocation. I would not reject it on grounds of type choice or because the outcome is fractional. But it asks for the student-optimal stable allocation, rather than a computational property of the sequential mechanism. Under the proposal’s assumptions, a mass version of deferred acceptance can process each type’s proposals and each program’s priority cutoff; types may split at a cutoff to fill capacity. The number of type-program proposal steps is bounded by \(|T||C|\). That makes the allocation problem a direct finite-support algorithmic extension of deferred acceptance.

The proposed limit-of-finite-SPE question does reconnect to Theorem 3.1: every finite market in the sequence already has the student-optimal stable matching as its SPE outcome. So the limit question is about how those stable allocations behave as masses and capacities converge. It may be mathematically interesting, but the sequential incentive result supplies the identification of each finite outcome; it does not supply a new computational problem for the continuum. And an exact continuum version of the mechanism has a deeper obstacle: its proof uses a last student and backward induction through finitely many turns. Atomless students have zero mass individually, while grouping a type into one strategic actor gives that actor control over a positive mass. Either choice changes the game whose implementation Theorem 3.1 establishes.

That leaves a credible continuous matching project, and it may be worthwhile in its own right. It also leaves the negative case weaker than a universal rejection: the paper’s central outcome is stable matching, so its authors could reasonably recognize the mass-allocation problem as theirs. My case against rests on the narrower claim that this is a new computational study of a neighboring problem, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
