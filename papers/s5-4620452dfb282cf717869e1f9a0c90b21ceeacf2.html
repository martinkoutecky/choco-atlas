The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(T\), house categories \(H\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_h\), does there exist a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,B}\) to integral bundles \(B\) of size at most each type's capacity such that no feasible challenger, coupled to the incumbent assignment type by type, has greater mass of applicants who prefer it than mass who prefer the incumbent?

The model it lives in

Each type specifies its acceptable houses, ranks, and individual capacity. The variable \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(B\); bundle masses sum to \(\mu_t\), and total mass assigned to each house is at most \(\beta_h\). A coupling \(\pi_{t,B,B'}\) represents the same type-\(t\) people under incumbent and challenger bundles. The incumbent is popular when every feasible challenger coupling has nonpositive net mass \(\sum_{t,B,B'}\pi_{t,B,B'}(\mathbf{1}[B'\succ_t B]-\mathbf{1}[B\succ_t B'])\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the existence question in Theorem 2 and can also express the weak-popularity existence question in Theorem 4. It leaves the characterizations and the paper's two-rank algorithm aside; Theorem 5's additional hardness restrictions are not analyzed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact-cover reduction remain hard when each applicant role is a type whose mass may split among integral bundles?
  • Can fractional assignments be purified or otherwise shown not to change existence for this popularity notion?
  • How does the continuous problem's complexity depend on support size \(\tau\) and the number of house categories?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is an existence problem for a *popular allocation of a large population divided into preference types*. A natural setting is a large placement or housing clearinghouse: many applicants have the same acceptable options, rankings, and assignment limits, while the available capacity of each option scales with cohort size. For example, \(N\) applicants might fall into \(\tau\) complete preference types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Each person still receives an integral bundle; only the mass of people receiving each bundle is fractional.

Use a finite set \(H\) of house or placement categories and a finite supported set \(T\) of applicant types. A type \(t\) specifies its acceptable categories \(N(t)\), their ranks \(r_t(h)\), and its capacity \(b_t\). The instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_h\ge0\). A feasible assignment is a table \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), where \(B\subseteq N(t)\), \(|B|\le b_t\), and \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\). It satisfies
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,B:\,h\in B}x_{t,B}\le\beta_h \quad\text{for each }h\in H. \]
Thus the average allocation to a type may be fractional, while every person represented in the assignment receives an allowable bundle.

To compare an incumbent assignment \(x\) with a challenger, use \(\pi_{t,B,B'}\ge0\) to describe how much of the same type-\(t\) population has bundle \(B\) under \(x\) and bundle \(B'\) under the challenger. Require \(\sum_{B'}\pi_{t,B,B'}=x_{t,B}\); the challenger is feasible when its bundles respect the house supplies. These couplings matter: they preserve the paper’s comparison between two matchings of the *same agents*, while allowing indistinguishable people of one type to be represented by mass.

My lead question is Popular \(b\)-Matching\(_\infty\). For each type, let \(\operatorname{sig}_t(B)\) count its assigned houses at each rank, in rank order, and compare signatures lexicographically as in the paper. The input is \((T,H,\mu,\beta)\), with each type’s ranks and capacity; the question is whether there is a feasible mass assignment \(x\) for which no feasible coupling to a challenger has
\[ \sum_{t,B,B'}\pi_{t,B,B'} \bigl(\mathbf 1[B'\succ_t B]-\mathbf 1[B\succ_t B']\bigr)>0. \]
A solution is such an \(x\), or a report that none exists. Capacities \(\beta_h\) are the supply co-modification: when normalizing an \(N\)-person instance, a house of capacity one has supply \(1/N\).

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: deciding whether a given \((G,b,r)\) has a popular \(b\)-matching is NP-hard even when edges use only two ranks, every applicant has capacity at most \(2\), and every house has capacity \(1\). The paper proves this by reduction from exact \(3\)-cover. The mirror keeps the same question—does a popular matching exist?—and the same rank-signature comparison and capacities on individual allocations. It replaces counting applicant votes with comparing the mass of applicants who prefer each allocation.

A second, worthwhile question is Weakly Popular \(b\)-Matching\(_\infty\), anchored by Theorem 4, proved here: deciding whether a weakly popular \(b\)-matching exists is NP-hard even when applicants use at most three ranks, there are no ties, applicants have capacity at most \(3\), and houses have capacity \(1\). For this mirror, use the same mass assignments and couplings. For a pair of bundles \(B,B'\), remove their common houses, sort the remaining ranks and pad each list with \(R+1\), where \(R\) is the maximum rank. If the lists are padded to length \(d\), define
\[ \Delta_t(B,B')=\sum_{i=1}^{d} \operatorname{sgn}\!\left(s_i(B\setminus B')-s_i(B'\setminus B)\right). \]
Using the paper’s orientation for this signed loss/gain factor, the assignment is weakly popular if no feasible challenger coupling has \(\sum_{t,B,B'}\pi_{t,B,B'}\Delta_t(B,B')>0\). The problem is to decide whether such an assignment exists and, if so, return its bundle-mass table.

For both questions, my working expectation is hardness transfers (Class B): the reductions’ combinatorics live in the incidence structure among applicant types and houses, rather than in counting a huge number of otherwise identical people. But neither theorem proves that expectation for a continuum. A possible route is to represent each applicant role by a large cohort of identical applicants and scale supplies with the cohort. The hard part is showing that fractional splitting of a type’s mass cannot evade the reduction’s constraints. Until that is proved, the expected classification is a conjecture, not a consequence of the paper.

That unresolved point is also the weakest part of the case. In the reductions, different applicants may have different neighborhoods, so the support size \(\tau\) could grow with the gadget. And a continuous type can split its mass among bundles, which a discrete applicant cannot. Those facts may let a prover recover tractability or reveal continuum-specific hardness instead. They do not make the questions artificial: the mirror still asks whether a large cohort with repeated, complete preferences has an assignment immune to a mass majority or to the paper’s weak rank comparison. For a fixed proposed assignment, testing for a dominating challenger is itself a linear optimization problem over the couplings; existence of an assignment that passes that test is the harder question.

This scope covers the existence questions in Theorems 2 and 4, not the paper’s other results, including its two-rank algorithm. The follow-up questions are whether the clone construction can establish a genuine discrete-to-continuous embedding, whether fractional assignments change existence, how complexity depends on \(\tau\), and whether the two-rank tractability boundary in the paper survives in the continuous model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the hardness story, not to the mirror itself. Both reductions encode their choices in individual graph structure. In Theorem 2, distinct applicants’ neighborhoods and unit-capacity houses make the exact-cover gadgets work; in Theorem 4, the truth assignment is encoded by an all-or-nothing pattern among individual applicants. Their support size \(\tau\) can grow with the reduction. Replicating each role into a large cohort and scaling supplies gives a genuine high-multiplicity regime, but fractional mass can split across bundles and may erase those gadgets. So neither theorem establishes that its hardness survives continuization.

That objection also falls short of defeating either question. The possibility that fractional splitting changes the answer is the computational question the programme wants to ask, not a reason the question is unworthy. And the proposed mass comparison has a coherent interpretation: within an atomless cohort of identical applicants, any coupling of incumbent and challenger bundles can be realized by assigning bundles to the same people. Large placement systems with repeated preference types and per-capita supplies are plausible settings for both ordinary and weak popularity. Weak popularity’s comparison is less familiar, but its type-level version is still definable.

I therefore cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The proponent’s expectation that either NP-hardness result transfers is unsupported; the continuous questions anchored by Theorems 2 and 4 remain worthwhile. The negative case is weak precisely because fractionalization threatens the reductions without undermining the questions.
