The continuous mirror question

Given finite left and right type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(a_i,b_j\) summing to one on each side, pair costs \(c_{ij},d_{ji}\), outside-option costs \(u_i,v_j\), and finitely many scenarios \(s\) with probabilities \(q_s\) and surviving fractions \(\alpha_i^s,\beta_j^s\), compute an exact minimum-cost mass matching \(x\) and its value. The constraints are \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq a_i\), and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq b_j\). Minimize \(\sum_s q_s[\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}(\alpha_i^s\beta_j^s(c_{ij}^2+d_{ji}^2)+\alpha_i^s(1-\beta_j^s)u_i^2+(1-\alpha_i^s)\beta_j^s v_j^2)+\sum_i(a_i-\sum_jx_{ij})\alpha_i^s u_i^2+\sum_j(b_j-\sum_ix_{ij})\beta_j^s v_j^2]\). Determine an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|I|+|J|\), the number of scenarios, and the rational input bit length.

The model it lives in

A two-sided population represented by preference-and-cost types carrying rational mass; the decision variable \(x_{ij}\) is matched mass between types, and the objective is expected post-departure social cost. The resulting linear transportation problem is a min-cost flow.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the relaxed expected-social-cost problem at \(\nu=1\) under finitely many cohort-departure scenarios; it leaves the regret term, the stability-constrained result, and the original single-agent departure model aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a bit-complexity analysis for exact optimization with rational masses, costs, and scenario probabilities.
  • Can the same mass-flow approach retain the paper's regret-to-benchmark term when post-departure benchmarks are defined over types?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is for one narrow mirror: Cohort Perturbation-Robust Assignment, the relaxed matching problem at \(\nu=1\). This is an algorithmic anchor because the paper has no numbered hardness result for its perturbation-robust problem. Its discussion of NP-hard social-cost objectives concerns other objectives, so it does not provide a hardness anchor here.

The anchor is Theorem 11, proved in this paper: for an \(n\)-by-\(n\) stable-marriage instance with the paper’s departure probabilities and parameter \(\nu\), the \(\nu\)-perturbation-robust matching *without a stability requirement* can be found in \(\mathcal{O}(n^4)\) time. The objective is the expected weighted sum of squared social cost and squared distance from the post-departure stable optimum. The proof reduces the relaxed problem to an assignment problem using Corollary 4; the paper cites Theorem 10 for the assignment algorithm and computes the needed post-departure benchmarks. I choose this result because the assignment formulation gives a direct route to masses. Theorem 9’s stable variant is also polynomial, but its rotation-poset construction is tied to individual agents, and I do not have as convincing a type-level mirror for it.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. There are left types \(I\) and right types \(J\), with rational masses \(a_i\) and \(b_j\), each side totaling one. A type specifies the agents’ preferences, pair costs, outside-option cost, and departure exposure. Agents in type \(i\) have the same cost \(c_{ij}\) for matching with any member of type \(j\), and outside-option cost \(u_i\); right-type agents have costs \(d_{ji}\) and \(v_j\). Preferences can rank counterpart types, with members of a type interchangeable. The instance also gives a finite list of departure scenarios \(s\), with probabilities \(q_s\). A scenario removes a specified fraction of one type’s mass uniformly within that type; the list includes the no-departure scenario. Write \(\alpha_i^s\) and \(\beta_j^s\) for the surviving fractions of each type.

The decision variable is \(x_{ij}\), the mass initially matched between types \(i\) and \(j\), subject to

\[ x_{ij}\geq 0,\qquad \sum_j x_{ij}\leq a_i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij}\leq b_j. \]

The question is to find an \(x\) minimizing the expected sum of squared costs among agents who remain, with a survivor whose partner leaves assigned the outside-option cost. Explicitly, minimize

\[ \sum_s q_s\left[ \sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\left( \alpha_i^s\beta_j^s(c_{ij}^2+d_{ji}^2) +\alpha_i^s(1-\beta_j^s)u_i^2 +(1-\alpha_i^s)\beta_j^s v_j^2 \right) +\sum_i\left(a_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\right)\alpha_i^s u_i^2 +\sum_j\left(b_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\right)\beta_j^s v_j^2 \right]. \]

A solution is an optimal mass-matching matrix \(x\), together with its objective value. This is the \(\nu=1\) endpoint of the paper’s objective, where only the expected social-cost term remains. The displayed objective is linear in \(x\), so the problem is a bipartite min-cost flow with \(O(|I||J|)\) variables. With \(S\) scenarios and rational input of bit length \(L\), I expect an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|I|+|J|,S,L\): Class A, tractable.

The regime is a large placement or labor market: many applicants and positions, but comparatively few shared preference-and-cost profiles. Positions can be represented by slot agents. For example, a national placement round might have far more participants than distinct applicant profiles and slot profiles. The flow’s fractional entries describe how a nonatomic mass of otherwise identical agents is split across integral matches; population mass is the object being modeled, not a divisible matching outcome imposed on a finite list of named people.

The cohort shocks are a material co-modification, but the paper itself points toward considering departures of sets of agents and observes that polynomial time depends on keeping the number of possible departing subsets manageable. A finite scenario list over types fits that stated direction. The model also has a discrete high-multiplicity interpretation: when the rational masses are scaled to integer type counts, the flow constraints have an integral network structure, so an optimal flow can be realized by an assignment of individual agents.

The weakest point is that this is not the paper’s full perturbation-robust objective. A single departure has zero mass in a normalized continuum, so I replace it with a positive-mass cohort shock; I also focus on \(\nu=1\), leaving out the regret-to-benchmark term. And type-level interchangeability introduces ties within a class, whereas the paper assumes strict rankings over individuals. Those are real changes. I think the authors could recognize this as a sensible continuous analogue of their *relaxed expected-social-cost* problem, especially given their own discussion of multi-agent departures, but I would not claim it mirrors Theorem 11 in full. Extending the model to the benchmark-regret term with faithful individual-level coupling, or adding the paper’s stability requirement, are the natural next questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s departure event is the loss of one named agent. In a normalized, atomless society, that agent has zero mass: the type distribution and the post-departure benchmark are unchanged. For the paper’s sum of costs, one departure affects only \(O(1)\) terms against a population of size \(N\), so its per-capita effect vanishes as \(N\) grows. The exact one-agent limit therefore loses the perturbation that motivates the paper.

The proposed cohort-shock model restores a nonzero effect by changing the event to the departure of positive mass. It also takes \(\nu=1\), dropping the regret-to-benchmark term. That makes it a narrower analogue, though: the paper itself identifies departures by sets of agents as a direction for extending its model, and the programme permits population-related co-modifications. Nor does the absence of a stability constraint defeat this anchor, since Theorem 11 is specifically the relaxed matching result.

There is also a real type-modeling constraint. In this paper, costs can depend on the identity of a particular partner. Agents are interchangeable only when the relevant preferences and costs agree across the type classes; grouping people who merely share their own preference list may lose those pair costs. But bulk placement markets with interchangeable applicant and slot profiles are a plausible regime in which the stronger condition holds. A fuller mirror could use positive-mass departure scenarios and retain the regret term, with type-level flows recording how surviving agents’ baseline and benchmark assignments relate.

So the best negative case is that the exact single-agent perturbation degenerates, while the proposed nondegenerate mirror changes the departure model and narrows the objective. That does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists: the paper’s own discussion makes cohort departures recognizable, and a plausible high-multiplicity assignment regime remains. The case against is therefore weak on the universal claim.
