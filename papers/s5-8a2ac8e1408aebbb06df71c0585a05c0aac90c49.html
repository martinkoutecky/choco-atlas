The strongest plausible mirror I see, though, is the finite-horizon welfare-maximization problem underlying the paper’s MMDP. Consider a large hospital network serving many patients who share complete clinical types: each type specifies the medical pathway, health and resource states, transition probabilities, and reward function. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of patients of type \(t\). A plausible regime is a large regional or seasonal cohort with many patients but a modest number of standardized disease and pathway profiles. Resource supplies \(b_{r,k}\) are measured per patient served, so they scale with the cohort.

For each type \(t\), time \(k\), state \(s\), and feasible resource-allocation action \(a\), let \(x_{t,k,s,a}\) be the mass assigned that action, and let \(y_{t,k,s}\) be the mass in that state. Given rational initial masses, transition probabilities, rewards, and per-capita resource capacities, ask for a policy—equivalently, feasible occupation masses—that maximizes expected total patient reward over the horizon. The solution must satisfy state-flow constraints and, for each resource \(r\) and time \(k\), an aggregate capacity constraint such as \(\sum_{t,s,a}q_r(a)x_{t,k,s,a}\le b_{r,k}\). This is a continuous-population version of allocating resources among patients whose health evolves stochastically. It preserves integral bundles for each patient; fractional values describe how mass of a type is divided among actions.

For this proposed question, I would expect tractability when the type-level state and action spaces are explicitly listed: the occupation-measure formulation is a linear program. That is a candidate Class A result, not a result established by this paper. It could lead to questions about the discrete-to-continuous approximation error, complexity when the patient MDPs are succinctly encoded, and whether the paper’s regret-based auction approaches the aggregate optimum.
