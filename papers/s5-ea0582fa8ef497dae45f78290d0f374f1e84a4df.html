The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(M\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho\), a finite set of weakly lexicographic preference types \(\mathcal T\), and rational type masses \(\mu\), does there exist a finite-support mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq M\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=\rho_g\), \(\mathrm{EF}_\infty\), and individual \(\mathrm{PO}_\infty\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity society is represented by weakly lexicographic types \(t\in\mathcal T\) with masses \(\mu_t\); variables \(x_{t,B}\) assign mass to integral bundles \(B\), supply constraints enforce \(\rho\), and the feasibility requirements are \(\mathrm{EF}_\infty\) and \(\mathrm{PO}_\infty\), with no objective.

The objection that survived

The hardness reduction uses many essentially distinct edge-agent roles, so its NP-hardness may not reflect population multiplicity and may disappear under a genuinely compressed type space; additionally, mass-supported Pareto improvements can interact nontrivially with envy constraints.

fatal: False

Open questions for a prover

  • Does continuous \(\mathrm{EF}\)-\(\mathrm{PO}\) existence remain hard, or does the equitable-colouring obstruction dissolve under type multiplicity?
  • Can feasible allocations and Pareto-improvement certificates be represented with support polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input encoding length?
  • Can the goods and chores algorithms be aggregated without expanding rational masses into exponentially many discrete agents and item copies?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible continuous mirror here. The strongest one keeps bundles indivisible for every individual agent and continuizes only the population and the supply of recurring item copies.

Let \(M\) be a finite set of goods or chores, and let \(\mathcal T\) be a finite set of weakly lexicographic preference types. A type \(t\) is a complete weak order over \(M\), including its indifference classes. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal T\). There is also a per-capita supply vector \(\rho\in[0,1]^M\): item \(g\) has \(\rho_g\) copies per unit mass of population. The restriction \(\rho_g\le1\) says that an individual receives at most one copy of each item category.

An allocation is a collection of masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), where \(B\subseteq M\) is an integral bundle:

\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=\rho_g. \]

Thus \(x_{t,B}\) may be fractional, but every unit of population mass assigned to \(B\) receives the integral bundle \(B\). This is not divisible-goods fair division. It is the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem: after clearing denominators, \(D\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) receive bundles of indivisible goods, while \(D\rho_g\) copies of \(g\) are available.

For the continuum notions, every bundle used by positive mass must satisfy the relevant pairwise condition against every other positive-mass bundle. For example, the allocation is \(\mathrm{EF}\) if, whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{u,B'}>0\), then \(B\succeq_t B'\). It is \(\mathrm{EFX}\) for goods if \(B\succeq_t B'\setminus\{g\}\) for every \(g\in B'\), and \(\mathrm{EF1}\) if this holds for at least one such \(g\). The chores definitions are the paper’s symmetric versions.

Pareto optimality is also individual, not merely average: an improving allocation must admit a mass-preserving reassignment from old bundles to new bundles such that every moved agent weakly prefers its new bundle and a positive mass strictly prefers it. This can be represented by variables \(z_{t,B,B'}\) coupling old and new bundles.

The regime is plausible in recurring institutional allocation: a large population of applicants, employees, students, or residents receives course seats, placements, shifts, or tasks. Agents share types because their preferences are generated by a finite number of roles, eligibility profiles, or standardized priority classes. The population may be enormous while \(|\mathcal T|=\tau\) is moderate. Item supplies scale with the population, as they must in any meaningful high-multiplicity version.

My lead anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper. It states:

Deciding whether an \(\mathrm{EF}\) and \(\mathrm{PO}\) allocation exists for a weakly lexicographic goods-only instance is NP-complete.

The corollary follows from the paper’s preceding Theorem 1, which proves NP-completeness of \(\mathrm{EF}\)-existence even when every agent has at most two indifference classes; the same construction yields the \(\mathrm{EF}+\mathrm{PO}\) result.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Goods EF–PO Existence. An instance consists of \(M\), rational \(\rho\), a finite type set \(\mathcal T\), rational type masses \(\mu\), and a weak order for every type. The question is whether there exists a mass allocation \(x\) satisfying the supply equations, \(\mathrm{EF}_\infty\), and \(\mathrm{PO}_\infty\), where the latter two are defined as above. A solution is an explicit finite-support list of bundles and masses \(x_{t,B}\).

This is recognisably the same problem: agents still receive integral bundles, preferences are still weakly lexicographic, and the requirements are exactly envy-freeness and Pareto optimality. Only the number of indistinguishable agents and item copies has been replaced by mass.

I would expect the discrete NP-hardness not to transfer automatically. The paper’s reduction uses one specially constructed edge-agent type and one color-agent type for essentially every graph object. Its combinatorics is an equitable integral coloring of named agents. In the population model, a type can be split across several integral bundles, and the equitable-coloring obstruction becomes a fractional mass-assignment question. My first prediction is therefore Class A in a genuinely compressed regime, via a configuration LP or fractional matching formulation followed by a Pareto-improvement cleanup. If hardness survives, it would more naturally be Class C—hardness caused by integral bundle supports and weak-preference geometry—not hardness inherited from the number of named agents. This is the strongest and most interesting mirror in the paper.

The second anchor is Theorem 2(B), proved here. The paper’s theorem says that, when Algorithm 1 is run with criterion \(F=\mathrm{EFX}\), it always returns an \(\mathrm{EFX}\) and \(\mathrm{PO}\) allocation for every weakly lexicographic goods-only instance. The algorithm uses the preference graph, alternating paths, and potential-envy graph. The paper presents the algorithm as polynomial time.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Goods EFX–PO Construction. The input is the same \((M,\rho,\mathcal T,\mu)\) goods instance. The required output is a finite-support integral-bundle allocation \(x\) satisfying the supply equations, \(\mathrm{EFX}_\infty\), and \(\mathrm{PO}_\infty\). The output may assign different integral bundles to different portions of the same type, but every pair of bundles in the positive-mass support must satisfy the EFX condition.

I expect this to be Class A. The finite algorithm already identifies the relevant structure through alternating paths and strongly connected components. In the high-multiplicity model, the natural question is whether those operations can be aggregated over type masses instead of repeated once per agent. The main open issue is not existence—the finite theorem gives existence after denominator expansion—but a polynomial algorithm in \((m,\tau,L)\) rather than in the potentially enormous denominator of \(\mu\) and \(\rho\). I would not use the paper’s MMS guarantees here: an atomless limit of the paper’s \(n\)-way maximin-share definition requires a separate convention.

The third anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. It states:

Given a weakly lexicographic chores-only instance, Algorithm 2 always returns an \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and \(\mathrm{PO}\) allocation.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Chores EF1–PO Construction. The input consists of a finite chore set \(C\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over weakly lexicographic chore types, and the associated preference orders. The output is a finite-support assignment of integral chore bundles satisfying the supply constraints, such that for every pair of positive-mass bundles, the potentially envious agent can eliminate the envy by removing one chore from its own bundle, and no feasible Pareto improvement exists.

This also looks Class A, though again the meaningful complexity question is compressed computation. The paper’s alternating-path algorithm, unavailable-chore monotonicity, and round-robin argument should have type-level analogues. A naive algorithm can expand denominators and invoke the finite theorem, but a genuine continuization result would compress that process into a flow or configuration algorithm whose running time depends polynomially on \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length.

The mirror does not cover the paper’s MMS propositions, the structural PO characterizations, or its examples. It covers one hardness result and two constructive results, with the lead question being whether the paper’s goods-side NP-hardness is really population hardness. The key weakness is that the paper’s original model has a finite inventory of individually named items, whereas the mirror introduces per-capita supplies of recurring item categories. That co-modification is substantial. Nevertheless, it is exactly the high-multiplicity regime the continuization programme is meant to study, and it preserves the paper’s central object: integral bundles assigned to agents with weakly lexicographic preferences.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent has proposed a plausible family of new high-multiplicity fair-division problems, but has not yet shown that any of them is a faithful, computationally useful mirror of the paper’s results.

For the \(\mathrm{EF}+\mathrm{PO}\) hardness theorem and Corollary 1, the reduction is especially hostile to continuization. Each edge agent has a distinct top indifference class determined by its incident vertices. In a generic graph, there are therefore \(\Theta(|E|)\) distinct agent types, while the colour agents are the only genuinely repeated type. Duplicating the instance merely gives many copies of essentially unique edge roles; it does not compress the population. If one insists on a moderate \(\tau\), the graph-colouring gadget disappears. If one retains all those edge types, the graph remains explicitly encoded in the type space and item catalogue. Either way, this is not a population-multiplicity explanation of the theorem’s hardness.

The proposed stronger model also changes more than the population size. It replaces one fixed set of named goods by recurring item categories with per-capita supplies, and allows a type to be split across several integral bundles. That may be a sensible new model, but then the question is no longer really “does the paper’s \(\mathrm{EF}+\mathrm{PO}\) obstruction survive high multiplicity?” It is feasibility of a distribution over configurations with a global support condition. Envy-freeness is imposed on every pair of bundles with positive mass, while Pareto optimality requires a second mass-coupling problem. This is not a straightforward configuration LP: choosing which bundles have positive support is itself combinatorial, and a Pareto-improvement cleanup can destroy envy-freeness.

The better version of this anchor is therefore a genuinely new question about support selection in a fractional assignment of indivisible bundles. That could be worthwhile, but its value would come from the new model, not from continuizing Corollary 1. The paper’s reduction supplies no reason to expect that this is the right or natural high-multiplicity regime. This is the strongest objection to the lead anchor, but it is not decisive: recurring courses, placements, or tasks with repeated preference roles remain plausible.

The goods-side \(\mathrm{EFX}+\mathrm{PO}\) algorithm is even less amenable to direct continuation. Its proof depends on a named agent having one current bundle, a priority set \(U\), a round order \(\sigma\), and a definite bundle cardinality. Once mass of one type is divided among several bundles, there is no single analogue of “the agent is selected next,” “the agent leaves \(U\),” or “the agent has one fewer good.” An alternating path must become a flow of mass between bundle configurations, and the potential-envy graph must be defined over types, bundles, or bundle-support components. These choices are not forced by the paper.

The best repaired formulation is again a direct mass-allocation problem over integral bundles. But then the finite theorem only gives a denominator-expansion construction: clear the denominators, run the discrete algorithm on potentially exponentially many agents and item copies, and aggregate the result. That is not polynomial in the encoding length of the masses. A genuine compressed algorithm would need a bounded-support theorem or a succinct output representation, neither of which follows from Algorithm 1. The obstruction is not that the answer might be easy or hard—either would be a legitimate programme result—but that the proposed anchor has not yet identified a canonical computational object corresponding to the paper’s algorithm.

The chores \(\mathrm{EF1}+\mathrm{PO}\) theorem has the same defect, with an additional one. Its proof relies on a round-robin chronology and on the “last available indifference class” of a particular agent. A positive mass of one type may simultaneously occupy bundles whose worst chores lie in different classes, so there is no unique class from which that type is about to pick. The natural mass formulation again becomes a support-selection problem with pair-specific existential witnesses: for every pair of positive-mass bundles, some chore may have to serve as the \(\mathrm{EF1}\) witness. That disjunctive support condition is not what Algorithm 2 proves.

The strongest repaired chores mirror is therefore not a continuization of Algorithm 2, but a new problem asking for a distribution over integral chore bundles satisfying pairwise \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and individual Pareto optimality. Denominator expansion proves rational feasibility, but only through a potentially enormous discrete instance. A compressed algorithm would be interesting, yet its interest is precisely that it requires new structure absent from the paper.

So the negative case can reject the proponent’s confidence, but not honestly reject the mirror programme altogether. The three anchors are not established continuous results; they are sketches whose main unresolved issues are the type-level representation, support size, and preservation of \(\mathrm{PO}\) alongside fairness. Nevertheless, at least the first anchor remains a sensible high-multiplicity question in recurring allocation settings, and the latter two remain legitimate compression problems. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proper verdict is “credible, but substantially under-specified,” not “no.”
