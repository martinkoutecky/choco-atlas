The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no anchor that meets the stated requirement. Its numbered findings are “Result 1” and “Result 2,” not theorems, lemmas, corollaries, or propositions asserting a computational complexity result. Result 1 reports that Gale–Shapley reduces unraveling costs more than the priority and linear-programming mechanisms in the experiment, that short-run agent simulations support this finding, and that long-run simulations instead put linear programming close to Gale–Shapley. Result 2 reports comparable unmatched counts across centralized mechanisms but more mismatches under the linear-programming mechanism. These are experimental and simulation findings, not complexity classifications. The LP formulation and genetic algorithm are methods used in the paper, not named computational results.

There is a plausible population-level model behind those findings: in a large residency market, types could specify each side’s preferences, productivity or payoff profile, and relevant strategic behavior; mass would be the fraction of applicants or positions of each type. Early agreements and reported rankings would determine mass flows into matches, and the objective could track early-contract costs and mismatches. That seems recognizable as the paper’s subject. But turning it into a precise computational question about the resulting costs or dynamics would be an extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror anchored to one of its qualifying results. The paper’s small, individualized experimental markets and its behavior-learning simulations also make that extension’s type structure and dynamics hard to take directly from the text.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper for a continuous mirror to take as its anchor. Its two numbered findings report experimental and simulation comparisons: Result 1 compares unraveling costs across mechanisms, and Result 2 compares unmatched and mismatched agents. Neither asserts a complexity result. The assignment LP, deferred acceptance algorithm, and genetic algorithm are tools used in the study; their presence does not make the paper a complexity analysis.

The strongest rescue of Result 1 is a large residency market represented by masses of complete agent types, with early contracts as matching flows and unraveling cost measured per capita. That is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. But reproducing the paper’s finding would require a model of strategic behavior or learning. The paper’s experimental comparison and simulation trajectories do not specify a computational problem such as deciding whether a low-unraveling outcome exists or computing one. Defining that problem would require adding a solution concept, learning rule, and strategic state. It could be worthwhile work, but it would be a new computational study of unraveling, not a continuization of a computational result in this paper.

The strongest rescue of Result 2 is to track the mass of unmatched and mismatched agents. Those quantities have natural fractional versions. Yet the reported mismatch finding depends on agents’ strategic list truncation. Fix truthful lists and the question becomes a mechanical assignment problem, no longer the finding in Result 2; preserve strategic truncation and the same missing behavioral model returns. Fractional mass itself is no obstacle. The problem is that neither version mirrors a complexity claim the paper made.

So I would reject this paper as a source of a continuous computational mirror. The limitation of that case is its reach: it does not prove that no adjacent continuous matching project could be worthwhile. A formal computational study of a continuum population adapting to matching mechanisms might be valuable, but the paper does not supply the computational result to be continuized.
