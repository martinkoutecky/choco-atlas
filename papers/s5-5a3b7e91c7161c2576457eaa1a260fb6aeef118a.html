What the mirror covers

The paper's named results characterize scrutiny rules axiomatically; the mass tally does not cover those characterizations. The continuum-of-voters discussion concerns prior work on core stability, not a computational result of this paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper studies a genuine collective nomination process. One can imagine a much larger assembly in which many delegates share a small number of relevant ballot types, so mass over nomination types would be a natural way to describe the electorate.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are axiomatic results, not claims about complexity or algorithms. Theorem 2, proved here, says that in the Gregorian format the supermajority scrutinies—those electing \(i\) exactly when \(i\) receives at least \(t\) nominations, for \(n-1\ge t>n/2\), and otherwise producing black smoke—are characterized by impartiality, monotonicity, positive unanimity, voter anonymity, and candidate neutrality. It gives two alternative characterizations using name independence in place of one of the symmetry axioms; all three characterizations are tight. Theorem 1 is also an axiomatic characterization. The cited Theorems HM1, HM2, M1, and M2 concern axiomatic impossibility or characterization results, not computational complexity. So there is no eligible anchor, and I can’t honestly provide one continuous computational problem per anchor.

A weak adjacent question would be: given rational masses \(\mu_t\) of ballot types \(t\), each nominating a candidate in a fixed set \(C\), and a threshold \(q>1/2\), return the candidate whose nomination mass is at least \(q\), or black smoke if there is none. Its input is the support and masses of ballot types; its solution is the resulting candidate or black smoke. Summing the masses gives a direct polynomial-time algorithm. But this is just evaluation of a specified threshold rule, not the problem Theorem 2 addresses—characterizing which rules satisfy the axioms.

The main obstacle is the paper’s treatment of electors as candidates too. In the Gregorian format, each elector may not nominate himself, and whether an elector becomes pope must be independent of his own ballot. Preserving that feature requires voter identity to be part of the type. In a large assembly, that can make the number of types grow with the number of electors, undermining the high-multiplicity regime. Dropping the feature makes the mass model cleaner, but weakens the connection to the paper’s question. The paper’s mention of a continuum of voters in its discussion of core stability is also not a computational population mirror: it is background for interpreting the two-thirds threshold.

So the positive case is modest: the nomination rule has an obvious aggregate representation, and large assemblies with repeated ballot types are conceivable. The weakest point is decisive under the requested standard: the paper’s contribution is axiomatic, and a computational mirror would change the question the authors actually studied.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the key fact: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, and the cited results on deadlock, characterize or rule out voting rules by axioms. They do not give the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The continuum of voters mentioned in the core-stability discussion comes from prior analytic work; it does not supply a computational result from this paper.

The proposed mass-based question—given ballot masses, identify a candidate who meets a fixed threshold, or return black smoke—is well-defined, but it only evaluates a rule already specified. For candidate \(c\), compute \(s_c=\sum_{t:\,t\text{ nominates }c}\mu_t\), then compare it with the threshold. This takes \(O(\tau)\) rational additions and comparisons. It does not capture Theorem 2’s question about which rules satisfy the axioms. Recasting that characterization for continuous populations would still be an axiomatic project. Searching for a valid threshold would be similarly slight: the rule is determined by the threshold, and the axioms do the characterizing.

A stronger computational proposal would add a problem such as finding the least costly mass of electors to persuade to make a candidate win. That may be useful in another project, but the paper supplies neither a cost model nor a persuasion problem. With finitely many ballot types and explicit conversion costs, the natural version is a small linear optimization problem over nomination masses. Harder variants would get their difficulty from added preferences, costs, or constraints, rather than from continuizing a result in this paper.

There is also a structural obstacle to a faithful high-multiplicity model. Here each elector is also a possible pope, and impartiality asks whether that particular elector’s own ballot can affect their chance of election. Their identity is part of the question the axioms test. If the model retains individual candidacies, aggregating electors by ballot type loses the identity information and still leaves candidate-specific scores. If it merges electors into cohorts, the outcome becomes a cohort rather than a person, and the individual impartiality condition changes. In an atomless population, any one elector has zero mass, so their ballot cannot affect aggregate scores; impartiality becomes vacuous. Giving candidates positive mass avoids that problem only by treating a candidate as a bloc or retaining individualized atoms.

So the paper’s proposed threshold evaluation does not survive as a substantive computational anchor, and the better attempts either preserve the paper’s axiomatic question or introduce a new optimization problem. That makes this a poor fit for the continuization programme as described. The universal claim is harder to defend: one could build a worthwhile computational study around mass persuasion in a specified large assembly. But it would need its own motivating cost model and research question; this paper does not provide them.
