The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: this paper suggests a sensible continuous-population optimization problem, but it has no eligible named computational anchor under your rule.

The closest named result is Theorem 13, proved in the paper: for \(k\le m\le 2k-2\), every \((k,m)\)-agreeable \(d\)-box society with \(n\) voters has a platform approved by at least \(n-m+k\) voters, and this bound is best possible. That is a sharp combinatorial guarantee, not a result classifying an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. The paper’s Discussion does say that agreement number for \(d\)-box societies can be computed in polynomial time for fixed \(d\), but that claim is unnumbered. Its nearby NP-hardness claim concerns recognizing whether an arbitrary graph has boxicity at most \(d\), a different problem, and is cited from elsewhere. I would not present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

The best candidate mirror is Maximum Agreement Mass for \(d\)-Box Types. An instance gives a dimension \(d\), a finite support of \(\tau\) voter types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has an approval box \(B_t=\prod_{j=1}^d[a_{tj},b_{tj}]\), with rational endpoints. The task is to return a platform \(x\in\mathbb R^d\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t:x\in B_t}\mu_t, \]
and the maximum value; equivalently, decide whether some platform is approved by mass at least a given rational threshold.

A plausible regime is a large apartment complex choosing a thermostat setting: many residents share one of a modest number of comfort intervals, while the population is large. In higher dimensions, types could be residents’ acceptable ranges over several policy attributes. The type is the complete approval box relevant to this task; its mass is the fraction of residents with that box. Thus the society itself is represented as a distribution over types. The platform space remains continuous, but continuity of the platform is not the proposed contribution.

This is a credible algorithmic extension of the paper’s agreement-number objective, and the authors would likely recognize that objective. For fixed \(d\), enumerate tuples of box endpoints and evaluate the covered mass; an optimum can be represented by such a tuple. This gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and the input bit length when \(d\) is fixed, with a direct bound of \(O(\tau(2\tau)^d\operatorname{poly}(L))\). With \(d\) variable, the straightforward method is exponential, leaving a genuine complexity question. The paper’s unnumbered fixed-\(d\) statement supports the tractable direction, but does not supply the required anchor.

The scope would be agreement maximization for \(d\)-box approvals. It would not turn Theorem 13 itself into a computational result: its guarantee assumes unit-weight voters, and a sharp weighted analogue would need to be established separately. Further questions include what mass-based local-agreement condition yields a sharp lower bound on maximum agreement mass, and what happens when dimension is part of the input.

My weakest point is also the decisive limitation: these authors studied existence bounds for finite societies, not complexity, and there is no named hardness result whose dissolution in the continuous population could be tested. So this is a plausible new computational question built around their central objective, but it is not a continuous mirror of a qualifying named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 13 is a sharp extremal guarantee: under a local agreeability condition, some platform receives approval from at least \(n-m+k\) voters. The other numbered results are also geometric or combinatorial theorems, not complexity results. Recasting Theorem 13 over masses would ask for a weighted local-to-global theorem, which may be interesting, but it would not be a computational question of the kind ChoCo studies.

The proponent’s Maximum Agreement Mass problem is the best attempt to find a computational counterpart. It has a sensible population model: many residents can share one approval box, and the task is to maximize the mass approving a platform. But it changes the paper’s central question. The paper asks what follows from local agreement among groups; maximum mass for a supplied society asks where to place the platform. The more faithful mass version would preserve the local-to-global guarantee, and so would remain a mathematical extremal question rather than a computational one.

There is a real computational problem in maximizing weighted overlap of boxes. For fixed dimension, the paper itself says the finite agreement number can be computed in polynomial time, and its type-weighted version is a natural extension. Letting dimension vary gives another complexity regime, but that does not make the original local-agreement theorem computational. Nor can I dismiss the weighted problem merely because its algorithm may be straightforward or its difficulty may lie in dimension: those are not valid reasons to reject a well-posed mirror. The proposed thermostat setting is also a convincing high-multiplicity scenario.

So the negative case is narrow. This paper does not anchor a direct mirror in a named computational result, and its closest faithful continuization falls outside ChoCo’s computational scope. But I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: weighted maximum agreement mass is a plausible computational question, even if it is better described as a new problem inspired by the paper than as a mirror of its main theorem.
