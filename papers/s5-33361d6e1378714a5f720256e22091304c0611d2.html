The continuous mirror question

Given finite supports \(B,S\) of complete buyer and seller order types with rational masses \(\mu_b,\nu_s\), including values, demands, supplies, reserve prices, and eligibility, form for each common denominator multiple \(N\) the market with \(N\mu_b\) buyer copies and \(N\nu_s\) seller copies. Run the paper’s clinching auction and eating mechanism. Is the normalized event measure \(\eta_N=N^{-1}\sum_{e\in E_N}\delta_{(p_e,A_e)}\), where \(p_e\) is the event price and \(A_e\) its incident seller types, convergent and exactly computable in time polynomial in support size and input bit length? Compute its limit and the limiting type-symmetric sharing kernel \(x_s(p,A)\), or determine why no such compact representation exists; establish whether the kernel remains envy-free and distributes at least \((1-1/e)\int p\,d\eta\) in revenue.

The model it lives in

A large reservation exchange represented by rational masses over complete buyer and seller order types. Define the continuous event-price measure as the normalized limit of the clinching events in finite markets replicated from those masses; represent the eating allocation by \(x_s(p,A)\), the share assigned to a representative seller of type \(s\).

The case FOR (proponent)

There is one qualification to the positive case: the paper has no numbered result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results are mechanism guarantees and impossibility results. The closest constructive anchors are Theorem 7 and Theorem 9, both proved in this paper. They support a narrow mirror about computing fair revenue shares, not a claim about the complexity landscape of the whole market.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large reservation exchange with many publisher inventory orders and advertiser campaign orders, drawn from relatively few recurring templates. A type must include everything the market uses: value, demand, reserve price, supply, and targeting connections. The orders sharing a type are interchangeable; the number of orders can be much larger than the number of types. For instance, an exchange serving many local publishers and campaigns might repeatedly see the same inventory and targeting templates. This is a plausible regime, though not every advertising market has it.

Here is a concrete model. Let \(B\) and \(S\) be finite sets of buyer and seller types, with rational masses \(\mu_b\) and \(\nu_s\); normalize \(\sum_{b\in B}\mu_b=1\), and let the seller-to-buyer mass ratio \(\kappa=\sum_{s\in S}\nu_s\) be part of the input. Buyer type \(b\) has value \(v_b\), per-order demand \(d_b\), and eligible seller types \(\Gamma_b\). Seller type \(s\) has reserve price \(\rho_s\) and supply \(\ell_s\). Edges between types encode targeting. As in the paper, seller supply can be split into unit-supply seller types. The efficient aggregate allocation has variables \(z_{bs}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_s z_{bs}\leq\mu_b d_b\), \(\sum_b z_{bs}\leq\nu_s\ell_s\), and \(z_{bs}=0\) for ineligible pairs; it maximizes trade value plus the reserve value of unsold supply. The society is continuous here: these are masses of orders, not a finite list of named participants. Fractional type-level flows can still describe a population in which each individual order receives an integral bundle.

Lead: the atomless clinching-event envy-free sharing problem, mirroring Theorem 7. That theorem says the paper’s eating mechanism is envy-free with respect to the clinching graph: each unit-supply seller eats from its highest-paying non-exhausted eligible buying event over time; each seller eats at most one unit, and each event is exhausted after one unit. If \(E_i\) is seller \(i\)’s set of clinching-graph events and \(r_i[e]\) is its share of event \(e\)’s price, the theorem proves that for every pair of sellers \(i,i'\),
\[ \sum_{e\in E_i}r_i[e]\ \geq\ \sum_{e\in E_i}r_{i'}[e]. \]
The theorem is proved here, not cited.

The mirror problem is: given the type masses and market data above, compute the efficient clinching outcome and its buying-event price measure \(\eta\), where an event \(e\) has price \(p(e)\) and a seller-type incidence relation inherited from the clinching graph. Then compute a revenue-sharing kernel \(x_s(e)\), interpreted as the fraction of an event unit assigned to a representative seller of type \(s\). Require \(x_s(e)=0\) when \(s\) is not incident to \(e\), \(\int x_s(e)\,d\eta(e)\leq 1\) for each seller type, and \(\sum_s\nu_s x_s(e)\leq 1\) for each event. For each seller type \(s\), let \(E_s\) be its incident events; the continuous envy-free condition is
\[ \int_{E_s}p(e)x_s(e)\,d\eta(e)\ \geq\ \int_{E_s}p(e)x_{s'}(e)\,d\eta(e) \qquad\text{for every }s'. \]
A natural objective is to maximize the total revenue distributed to sellers,
\[ \int p(e)\sum_s\nu_sx_s(e)\,d\eta(e), \]
while preserving the efficient allocation and buyer payments. The output is the event representation and the sharing kernel. I expect this to be tractable when the type and price-event representation has polynomial size: the sharing stage is a linear program. Establishing that compact representation for the continuum clinching process is part of the question, not something Theorem 7 proves.

A second, distinct guarantee question, anchored by Theorem 9, asks for a feasible continuous sharing kernel with total payout at least \((1-1/e)\) times total buyer revenue. Theorem 9 proves that the paper’s eating mechanism achieves this bound in the finite market; it is also proved here. In the continuum model, the corresponding question is whether one can compute the efficient event-price representation and an envy-free share meeting that same budget-balance guarantee. The expected direction is tractability for finite type support and a compact event encoding: the eating rule supplies a candidate solution, while the linear program above can optimize the payout. This is a prediction for the mirror, not a theorem of the paper.

These questions cover the paper’s revenue-sharing results, not its full mechanism-design agenda. They preserve the two-sided market, the eligibility graph, the welfare-maximizing allocation, and the attribution of buyer payments to sellers. The paper’s use of a fractional eating process is not itself the continuization; the proposed mirror changes the buyer and seller populations into distributions over complete order types. Further questions include the exact optimal payout under envy-freeness and whether the paper’s monotonicity guarantees persist under aggregation.

The weakest point is the continuum treatment of buyers. In an atomless population, one order has zero mass, which can change the individual externalities underlying VCG payments and therefore the clinching-event prices. The proposed mirror needs a defensible aggregate clinching process that preserves those prices; merely making the eating step fractional would not suffice. That risk is why I would keep the claim to the revenue-sharing subproblem and not assert that the paper’s buyer-incentive-compatibility results carry over unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch between the anchors and ChoCo’s target. Theorem 7 says the eating mechanism is envy-free; Theorem 9 gives its budget-balance guarantee. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper already describes the eating mechanism as polynomial-time computable. So a continuous question asking whether that same mechanism retains those properties is a limit analysis of a mechanism guarantee, not a new complexity result. A question asking for the *maximum* envy-free payout would be computational, but it changes the objective: neither theorem claims that the eating mechanism is optimal. That objection applies to both anchors.

The best proposed market regime is still credible: many advertisers and publishers using a small set of complete order templates, including their values, demands, supplies, reserves, and targeting. But it creates a difficult tradeoff. If targeting neighborhoods vary enough to matter, they belong in the types and the support may grow with the number of participants, leaving little compression. If neighborhoods repeat and support stays small, welfare allocation is an explicit capacitated matching problem over the type graph. Its mass version is a flow or LP; the paper’s eating rule supplies a direct candidate for sharing. There is no evident exponentially large family of choices or pricing problem of the kind that makes a continuization computationally distinctive.

This does not establish that a compressed clinching and event-price representation is easy. Rational masses can encode multiplicities too large to expand into individual orders, and the paper does not show how its event structure compresses by type. A new algorithm for that representation could be worthwhile. But that would be a new high-multiplicity implementation question, rather than a computational result anchored by either Theorem 7 or Theorem 9.

So the negative case is strongest against the *claimed anchors*: their continuous versions appear either to restate mechanism guarantees or to introduce a new optimization objective. It is not strong enough to rule out every useful mirror. A repeated-template reservation market is a sensible population regime, and a nontrivial compressed algorithm could make its mirror worthwhile.
