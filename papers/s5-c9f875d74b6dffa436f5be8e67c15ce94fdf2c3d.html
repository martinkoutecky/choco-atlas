The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying anchor: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Sections 3–4 calculate popularity and universality for a five-person committee, and Table 5 reports the resulting winner. Those calculations are computational work, but presenting them as a named result about membership in P or hardness would invent an anchor.

The best honest foothold is an unanchored population version of that calculation. Let each candidate \(\alpha\) have a binary profile \(x^\alpha\in\{0,1\}^q\) over \(q\) policy questions. Voter types are answer vectors \(t\in\{0,1\}^q\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one; let \(w_j\) be the question weights. For each candidate and question, set
\[ r_{\alpha j}=\sum_{t:t_j=x^\alpha_j}\mu_t. \]
Given the candidate profiles, weights, and support of the voter distribution, choose a candidate maximizing
\[ F(\alpha)=\frac12\left(\sum_{j=1}^q w_jr_{\alpha j} +\sum_{j=1}^q w_j\,\mathbf{1}[r_{\alpha j}\ge\tfrac12]\right), \]
the paper’s mean of popularity and universality. A plausible regime is a large public consultation on a library name or other civic choice: many respondents, but relatively few distinct answer vectors. This keeps the paper’s policy-representation question intact while making the population the continuous object. The stated problem is directly computable from the supported types, so I would expect it to be tractable.

Its weakness is decisive for your requested standard: this is a plausible extension of the paper’s method, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper. The article neither establishes this population regime nor presents an algorithmic complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper gives no named computational result to mirror. It presents an application of the Third Vote to five committee members and three shortlisted names. It states no general algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or hardness result. The proponent agrees there is no qualifying anchor; their population calculation is an extension of the method, not a mirror of a result in the paper.

A large public consultation is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, so the paper’s small committee is not a reason to reject the population model. But in that setting, the proposed calculation remains straightforward. For question \(j\), let \(p_j\) be the mass answering yes, and let \(x_{\alpha j}\in\{0,1\}\) describe candidate \(\alpha\). Its representativeness is \(p_j\) when \(x_{\alpha j}=1\), and \(1-p_j\) otherwise. Thus the indices depend only on the \(q\) marginal yes-masses, not on correlations among voter types. With an explicit candidate list, compute those marginals from the \(\tau\) supported types and score every candidate in time polynomial in the input size. This is a useful population-level way to report the paper’s indices, but it adds no computational question of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart.

The strongest repair would be to add a succinctly described set of feasible candidates, or to ask for the least mass change that makes a chosen candidate win. Either could define a new computational problem. But the paper supplies neither an implicit candidate-generation problem nor a model of campaigning, costs, or robustness. Those additions might be worthwhile research; they cannot turn the paper’s calculation into a computational result it did not make. In the first case, the paper’s shortlist is simply replaced by a new optimization problem; in the second, a new intervention model is introduced.

So the negative case is strong as a verdict about this paper: its natural continuous population version is plausible, but it mirrors an illustrative calculation, not a computational contribution, and the direct calculation appears routine. The universal claim is less secure. A later paper could combine the Third Vote with a substantive candidate-generation or intervention problem, and the consultation setting makes high multiplicity credible. That would be a case for studying a new problem inspired by this paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its results.
