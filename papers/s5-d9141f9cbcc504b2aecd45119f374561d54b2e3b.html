The continuous mirror question

Given four additive valuation types of mass \(1/4\) over nine indivisible good categories, each with supply \(1/4\) per capita, does there exist a complete mass assignment of integral bundles such that every positive-mass type-bundle pair satisfies \(\mathrm{EFX}_0\) against every bundle receiving positive mass?

The model it lives in

Let \(T=\{1,2,3,4\}\) have \(\mu_t=1/4\), and let \(G\) contain nine categories. Choose \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) with \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=1/4\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=1/4\) for each category. Require that whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(\sum_s x_{s,B'}>0\), \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(B'\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in B')\.

The objection that survived

The paper states no input-to-allocation runtime; its finite certified menus still provide a selector for this fixed-size setting, so that limits the computational contribution rather than defeating the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 for four valuation types and up to nine categories, with Lemma 1 supplying the fixed-assignment test. It does not cover variable support size \(\tau\), more than nine categories, or a general complexity frontier.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(\tau\) and \(m\), does every rational valuation distribution with prescribed category supplies admit a type-wise \(\mathrm{EFX}_0\) assignment into integral bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor test has an empty set: the paper names no theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, FPT, or similar. Its main result is an existence theorem. The closest computational anchor is Theorem 1 (Main), proved here: every instance with four agents, at most nine indivisible goods, and arbitrary nonnegative real additive valuations has a complete \(\mathrm{EFX}_0\) allocation. The paper computes with that existence question through Lemma 1 (Encoding), proved here: for a fixed allocation \(A\), failure of \(\mathrm{EFX}_0\) is exactly the disjunction, over \(i\ne j\) and \(g\in A_j\), of \(v_i(A_i)<v_i(A_j)-v_i(g)\). The lemma is the formulation used in the paper’s machine checks; it is not a complexity classification.

My best positive mirror is Four-Cohort Complete \(\mathrm{EFX}_0\) Feasibility. Imagine a large population in four equal cohorts, with everyone in a cohort sharing the same complete additive valuation over nine categories of indivisible goods. Each category has \(1/4\) unit of supply per unit of population, so for a population of size \(N\), there are \(N/4\) copies of each category. This describes, for example, repeated allocation rounds with four stable preference cohorts and goods replicated as the cohorts grow. The number of agents can be large while the number of distinct types stays at four.

Formally, let \(T=\{1,2,3,4\}\), \(G=\{g_1,\ldots,g_9\}\), and let each type \(t\) have an additive valuation \(v_t\) (binary rational values for a computational version). Its mass is \(\mu_t=1/4\). For each bundle \(B\subseteq G\), let \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving that bundle. Require
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\frac14 \quad(t\in T), \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni g}x_{t,B}=\frac14 \quad(g\in G). \]
Thus every agent gets an integral bundle, while the allocation of the population across bundles is measured continuously. Call \(x\) feasible if, whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(B'\) is assigned positive mass, then for every \(g\in B'\),
\[ v_t(B)\ge v_t(B'\setminus\{g\}). \]
The question is whether such an \(x\) exists. It is a feasibility question, with no objective.

Theorem 1 implies a yes answer for every instance of this mirror. Apply it to the four type valuations as a four-agent instance, and let \(A_t\) be the bundle assigned to agent \(t\). Set \(x_{t,A_t}=1/4\) and all other \(x_{t,B}=0\). Each good is assigned once among the four agents, so the supply constraints hold; the theorem’s \(\mathrm{EFX}_0\) inequalities give the required population inequalities. The expected classification for this exact, fixed-size mirror is therefore tractable: the answer is always yes, and the finite allocation space can also be checked directly. This is a transfer of the paper’s existence result, not evidence that continuous population has dissolved a hardness result.

This is a credible high-multiplicity lift of their underlying allocation question: additive preferences, indivisible bundles, complete use of goods, and the same \(\mathrm{EFX}_0\) comparison remain. But it covers only Theorem 1’s four valuation types and nine good categories. Questions about unequal cohort masses, more types, or growing numbers of goods would need their own definitions and proofs; Theorem 1 does not settle them.

The weakest point is also substantial: the paper’s difficult result concerns four individual agents and a small number of goods, and the proposed clone model inherits its yes answer directly. The paper’s continuous variables describe valuations of those four agents; they do not make the society continuous. The mirror changes the population regime, but the anchor supplies no complexity frontier for that regime, so authors could reasonably see this as a natural replication of their theorem rather than a new computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the paper’s own result types. Theorem 1 is an existence theorem for exactly four agents and at most nine goods; Lemma 1 encodes when one fixed allocation fails EFX. Neither classifies the complexity of an input problem. The week of solver work verifies a universal mathematical claim; it is not an algorithm whose running time answers an input-dependent computational question.

The best direct population mirror is the four-cohort model in the case for: four valuation types of mass \(1/4\), nine good categories with per-capita supply \(1/4\), and an EFX allocation of integral bundles to the population. But Theorem 1 gives “yes” for every valuation profile in that model. With four types and nine categories fixed, there are only \(4^9\) allocations to check. The population can grow without bound while the model’s relevant dimensions stay fixed. That makes the mirror a valid restatement of the existence theorem, but a poor target for a complexity landscape.

A stronger candidate lets the support size \(\tau\) vary: a distribution over complete additive valuation types, per-capita supplies of indivisible categories, and a mass assignment to bundles that satisfies EFX across all bundles used. This is a sensible high-multiplicity question. The paper’s theorem, however, covers four agents, not arbitrary \(\tau\); it supplies no reduction or structural result for this model. Studying it could be worthwhile, but it would be a new EFX problem rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Lemma 1 does not provide a separate foothold. Its continuous analogue would say that any two bundles receiving positive mass must satisfy the corresponding EFX inequalities for each type receiving one of them. That is useful for checking a proposed assignment. Finding the assignment also means choosing its support, so the mass variables alone do not turn the condition into the paper’s fixed-allocation encoding. With four types and nine categories the possibilities are finite and tiny; with variable \(\tau\), the hard part is a new support-selection problem that Lemma 1 does not resolve.

So the negative case is strong for this paper as an anchor: its continuous-looking machinery ranges over valuations, while the society remains four named agents, and its main result is existence rather than computational complexity. It is weaker as a universal claim. A variable-support, high-multiplicity EFX problem could be a worthwhile independent project; the paper simply gives little reason to treat it as the continuous continuation of its results.
