The continuous mirror question

Given a finite value support \(V\), a rational law \(F\) on \(V\), and positive supply and demand rates \(\alpha_S,\alpha_B\), let \(\mu_S=\alpha_S F\) and \(\mu_B=\alpha_B F\). At each integral scale \(N\), draw \(N\alpha_S\) sellers and \(N\alpha_B\) buyers independently from \(F\), add one buyer drawn from \(F\), and run BTR. Does normalized expected BTR gains from trade converge to the optimal mass-matching gains from trade for \((\mu_S,\mu_B)\), and what is the limiting gap from the baseline fluid optimum?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic double auction with role-specific value types \(V\), seller and buyer masses \(\mu_S,\mu_B\), and matching variables \(x_{b,s}\geq 0\). Maximize \(\sum_{b,s\in V}(b-s)x_{b,s}\), subject to \(\sum_s x_{b,s}\leq\mu_B(b)\), \(\sum_b x_{b,s}\leq\mu_S(s)\), and \(x_{b,s}=0\) when \(b<s\). Define the fluid BTR outcome as the scaled limit of the finite threshold-price mechanism, then compare its normalized expected gains from trade with this optimum.

The proposed fixed added mass δF is macroscopic, while one added buyer has vanishing mass under scaling; taking δ to zero also removes the finite-sample comparison.

What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a plausible fluid-market analogue, but this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor. Its named results concern welfare guarantees for mechanisms; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. So the candidate below is a useful positive intuition, but it does not meet your anchor requirement.

The closest named result is Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper. It says that for any numbers of sellers and buyers, if all their values are drawn i.i.d. from the same distribution \(F\), then BTR with one additional buyer has expected gains from trade at least the expected optimal gains from trade in the original market:
\[ \btr(m_S,m_B+1)\geq\opt(m_S,m_B). \]

A plausible regime is a large exchange with many small buyers and sellers whose values come from a shared, finite set of value types. Let \(\mu_S(v)\) and \(\mu_B(v)\) be the masses at value \(v\), with both measures induced by the same value law \(F\), scaled by the supply and demand totals. A trade plan is a nonnegative mass \(x_{b,s}\) matched between buyer value \(b\) and seller value \(s\), bounded by those type masses; its objective is aggregate gains from trade, \(\sum_{b,s}(b-s)x_{b,s}\).

The lead candidate is the Fluid BTR Boundary Problem: given these rational type masses and an added buyer mass \(\delta F\), compute the maximum gains from trade and determine whether a BTR-style threshold-price trade plan with weak budget balance attains it; then characterize the limit as \(\delta\to0\). In the atomless fluid model, the one-unit reduction in finite BTR has zero mass, so I expect the fluid rule to attain the first-best. For finite support, the underlying optimization looks tractable by sorting values and matching mass. This candidate covers the welfare comparison in Theorem 3.1, not the paper’s sample-pricing results or its distribution-specific lower bounds.

The soft spot is also the reason this is not a strong ChoCo case: passing to mass makes the additional buyer negligible and washes out the finite-agent trade reduction that Theorem 3.1 analyzes. Dominant-strategy incentives for an individual infinitesimal agent also need a careful interpretation. So the mirror is plausible as a large-market welfare question, but it mostly becomes a simple fluid limit—and the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch with ChoCo’s computational target, rather than a claim that these markets lack repeated types. Large exchanges with many buyers and sellers sharing the same value type are a credible high-multiplicity regime. But Theorem 3.1 gives an expected welfare guarantee for BTR; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s other named results are also welfare bounds or mechanism impossibilities, and its open problem asks for a sharper bound on the number of added buyers. There is no algorithmic problem here with an encoded input and a complexity classification to continue.

The proposed fluid version also loses the feature that makes Theorem 3.1 interesting. Its extra buyer is one random individual, and BTR’s loss is a reduced marginal trade. With finite value support and a normalized population of size \(N\), both have mass \(1/N\); their contribution to per-capita welfare disappears as \(N\) grows. A fixed added mass \(\delta\) avoids that disappearance, but describes macroscopic recruitment rather than one extra buyer. And replacing random draws by deterministic type masses removes the order-statistic variation and exchangeability used in the theorem’s coupling argument. That is a change to the question, not a continuous rendering of its comparison.

The best repair would retain integer type counts and represent the realized population by a high-multiplicity histogram, perhaps computing expected BTR welfare from the underlying sampling distribution. That preserves the theorem’s stochastic market, but it keeps the finite-agent randomness that the fluid model removed and still supplies no computational result from this paper to mirror. A second repair—asking how to compute the optimal feasible mechanism from type masses—would be a different project: it replaces the paper’s comparison of a simple prior-independent mechanism with an optimal benchmark by distribution-aware mechanism optimization.

So I would reject this as a direct ChoCo mirror, while conceding the negative case is not universal proof. The market regime itself is sensible, and a new computational study of mechanism evaluation or optimization on compressed type distributions could be worthwhile. The case against is that such a study would be adjacent to this paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.
