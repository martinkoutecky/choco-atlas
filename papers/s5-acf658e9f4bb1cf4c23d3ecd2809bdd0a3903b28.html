The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind: no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a problem is hard, tractable, or otherwise computationally classified. Its results are simulation findings, reported in the figures and prose. I can’t honestly supply the required numbered anchor or a continuous problem per anchor without inventing one.

There is a natural population-level question suggested by the experiments: represent students by their true preference rankings and school priorities, let each type have a mass, scale school capacities as population shares, and compare Boston and student-proposing Gale–Shapley when some mass follows Strategies A, B, or C. One could ask how much mass benefits by changing its reported preferences. That is a plausible continuous analogue of the paper’s experimental comparison, but it is a proposed question, not a mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result in this paper to mirror. It proves no theorem about complexity, exact algorithms, approximation, or parameterization. Its claims come from simulations: for a particular preference generator, \(2{,}000\) students, \(20\) schools, and three hand-designed strategies, Boston appears to benefit sophisticated students more often than student-proposing Gale–Shapley. That is an empirical comparison, not a classification of a computational problem.

The proponent’s proposed population model is coherent. Students could be grouped by complete types—true preferences, school-priority profiles, and any other attributes the model uses—with school capacities scaled to population shares. One could then measure how much mass benefits when a specified share follows Strategies A, B, or C. School choice is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; the paper’s use of individual positions and siblings does not rule out a sensible type-based regime.

But that model would turn the paper’s experiment into a continuous comparative-statics study. With the strategies fixed, the task is to transform type masses and run the two matching procedures. To get a computational problem closer to ChoCo’s target, one would need to add an optimization question, such as finding the most effective manipulation. That could be interesting, but it is new content: this paper neither asks nor answers it. The alternative individual-deviation model also changes the paper’s experiment, which measures outcomes when a positive fraction of students alter their reports together.

So the proposed mirror survives as a possible follow-up about continuum school choice, but not as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that boundary. It cannot honestly establish that no continuous school-choice question would be worthwhile; it can establish that this paper gives no computational result from which the programme’s intended complexity mirror can be built.
