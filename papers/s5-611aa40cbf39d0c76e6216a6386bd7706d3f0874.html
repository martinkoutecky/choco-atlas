The continuous mirror question

Input a finite set of types \(T\), object categories \(J\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), per-capita category capacities \(b_j\), and, for each type \(t\), an eligibility set and a partial order over eligible categories. On an atomless population partitioned into type groups of masses \(\mu_t\), decide whether there exist at most fixed \(k>1\) feasible matchings \(F=(f_1,\ldots,f_k)\) such that, for every feasible competing matching \(g\), the mass of people who strictly prefer some \(f_i(\omega)\) to \(g(\omega)\) is at least the mass who strictly prefer \(g(\omega)\) to every \(f_i(\omega)\). Each matching assigns at most one category to each person and assigns category \(j\) to mass at most \(b_j\); incomparability contributes to neither strict-preference mass.

The model it lives in

An atomless population with finite preference-and-eligibility types \(t\) of rational masses \(\mu_t\), and object categories \(j\) with capacities \(b_j\) per unit population. Represent \(F\) by masses \(x_{t,\mathbf{j}}\) over each type’s \(k\)-tuples of assignments \(\mathbf{j}\in(J\cup\{\emptyset\})^k\), summing to \(\mu_t\); each coordinate obeys the category capacities. Ask whether such a list of at most \(k\) assignments defeats every feasible measurable competitor under the paper’s mass-based weak Condorcet comparison.

What the mirror covers

Covers only Theorem 6.1’s fixed-\(k\) popular-set decision problem under partial orders; it leaves the dimension bounds, Pareto-optimality results, matroid-constrained cases, and arborescences outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(k\), what is the exact complexity of deciding whether a popular set exists in this atomless model?
  • Does fractional splitting erase the \((k+1)\)-Dimensional Matching reduction, or can hardness be shown for a natural finite-type regime?
  • What formal replication or approximation dictionary connects these continuous solutions to discrete popular sets?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s fixed-size popular-set problem. It is the closest match to the paper’s central question: when no single matching defeats every competitor, can a small set of matchings do so?

The anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It says that for every fixed integer \(k>1\), it is NP-complete, given a matching instance with partial-order preferences, to decide whether there is a popular set of at most \(k\) matchings. Here “popular” means weakly Condorcet-winning: against every competing matching, at least as much population prefers some member of the set as prefers the competitor to every member. The proof reduces from \((k+1)\)-Dimensional Matching; its membership argument checks a proposed set using maximum-weight matching.

Here is a continuous version that keeps those comparisons intact. Let \(T\) be a finite set of agent types and \(J\) a finite set of object categories. A type \(t\) specifies both the objects its agents may receive and their partial order over those objects. The input gives each type a rational population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and each object category \(j\) a capacity \(b_j\) per unit of population. Take an atomless population \(\Omega\), partitioned into type groups \(\Omega_t\) of mass \(\mu_t\).

A continuous matching is a measurable assignment \(f:\Omega\to J\cup\{\emptyset\}\): each person gets at most one object, and the mass assigned to category \(j\) is at most \(b_j\). Thus the fractional quantities describe how much population receives each category; each individual still receives an integral object or remains unmatched.

Given \(k\) such matchings \(F=(f_1,\ldots,f_k)\), define \(W(F,g)\) as the mass of people who strictly prefer at least one assignment in \(F\) to their assignment under a competing matching \(g\). Define \(L(F,g)\) as the mass who strictly prefer \(g\) to every assignment in \(F\). Incomparability counts as neither strict preference, as in the paper. The Fixed-\(k\) Popular Continuous Matching problem asks whether there are at most \(k\) feasible matchings \(F\) such that \(W(F,g)\ge L(F,g)\) for every feasible \(g\). A solution is the list of assignments itself. Equivalently, for fixed \(k\), it can be described by masses of agents of each type receiving each \(k\)-tuple of object categories across the list; those masses must sum to \(\mu_t\) for each type and satisfy the capacity bounds in every matching.

A credible regime is a large course-allocation system: many students, relatively few preference-and-eligibility types, and many seats in each of a relatively small number of course categories. Partial orders fit cases where students cannot reliably distinguish some courses. Matching a mass of students to course categories is then the natural unit of analysis, while per-capita seat supplies capture how capacity scales with enrollment. This is a co-modification of the matching model that the continuization programme explicitly allows. The paper’s authors should recognize the mirror: it retains their matching alternatives, partial-order comparisons, majority test, and fixed-size popular set.

My forecast for this mirror is Class B: hardness should transfer, because Theorem 6.1’s reduction encodes \((k+1)\)-Dimensional Matching in the incidence structure of agents and objects, rather than relying only on a large count of voters. But the theorem does not establish that forecast. Atomless type groups can split across assignments, creating competitors and popular sets unavailable in a discrete instance. A high-multiplicity dictionary—and a reduction that survives this fractional freedom—would be needed before claiming transferred hardness. One useful starting point is that, for a proposed set described by rational trajectory masses, its most damaging competitor can be found by a maximum-cost flow. Finding a set whose worst competitor never gains is the harder open question.

This case covers Theorem 6.1’s fixed-\(k\) decision problem under partial orders; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s dimension bounds, Pareto-optimality results, matroid constraints, or arborescences. The main follow-up questions are whether the continuous problem is tractable for fixed \(k\), whether its hardness persists when the number of types is small relative to the population, and how its solutions relate to discrete popular sets under replication.

The weakest point is the move from individually named objects to object categories with capacities that scale with the population. It is natural for seats or other replicated resources, and the programme permits this kind of co-modification, but it is still a real modeling choice. The other vulnerability is the fractional splitting of each type group: it is exactly what makes this a continuous mirror, and exactly why the discrete NP-completeness result cannot simply be carried over.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 6.1’s reduction uses one-off scarce objects. Its \((k+1)\)-Dimensional Matching gadget depends on which particular object an agent can take and on each object serving at most one agent. If the number of objects stays fixed while \(N\) agents become a unit-mass population, each object has normalized capacity \(1/N\). In the limit, almost everyone is unmatched, and all comparisons between feasible matchings concern zero mass. If instead object-category capacities stay positive per capita, those objects are replicated with the population, so the one-off bottlenecks in the reduction disappear. Keeping those bottlenecks requires retaining individual slots whose per-capita capacities shrink with \(N\), rather than a fixed supply of object types.

That is a real warning against claiming that this theorem’s hardness transfers. It does not defeat the stronger mirror, though. The proposed capacity-scaled model is well-defined: types include both preferences and eligibility, and masses over \(k\)-tuples preserve how the *same people* compare the \(k\) assignments with a competitor. It has no identity-dependent objective. Most importantly, scaling course seats with enrollment is a natural instance regime, and the programme explicitly permits supply to scale with population. The resulting question changes the object-supply regime, but the matching, partial-order comparisons, and fixed-size popular set remain. The paper itself situates capacitated matching among the relevant models.

I found nearby continuum matching work on convergence to continuum deferred-acceptance outcomes and work on fractional matching stability and optimality; neither appears to answer the fixed-size popular-set complexity question ([continuum-market analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.3982/TE5478), [fractional matching study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12259)). That is not proof that no such treatment exists.

So the honest negative case is weak: the unit-capacity hardness gadget does not carry over automatically, but the capacity-scaled mirror survives and looks worth asking. I cannot defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
