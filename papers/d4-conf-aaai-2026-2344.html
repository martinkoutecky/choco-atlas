The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper has a plausible high-multiplicity pricing problem behind it, but no anchor that satisfies your stated rule. Its displayed theorems and corollary concern existence of incentive-compatible core prices and the relationship between revenue and deviation regret; they do not classify a computational problem as tractable or hard. Section 2.0.1 says winner determination is NP-complete, but that is an unnumbered background claim. Section 5’s BPS and BO formulations are computed with, but are not numbered theorems. I would not present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

The scenario itself is credible: a large market with many buyers drawn from a small catalogue of complete bidder types. A type would include its package valuation, bid menu, and admissible valuation set—the information the auction designer has about that bidder. Mass is the fraction of buyers of each type. This could describe repeated procurement rounds or markets for replicated standardized lots, where supply scales with the buyer population. The regime is \(n\gg\tau\); it is not every combinatorial auction, especially one with a fixed number of indivisible licenses.

If the paper’s computed pricing section could serve as the anchor, I would nominate High-Multiplicity Weakest-Type Core Pricing. An instance gives item categories with per-capita supplies \(q\), a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), explicit XOR bid menus and rational valuations for each type, and polyhedral admissible type spaces \(\Theta_t\). An allocation is \(x_{tS}\ge0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned the whole bundle \(S\), with \(\sum_S x_{tS}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S\ni g}x_{tS}\le q_g\). Thus the allocation is fractional in aggregate, while each member of the assigned mass receives an integral bundle.

The task is to find an efficient allocation and type-level winner prices \(p_t\) that are individually rational and satisfy the core inequalities for every measurable coalition, while minimizing total payment \(\sum_t y_t p_t\), where \(y_t\) is the winning mass of type \(t\). Require \(p_t\) to be at least the type’s weakest-type pivot benchmark, defined as the per-agent externality in the limit of adding an infinitesimal mass of that type under \(\Theta_t\). A solution is the allocation and prices, or a certificate that no such core price vector exists. This mirrors the paper’s computation of weakest-type prices and its minimum-revenue core above those prices; it does not claim that the paper proves this continuous problem.

For this specified finite-menu, polyhedral model, I would expect a Class A result: welfare allocation is an LP over type-bundle masses, and the weakest-type and coalition-separation problems appear amenable to LP duality and column generation. Further questions include whether the marginal pivot limit always exists, what finite-market approximation error it gives, and how the result changes with correlated type spaces or implicit bundle languages. The mirror would cover the pricing computations and core-price selection, not the paper’s experimental fairness claims or equilibrium analysis.

The soft spot is substantial: co-scaling supply is essential to avoid a fixed stock of goods serving zero population mass, and the marginal pivot interpretation needs careful justification. More decisively, under your anchor requirement this remains a proposed mirror without a qualifying numbered result. I would present it as promising programme work, not as a complete anchor-based case for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Under the programme’s anchor rule, the case for identifies no result that can carry the mirror: the paper states no complexity theorem. Its impossibility theorem and incentive-minimization results concern mechanism properties; Section 5 gives formulations and constraint-generation routines, but no complexity classification. Winner determination’s NP-completeness is background. So there is no named computational result here whose continuous counterpart this paper establishes or classifies.

The strongest candidate is the proposed high-multiplicity weakest-type core-pricing problem. But its clean LP model changes what the paper computes. The paper’s WT price is a named bidder’s externality against the other bidders, minimized over that bidder’s type space conditional on their reports. Its core and regret notions likewise refer to individual bidders and coalitions. In a society represented only by masses, a single bidder has measure zero: normalized welfare changes by zero when they are removed, so the literal WT externality vanishes. Recovering a finite per-bidder price requires taking a marginal derivative or adding a distinguished atomic bidder to a continuum. Either choice introduces a new object: the derivative need not be unique at allocation ties, while the atom is precisely an individual the mass model did not aggregate. Neither is the paper’s WT calculation carried over unchanged.

There is a second gap in the proposed compression. A paper type would need to include a bidder’s full valuation vector, bid menu, and admissible-type information. But the paper permits a *joint* type space: what is admissible for one bidder can depend on the reports of others. A separate polyhedral set \(\Theta_t\) for each population type does not generally preserve that dependence. Keeping it would require a richer model of correlations among bidder profiles, beyond the proposed distribution over types. Restricting to independent, type-wise constraints makes the LP cleaner, but it removes part of the information structure that motivates WT pricing in this paper.

The replicated-market scenario is not absurd. Buyers with repeated package valuations and supplies that scale with the population could be a sensible auction regime. But its mass allocation and measurable-coalition core describe a large-market pricing problem. If the goal is to retain the paper’s individual incentives and pivotal externalities, one must keep marginal agents or atoms; if the goal is to aggregate everyone, the strategic and core questions change. That makes it a plausible adjacent project, not a clear continuous mirror of one of this paper’s results.

I would therefore reject the proposed mirror as an anchor-based case for this paper. I would not claim the stronger universal negative honestly: replicated procurement markets could make a worthwhile continuous computational problem, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as related to their WT pricing work. The negative case is strongest on the mismatch with the paper’s stated results and weakest on the claim that no useful scenario exists.
