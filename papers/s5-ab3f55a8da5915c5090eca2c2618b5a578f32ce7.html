The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The paper gives constructive choice procedures and uses matching and greedy algorithms, but it does not state a named complexity result about computing them.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for any choice rule satisfying non-wastefulness, no justified envy, and compliance with vertical reservations, the admitted set under the meritorious Over-and-Above rule Gale dominates the admitted set under that rule, for every applicant set. That is a substantive result about the quality of a choice rule, but it is not a complexity result. So it cannot honestly serve as a required anchor. The following is the strongest continuous analogue I see, conditional on accepting Theorem 1 as a near-anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is large public recruitment or university admissions, with millions of applicants but far fewer combinations of relevant characteristics. A type could specify a merit-score band and the applicant’s complete pattern of vertical-reservation eligibility, including overlapping EWS and caste-based eligibility. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants of that type. Position capacities also become population shares, so the open and reserved capacities scale with the applicant pool. This is credible only where applicants really are interchangeable within a type; a national exam with many repeated score bands and common eligibility categories is a stronger example than a small hiring committee.

The paper assumes distinct individual merit scores. A type-level model would need the modest co-modification of score bands, with applicants at a tied cutoff divisible for allocation purposes. That changes the tie convention and should be made explicit.

I would call the candidate problem Continuous Meritorious Over-and-Above Allocation. The instance is a finite support of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), merit scores or ordered score bands, vertical-eligibility sets \(\rho_t\), and rational capacity shares \(\kappa_c\) for each protected category \(c\). The open-category share is \(\kappa_o=1-\sum_c\kappa_c\). A solution is an admitted mass \(y_t\) for every type and a feasible assignment of that mass to open or eligible protected positions, respecting type masses and all capacity shares.

The rule first assigns the open-category share to the highest-merit mass. From the remaining mass, it chooses a maximum-size assignable set for the protected positions, breaking ties between such sets in favor of higher merit bands. Equivalently, the protected assignment must be feasible as a fractional matching between remaining types and eligible categories; the selected type masses must be lexicographically merit-maximal among maximum-size feasible selections. The question is whether one can compute such an allocation from the binary-encoded masses and capacities, and whether its admitted merit distribution dominates that of every allocation satisfying the mass versions of the paper’s three axioms. In the continuous version, Gale dominance can be expressed as having at least as much admitted mass above every merit threshold.

I expect this problem to be tractable in \(\tau\), the number of active types, the number of categories, the number of score bands, and the input bit length: fractional matching reduces to flow, and the merit priority can be handled by a greedy sequence over score bands. That is a plausible Class A direction, not a result established by this paper. Useful next questions are whether the mass dominance claim follows under the stated tie convention, whether integral flows recover the finite outcome whenever masses and capacities come from integer counts, and how the answer changes when horizontal traits are included.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s result is already about finite populations of arbitrary size, and its central contribution is axiomatic rather than complexity-theoretic. The continuous version could be useful for computing aggregate outcomes in a compact, high-multiplicity instance, but it does not yet expose a discrete hardness result that might dissolve. A referee could reasonably view it as an aggregate implementation of the authors’ existing rule, rather than the computational mirror of one of their named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror has no eligible result to anchor to. Theorem 1 proves a merit-dominance property for a choice rule; Theorems 2–5 characterize rules under stated axioms. None gives a complexity result. The paper uses matching and greedy procedures to define or prove properties of its rules, but it does not classify the complexity of computing them. So “compute the continuous allocation” adds a computational question to the paper; it does not mirror one of its named computational results.

The proposed high-multiplicity setting is plausible. If many applicants share a merit score and the same full eligibility profile, their mass can represent a real applicant cohort, and capacities can scale with the applicant pool. That makes the aggregate allocation question coherent. The score issue is a real qualification: the paper assumes distinct individual scores, so exact scores make applicants different types. Grouping scores into bands requires a tie extension. A stronger version could use discrete exam scores with many repeated values and ask whether the authors recognize that extension. That is not, by itself, a decisive objection.

Even with that repair, the continuous question concerns computing an aggregate choice rule and extending Theorem 1’s dominance property. It does not arise from a complexity result in this paper. To make it a ChoCo complexity mirror, one would have to add a new computational task—such as changing the applicant population at minimum cost—and specify its action costs and objective. That may be worthwhile research, but it would be a new problem built around the paper’s allocation model, rather than a continuization of one of its results.

So the case against is strongest on fit and weakest on the universal claim. The paper supplies no named computational result for the proponent to mirror, which is decisive under the stated anchor rule. But the applicant-cohort model is sensible, and I cannot honestly establish that no continuous computational question based on it could be worthwhile.
