The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) of binary valuation vectors over one class \(G\) of identical indivisible units and divisible classes \(D\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b\) and \(c_d\), return a finite list of actions \((t,q,x)\) with masses defining \(\nu\), where \(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\)\), \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{D}\)\), goods go only to types that value them, the type marginal is \(\mu\), \(\int q\,d\nu=b\)\), and \(\int x_d\,d\nu=c_d\)\), minimizing \(\int (v_{tG}q+\sum_{d\in D}v_{td}x_d)^2\,d\nu\).

The model it lives in

A mass allocation over complete binary valuation types: each person receives an integral count of identical indivisible units and divisible quantities, type masses and per-capita supplies are fixed, and the objective is mean squared utility.

What the mirror covers

Covers the square-sum subcase of Theorem 6.1 with identical indivisible goods; it leaves aside the theorem's full range of \(\Phi\), the MNW and egalitarian hardness corollary, and the paper's algorithmic and proximity results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does this problem admit an exact polynomial-time algorithm in \(|T|, |D|, L\), for rational input, using a configuration LP and an efficient pricing step?
  • Can an optimum always be returned with polynomially many rational action atoms, and how closely can it be implemented in a finite replicated society?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 6.1, proved in the paper (and labeled a restatement of Theorem 1.2 in the introduction). It says that, for every fixed symmetric strictly convex \(\Phi\), finding a \(\Phi\)-minimizer is NP-hard even when all indivisible goods are identical; hence finding a \(\Phi\)-fair allocation is NP-hard. The proof reduces from 3-dimensional matching. For a precise mirror, I would take its square-sum case, \(\Phi(z)=\sum_i z_i^2\), which the paper names as a typical symmetric strictly convex objective.

Call the continuous problem Mass Square-Fair Mixed Allocation. Its input is a finite set \(T\) of complete binary valuation types over indivisible good classes \(G\) and divisible good classes \(D\); rational type masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\); and rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\) and \(c_d\). An allocation is a mass distribution \(\nu\) over actions \((t,q,x)\), where \(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}^{G}\) is an individual’s integral bundle of indivisible goods and \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{D}\) is their divisible bundle. A type’s mass may split across actions, but each individual action still gives whole units of indivisible goods. Require \(\nu\)’s type marginal to be \(\mu\), and require \(\int q_g\,d\nu=b_g\) and \(\int x_d\,d\nu=c_d\) for every good class. Goods a type does not value cannot be assigned to it. The action’s utility is \(U(t,q,x)=\sum_{g\in G}v_{tg}q_g+\sum_{d\in D}v_{td}x_d\). The task is to find a feasible, finitely supported \(\nu\) minimizing \(\int U(t,q,x)^2\,d\nu\). A solution lists the action atoms, their masses, and their divisible bundles.

This is a per-capita high-multiplicity formulation: a finite society with \(K\mu_t\) people of type \(t\) has \(Kb_g\) indivisible units and \(Kc_d\) divisible units, when those quantities are integral. Its normalized square-sum objective is the finite population’s mean squared utility. The mass distribution preserves differences in utility among people of the same type; replacing a type by just its average utility would not preserve that objective.

A plausible setting is a large, recurring allocation programme: many applicants with a small or moderate number of eligibility and preference profiles receive whole placements or equipment units alongside divisible training funds or support hours. The complete valuation vector defines type. The population grows while the programme’s inventories grow with it, so the number of people can be much larger than the number of types. The paper’s 3DM construction itself has many types as the reduction input grows, but any such instance can be repeated \(K\) times, making the population arbitrarily large relative to its support. I think the authors would recognize this as their fairness problem in a high-multiplicity regime: binary additive valuations, mixed goods, whole indivisible units per person, and a symmetric strictly convex fairness objective remain in place. Scaling inventories with the population is the necessary co-modification.

My expectation is Class A for this mass problem, through convex optimization with a configuration-pricing step. Once a type’s mass can split among integral bundles, the indivisible goods become aggregate supply constraints. For binary additive valuations, a pricing query appears to reduce to choosing valued units according to their shadow prices. A prover would need to establish polynomial bit complexity and exact optimality, including how the continuous divisible allocations interact with the bundle choices. Natural follow-ups are the complexity in \(|T|,|G|,|D|\) and input bit length \(L\), whether a finite-support optimum always has a polynomial-size representation, and how well a continuous optimum can be implemented in a finite replicated population.

The theorem does not settle this mirror’s complexity. Its 3DM reduction selects whole agents for hyperedges; in the mass model, members of one hyperedge type can split across different bundles. Fractional choices may erase the matching obstruction. That is my weakest point and also why the question is worth asking: the theorem supplies a concrete hardness boundary to test, while the answer for the continuous population remains open. This case covers Theorem 6.1’s square-sum subcase and its identical-indivisible-goods regime, not the theorem’s full range of \(\Phi\) or the paper’s other results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with Theorem 6.1, but it does not defeat the mirror. The theorem is a genuine computational result, and its square-sum instance is a clean anchor. Its 3DM reduction, however, depends on selecting whole edge-agents: the converse uses the overlap between two selected hyperedges to force some named agents above utility \(0.6\). In a mass model, a hyperedge type can split across bundles. Replicating each type \(K\) times and scaling supplies by \(K\) preserves a large finite population, but it can turn the reduction’s matching choice into fractional selection.

That is the best objection to the *hardness transfer*, not to asking the question. The programme specifically treats a changed answer as a worthwhile result. And the proposed mirror has a credible interpretation: types are complete valuation profiles over item classes; each person still receives whole indivisible units; and \(\int U^2\,d\nu\) preserves differences in utility among people of the same type. With rational masses and supplies, it also has a finite-replication interpretation. A recurring allocation programme with many applicants per valuation profile is plausible.

The paper’s discussion of divisible-only markets and discrete-convex allocation does not establish that this continuous-population computational problem is already solved. Nor does the apparent opportunity for configuration optimization count against it: an efficient pricing method would be the kind of tractability result the programme seeks.

So the honest case against is weak. The reduction may collapse under continuization, but that is a reason the mirror could reveal something new, not a reason it is unworthy. I cannot defeat the square-sum anchor on the supplied evidence.
