What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror would concern the implementation guarantees in Theorem 1 and Proposition 1; the paper's mechanism constructions and discussion of weak honesty do not provide separate computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the programme’s anchor rule. The closest results are Theorem 1—proved in this paper—and Proposition 1, also proved here. Theorem 1 says that if an SCF is incentive-compatible and the information environment satisfies NCKS and ID, then the SCF is uniquely implementable in a Bayesian Nash equilibrium. Proposition 1 gives the corresponding result for the special information-elicitation problem: under NCKS and ID, any SCF is uniquely implementable. These are existence and implementation theorems, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classified. The paper gives a mechanism construction, but makes no complexity claim about computing or solving the underlying problem.

A population mirror would also require substantial new modelling. The paper has finitely many strategic agents with distinct roles in a finite epistemic type space; its probability distributions describe beliefs over states and agents’ epistemological types, not population shares. Its distribution-valued messages and stochastic allocations continuize reports and outcomes, not the society. Replacing the finite agents with mass over types and asking for unique implementation in an atomless population might be an interesting extension, but it would need new assumptions about aggregate mechanisms and higher-order beliefs, and it would not mirror a qualifying named result in this paper.

So there is no defensible continuous problem to hand to a prover *per anchor*. The paper has important named theoretical results, but no named computational result of the kind this programme asks us to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 1 proves an existence claim: every incentive-compatible social choice function is uniquely implementable when NCKS and ID hold. Proposition 1 proves the corresponding claim for information elicitation. Neither studies the complexity of deciding a computational problem, optimizing an intervention, or finding an approximation or parameterized algorithm. The paper gives an explicit mechanism construction, but does not identify a computational bottleneck for that construction. Under ChoCo’s anchor rule, that is decisive: there is no named computational result here to continuize.

The best proposed mirror of Theorem 1 would take a large society whose complete agent types include their signal structures, utilities, and epistemic positions, then ask whether an aggregate social choice function can be uniquely implemented under mass versions of NCKS and ID. That is a coherent extension, but it changes the question from the theorem’s implementation guarantee into a new population mechanism-design problem. To make it computational, one would need to add an objective such as minimizing mechanism cost or classify the complexity of synthesis. Neither objective is in the paper, and the theorem supplies no latent search problem for it to inherit. Its explicit scoring-rule construction is a recipe for implementation, not a complexity landscape.

Proposition 1 offers even less of an anchor: allocation payoffs are irrelevant, and the result concerns eliciting private information through nested scoring rules. A mass version could ask how to elicit an aggregate signal distribution from a large society. But that requires choosing an aggregate target, defining continuum beliefs and equilibrium, and deciding what computational task to study. Those choices may produce worthwhile research; they do not turn Proposition 1 into a computational result of this paper. Both candidate anchors therefore fail for the same underlying reason.

I would not claim that no plausible continuum extension exists. For example, a public decision based on proportions of citizens holding different signals could motivate one, if suitable mass versions of NCKS and ID were developed. The negative case is weaker on the absolute claim that no such scenario could ever be worthwhile. Its stronger, narrower conclusion is that any such project would be a new continuous implementation theory with a newly supplied computational question—not a continuous computational mirror of either result this paper proves.
