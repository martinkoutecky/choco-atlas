The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and directed balanced FEN relations \(q_{ts}\in\{-1,0,+1\}\), decide whether there exists a partition into finitely many coalition blocks \(K\), represented by masses \(x_{tK}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_K x_{tK}=\mu_t\), such that no move transfers \(0<\varepsilon<x_{tK}\) of type \(t\) from \(K\) to another block or a new block and yields a positive signed mass vote for every sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\). Each resident votes according to the sign of their balanced-utility change, and moved mass compares its utility in the destination with its utility in the source.

The model it lives in

A continuum of role-types with mass \(\mu_t\) and directed relation table \(q_{ts}\); coalitions are mass allocations \(x_{tK}\), type-\(s\) utility in block \(K\) is \(u_s(K)=\sum_t q_{st}x_{tK}\), and deviations transfer an arbitrarily small positive mass of one type between blocks.

The objection that survived

The type-pair relation matrix imposes dense, role-uniform interactions unlike the paper’s sparse, identity-specific network examples, and an infinitesimal cohort switch is not literally a named-agent move.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s existence question for locally popular partitions in balanced FEN games; it leaves the other preference domains, local-stability results, and clustering experiments unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-completeness persist when type mass may be split fractionally among coalition blocks, or does that freedom make existence easier?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuum Balanced Local-Popularity Existence. The anchor is Theorem 9 (⋆): in FEN-B games, deciding whether a locally popular partition exists is NP-complete. The authors prove this result here, by reduction from 3-COLORING; the hardness construction uses asymmetric friendship and enmity relations. This is a useful anchor because it asks about existence of the paper’s central stability notion, and its reduction places combinatorial structure in the agents’ relationships.

A plausible regime is a large online community or federation whose members fall into repeated roles. There might be hundreds of thousands of people but only dozens or hundreds of roles; a person’s friend, enemy, or neutral judgments depend on the other person’s role. This fits the paper’s interpretation of agents as people or data points with local relationships, while making each role a complete type for the model. Individuals still belong wholly to one coalition; the continuum represents the mass of indistinguishable people in each role.

Formally, an instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and a directed relation \(q_{ts}\in\{+1,-1,0\}\) for each ordered pair of types: friend, enemy, or neutral. A coalition partition is described by masses \(x_{tK}\), where \(x_{tK}\) is the amount of type \(t\) in coalition \(K\), and \(\sum_K x_{tK}=\mu_t\). A type-\(t\) member of \(K\) has balanced utility \(u_t(K)=\sum_s q_{ts}x_{sK}\).

The question is whether such a partition exists with no locally dominating mass switch. A switch moves an amount \(\varepsilon>0\) of one type from its current coalition to another coalition or to a new one. Compare every person’s coalition utility before and after the switch; the signed vote is the mass who prefer the new partition minus the mass who prefer the old one, with indifferent people contributing zero. Call the switch locally dominating if this signed vote is positive for every sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\). The requested solution is a mass allocation \(x\) for which no such switch exists. This is a population-continuous version of Theorem 9’s underlying existence problem, not a question about fractional outcomes.

I would initially expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the 3-coloring structure is encoded in the interaction pattern among types, rather than in different prices or preferences for named individuals. The follow-up is to prove that fractional splitting of type mass cannot create spurious locally popular partitions—or to discover that it does and changes the classification. The regime can have many members per type even when \(\tau\) grows with the hard instance.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it keeps balanced FEN preferences, coalition partitions, voter comparisons, and one-type-at-a-time deviations, while expressing the population and the deviation at mass scale. The soft spot is exactly that scaling. Their switch moves one indivisible agent; this mirror tests arbitrarily small mass transfers in a block-homogeneous society. That is a substantive continuum analogue, not a proved equivalence, and the hardness reduction must survive the extra freedom to split mass.

This case covers Theorem 9 only. It does not claim mirrors for the local-stability results, the other preference domains, or the clustering experiments.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 9, and it is a genuine computational result: deciding whether a locally popular partition exists in asymmetric FEN-B games is NP-complete. The strongest objection is about whether its local notion survives a population continuum.

The input is an interaction graph. An agent’s complete type must capture how that agent relates to the other agents, and in a typical sparse network many agents have different neighborhoods. Compressing them into a modest number of roles with relations \(q_{ts}\) assumes those relationships are uniform across whole type pairs. That is a dense, block-structured society. It does not describe the local, identity-specific relationships that motivate the paper’s network and clustering applications.

There is also a mismatch in the deviation. A single person has zero mass in an atomless population, so moving that person changes neither the population distribution nor a population-wide vote. The proposed \(\varepsilon\)-switch avoids this by moving positive mass of one type. But that is a coordinated cohort deviation: even as \(\varepsilon\) becomes small, it can change the utilities of entire source and destination cohorts. Recovering the finite model’s one-person vote by taking a derivative or rescaling the vote margin would add a new stability criterion. For sparse networks, representing who is affected would also require edge or neighborhood information beyond the type-mass matrix.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A large federation whose members share role-based relationships could plausibly have uniform \(q_{ts}\), and its infinitesimal mass-switch question is well-defined. It is a different, dense block-population model with cohort deviations, but the authors could reasonably recognize it as a useful extension of Theorem 9. I therefore cannot honestly support the requested universal negative. The strongest case against is narrower: the paper does not itself motivate this role-uniform regime, and the proposed mirror should not be presented as preserving the paper’s single-agent local-popularity problem.
