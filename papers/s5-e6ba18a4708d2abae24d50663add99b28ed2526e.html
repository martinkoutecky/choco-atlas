The case FOR (proponent)

There is a limited but genuine positive case. The paper’s subject is a committee of many agents whose complete types are consistent judgement vectors. In a large committee, replacing counts of judgement types by masses is natural: for the XOR agenda with \(m-1\) premises, there are only \(\tau=2^{m-1}\) possible types, while the committee may contain millions of interchangeable judges. A type is the full vector \(x\in\mathcal X\), and \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of the committee of that type.

The serious limitation is that the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. It proves no theorem asserting membership in P, NP-hardness, FPT, W[1]-hardness, or a comparable algorithmic classification. Its named results are quantitative structural and impossibility theorems. Thus there is no formally eligible complexity anchor under the stated rubric.

If a structural theorem is accepted provisionally, my strongest lead is Theorem 6.3, proved in this paper. It states that for \(m\ge3\), with agenda
\[ \mathcal X=\left\langle A_1,\ldots,A_{m-1},\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m-1}A_j\right\rangle, \]
for every \(\epsilon<1/6\), every aggregation mechanism \(F\) over \(n\) voters that is both \(\epsilon\)-independent and \(\epsilon\)-consistent is within distance \(m(2m+3)\epsilon\) of some aggregation mechanism \(G\) that is exactly independent and exactly consistent. Here distance is normalized Hamming distance under the paper’s uniform distribution over consistent profiles. This is proved here, using Fourier analysis; it is not merely cited.

A plausible continuous analogue would be the following.

Continuum XOR Mechanism Repair. An instance consists of the XOR agenda \(\mathcal X\), a nonatomic population \(I=[0,1]\), and a society distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal X)\), where \(\mu_x\) is the population mass holding judgement type \(x\). To retain the paper’s approximate-probabilistic viewpoint, the instance may also specify a rational finite-support distribution \(Q\) over possible society distributions \(\mu\), together with a finitely represented aggregation rule \(F\) mapping \(\mu\) to a judgement vector in \(\{0,1\}^m\).

The decision variable is another rule \(G\). It must be population-independent in the sense that, for every issue \(j\), there is a univariate function \(g_j\) with
\[ G_j(\mu)=g_j\!\left(p_j(\mu)\right), \qquad p_j(\mu)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal X:x_j=1}\mu_x, \]
and it must be consistent:
\[ G(\mu)\in\mathcal X \]
for every relevant society \(\mu\). Its objective is to minimize
\[ d_Q(F,G)=\Pr_{\mu\sim Q}[F(\mu)\ne G(\mu)]. \]
The associated decision problem asks whether there exists such a \(G\) with \(d_Q(F,G)\le r\); a solution is an explicit population-level independent, consistent repair rule.

This is recognizably the paper’s approximate-aggregation question after quotienting interchangeable voters by their type masses. The expected direction is tractability for fixed \(m\) and a finite type set: the XOR condition becomes a system of linear relations among values of the one-dimensional functions \(g_j\), and the paper’s Fourier argument suggests a dimension-free repair or testing procedure. With \(m\) variable, the repair problem could acquire hardness from the agenda or the shared marginal coordinates; that would be a separate question rather than a direct transfer of a result in this paper.

The weakest point is substantial. The finite theorem’s exact mechanisms are built from functions of named voter coordinates, including oligarchies and Boolean linear characters. In an atomless anonymous population, an individual has zero mass, and parity over an arbitrary coalition of individuals has no direct analogue. If one retains named positive-mass blocs to recover those mechanisms, one risks merely disguising the original finite-voter model. Moreover, the paper’s inconsistency and dependency indices are probabilities over finite profiles; in a genuine continuum, empirical masses concentrate and the original probability space partly collapses.

So the strongest honest conclusion is: the paper offers a credible structural starting point for a continuous-population mirror, especially for large anonymous committees and XOR-style agendas, but it offers no qualifying named computational anchor. Under the programme’s strict grading rule, this is a promising reformulation rather than a fully supported positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection comes before modelling: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results are structural stability and impossibility theorems, not claims of membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, approximation guarantees, parameterized complexity, or algorithms. The property-testing discussion explicitly says that the paper analyses a particular family of tests, rather than solving the query-complexity problem. Thus Theorem 6.3 cannot support a ChoCo mirror under the programme’s anchor rule.

Theorem 6.3 also does not survive as the same question over a population distribution. Its input is a mechanism
\[ F:\mathcal X^n\to\{0,1\}^m, \]
where \(F_j\) may depend on the entire named-voter column \(X^j\). Independence means independence across issues, not anonymity with respect to voters. Replacing a profile by its type-mass vector \(\mu\) therefore loses information that the theorem permits \(F\) to use.

For example, take the XOR agenda \(\langle A,B,A\oplus B\rangle\), with consistent types \(u=000\) and \(v=110\). The two profiles
\[ (u,v)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(v,u) \]
have the same population distribution: half the voters are type \(u\), half type \(v\). But the dictatorship of voter \(1\),
\[ F_j(X)=X^j_1, \]
is exactly independent and exactly consistent, while returning \(u\) on the first profile and \(v\) on the second. No function of \(\mu\), nor of the issue marginals \(p_j(\mu)\), can represent it.

This is not merely a defect of the proponent’s particular \(g_j(p_j)\) formulation. The Fourier proof itself identifies the problem: the relevant exact mechanisms include dictatorships, oligarchies, and parity characters on coalitions of named voters. To preserve those mechanisms, voter identity or role must be included in the type. Then the “type space” contains one type per role, each with mass \(1/n\); in an atomless limit every individual role has zero mass. That is either the finite model in disguise or a continuum in which the theorem’s mechanisms disappear. Parity over an atomless coalition has no canonical mass-level analogue.

The proposed distribution \(Q\) over society distributions does not repair this. The paper’s probability space is the uniform product distribution over profiles in \(\mathcal X^n\). Its inconsistency and distance measures distinguish profiles that have the same histogram. Pushing that measure forward to empirical distributions destroys precisely the information on which \(F\) may depend. If one instead introduces an arbitrary \(Q\) over mass vectors, that is a new stochastic model, not the continuous form of the paper’s measure.

The strongest rescue would restrict attention to anonymous mechanisms, so that \(F_j\) genuinely factors through the mass \(p_j(\mu)\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity problem, but it changes the theorem’s central hypothesis. One would need a new representation theorem, a new approximation theorem, and an input model for the functions \(g_j\). The paper proves none of these. With finite-support \(Q\), the resulting repair task may reduce to assigning values on finitely many observed marginal points; with universal quantification over all \(\mu\), the representation and computational model become unspecified. Either way, it is an original anonymous aggregation problem rather than a continuization of Theorem 6.3.

The same objection applies to Theorems 6.1, 7.1, 7.3, and Corollary 6.4. They concern Boolean functions on named finite coordinates and probabilistic distance over finite profile spaces. Large committees with repeated judgement types are certainly a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but the paper’s mechanisms are not functions of judgement-type multiplicities. The population story is natural; the theorem-to-mass-space bridge is missing.

The absolute claim that no related continuous aggregation problem could ever be worthwhile is too strong as a modelling claim. An anonymous XOR-repair problem could be interesting. But it would be a new ChoCo problem without a computational result from this paper to mirror. Under the programme’s stated gate, the correct negative verdict is therefore strong: no qualifying anchor survives, and the only plausible population formulation changes the object whose structure the paper actually proves.
