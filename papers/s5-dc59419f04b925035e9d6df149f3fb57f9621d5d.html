The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is the underlying STV margin problem. A type is a ballot ranking \(s\); mass \(\mu_s\) is the fraction of voters with that ranking. For a large election with many voters but a comparatively small set of distinct rankings, a mirror would take a rational distribution \(\mu\), a fixed seat count and tie-breaking rule, and its current WIGM winner set \(W\). It would choose transfers \(x_{s,t}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_t x_{s,t}\leq\mu_s\), to form \(\mu'_s=\mu_s-\sum_t x_{s,t}+\sum_t x_{t,s}\). The objective is to minimize \(\sum_{s\ne t}x_{s,t}\), subject to WIGM on \(\mu'\), using continuum quota \(1/(N+1)\) for \(N\) seats, producing a winner set different from \(W\). A solution is an optimal transfer and the resulting altered society. That asks the same substantive question as the paper’s margin: how much of the electorate must change its ballot to change who wins. The paper’s large elections make the scenario credible, though it does not report their ranking support sizes.

But this is not an anchored positive case under your requirements. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. It reports empirical lower bounds and runtimes, and gives a MINLP formulation in Appendix B that its algorithm uses, but neither is a qualifying numbered result. So there is no anchor number to quote, restate, or assign a continuous problem to.

That missing anchor is also the weakest point: the proposed mirror is plausible, but the paper does not establish a complexity classification for it or for the lower-bound computation it improves. The mirror’s tractability remains an open question; its finite election orders and WIGM transfer rules leave substantial combinatorial structure even when ballot counts become masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only proposed anchor is the STV margin. The paper gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational result for it. It reports improved lower bounds and running times on particular elections, and gives a MINLP formulation used by the algorithm. Neither yields a result-level claim to continuize.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is coherent: represent ballot rankings by types, let \(\mu\) give their population shares, and minimize the mass whose rankings must change to alter the WIGM winners. But that asks a new optimization question; it does not transport a computational result established in this paper. The paper does not claim a complexity classification or prove a general algorithmic guarantee whose continuous counterpart could be compared with it. The proponent’s own concession therefore defeats the mirror as an anchor.

That is the honest limit of the negative case. I cannot establish that this mass-based STV margin question would be worthless as independent research: for large electorates with repeated rankings, it is a sensible question. The case against is that it is not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.
