The case FOR (proponent)

There is no honest computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results—Proposition 1, Corollaries 1–3, Lemma 1, and Propositions 2–3—prove structural facts about stability, orders, and ample systems. The final theorem, unnumbered in the text, proves that stable systems form a nonempty complete lattice. None asserts a complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The finite-\(E\) iteration is said to terminate, but the paper gives no complexity bound for it. So there are zero anchors of the kind your brief requires.

The best positive lead is a new extension, not a mirror of a named computational result: a large labor market with worker and firm cohorts grouped by complete contract preferences and choice behavior, represented by type masses, and stable allocations represented by flows of contract mass. That scenario is plausible for high multiplicity, and the paper’s stable-contract framework is relevant. But the paper’s own aggregation to two agents makes population size less central, and defining stability for fractional flows would add modeling choices the paper does not make. Its section on graduated contracts concerns contract levels, not a continuous population.

I would not present that extension as an anchored continuous mirror. The honest positive case is limited to saying that the paper suggests a plausible direction for a follow-on model; it supplies no named computational result for this task to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its propositions, corollaries, lemma, and complete-lattice theorem establish existence and order structure for stable systems. The finite-\(E\) iteration gives a construction, but the paper specifies neither an input representation for the choice functions nor a complexity bound. Its main theorem also has no population-size parameter: the framework has two aggregate choice operators, \(F\) and \(W\), regardless of how many people a market story might describe.

The strongest proposed extension is a large labor market with worker and firm types, each assigned a mass, and stable allocations represented by flows of contracts. A careful version could retain each type’s full choice behavior, add type capacities, and define blocking in terms of positive mass. That is a plausible research direction, but the paper provides no such model. It would need new rules for how a type chooses fractional contract flows, how worker and firm masses are coupled, and what counts as a block. The paper’s lattice result supplies no direct answer to those questions. Aggregating the new model back into \(F\) and \(W\) would remove the type-level masses and matching information that made the extension interesting.

Section 6 does not bridge that gap: graduated contracts change the contract space, while the agents remain governed by choice functions. A mass-flow model could be worth studying on its own, but it would add a new computational problem rather than continue one this paper analyzed.

So the negative case is strong for the task’s standard: there is no worthwhile *mirror of a computational result in this paper*, because there is no computational result to mirror. The broader claim that no related continuous-market project could ever be worthwhile is harder to defend; the proposed labor-market model is plausible as a separate project. The case is weakest precisely at that boundary.
