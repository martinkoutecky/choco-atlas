The continuous mirror question

Given \(q\), a finite support \(T\) of complete interval-valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and cake supply scaled per capita, find \(q\) mutually \(\varepsilon\)-near \(q\)-piece cut vectors \(X^{(r)}\) and mass assignments \(x_{t,r,j}\geq 0\). Require \(\sum_{r,j}x_{t,r,j}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,r}x_{t,r,j}=1/q\), and require every positive assignment to give type \(t\) a most-preferred piece under cut \(X^{(r)}\); determine the complexity in \(q\), \(|T|\), input bit length, and precision.

The model it lives in

A population distributed over complete interval-valuation types, with mass \(\mu_t\) per type and per-capita-scaled cake supply. The decisions are nearby cut vectors and a mass transport from types to whole piece indices; each piece index serves mass \(1/q\), and assigned mass must receive a most-preferred piece.

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s PPAD-completeness result for approximate envy-free cuts. It leaves aside the oracle query bounds, including Theorem 4, and the three-player monotone-utility algorithm in Theorem 5.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does PPAD-hardness persist when the type support is small relative to \(q\)?
  • What scaled utility and resource representation captures one whole parcel per person as \(q\) grows?
  • Can arbitrary rational type masses always be assigned envy-freely in the limiting model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s approximate envy-free cut problem, where repeated valuation profiles form large cohorts.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that finding an approximate envy-free allocation using \(d\) cuts for \(d+1\) people is PPAD-complete. The paper’s discrete approximation asks for \(d+1\) nearby cut vectors and a permutation assigning each player a different piece index, such that each player prefers their assigned piece in at least one of those cuts. The paper proves membership using Sperner’s problem and proves hardness by reduction from 2D BROUWER. The BROUWER hardness it reduces from is cited; Theorem 3 itself is proved here.

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study. Call it Mass Envy-Free Cake Cutting. Let \(q=d+1\). An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and one interval-utility function \(u_t\) per type, represented as in the paper. Let \(q\mu_t\) be an integer for each type, so the instance has a discrete \(q\)-person counterpart: \(q\mu_t\) people of type \(t\). A solution consists of \(q\) nearby \(q\)-piece cut vectors \(X^{(0)},\ldots,X^{(q-1)}\), plus masses \(x_{t,r,j}\geq 0\). Here \(x_{t,r,j}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned piece index \(j\) using cut \(X^{(r)}\). Require

\[ \sum_{r,j}x_{t,r,j}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t,r}x_{t,r,j}=\frac1q \quad\text{for every piece index }j. \]

Whenever \(x_{t,r,j}>0\), piece \(j\) must be a most-preferred piece for type \(t\) under cut \(X^{(r)}\). The cuts must be adjacent at the chosen grid resolution, as in the paper. In the exact limiting version, the cuts coalesce to one partition; each type is assigned only to pieces it values maximally, and each piece index serves mass \(1/q\).

This is a direct mass relaxation of the paper’s assignment rule. In a discrete \(q\)-person instance, each person has mass \(1/q\), and the paper’s permutation gives each piece index exactly one person. Aggregating those assignments by type gives the \(x\) variables above, with entries in multiples of \(1/q\). The mirror allows arbitrary mass assignments, so people with the same complete valuation can be split across pieces when those pieces tie or are sufficiently close in value. The question is the envy-free allocation itself, not an encoding for it.

A plausible regime is a large population receiving individual plots or portions of a resource, where many people share the same complete valuation over locations and goods. For example, residents may fall into a small number of preference profiles over a land allotment whose supply scales with the population. A type records everything relevant to valuation, and \(\mu_t\) is its population fraction. The intended regime has \(q\) large and \(\tau=|T|\) much smaller than \(q\). The interval cake is already continuous in the paper; the mirror’s continuization is the population distribution over valuation types.

I would provisionally expect hardness to transfer, because the paper’s fixed-point difficulty lies in the cut simplex and the valuation-based preference map, which remain in this mirror. The mass assignment is a transportation problem once the cuts are fixed. But Theorem 3 does not prove hardness for this version: the reduction must withstand fractional splitting of repeated types. The particularly interesting follow-up is whether hardness survives when \(\tau\ll q\), or whether aggregation makes that regime tractable. Other questions include whether arbitrary rational masses always admit a solution, and what complexity bound is possible in \(q,\tau,L\), and the grid precision.

My weakest point is the change in how a piece is assigned. In the paper, each piece goes to one individual; here, a continuum of mass may be assigned across piece indices. That is a natural high-multiplicity relaxation if the mass can be assigned among people of the same type, but a reader insisting on the paper’s exact single-cake, one-person-per-piece allocation may reject the mirror unless the per-capita resource accounting is made explicit. Also, the paper’s PPAD-hardness result does not establish hardness in the low-support, high-multiplicity regime. The mirror is strongest as a concrete new problem the authors could recognize; its complexity classification remains to be proved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a real computational anchor: it proves PPAD-completeness for approximate envy-free cake cutting. So the negative case cannot rest on the absence of a result. The problem is that its population size also determines the outcome: \(q\) people require \(q\) pieces, \(q-1\) cuts, and a cut description whose dimension grows with \(q\). Its PPAD-hardness already appears with three players. A large population of repeated valuation types would be a new regime, not a continuum relaxation of the instances establishing that theorem.

The proposed mass formulation is coherent for finite \(q\): with \(q\mu_t\) integral and mass \(1/q\) per piece index, it compresses a finite roster of \(q\) people. But it keeps \(q\) explicit in the cuts and assignments. Sending \(q\) to infinity removes the finite list of pieces the problem allocates. On the fixed cake \([0,1]\), pieces for most recipients must shrink toward zero; under the paper’s Lipschitz utilities, their values shrink with their lengths. Rescaling utilities or cake supply to preserve nontrivial per-person values requires a new resource and preference model whose inputs change with \(q\).

A continuum of piece locations could be modeled with a type-to-location assignment, but that changes the object the theorem studies. A positive mass of identical agents would need distinct whole pieces while each envies the others’ pieces; the proposed mass variables do not describe that continuum allocation. Allowing cohorts to share pieces or giving them lottery shares would require new feasibility and envy definitions. Those may be worthwhile fair-division questions, but they need an independent model and complexity statement.

So I would reject this as a worthwhile *mirror of Theorem 3*: its finite version remains a compressed finite-player problem, and its continuum version needs a different allocation model. The negative case is weakest against a large-market land or resource-allocation model with supply scaled per capita. Such a model could be valuable, but the paper’s fixed-cake theorem does not supply it.
