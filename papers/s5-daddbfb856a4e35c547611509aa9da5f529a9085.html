The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) goods, \(N\), and a finite list of complete additive valuation types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each good \(j\) has supply \(1/N\), decide whether there is an allocation assigning each unit of mass an integral bundle \(B\subseteq[m]\) that is ex-post envy-free and attains the maximum social welfare over all supply-feasible allocations; if so, compute one, otherwise report infeasibility. State complexity in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the binary input length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A continuous population of mass \(1\) over complete valuation types, with bundle-assignment variables \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\), type-mass constraints \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), and per-capita goods supplies \(1/N\). Every unit of mass receives an integral bundle; whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{t',B'}>0\), require \(u_t(B)\ge u_t(B')\). The allocation must attain the unrestricted maximum of \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)\).

The objection that survived

The mirror replaces the theorem’s online algorithm on independently generated random utilities with an offline problem over arbitrary valuation-type masses, so it does not carry over the distinctive sampling guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the envy-free and maximum-social-welfare allocation target of Theorem 2.2. It leaves out online arrival, limited utility sampling, the random-instance guarantee, and the paper’s other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether a supply-feasible allocation is both ex-post envy-free and globally welfare-optimal, as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?
  • Does the product-distribution specialization have additional structure that makes the problem tractable when the type distribution is given implicitly?
  • Which hardness results for finite-agent fair allocation survive when type masses can be split across bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is the fair-and-efficient allocation target in Theorem 2.2, which the paper proves. In its discrete-utility setting, each good’s utility distribution has finite support in \([0,1]\), with \(1\) in its support. When \(m=\Omega(n\log n)\), sampling \(s=2\log m/\alpha_{\min}\) agents independently for each arriving good and assigning it to the sampled agent who values it most gives, with high probability as \(n\to\infty\), an online allocation that is both envy-free and maximum-social-welfare. Here \(\alpha_{\min}\) is the lower bound used in the paper on the probability of drawing value \(1\). The proof is in the paper; it relies on sampling a value-\(1\) agent for every good and then bounding envy.

A plausible regime for the population version is a large housing or relief allocation, with many applicants but only a modest number of complete, coarse valuation profiles over a large catalogue of non-identical units. A type is one applicant’s entire additive valuation vector over the goods; its mass is the fraction of applicants with that vector. With \(N\) as the population scale and \(m=\Omega(N\log N)\), each good has per-capita supply \(1/N\). The units remain indivisible for each person, while population masses and supplies are fractional. This is the co-modification needed to make a population limit meaningful. The paper’s theorem does not establish that its random instances have a small number of such types.

My lead question is Type-Mass EF–MSW Allocation. An instance gives \(m\) goods, a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation vectors \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j=1/N\). In the discrete-utility specialization, one can take \(T=\prod_{j=1}^m\Omega_j\), with masses induced by the item distributions. A solution specifies \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(B\subseteq[m]\), such that
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B:\,j\in B}x_{t,B}=q_j. \]
Every person therefore gets a whole bundle, though a type’s mass may be divided among different bundles. Require ex-post envy-freeness: whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{t',B'}>0\), \(u_t(B)\ge u_t(B')\). Among all allocations satisfying the supply constraints, maximize
\[ \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B). \]
The problem is to find an envy-free allocation attaining this unrestricted maximum, or report that none exists. Complexity should be stated in \(m\), the number of listed types \(\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\). This mirrors Theorem 2.2’s EF-plus-global-welfare objective; it does not claim to continuize the theorem’s online information and sampling guarantees themselves.

For this unrestricted problem, I would initially expect hardness to transfer: mass splitting relaxes population counts, but each person still receives an integral bundle, so the item-level combinatorics remain. That is a conjecture, not a result of this paper. Natural follow-ups are whether the product-distribution case is always feasible, whether the problem is tractable when \(\tau\) is small, and whether a continuous solution corresponds to high-probability solutions in finite samples.

The main weakness is that the mirror may be much stricter than the anchor. In the continuous model, envy-freeness must hold for every positive-mass type, including rare valuation profiles that a finite sample may never contain. Also, the product support \(\prod_j\Omega_j\) can be enormous; continuous utility draws usually give agents distinct full valuation vectors. The theorem therefore motivates this population-level question but does not show that it has feasible solutions or a small-\(\tau\) regime. The case for the mirror rests on the recognizability of the underlying problem—allocating indivisible goods to achieve envy-freeness and maximum welfare—and on the plausibility of many people sharing complete coarse valuation profiles in some large allocation settings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2.2 is a genuine computational anchor, but its main contribution is a *query-efficient online algorithm*: it samples \(O(\log m)\) agents for each arriving good instead of inspecting all \(n\), while retaining envy-freeness and maximum welfare with high probability. The proposed type-mass problem drops the online and limited-information features and asks for an offline allocation over bundles. That is a coherent fair-division question, but it no longer mirrors the computation that makes Theorem 2.2 distinctive.

The obstacle to a closer mirror is the paper’s type structure. A type must specify an agent’s complete valuation vector over all \(m\) goods. With non-atomic utility draws, two agents have the same vector with probability zero, so the realized society has \(\tau=n\): aggregation provides no compression. With ordinary nondegenerate finite-support product distributions, the number of possible vectors grows as \(\prod_j |\Omega_j|\), and distinct observed types again become typical as \(m\) grows. Since the theorem’s regime has \(m=\Omega(n\log n)\), explicitly listing the induced type masses can itself be exponentially larger than the paper’s input. A succinct distribution-oracle version avoids that listing, but becomes a different, implicit-input problem over a diffuse type space.

The better population story—many people sharing exact valuation profiles—can describe a sensible high-multiplicity fair-division problem. But to preserve the paper’s regime, it needs exact profiles across a growing catalogue of non-identical goods. The paper’s independent draws do not naturally produce repeated profiles there. Introducing a fixed set of archetypes instead changes the cross-good dependence and the population generator; it may be worth studying on its own, but it does not carry over the paper’s sampling result or its random-instance model. Grouping agents by rankings or coarse valuations does not fix this: the paper’s envy and welfare comparisons use the full cardinal vectors.

So the negative case is strongest against this paper as a continuization target, not against high-multiplicity fair division generally. The archetype variant remains plausible, but the connection to the paper’s central result is thin: its theorem exploits repeated sampling from a random utility model precisely where exact complete types generally fail to repeat.
