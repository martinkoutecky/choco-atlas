The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The article has no computational theorem, so it cannot supply a direct continuous mirror of a proved complexity result. But that does not make the computational question invented from nothing. Section 7.1 presents Borda as a concrete method for estimating comparable welfare differences from preference rankings, and explains its utilitarian interpretation. That gives a specific aggregation procedure to study.

For a population that ranks a finite set of policy states, represent society by a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings. The continuous Borda score is \(B_\mu(x)=\sum_t \mu_t b_t(x)\), where \(b_t(x)\) is state \(x\)’s Borda score for type \(t\). A natural computational question is how much mass, and at what cost, must change its ranking for a target state to become a Borda winner. In large public consultations or policy polls, percentages and population-wide robustness are meaningful quantities; rankings and change costs can be included in each type. This is a high-multiplicity population model, not continuity of outcomes or uncertainty.

That question is an extension of the article’s Borda discussion, not a result it proves, and the distinction is the weak point of the positive case. Still, it is concrete enough to be a worthwhile computational mirror: it studies the robustness of the welfare-estimation procedure the article itself describes, and it fits the programme’s continuous transfer and winner-robustness questions. The missing theorem limits how direct the mirror can be; it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists.
