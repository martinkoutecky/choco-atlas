The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 1.4(c)

The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\), \(R\), an \(R\)-fat estate \(C_0\), a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types with masses \(\mu_t\), and a bounded integrable density \(v_t\) for each type, let \(N=k^d\), choose counts \(n_t(k)\) with \(\sum_t n_t(k)=N\) and \(n_t(k)/N\to\mu_t\), and set \(C_k=kC_0\) with density \(v_{t,k}(x)=v_t(x/k)\). For given \(\alpha,\beta\geq0\), do there exist, for every sufficiently large \(k\), pairwise-disjoint allotments \(X_i^k\subseteq C_k\) such that each type-\(t\) household receives utility at least \(\alpha V_t(C_0)\), and every household weakly prefers its allotment to every other household’s allotment, when utility is evaluated using the best contained \(Rk^\beta\)-fat plot?

The model it lives in

An asymptotic family of geometric division instances summarized by the limiting type distribution \(\mu\), with land supply scaled per capita, ordinary plots assigned to each household, and envy checked between actual allotments.

The objection that survived

The witnesses remain finite \(N\)-agent partitions with individual envy comparisons, and the usable-shape bound varies with \(k\); the paper does not itself provide a mass-level allocation over types.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.4(c), the many-agent \(1/n\)-proportionality guarantee and its fatness tradeoff. It leaves the two-agent bounds, relative-proportionality results, and other geometric settings aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What encoding or valuation-access model makes deciding or constructing these allocation families a computational problem?
  • What is the feasible frontier between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), and can finite support size \(\tau\) improve the fatness bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is Theorem 1.4(c), though the paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its strongest anchor is a constructive fair-division result, not a complexity result.

Theorem 1.4(c), proved in this paper: For any \(d\)-dimensional \(R\)-fat land \(C\) and \(n\geq2\) agents, if usable plots may be \(\lceil n^{1/d}\rceil R\)-fat, there is an envy-free allocation giving every agent at least \(1/n\) of their total land value. The theorem states this guarantee is exact: \(\prope(C,n,S)=1/n\). Its construction uses knife-tuples and the paper’s allocation lemmas.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large housing development whose households fall into a small number of valuation types: for example, types that prioritize different soil, water, or transit features. Each type is a complete valuation description, so members of a type have the same density and geometric preferences. Let the number of households grow while the number \(\tau\) of types stays fixed, with type frequencies approaching a distribution \(\mu\). The land bank grows with the population so that per-household land and value remain meaningful.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Asymptotic Typed Fat-Land Division. An instance consists of \(d\), \(R\), a base \(R\)-fat estate \(C_0\), a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu\), and a bounded integrable value density \(v_t\) for each type. For each integer \(k\), take \(N=k^d\) households with type counts whose proportions converge to \(\mu\), and a homothetically scaled estate \(C_k=kC_0\), with density \(v_{t,k}(x)=v_t(x/k)\). Thus \(V_{t,k}(C_k)=N V_t(C_0)\).

Given targets \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), the question is whether there is, for every sufficiently large \(k\), a pairwise-disjoint allocation \(X_1^k,\ldots,X_N^k\subseteq C_k\) such that each household’s effective utility is evaluated exactly as in the paper, using the best \(Rk^\beta\)-fat usable plot inside its allotment. The allocation must be envy-free, and a type-\(t\) household must receive utility at least \(\alpha V_t(C_0)=\alpha V_{t,k}(C_k)/N\). A solution is a family of such finite allocations, or a uniform rule producing them. Each household still gets an ordinary geometric allotment; only the type frequencies are represented by mass.

Theorem 1.4(c) gives a positive answer at \((\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)\): for \(N=k^d\), its fatness bound is \(Rk\), and its proportionality guarantee is precisely the per-household benchmark \(V_{t,k}(C_k)/N\). This mirrors the theorem’s underlying allocation problem rather than treating continuous land as the new contribution. The new question is how the guarantee and required plot shape behave as the population becomes a distribution over repeated types.

I would expect the endpoint \((1,1)\) to be Class A in the programme’s structural sense: the paper’s geometric construction already establishes feasibility there. That is not a polynomial-time claim. The many-agent proof proceeds through a limiting Sperner construction, and the paper gives no bit-complexity analysis. Further questions include whether finite type support improves the shape bound, what the optimal \(\alpha\)-versus-\(\beta\) frontier is, and what finite-precision guarantees can be computed from an explicit encoding of the densities.

The authors would likely recognize this as their problem: the agents still value usable geometric plots through value densities, envy is still compared between allotted regions, and the result still trades fairness against plot shape. The \(Rk\) allowance also makes the tradeoff visible: as population grows, plots may become increasingly thin.

The weak point is the scaling premise. On one fixed estate, an atomless population cannot each receive a positive-area disjoint plot. Scaling the estate with the number of households makes the finite instances coherent, but the resulting limit is an asymptotic family of allocations, not a literal division of one fixed estate among a continuum of people. And if the required anchor must be a standard complexity-class theorem, this paper supplies none; its constructive existence theorem is the strongest honest foothold. My case covers Theorem 1.4(c), not the paper’s two-agent constants, relative-proportionality results, or strategic questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch in the paper’s results: none is a computational-complexity result. Theorem 1.4(c) proves an existence guarantee and gives a construction; it does not classify the runtime or complexity of a computational problem. Its inputs may be arbitrary Borel lands and bounded integrable value densities, with no finite encoding or access model. And the many-agent construction uses an infinite Sperner refinement. A ChoCo-style complexity question would first need to specify how lands and valuations are represented, what queries are allowed, and what counts as an approximate output. That could be worthwhile work, but it is not a complexity result from this paper being mirrored.

The proponent’s household scenario is plausible, and repeated valuation types support a high-multiplicity regime. The obstacle is what the theorem allocates: one disjoint geometric plot to each named household, with envy checked against every other household’s actual plot. A type mass and an aggregate bundle do not preserve that condition. For example, an average utility for a type can conceal that some members receive much worse plots than others. To retain the theorem’s guarantee, the model must keep individual plots and individual comparisons, so each finite instance still has \(N\) agents and \(N\) allocations.

A literal atomless limit does not fix this. In a finite land estate, at most countably many pairwise-disjoint allotments can have positive area. Almost every agent in a continuum population would therefore receive a null allotment, whose utility is zero under the paper’s full-dimensional usable-piece model. The per-agent benchmark also tends to zero as \(N\) grows, leaving a vacuous limit. Scaling the estate with \(N\) avoids that collapse, but then the limit must preserve the fine-scale parcel structure that the type distribution \(\mu\) omits.

The proposed scaling is the strongest repair, but it remains a sequence of finite instances: \(\mu\) records limiting type frequencies while each allocation and envy comparison is still finite. It also lets the fatness bound grow as \(Rk\); as \(k\) grows, there is no fixed family of usable shapes in the limit. This does not invalidate the finite problems. It means the proposed limit does not retain a fixed version of the paper’s shape-constrained allocation model. Replicating local estates or introducing a distribution over parcel assignments might preserve useful geometry, but then one must define how actual envy-free allocations relate to aggregate or randomized assignments. That is a new model and computational objective, not a continuous-population formulation supplied by Theorem 1.4(c).

So I would reject this anchor as a ChoCo mirror of a computational result. The negative case is strongest against the particular theorem and its proposed scaling; it is not a proof that no valuable high-multiplicity fair-division problem can be built. A finite encoding of geometric parcels and type frequencies could support one, but this paper leaves those choices—and the resulting computational question—to future work.
