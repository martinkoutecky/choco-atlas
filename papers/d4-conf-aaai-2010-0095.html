The continuous mirror question

Given \(2^r\) candidates, a target \(c^\star\), a fixed tie-breaking order, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings, orient each candidate pair toward its majority-preferred candidate, resolving ties by the order. If every candidate that defeats \(c^\star\) has outdegree at most \(c^\star\)’s outdegree, construct a balanced single-elimination bracket in which \(c^\star\) wins.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types, with \(\tau\) supported types and binary rational masses. The masses determine pairwise-majority outcomes; candidates and brackets remain discrete, and the output is a bracket rather than an optimized mass transfer.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3’s promised structural case for electorate-induced majority tournaments. It does not cover Theorem 1’s arbitrary missing-match graphs; Theorem 2 concerns complete binary arborescences, not this binomial-arborescence anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • For unrestricted rational ranking distributions \(\mu\), can one decide in polynomial time whether any balanced bracket elects \(c^\star\), or is this problem NP-hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is an electorate-level version of the paper’s cup-voting problem. The contestants remain discrete; the population being continuized is the electorate whose pairwise majorities decide the matches. That fits the paper’s voting interpretation directly: it describes candidates competing by majority comparisons along a binary voting tree.

Call the lead problem Dominating-Losers Continuous Agenda Bracketing. Its input is a candidate set \(C\) with \(|C|=2^r\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) strict rankings, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a fixed tie-breaking order, and a target candidate \(c^\star\). For each pair \(c,d\), orient the edge toward the candidate preferred by more than half the mass; use the tie-breaker on equal masses. Write \(d_\mu(c)\) for the number of candidates \(c\) defeats. The input is promised to satisfy
\[ d_\mu(d)\le d_\mu(c^\star) \quad\text{for every }d\text{ that defeats }c^\star. \]
The task is to produce a balanced single-elimination bracket, using every candidate once, in which \(c^\star\) wins. A solution is the bracket itself. A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballots: \(n\) voters but only \(\tau\ll n\) distinct rankings. Each ranking type is a complete preference description, and its mass is its fraction of the electorate.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: for a tournament graph \(G=(V,E)\), if every in-neighbor \(v\) of \(A\) has \(\deg_{\mathrm{out}}(v)\le \deg_{\mathrm{out}}(A)\), then a binomial spanning arborescence rooted at \(A\) can be constructed in polynomial time. The paper identifies that arborescence with a balanced single-elimination tournament won by \(A\). Applying the theorem to the tournament induced by \(\mu\) gives the promised bracket. Constructing the pairwise-majority graph takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the bit length of the masses, so this promised continuous problem is tractable in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).

This is a plausible analogue because the society is explicitly a distribution over voter types, while the question remains whether agenda control can make the target win. The distribution changes the pairwise outcomes; it does not fractionalize candidates or brackets. The result therefore mirrors a real constructive agenda-control theorem, though only on the stated structural regime.

My case covers Theorem 3, not the paper’s general NP-completeness result. Theorem 1 concerns arbitrary directed graphs where missing edges make some matches unavailable; a voter distribution with pairwise-majority comparisons instead induces a complete tournament. The paper itself says hardness for balanced tournament fixing on unweighted tournament graphs remains open, so I would not claim that Theorem 1 supplies a hardness anchor for this mirror.

The weak point is that this is a restricted tractable slice, and the algorithmic content comes from the theorem’s graph structure, not from continuous optimization. It establishes a sensible continuous-population input and a concrete constructive question, but does not settle unrestricted agenda control. The next questions are whether the unrestricted target-win problem on distributions \(\mu\) is tractable, and how the set of distributions supporting a winning bracket changes as \(\mu\) moves through the preference simplex.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about payoff, not legitimacy. In the proposed Theorem 3 mirror, the ranking masses matter only through the signs of the pairwise majority margins
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[c\succ_t d]-\mathbf{1}[d\succ_t c]\bigr). \]
Once those signs are known, the degree condition and the bracket construction are exactly the graph problem already solved in the paper. The continuous input adds a representation and preprocessing step; the algorithm does not optimize over population mass. That makes this a thin ChoCo contribution if the aim is to bring continuous optimization into reach. But it is only a case for prioritizing other papers: under the programme’s stated criteria, a tractable or already answered mirror is still a valid mirror.

The proposed Theorem 3 anchor itself survives scrutiny. Voter rankings are complete types, repeated ballots are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the electorate’s aggregate determines the match outcomes. The fact that candidates remain discrete does not invalidate population continuization. Nor can I dismiss the mirror because its bracket is unchanged by scaling the electorate: the distribution still determines which tournament the agenda designer faces.

A stronger mirror asks, for an unrestricted rational ranking distribution \(\mu\) and target \(c^\star\), whether any balanced bracket elects \(c^\star\). That is the paper’s tournament agenda-control problem on the majority tournament induced by \(\mu\), and the paper identifies this problem as open. The paper also gives a more direct hardness anchor: Theorem 2 proves NP-completeness for complete binary arborescences on tournament graphs. Any tournament can be realized as the pairwise-majority relation of a strict-ranking profile with polynomial support and rational masses, so that hardness carries to a continuous-electorate input. Theorem 3 then gives a tractable structural regime within this broader problem.

The arbitrary-digraph Theorem 1 is a weaker fit: voter majorities produce a complete tournament, so its missing-match restrictions need an additional model feature. Still, that does not affect the electorate mirrors of Theorems 2 and 3. My negative case is therefore weak: the mirror may offer little new continuous-optimization machinery, but I cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
