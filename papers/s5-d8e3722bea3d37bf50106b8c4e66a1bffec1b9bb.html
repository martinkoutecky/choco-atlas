What the mirror covers

The proposed problem applies Theorem 4's all-pairs outcome comparison to cohort ballot shifts; it does not carry over the paper's single-voter better-reply result in Lemma 1 or Proposition 2 as such.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a cohort version of plurality better replies. There is an important qualification: the paper names no theorem asserting an NP-hardness or complexity-class result. Its closest computational foothold is Theorem 4, proved here. The theorem says that for any strict ranking \(Q\) and outcome sets \(X,Y\subseteq C\), these are equivalent: \(X\) stochastically dominates \(Y\) under uniform tie-breaking; \(X\succ Y\) follows from \(Q\), axioms K+MD+R, and transitivity; every utility function consistent with \(Q\) gives \(X\) higher utility than \(Y\); and \(X\) match-dominates \(Y\). For the last condition, if \(|X|=k\le K=|Y|\), rank both sets from least to most preferred and partition \(Y\) into \(k\) consecutive blocks \(Y_j\) of sizes \(\lceil jK/k\rceil-\lceil (j-1)K/k\rceil\). Each \(x_j\) must be at least as preferred as every member of \(Y_j\), with strictness if any comparison is strict or \(K\bmod k\ne0\). If \(|X|>|Y|\), reverse the ranking and swap the sets. This gives a direct polynomial-time test for the underlying outcome-comparison problem, though the paper does not state a complexity bound.

My lead mirror is Continuous Plurality Cohort Better-Reply. An instance has candidates \(C\), a finite support of types \(t\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type is a pair \((Q_t,a_t)\): a complete ranking and the type’s current plurality ballot. The instance also designates a type \(t^\star=(Q^\star,a^\star)\). Let \(W\) be the current plurality winner set. The question is whether there are a destination ballot \(b\ne a^\star\) and a rational mass \(x\in(0,\mu_{t^\star}]\) such that moving mass \(x\) of type \(t^\star\) from \(a^\star\) to \(b\) produces a new winner set \(W'\) that strictly stochastically dominates \(W\) for \(Q^\star\), under uniform tie-breaking. A solution is the witness \((b,x)\); a “no” instance has no such pair. Theorem 4 supplies the exact test of \(W'\succ_{\mathrm{SD},Q^\star}W\).

A plausible regime is a very large recurring election in an association or union, with a modest candidate slate and many voters concentrated in a relatively small number \(\tau\) of preference-and-ballot types. The mass is the fraction of voters in each type; \(n\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). The continuous decision variable captures a cohort’s fractional shift without imposing one-person granularity. This is a population model, not a claim that the paper’s random tie-breaking lottery itself is a continuous population.

This version looks tractable. For a fixed destination \(b\), only the plurality scores of \(a^\star\) and \(b\) vary with \(x\). The winner set changes only at finitely many score-crossing values. Enumerating those values and one rational point in each interval, then applying Theorem 4’s match-domination test, decides the question in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). I would expect Class A. Natural next questions include minimizing the mass needed for improvement and allowing several types to move while requiring every participating type to improve.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors the paper’s preference comparison over plurality winner sets and applies that comparison to a continuous population. It does not continuize the paper’s axioms or its lottery; it leaves other results and outcome rules aside.

The weak point is also the main qualification. The paper studies a single voter’s better reply. In a continuum, one voter has zero mass and cannot change the winner set, so this mirror gives a positive mass cohort a coordinated move. That is a natural high-multiplicity extension—members of the cohort share the ranking and current ballot, and all prefer the resulting outcome—but the authors could reasonably regard it as a coalition problem rather than their original unilateral one. And although Theorem 4 yields a polynomial comparison test, it is not itself a named complexity theorem. So this is a defensible, bounded positive case, not a direct continuum translation of a stated hardness or algorithmic result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4 is not a computational result. It characterizes when one finite outcome set stochastically dominates another, using match-domination and the axioms K+MD+R. It has no population input, mass vector, algorithmic task, or complexity claim. Lemma 1 and Proposition 2 connect that preference relation to better replies after a *single voter* changes their ballot, but they also give characterizations, not a complexity result. So the proposed cohort problem does not mirror a computational result in this paper; it adds a new aggregate-action problem and uses Theorem 4 to evaluate its outcomes.

There is a real continuum obstruction to the paper’s own better-reply notion. A single voter has zero mass, so changing that voter’s ballot leaves the plurality scores and winner set unchanged. The strict improvement the paper studies disappears. The proponent repairs this by letting a positive mass \(x\) of one type move together. That is coherent, and repeated elections with large blocs make a plausible setting for it, but the actor is now a coordinated cohort rather than a voter making a unilateral move. The same ranking across the cohort makes their preferences align; it does not supply the coordination or change the fact that the paper’s result concerns unilateral replies.

The best repair of that repair would be to ask for the minimum mass of a type, or a collection of types, whose coordinated ballot changes produce a strictly preferred winner set. That could be a sensible new problem. But its central question is now which transfers are feasible and what mass they require; Theorem 4 supplies only the endpoint preference test. In a one-type move, the plurality scores change as \(s_a-x\) and \(s_b+x\), so the population part is a threshold calculation over winner-set changes. That may be useful, but it does not uncover a complexity question the paper itself posed.

The preference extension alone does not provide another population mirror: it compares outcomes independently of who constitutes the society, and making the lottery or outcome space continuous would continuize the wrong object. High multiplicity is not an objection here—many voters sharing ballot and preference types is plausible. The problem is that the paper supplies no computational result about that regime to carry over.

I can make a strong case against calling the cohort question a continuous mirror of this paper. I cannot honestly establish the stronger universal claim that it is not worth ChoCo’s time: the cohort problem is well-posed and could be worth studying as a new collective-manipulation problem. That is where the negative case is weakest, and a reader could reasonably decide the proposed mirror survives on that basis.
