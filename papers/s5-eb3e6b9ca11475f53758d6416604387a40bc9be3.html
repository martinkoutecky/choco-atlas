The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying anchor in this paper. Its named results are analytical: Theorem 1, proved here, says that a Bayesian agent learns the true state almost surely when every false state has a signal distribution at positive KL divergence from the truth; Corollary 1 describes the limiting beliefs when some states are observationally equivalent. Neither is a result about computational complexity. The paper says repeated Bayesian inference in networks can become computationally intractable, but it gives no named hardness or algorithmic result establishing that claim.

The most plausible continuous direction would assign mass to agents with identical signal structures and interaction roles, then study their belief trajectories. But the paper’s updates depend on individual private signals and paths through a finite directed graph. A type-level model would need to preserve that history-dependent variation; making type include each agent’s full network position risks losing the high-multiplicity regime. And a question about convergence or learning rate would remain analytic unless paired with a computational task.

So I can’t honestly give a continuous computational problem per anchor: the paper contains no named computational result to mirror. A continuum-population extension may be worth studying, but presenting it as a mirror of this paper’s computational result would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest ground is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 characterize learning and limiting beliefs; the circle and tree results give convergence rates. The paper says Bayesian inference on networks can become computationally intractable, but it neither defines a computational problem nor proves a complexity or algorithmic result about it.

The best population version of Theorem 1 would give mass to cohorts with identical signal structures. That produces a sensible analytic question about which states the population can distinguish. But with finite support, the relevant KL divergences are weighted sums over types. Turning that into a complexity question—say, choosing signal structures or searching for a signal threshold—would add an optimization problem the paper does not pose. The same issue applies to the circle and tree results. One could replicate identical network motifs and give each node role and signal structure its own type. But the paper’s rates depend on the specific path or root circle. A population mass over signal structures alone loses that wiring; adding motif roles and edge relationships preserves it, but then the results apply separately to each motif.

The strongest rescue is to take the paper’s intractability motivation as the anchor and ask for the complexity of exact Bayesian inference in a high-multiplicity network. Yet agents with the same structural type receive different private signals and develop different beliefs. To compute an individual’s exact posterior, a type must retain the relevant realized history and network context; aggregating those away changes the inference task. A distribution over histories might support a new population-level inference problem, but the paper supplies no such objective or result to mirror.

So the negative case is strong for this paper, though not a proof that no useful follow-on question exists. Repeated cohorts could support new computational work on inference or network design. The weakness is that those would be new problems built from the model’s ingredients, rather than continuous mirrors of the paper’s computational contribution: it has none.
