The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Propositions 1–9 are axiomatic characterizations of group utility functions and coalition influence; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. So I can’t honestly provide one continuous problem per qualifying anchor.

The closest result is Proposition 7, proved here: for a group expected utility function that is a normalized weighted sum of individuals’ utilities, a coalition sharing a preferred winner can impose that winner exactly when its members’ weights sum to more than \(1/2\). One can imagine a population version with types carrying preferences and influence weights, mass \(\mu\), and aggregate utility \(U(a)=\int w\,u(a)\,d\mu\); the corresponding coalition condition would use the integral of \(w\) over the coalition. But that is a continuum restatement of an axiomatic threshold, not a computational question studied by the paper. Turning it into an optimization or complexity problem would introduce a new problem without a named computational result here to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is the paper’s own result structure: none of Propositions 1–9 classifies a computational problem. They characterize which group utility functions satisfy axioms and when a specified individual or coalition can impose an outcome. The paper gives no algorithmic input model or computational task whose complexity a population limit could change. That is a decisive scope problem for this programme.

Proposition 7 is the closest candidate. In a continuum model with normalized influence weights, one could write \(U(a)=\int w(t)u_t(a)\,d\mu(t)\). A coalition \(S\) with a common preferred winner can impose it when \(\int_S w(t)\,d\mu(t)>1/2\); the weak-winner version uses \(\ge 1/2\). This is a sound measure-theoretic restatement of the paper’s axiomatic threshold. In an atomless society, the single-person conditions become vacuous, while the coalition condition remains meaningful. Neither change creates a computational result.

A stronger proposal would ask for the smallest population mass that can impose a winner, or for the most powerful coalition available in a given society. That is a coherent new optimization problem. But the paper asks whether a specified coalition can impose a winner; it does not define coalition selection as an objective. Making that a computational question requires choices the paper leaves open, such as how coalition members are selected, what their participation costs are, and how their reports are represented. The resulting problem might be worth studying on its own, but it would not be a computational mirror of a result in this paper. The same issue applies to the other propositions: continuizing their axioms and thresholds does not supply the missing computational question.

This negative case has a clear limit: a continuous model of coalition influence could be useful adjacent research. The case against is that the article provides no computational result for that project to mirror, so its value cannot rescue this paper as a ChoCo continuization target.
