The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying computational anchor. It contains no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest results are about properties of the debate protocol: Lemma 1 relates edge control to membership in the majority merged system; Proposition 4.3 proves that the merged outcome is reachable under a stated condition on edge control; and Proposition 4.4 studies the effect of reinforcement moves. These are substantive results, but they do not assert the computational complexity of a problem. So there are zero anchors under your rule, and no anchor-based continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The most plausible follow-up mirror would treat each agent’s complete attack relation on a shared finite argument set \(A\) as their type. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\tau\) supported types; an agent’s mass is their fraction of the population. The PRO and CON masses are determined by whether the issue \(d\) belongs to that type’s grounded extension. The merged graph retains an attack when its supporting mass exceeds its opposing mass.

One could then ask: given \(A\), \(d\), and \(\mu\), is there a legal finite schedule of mass moves—relevant assertions or contradictions, respecting the alternating groups and each agent’s move limit—that reaches a stable board whose issue status matches the majority merged system? A solution would be such a schedule. The paper’s Proposition 4.3 suggests replacing its cardinality comparisons with mass comparisons to obtain a sufficient condition for reachability, but that extension is not proved here.

This has a plausible population regime: users on a large online debate platform could share a finite set of arguments and one of relatively few views about which arguments attack which. The main weakness is that the paper’s protocol relies on moves by individual agents and a finite termination argument. Allowing divisible mass to move requires defining new continuous dynamics, so this is a credible research direction, but not a result-backed positive mirror of this paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the paper has no computational-complexity result to continue. Lemma 1 and Propositions 4.3–4.4 concern edge control, outcome reachability, and reinforcement in a debate protocol; none classifies an algorithmic problem. A mass-weighted version of those claims could be interesting, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed reachability question also runs into the protocol’s defining mechanic. A turn consists of one individual move, permitted only when that move changes the issue’s grounded status. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass and cannot change that status. Letting a positive mass move at once repairs this, but changes the mechanic: it bundles many individual proposals into a collective action, despite the paper’s asynchronous, uncoordinated, first-relevant-move process. A type refinement that tracks each agent’s move history can represent which moves remain available, but it cannot make an infinitesimal move pivotal. Conversely, preserving one-agent turns retains atoms rather than a continuous society.

Taking limits of finite populations does not settle the problem cleanly. With \(N\) agents, one move changes an edge weight by \(1/N\), while the protocol requires each legal move to flip a Boolean grounded-status condition. As \(N\) grows, that can leave no finite sequence of meaningful moves, or force a limit with infinitely many flips or grouped moves. Each repair defines a different dynamic. Replacing headcounts by masses in Lemma 1’s edge comparisons is straightforward; it does not by itself establish that Proposition 4.3’s reachability argument or Proposition 4.4’s reinforcement analysis carries over. Those results depend on the sequence of individually pivotal moves.

There is a plausible large-platform regime in which many users share a small number of argumentation systems, so I would not claim multiplicity is inherently implausible. Nor does the case against rest on the answer being unchanged or on hardness transferring. The narrower concern is that the proposed mirror requires inventing a mass-action protocol that changes the paper’s central timing and agency assumptions, and then asks a new reachability question rather than continuing a computational result. That makes it a weak fit for this programme. The negative case is strongest on that fit; it cannot honestly establish that no continuous protocol inspired by the paper could ever be worth studying.
