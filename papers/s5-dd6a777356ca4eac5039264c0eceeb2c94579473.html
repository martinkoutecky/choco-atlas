The continuous mirror question

Given a rational finite-support distribution μ over complete utility types and an ordered stream of item kinds with per-capita supplies \(q_j\), run the paper’s randomized item-by-item ENVY rule on populations of size \(N\), with \(Nμ_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) copies at stage \(j\). In the \(N\to\infty\) expected mass-flow limit, is the utilitarian competitive ratio against the offline allocation unbounded, even with two utility types?

The model it lives in

A fixed-support continuum of households, each receiving an integral bundle, with item supplies scaling per capita. The state tracks mass by utility type and current bundle; ENVY’s original randomized serial rule induces mass flows among feasible recipients. Compare the online population’s average utility with the offline optimum for the same supplies.

The objection that survived

The proponent’s whole-batch minimization is not shown to be the limit of the paper’s uniformly randomized serial mechanism; the corresponding mass-flow limit and its competitive ratio still need analysis.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the utilitarian lower-bound phenomenon in Theorem 7 for ENVY. It leaves Theorem 6’s tie-sensitive GINI claim, the other online results, the offline hardness claims in unnumbered prose, the axiomatic results, and the experiments aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the randomized serial ENVY rule have a well-defined expected mass-flow limit for rational finite-support utility distributions and per-capita item streams?
  • Is its utilitarian competitive ratio unbounded in that limit, even with two utility types?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow, credible positive case in the paper’s online-mechanism results. It does not extend to the paper’s offline NP-hardness claims: those appear in unnumbered prose, so they cannot serve as the numbered anchors your criteria require. Theorem 6 and Theorem 7 are proved here and give named results about online allocation performance.

A plausible regime is a large food bank serving many households with a small number of shared utility profiles. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of households of complete type \(t\), including their utilities for each product category. A finite sequence of product batches arrives online; batch \(j\) contains \(q_j\) units per household, with \(0<q_j\le 1\). Assigning a batch means assigning one indivisible item to each household in some mass \(q_j\). Thus the mass assigned can be fractional while every household’s bundle remains integral.

To specify the online policy, let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) currently holding bundle \(B\). At stage \(j\), choose masses \(y_{t,B}\) to receive the arriving item, with \(0\le y_{t,B}\le x_{t,B}\), \(\sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}=q_j\), and \(y_{t,B}=0\) for types that do not value the item positively. Update the bundle distribution and compute the population Gini index
\[ G(x)= \frac{\sum_{t,B}\sum_{s,D}x_{t,B}x_{s,D} \left|u_t(B)-u_s(D)\right|} {2\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)}. \]
The continuous GINI policy picks a feasible batch assignment minimizing the updated \(G\), using only the current state and arriving batch. The continuous ENVY policy does the same with the envy index, obtained by replacing the numerator’s absolute difference with \(\max\{0,u_t(D)-u_t(B)\}\) and the denominator with \(\sum_{t,B,s,D}x_{t,B}x_{s,D}u_t(D)\). Compare each online outcome with the best allocation of the same batches when their full sequence is known.

My lead is Theorem 7: “The utilitarian competitive ratio of ENVY is at least \(n/2\) whilst its egalitarian competitive ratio is unbounded.” This result is proved here. Its first claim has a direct mass-based mirror: for \(k\) stages, let a high-value type have mass \(\alpha=1/k\) and value each item at \(k\), and let the remaining low-value type value each item at \(1\). Each stage supplies \(q_j=\alpha\). When the online rule gives the first batch to the high-value cohort and each later batch to fresh low-value mass, its utilitarian welfare is about \(2\); offline allocation gives all \(k\) batches to the high-value cohort for welfare \(k\). The ratio grows on the order of \(1/(2\alpha)\). For every \(k\), the high-value cohort can still contain many people if the underlying population is much larger than \(k\). The theorem’s egalitarian lower bound also has a cohort version: with two types of mass \(1/2\), utilities \((1,1)\) and \((\epsilon,0)\), and two batches of mass \(1/2\), ENVY can give both goods to the first type, leaving egalitarian welfare zero; an offline allocation can give one good to each type, achieving egalitarian welfare \(\epsilon\).

Theorem 6 is a balanced-type companion and gives a particularly compact mirror: “The utilitarian and egalitarian competitive ratios of GINI are unbounded.” It is proved here using two agents, two items, and crossed utilities. In the batch mirror, two types each have mass \(1/2\); their utilities for the two item kinds are \((1,\epsilon)\) and \((\epsilon,1)\). The two batches each have mass \(1/2\). Assigning the first batch entirely to either type is Gini-minimizing; assigning the second to the other type then makes everyone’s utility equal. A tie resolution that sends the first batch to the type valuing it at \(\epsilon\) gives welfare \(\epsilon\), while the offline allocation gives welfare \(1\). The ratio is \(1/\epsilon\), for both utilitarian and egalitarian welfare.

The precise questions to hand a prover are: for each of these policies and each welfare measure, is the competitive ratio over rational finite-support mass profiles and ordered batch streams unbounded? Equivalently, for every \(K>1\), can one exhibit an instance and a legal tie-resolved online trajectory whose ratio to the offline optimum is at least \(K\)? A solution is a rational profile, batch stream and online trajectory, together with an offline optimum establishing the ratio. The expected answer is yes for all four policy-and-welfare pairs, by the constructions above. This predicts unbounded performance loss in the continuous model; it is not a prediction of transferred NP-hardness or a Class A/B/C complexity classification. The mirror covers only these online performance results.

The weakest point is the batch model. The paper processes goods one at a time; this mirror groups many identical arrivals into a stage, which is a co-modification of supply that scales with the population. It is plausible for bulk food distribution, and individuals still receive integral bundles, but it needs the authors’ and a referee’s acceptance as the right online analogue. Tie handling also matters: the GINI construction establishes the worst-supported-outcome version. Whether an appropriately defined random tie rule has the same unbounded ratio in expectation is a separate question. Theorem 7’s rare-cohort construction is the stronger bridge to high multiplicity: its parameter becomes a positive type mass, while the number of distinct types stays at two.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a mismatch between the paper’s named results and the mirrors the proponent constructs. The paper’s NP-hardness claims for offline minimization appear as unnumbered prose. Theorem 6 and Theorem 7 instead bound the welfare performance of randomized, one-item-at-a-time online rules. They say nothing about the complexity of computing an optimal allocation or of computing those rules’ choices. Their continuous versions would therefore be questions about online performance, a looser fit with ChoCo’s complexity programme.

Theorem 6’s proposed mirror also relies on an adverse tie resolution. The paper says GINI chooses uniformly among feasible agents; the proponent’s mass construction sends an entire batch to one type on the bad tie. Scaling that mechanism to many agents naturally spreads a batch across feasible agents according to the random selection rule. In the limit, the bad whole-cohort assignment need not describe the resulting mass flow. The proponent acknowledges this: the construction supports a worst-case outcome in the mechanism’s support, while expected performance under a random tie rule is a separate question. A mirror of the latter would require a new argument.

Theorem 7 has a related gap. Its proposed lower bound requires ENVY to assign successive batches to particular cohorts, including fresh low-value mass. The stated continuous policy chooses a minimizing batch assignment over the whole mass distribution. That is a new batch policy, rather than the paper’s rule for assigning one arriving item to one uniformly selected feasible agent. Its trajectory may be a sensible model, but the paper’s theorem does not establish that it follows from the randomized mechanism.

A stronger and more faithful question would take \(N\) households with a fixed finite set of complete utility types, supply each arriving product in proportion to \(N\), run the paper’s randomized rule at individual level, and study the limiting expected mass flow and its competitive ratio. That construction handles integral bundles and gives a plausible large food-bank scenario. It also exposes the real limitation of this negative case: the paper itself discusses food-bank allocation, and proportional bulk deliveries are credible. ChoCo’s scope allows supply to scale when population continuization requires it. The resulting mean-field online allocation question could be worthwhile, even though Theorems 6 and 7 do not settle it.

So the proponent has not justified the specific ratio claims from the paper’s randomized mechanisms; both need a careful definition and fresh analysis. That is a substantial objection to these anchors as presented. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. A finite-type, per-capita-supply model is plausible, and the negative case is weak precisely there.
