What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the theoretical welfare-maximizing threshold in Observation 1; it does not cover the experimental findings on rule choices, heterogeneity, or voting behavior.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor screen has a clear result: this paper has no named computational result. Its numbered “Result 1”–“Result 7” are empirical findings, and it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification. The closest formal result is Observation 1, which is not a computational result: for \(n\) voters whose valuations are independently drawn from a common distribution with no mass at zero, sincere voting is weakly dominant, and among threshold rules the expected-surplus-maximizing threshold is
\[ k=\left\lceil \frac{nE^-}{E^-+E^+}\right\rceil, \]
where \(E^+=\mathbb E[\theta\mid\theta>0]\) and \(E^-=\lvert\mathbb E[\theta\mid\theta<0]\rvert\). The paper gives the argument in Section 2 and notes that the result is also in prior work. So there is no eligible anchor under your rule. Still, Observation 1 offers a plausible, if modest, continuous mirror.

Call it Continuum Optimal Quorum. An instance is a finite set of valuation types \(\Theta\subset\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}\), with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to one. A type is a resident’s payoff \(\theta\) from adopting policy \(A\), with payoff zero from status quo \(B\); \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of the population with that payoff. A natural regime is a large electorate with many residents but only a few standardized payoff classes, as in the paper’s finite-support valuation distributions. The decision variable is a quorum \(q\in[0,1]\): adopt \(A\) if at least fraction \(q\) votes for it. Under sincere voting, the share voting for \(A\) is \(p_+=\sum_{\theta>0}\mu_\theta\). The objective is to maximize per-capita total surplus: \(G^+-G^-\) if \(A\) passes and zero otherwise, where \(G^+=\sum_{\theta>0}\mu_\theta\theta\) and \(G^-=\sum_{\theta<0}\mu_\theta|\theta|\). The problem asks for an optimal quorum \(q\).

For instances with both positive and negative types, let \(E^+=G^+/p_+\) and \(E^-=G^-/(1-p_+)\). A canonical solution is
\[ q^*=\frac{E^-}{E^-+E^+}. \]
It selects \(A\) when aggregate surplus is positive and \(B\) when it is negative; when surplus is zero, either outcome is optimal. Thus the continuous problem is tractable by rational arithmetic polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\). It is a population limit of Observation 1: the finite quorum \(k/n\) differs from \(q^*\) by less than \(1/n\). Further questions include finite-population welfare loss from rounding, and how the choice changes when valuation masses or payoffs are uncertain.

This is a reasonable population mirror of the paper’s theoretical voting problem: payoff types are complete descriptions for the payoff calculation, and a few types can represent a very large society. The original authors could recognize the quorum problem as the limit of their model. Its weakness is substantial, though: the paper’s central contribution is experimental rule choice in groups of five, not computation, and the limiting optimization is essentially solved by the paper’s own formula. The paper itself also leaves open whether the observed behavioral biases extend to large electorates. So this is a coherent, tractable mirror of one theoretical result—not an honest complexity anchor or a mirror of the experimental findings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here. Observation 1 gives a welfare-maximizing threshold, and Results 1–7 report experimental findings; none classifies the complexity of a computational social choice problem. That leaves Observation 1 as the proponent’s sole anchor.

Its continuous quorum version is a faithful limit, but the limit removes the population uncertainty that makes thresholds consequential in the finite model. With fixed type masses, let \(p_+=\sum_{\theta>0}\mu_\theta\), \(G^+=\sum_{\theta>0}\mu_\theta\theta\), and \(G^-=\sum_{\theta<0}\mu_\theta|\theta|\). Sincere voting gives a deterministic share \(p_+\) for \(A\). A quorum \(q\) therefore selects \(A\) exactly when \(q\le p_+\). If \(G^+>G^-\), every such quorum is optimal; if \(G^+<G^-\), every quorum above \(p_+\) is optimal; if they are equal, all quorums tie. The finite formula’s rounding error disappears, and the ratio \(E^-/(E^-+E^+)\) no longer identifies a substantively distinct rule. The interesting object—the welfare trade-off across random realized vote counts—has collapsed to comparing aggregate gains and losses.

The strongest repair is to let the population composition itself be uncertain, perhaps because a common shock changes the masses of valuation types. That preserves a real quorum-choice problem. With a finite list of possible societies, though, the relevant quorum candidates are just their positive-vote shares; one can sort those breakpoints and evaluate expected welfare. A richer correlation or common-shock model could raise further computational questions, but it adds uncertainty over societies to the paper’s fixed-distribution model. The paper itself flags correlated types as a route to making large elections meaningful, which is the strongest opening for a proponent. It also makes this a proposed extension, rather than a computational mirror of Observation 1.

The experimental findings offer no other computational anchor. A continuum distribution of behavioral styles could summarize how often subjects choose particular rules, but that would recast empirical heterogeneity as population data without formulating an algorithmic problem. Turning it into one would require adding an intervention or optimization task absent from the paper.

So the negative case is strongest for a mirror of the paper as written: it has no named computational result, and its one plausible limit loses the finite-population trade-off. The universal claim remains less secure. A correlated-population extension could be a sensible research direction, and the authors’ own discussion gives it some footing; the paper alone does not establish that such an extension would be unworthy.
