Why no mirror

Theorem 5 is a computational result, and the scaled market is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But the proposed common-utility formulation fails when a type is only partly served: matched and unmatched agents of that type can have different utilities. An anonymous utility-distribution version could be a worthwhile extension, but it is unclear whether that preserves the paper’s bidder-optimality question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that for an auction in general position—with \(n\) bidders, \(k\) items, bidder–item values, maximum prices, and minimum prices—a unique bidder-optimal stable matching exists, and the paper’s algorithm finds it in \(O(nk^3)\) time. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result; the paper offers no comparable named hardness result to prefer. Its underlying problem is the computation of the matching and prices.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large ad exchange clearing many independent placement contracts, with many more bidder units than distinct bidder profiles. Treat one unit as demand for one placement: a type \(a\) is the complete vector of its values, maximum prices, and minimum prices across all slot classes. Units with identical vectors are indistinguishable for this problem. The mass \(\alpha_a\) of type \(a\) is its fraction of bidder demand. Because a fixed finite supply of slots would allocate a vanishing fraction of that population, slot supply must scale with it: let \(\beta_b\) be the per-capita supply of slot class \(b\). This is a co-scaling of supply to make the population limit meaningful.

I would hand a prover this problem: Continuous Bidder-Optimal Stable Assignment. Its input is finite supports \(A\) and \(B\) of bidder and slot types, binary-rational masses \(\alpha_a\) summing to \(1\), binary-rational capacities \(\beta_b\), and binary-rational data \((v_{ab},m_{ab},r_{ab})\) for every pair. A solution is an exact rational mass flow \(x_{ab}\), type utilities \(u_a\), and slot-class prices \(p_b\). Require \(x_{ab}\ge0\), \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\alpha_a\), and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\beta_b\). Whenever \(x_{ab}>0\), require \(r_{ab}\le p_b\le m_{ab}\) and \(u_a+p_b=v_{ab}\). Unmatched mass has zero utility and unused slot capacity has zero price: \(u_a(\alpha_a-\sum_b x_{ab})=0\) and \(p_b(\beta_b-\sum_a x_{ab})=0\). Stability requires, for every pair \((a,b)\), at least one of \(u_a+p_b\ge v_{ab}\), \(p_b\ge m_{ab}\), or \(u_a+r_{ab}\ge v_{ab}\). Finally, bidder-optimality means that \(u_a\) is at least the utility of type \(a\) in every other feasible stable mass assignment, for every \(a\). Use the paper’s general-position assumption on the finite type-level alternating-walk graph, with a fixed type-level tie-break.

I expect this problem to be Class A: a capacity-aware version of the paper’s augmenting-path method could operate on the finite supports rather than expanding each mass into individual bidders. The natural target is a running time polynomial in the support sizes and input bit length, independent of the population denominator. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result established by Theorem 5. It prompts further questions: does the compressed algorithm have polynomial bit complexity; which degeneracies still admit a bidder-optimal mass assignment; and what happens to strategic guarantees when a positive-mass cohort can coordinate? The mirror covers Theorem 5’s bidder-optimal stable matching computation; it does not claim to continue Theorem 6’s individual dominant-strategy result.

The soft spot is that this changes the market’s scale as well as its population: advertiser units must be independent, with no cross-placement budget constraint, and slot supply must grow with bidder mass. The paper studies individually matched bidders and a finite item set. A fixed-\(k\) population limit would make almost all bidder mass unmatched, so the capacity co-scaling is essential; it is also the part authors may view as a substantial extension. Still, the types preserve exactly the value and price-bound information their matching problem uses, and large exchanges with repeated independent placement units offer a plausible regime in which the resulting question is recognizable as theirs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for finding a bidder-optimal stable matching. The strongest objection is that bidder-optimality is defined for each named bidder, while the proposed continuous input records only type masses.

That difference matters when slots are scarce. Take one bidder type of mass \(1\), with \(v=10\), \(m=5\), and \(r=0\), and a slot capacity of \(1/2\). Allocating half the mass at price \(5\) is stable: matched agents get utility \(5\), and unmatched agents cannot block because the price reaches their maximum. But any choice of which half gets the slot can be reversed. No allocation makes every individual at least as well off as in every other stable allocation. In the discrete version, this is the paper’s own degenerate case: two identical bidders compete for one slot, and neither possible winner is preferred by both bidders.

The proposed type-level formulation exposes the problem: a common utility \(u_a\) cannot represent both the matched agents’ positive utility and the unmatched agents’ zero utility. Replacing \(u_a\) with a distribution of assignments can represent the outcome, but it does not restore bidder-optimality. Comparing utilities pointwise across two such distributions requires saying which individual in one outcome corresponds to which individual in the other. A type mass supplies no such correspondence. Adding individual tie-breaking tags would supply it, but then the support grows with the population and the high-multiplicity compression disappears. Comparing distributions by an aggregate welfare criterion would be a different objective.

Co-scaling slot supply with advertiser mass avoids the vanishing-population objection, but it does not solve this identity problem when supply remains scarce relative to demand. Pooling many page views into one market might also create a plausible bulk-inventory model, but repeated impressions and bidder budgets change the paper’s one-bidder, one-slot assignment setting.

So the negative case is strongest against an exact continuous mirror of Theorem 5 in the scarce-slot regime: its central, bidder-by-bidder optimality condition has no canonical type-mass counterpart. I cannot rule out a worthwhile model where cohorts are fully served, or one that adopts a new distributional objective. Those possibilities make the universal claim—that no scenario is worthwhile—less than airtight.
