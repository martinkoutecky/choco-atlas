The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is Proposition 7, proved in this paper. It considers regular sequences of economies in which the object set and endowment types stay fixed as the population grows, while the proportions of each preference and endowment type converge to positive limits. It proves that every regular BTM satisfies bounded invariance, and every continuous regular BTM is asymptotically strategy-proof. That makes a high-multiplicity regime credible: for example, a large housing or school allocation system where a type is a complete preference ranking together with an endowment vector, and its mass is its share of the population. The proof itself aggregates agents by these types. Fractional, per-capita supplies would fit the model’s existing exchange structure.

That is strong evidence that a population-based mirror is sensible, but Proposition 7 is not an eligible computational anchor under your criteria. Its result concerns incentive properties, not complexity or algorithms for computing a solution. Likewise, Proposition 1 says every BTM is individually rational and sd-efficient—“efficient” here means allocation-theoretically efficient, not polynomial-time. Lemma 1 proves existence of a maximum solution to the equation system; the paper’s prose says the systems can be solved efficiently, but no numbered result asserts a complexity bound for that computation.

The continuous eating procedures and fractional assignments are also not anchors: they continuize the allocation process or outcome while the population remains finite. So the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, and there is no compliant anchor from which to state one continuous problem per result. Proposition 7 supports the regime, but cannot carry the required computational case.

My weakest point is therefore decisive for this assignment: the best evidence that large populations are natural in the paper is an asymptotic strategic result, while the paper’s computational claims are informal and secondary. A complexity question about computing a BTM on rational type masses could be motivated by the model, but it would be a new question prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror anchored in one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s sole anchor, Proposition 7, does establish a credible high-multiplicity regime: the paper explicitly studies economies whose type proportions converge as the population grows. I would not use that as an objection. But Proposition 7 is about incentive properties, not computational complexity. Its strongest possible continuous restatement—ask whether an individual can gain by changing type in an atomless society—loses the theorem’s substance: a single person has zero mass, so a report change does not alter the population distribution. The paper’s result concerns the diminishing effect of an individual in large *finite* economies. A quantitative bound on that finite-population effect could be interesting, but it would remain an asymptotic incentive question, not a computational mirror.

The best alternative is to choose a concrete mechanism such as Equal-BTM and ask how to compute its allocation from a distribution over preference and endowment types, with supplies scaled per capita. That is a coherent population model, and the paper’s type-based proof gives reason to think it can be represented compactly. But it does not mirror a named computational result here. The numbered results establish efficiency, fairness, characterizations, and incentive properties; the computational claim is the informal statement that the mechanism solves linear equations efficiently. The paper does not pose a complexity problem for computing its output. Turning that implementation step into a complexity question would be a new question prompted by the paper, rather than a continuous version of one of its computational results.

So the negative case is strongest on the assignment’s stated criterion: there is no eligible computational anchor. It is weaker as a universal claim. The paper itself gives a plausible population regime, and a researcher could sensibly study its mechanism on type masses. I cannot honestly argue that no continuous-population model is worthwhile in any scenario; I can argue that this paper offers no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror.
