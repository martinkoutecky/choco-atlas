A continuous version of one of these claims could be a game-theoretic extension, but it would not mirror a computational result of this paper.

The best population scenario is many postal items or service users sharing the same schedule requirements. Yet under the paper’s fixed, non-rival route costs, multiplicity does not affect the central optimization. For any coalition containing users of a given type, copies of that type can all use the same schedule; adding more copies does not change the periods that must be covered. Consequently, for a continuous society \(\mu\), the minimum schedule cost depends on \(\operatorname{supp}\mu\), not on the positive masses assigned to its types. Mass-weighting costs or making service intensity depend on volume could change that, but it would introduce a different cost model; the paper’s postal application explicitly sets aside the volume-based allocation stage.

One could study a continuum version of core stability or Shapley allocation for these users. That may be a sensible new cooperative-game question, but it supplies no computational claim from this paper to continue. Making it a computational project would require adding a target—such as the complexity of finding an optimal schedule or a core allocation—that the paper itself does not analyze.
