The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is continuous STV tabulation, but this paper has no admissible named anchor: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Algorithm 1, the STV procedure, is the closest formal result; it describes how to count ballots but makes no complexity claim. The package’s implementation claims and election examples do not supply the required anchor. So I can’t honestly give an anchor-based mirror without inventing a result.

An unanchored mirror that seems faithful would be Exact Mass-STV Tabulation. An instance gives \(m\) candidates, \(s\) seats, a finite support of \(\tau\) complete ballot types (including weak rankings if equal preferences are allowed), and a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). It also specifies the quota convention, tie-breaking rule, and, if relevant, a marked candidate group and number of reserved seats. The task is to return the exact count trace and elected order by applying the paper’s STV transfer, elimination, equal-preference, and reserved-seat rules to those masses. This is an evaluation problem, not an optimization problem, so it has no cost objective.

A plausible regime is a large membership election where \(N\) voters produce only \(\tau\) distinct rankings, with \(N\gg\tau\). Each type is one complete ballot pattern; its mass is the share of voters submitting it. That preserves the ballot information and transfer logic the paper uses. The authors might recognize it as their tabulation problem for a large electorate: the paper itself illustrates STV on nearly 30,000 votes. A support-based simulation appears likely tractable in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length, but that would be a new result, not one proved here.

The weak point is that the paper is aimed chiefly at small organizations and selectorates, and its absolute \(\varepsilon\) and finite tie procedures need explicit conventions in a normalized mass model. More fundamentally, it offers no hardness or algorithmic-complexity anchor for this mirror to explain. Continuous STV robustness—how much ballot mass must change to alter the elected set—is a natural follow-on question, but it too would extend beyond the paper’s stated results.

Exact Mass-STV is the strongest proposed rescue. It is a sensible model: treat complete ballot patterns as types, assign rational masses, and specify the quota and tie-breaking conventions. The nearly 30,000-voter Dublin example makes a high-multiplicity setting plausible; the paper’s focus on small electorates does not erase it. I cannot dismiss this mirror on grounds of missing multiplicity, lost identity information, or a degenerate limit. Its weakness is narrower: it formalizes the paper’s tabulation procedure as a new computational question, rather than mirroring a complexity result the paper claims. Calling the likely answer easy would not fix that gap; an easy answer can still be worthwhile.

The robustness suggestion has an antecedent in the paper’s informal observation that larger electorates can be less sensitive to small changes. A question such as the minimum mass shift needed to change the elected set is well-posed and could be interesting. The paper, however, gives no robustness measure or computational result to mirror. That question adds a new objective and becomes a follow-on research problem.

So the negative case is strong only under the paper-specific anchor requirement: none of the proposed mirrors is anchored in a claimed computational result. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weak. Mass-STV tabulation and mass-based winner robustness remain plausible research questions; this paper simply does not establish either as a mirror of one of its computational results.
