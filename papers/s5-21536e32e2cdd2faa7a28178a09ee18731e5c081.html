But the paper permits choice functions to distinguish named profiles with identical ranking counts, while the mirror replaces those profiles with a single distribution and replaces fixed-coalition control with a movable mass budget. That makes this a re-modeled logic project rather than a continuous-population version of the paper’s result.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Proposition 1 only; the Arrow, Muller–Satterthwaite, and Sen results are axiomatic impossibility results and are not computational anchors here.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is one modest but genuine computational anchor here: Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for fixed finite \(N\) and \(X\), deciding whether a formula of \(L[N,X]\) is valid is decidable. Validity means truth at every profile in every model of a resolute social choice function. The proof is by enumerating the finitely many models and worlds. I would use this as the lead. The paper’s Arrow, Muller–Satterthwaite, and Sen results are named theorems, but they are impossibility results about social choice properties, not computational-complexity results of the kind this task requires.

A close continuous mirror would be Continuous-Population SCF-Logic Validity. Fix a finite candidate set \(X\), and let each type \(t\in T=L(X)\) be a complete ranking of the candidates. A society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), so \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society with ranking \(t\). This fits a high-multiplicity regime: many voters, with relatively few distinct rankings in the support, of size \(\tau\).

The logic’s outcome proposition \(x\) means that \(x\) wins. Add propositions \(M_{t,q}\), meaning that at least rational mass \(q\) has type \(t\). Replace a finite coalition’s ability to change ballots with \(\Diamond_\alpha\): from \(\mu\), this says that some \(\nu\) satisfies the formula after at most \(\alpha\) mass has changed type. Precisely, require \(\tfrac12\sum_{t\in T}|\mu_t-\nu_t|\le\alpha\). This is the natural anonymous analogue of a coalition changing its members’ ballots.

An instance consists of \(X\) and a finite formula \(\varphi\), with its rational thresholds and mass budgets encoded in binary. A model is any resolute choice rule \(F:\Delta(T)\to X\); as in the paper, impose no additional regularity on \(F\). The question is whether \(\varphi\) is valid: does it hold for every such \(F\) and every society \(\mu\)? The answer is yes or no; there is no optimization objective in this anchor. A no-instance has a semantic counterexample consisting of a rule and a society where the formula fails. The relevant parameters include \(m=|X|\), \(|T|=m!\), formula length \(L\), rational bit length, and the support size \(\tau\) of any counterexample.

This is recognizably connected to the authors’ problem: their paper studies what SCF formulas say across profiles and what coalitions can enforce by changing ballots. Here the worlds are population distributions, and control is expressed as transferring mass between ranking types. The scope is deliberately just the validity result; I would not present the Arrow, Muller–Satterthwaite, or Sen theorems as mirrored by this question.

My tentative expectation is Class C, though weakly: the finite proof of Proposition 1 depends on enumerating finitely many worlds and SCFs, whereas this version has a continuum of societies and unrestricted choice rules. Any hardness would be continuum-specific, not a transfer of hardness from a discrete population problem. The most useful next questions are whether every invalid formula has a rational counterexample with bounded support and bit length, and whether validity admits a finite polyhedral or LP-based reduction. Such a reduction could move the problem toward Class A.

The weakest point is that this mirrors the paper’s logic and its decidability question, rather than a conventional election task such as bribery or control. The authors’ coalitional modality makes the mass-transfer extension plausible, but the resulting problem would still need their recognition as a worthwhile continuous version of their formal reasoning problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only clear computational anchor. Arrow’s, Muller–Satterthwaite’s, and Sen’s theorems are axiomatic results, not complexity results. Proposition 1 establishes decidability by enumerating all social choice functions and profiles for fixed finite \(N\) and \(X\). It gives no useful complexity bound when those parameters scale.

The proposed continuous logic is coherent: societies could be distributions over rankings, outcome rules could act on distributions, and a mass-change modality could replace coalition moves. But that changes the anchor’s semantics. The paper allows a social choice function to distinguish two profiles that differ only by swapping voters’ ballots; both profiles have the same distribution over rankings. Its modality also refers to a fixed coalition of named agents. A total-variation budget instead allows any mass to move, with the affected people potentially changing at each step. Preserving the original distinctions requires identity-specific types, which undermines the high-multiplicity model. Using anonymous rules and mass changes avoids that problem by defining a new metric-modal logic.

That new logic could be a worthwhile computational project, perhaps with complexity measured in the formula size, candidate count, and rational bit lengths. The paper’s focus on formal reasoning and SAT makes author recognition plausible. Still, it is not a direct high-multiplicity relaxation of a computational problem studied in the paper; the original decidability result is an exhaustive check over a fixed finite state space, and the continuous proposal asks about validity over a new space of rules and societies. The negative case is therefore strongest on fit with the programme, not on any fundamental obstruction. I can’t honestly claim the mirror has no potential; its weakness is that it looks more like a new continuous-logic project than a continuation of this paper’s computational results.
