The continuous mirror question

Given a finite edge-weighted graph \(G=(V,E)\) with shortest-path metric \(d\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(V\), and an integer \(k\), decide whether there is a set \(Y\subseteq V\) of \(k\) centers such that for every \(z\in V\setminus Y\) and every submeasure \(0\leq\nu_v\leq\mu_v\) with \(\sum_{v\in V}\nu_v\geq 1/k\), \(\sum_{v\in V}\nu_v d(v,z)\geq\sum_{v\in V}\nu_v d(v,Y)\), where \(d(v,Y)=\min_{y\in Y}d(v,y)\).

The model it lives in

Types are graph vertices with mass \(\mu_v\); the decision variable is a set \(Y\) of \(k\) indivisible centers. Feasibility means no submeasure of mass at least \(1/k\) can strictly lower its total distance by moving to one new vertex.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 17’s existence question at \(\alpha=1\). It leaves the other fixed values of \(\alpha\), the paper’s approximation bounds, and its experiments unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(\alpha\neq1\), does NP-hardness persist when blockers may take fractional mass and the entitlement threshold is \(\alpha/k\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Mass-\((\alpha,1)\)-Core Existence, the continuous counterpart of the paper’s exact distance-improvement question.

Theorem 17, proved in this paper by reduction from Minimum Vertex Cover, says that for every fixed constant \(\alpha\ge1\), deciding whether an \((\alpha,1)\)-core clustering exists is NP-complete. The hardness holds in a graph metric with one agent at each vertex and with the vertices as the possible center locations. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the graph and the choice of centers.

For the mirror, take a finite edge-weighted graph with shortest-path metric \(d\), vertex set \(V\), and possible center locations \(M=V\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(V\), with \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=1\); its positive-mass vertices are the \(\tau\) types. A type is a location, since location determines every distance cost in this problem. Given \(k\) and fixed \(\alpha\), ask whether there is a set \(Y\subseteq V\) of \(k\) centers such that, for every new location \(z\in V\setminus Y\) and every submeasure \(\nu\) with \(0\le\nu_v\le\mu_v\) and \(\sum_v\nu_v\ge\alpha/k\),
\[ \sum_{v\in V}\nu_v d(v,z)\ \ge\ \sum_{v\in V}\nu_v d(v,Y), \qquad d(v,Y)=\min_{y\in Y}d(v,y). \]
A solution is such a center set \(Y\); otherwise the answer is no. This keeps the paper’s \(k\) indivisible facilities and its one-center coalition deviation intact. It changes the population into mass: a coalition may take part of a type’s mass, and its entitlement is the fraction \(\alpha/k\) of society.

A plausible regime is public-service planning for a large city: many residents share modeled travel costs because they live at the same service sites, while the number of occupied sites \(\tau\) is far below the number of residents \(N\). The masses are site population shares. The instance can be recorded with rational masses and edge lengths, with complexity measured in \(\tau\), graph size, \(k\), and bit length \(L\), rather than \(N\). This is a natural object for the authors: their fairness condition is already about groups’ aggregate distance, and a fractional population makes group size a share of the city.

I expect the mirror to remain hard, in Class B: the source theorem’s hardness comes from vertex-cover structure in the metric and center locations, rather than from the number of people. For a fixed proposed \(Y\), checking whether a particular location admits a blocking submeasure is a linear optimization problem over the \(\nu_v\); the challenge is choosing \(Y\). That makes the search question precise and computationally meaningful.

The weakness is that Theorem 17 does not prove this hardness transfer. Its reduction reasons about integer-sized coalitions, whereas the mirror allows fractional mass at each vertex; the rounding at the entitlement threshold can change whether a blocking coalition exists. A robust version of the vertex-cover reduction is needed before claiming NP-hardness for the mirror. That gap is itself a useful question: does hardness persist when the number of residents grows while the support stays tied to the graph?

This case covers Theorem 17’s general-metric exact-improvement existence result only. It does not claim mirrors for the paper’s line and tree bounds, its \((1,\beta)\) results, or its experimental findings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proponent’s all-\(\alpha\) hardness claim. In Theorem 17’s construction, \(n/k=2\), so discrete blocking coalitions have size at least \(\lceil 2\alpha\rceil\). The mass mirror’s threshold is \(2\alpha\) type-masses. When \(2\alpha\) is not an integer, fractional mass can change which coalitions block, and the theorem does not establish hardness transfer for those values. The reduction also uses one agent per graph vertex, so it does not itself demonstrate a large population with small support.

That objection does not defeat the mirror. At \(\alpha=1\), the thresholds align exactly: the reduction has \(n=2k\), and a blocking coalition has mass at least \(1/k=2/n\). With uniform type masses \(1/n\), rescale a submeasure to \(x_v=n\nu_v\). For fixed centers \(Y\) and deviating location \(z\), finding a blocking submeasure is maximizing
\[ \sum_v x_v\bigl(d(v,Y)-d(v,z)\bigr) \]
subject to \(0\le x_v\le1\) and \(\sum_v x_v\ge2\). This polytope has integral extreme points. So any fractional blocker yields a discrete blocking coalition of at least two agents, and every such discrete coalition gives a mass blocker. Theorem 17’s reduction therefore transfers at \(\alpha=1\).

The high-multiplicity scenario is also credible: residents at the same service site have identical distance costs, and the paper itself motivates clustering with public-service planning. Replicating each site’s residents equally can make the population much larger than the support without changing the mass distribution. The continuum does not make the entitlement vanish; it remains \(1/k\), and the graph’s facility choices still determine the problem.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. Its continuous existence question is precise, the paper has a named computational result to mirror, and at least the \(\alpha=1\) version has a direct hardness transfer. The caveat about other \(\alpha\) values limits what the discrete theorem proves; it does not make the mirror unworthwhile.
