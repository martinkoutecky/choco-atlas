What the mirror covers

The candidate calculation covers the ICU example’s allocations under no priority, absolute priority, over-and-above reserves, and minimum-guarantee reserves; it leaves the paper’s ethical rationales, policy choices, and adoption discussion outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is continuous reserve-outcome computation. A type would specify everything relevant to allocation: group membership, clinical priority, eligibility for each reserve, and any tie-breakers. Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita supply \(q\), reserve capacities, category processing order, and priority rules, the task is to compute \(x_{t,r}\): the mass of type \(t\) receiving a unit through category \(r\). Each type’s total allocation is at most \(\mu_t\); each category’s allocation is within capacity and eligibility; and allocation follows the specified processing and priority rules, allowing a cutoff to split a type’s mass. The output is the allocation by type and category, and the resulting shares for groups such as essential personnel and the general community.

This is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s concrete allocation calculations. It preserves the policy details the authors emphasize—especially reserve size and processing order—while expressing them in population shares. For a fixed finite type support and fixed priority rules, I would expect this outcome-computation problem to be tractable.

But that is only a candidate mirror, not an anchor. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its worked example—100 ICU beds, 80 essential personnel, and 120 community patients—calculates illustrative outcomes for four systems: \(40/60\) with no priority, \(80/20\) with absolute priority, \(52/48\) with an over-and-above reserve, and \(40/60\) with a minimum-guarantee reserve. Those calculations are not a named complexity result. The paper also does not formulate a computational problem or establish an algorithmic bound.

So, under the requested anchor rule, there are no eligible anchors and no per-anchor continuous problems to state. The worked example supports the plausibility of the candidate mirror, but not a claim that this paper contributes a computational result whose discrete hardness might dissolve in the continuum. My weakest point is that the proposed problem formalizes calculation of a fixed reserve system’s outcomes; it does not capture a complexity question the paper itself poses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no eligible anchor: this paper states no numbered computational result. It is a perspectives paper about reserve systems’ ethical rationale, design levers, and adoption. Its ICU example illustrates how processing order can change group allocations; it does not analyze the complexity of computing allocations. That leaves no result here whose computational question can be continuized.

The proposed type-mass allocation problem is coherent, but it is better understood as a formal implementation calculator than as a mirror of a result in this paper. The paper’s central task is to explain how reserve size, category-specific priorities, and processing order let planners accommodate competing ethical goals. Turning those choices into an optimization problem would require specifying a welfare objective or tradeoff that the paper deliberately leaves to planners. That would add a new research question. Keeping the choices fixed and computing allocations by type would track the paper’s examples, but would not supply the missing computational contribution.

That is the strongest case against, but it does not establish the universal claim. High multiplicity is sensible for vaccine and hospital populations, and type masses preserve the group shares and priority criteria the paper discusses. The outcome-computation mirror could be useful in practice, and the authors might recognize it as relevant. I can argue that it is not a computational result this paper contributes; I cannot honestly rule out that it is a worthwhile continuous problem in its own right.
