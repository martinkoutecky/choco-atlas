The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive mirror case that this paper can honestly carry under your criteria. Its natural population model would be a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types, where each type is a logically closed, consistent set of judgments and \(\mu_t\) is the share of voters holding those judgments. A large electorate repeatedly considering the same standardized policy propositions is a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

But the paper contains no named computational result: it has no numbered results, and none of its results classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest is its unnumbered Theorem: under UD, PO, SLC, WIIA, and LIA, the decisive sets form a filter. Corollary 1 adds SC and a finite voter set, concluding that the voting function is oligarchic. These are axiomatic representation results, not computational anchors.

Turning them into questions about continuous populations would continuize the paper’s axioms and representation problem, while adding an algorithmic task would invent a new problem rather than mirror one of the paper’s results. So there is no lead anchor, no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover, and no honest A/B/C prediction. The weakest point is decisive: although the population setting is plausible, the paper’s contribution lies outside the programme’s computational scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no computational anchor, and that is decisive here. The paper’s central Theorem characterizes decisive sets as a filter; Corollary 1 says that with finitely many voters the voting function is oligarchic; Corollary 3 adds that anonymity leaves only the full electorate decisive. These are representation results about which voting functions satisfy axioms, not results about the complexity of solving a computational problem.

A repeated policy electorate is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. One could represent it by a distribution over logically closed, consistent judgment sets. But that gives a profile, while the paper’s conclusions concern a voting function across *all* admissible profiles and propositions. The mass vector alone does not specify that function. And replacing finite decisive coalitions with measurable groups or almost-everywhere agreement would be a reasonable axiomatic extension, but it would still be a representation question, not the complexity mirror this programme seeks.

The strongest attempt to make a computational mirror would add a task: for example, decide whether a supplied voting rule satisfies the paper’s axioms, or construct a rule satisfying them. That requires an encoding for the rule and its domain, and usually a finite logical framework. Neither task is asserted or analyzed in the paper; each adds the computational problem that would need to be studied. Even if worthwhile in its own right, it would not mirror a named result here.

So there is no anchor to defeat one by one. The obstacle is not that a continuous population is implausible, or that the results would remain unchanged. It is that the paper’s contribution is axiomatic rather than computational. A continuous treatment could extend its mathematics, but it would fall outside ChoCo’s stated question.
