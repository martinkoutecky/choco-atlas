The continuous mirror question

Given a finite simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) with no isolated vertices and positive rational type masses \(\mu_e\) summing to \(1\), let each edge type \(e\) have an atomless population \(\Omega_e\) of mass \(\mu_e\). For each measurable coalition \(X\), let \(F(X)=\{e:\lambda(X\cap\Omega_e)=\mu_e\}\), \(V_X=\{v:\delta(v)\subseteq F(X)\}\), and \(\gamma_\mu(X)=\alpha(G[V_X])\). Does there exist a family of nonnegative measurable payoff densities \(f_X\) satisfying \(\int_X f_X\,d\lambda=\gamma_\mu(X)\) for every \(X\), and \(f_X(i)\le f_Y(i)\) almost everywhere whenever \(X\subseteq Y\)?

The model it lives in

An atomless society partitioned into edge types \(e\in E\), with rational masses \(\mu_e\). A type contributes to project availability only when its full mass is in the coalition; the coalition’s value is the maximum independent-set size among the available projects. The decision variable is a measurable payoff-density family indexed by coalitions, subject to efficiency and monotonicity.

The objection that survived

The continuum treats unanimity up to measure zero, so omitting one finite participant disables a project while omitting a null set does not; the proposed equivalence does not establish convergence from those finite coalitions.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Atomless PMAS Recognition for Independent-Set Games. It continuizes the participants while keeping the paper’s projects, cooperation rule, value function, and question—whether the value can be shared so each participant’s payoff never falls as the coalition expands.

Take a finite simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) with no isolated vertices. Each edge \(e=\{u,v\}\) is a participant type: its members are interchangeable participants qualified for projects \(u\) and \(v\). Let \(\Omega_e\) be an atomless population of that type, with rational mass \(\mu_e>0\), where \(\sum_{e\in E}\mu_e=1\). A plausible regime is a large consortium with many participants but relatively few distinct pairs of projects for which they are qualified: the number of people is large, while the support size \(\tau=|E|\) is modest.

A coalition is any measurable \(X\subseteq\Omega=\bigsqcup_{e\in E}\Omega_e\). Write \(x_e=\lambda(X\cap\Omega_e)\), and let \(F(X)=\{e:x_e=\mu_e\}\) be the types fully represented in it, with equality understood up to measure zero. Set
\[ V_X=\{v\in V:\delta(v)\subseteq F(X)\}, \qquad \gamma_\mu(X)=\alpha(G[V_X]). \]
Thus a project is available only when all participants qualified for it are in the coalition, and the coalition’s value is the maximum number of mutually compatible available projects.

A solution is a family of measurable, nonnegative payoff densities \(f_X\) for every coalition \(X\). It must satisfy
\[ \int_X f_X\,d\lambda=\gamma_\mu(X) \]
for every \(X\), and \(f_X(i)\le f_Y(i)\) for almost every \(i\in X\) whenever \(X\subseteq Y\). Density is payoff per unit of population mass. The decision question is whether such a family exists for the given \(G\) and rational masses \(\mu\). There is no optimization objective: as in the paper, the question is existence of an efficient, monotone allocation scheme.

The lead anchor is Corollary 10 (proved in this paper): “The population monotonicity of an independent set game can be determined efficiently.” Its structural basis is Theorem 9, also proved here: the game is population monotonic if and only if (i) every vertex is within distance two of a pendant vertex, and (ii) no two non-pendant edges of type I are incident; type I means a non-pendant edge incident to no pendant edge. The paper says these conditions can be checked efficiently using BFS. There is no named hardness result in the supplied paper, so this algorithmic result is the appropriate anchor.

The proposed continuous problem is exactly equivalent to the paper’s finite problem on the support graph. Given a discrete PMAS \(z^S_e\), for each coalition \(X\) give members of every fully represented type \(e\) density \(z^{F(X)}_e/\mu_e\), and give members of partially represented types density zero. Efficiency follows because each full type receives total payment \(z^{F(X)}_e\). Monotonicity follows from the discrete PMAS condition as the set of fully represented types can only grow. Conversely, restrict any continuous scheme to coalitions \(X_S=\bigsqcup_{e\in S}\Omega_e\) and set \(z^S_e=\int_{\Omega_e}f_{X_S}\,d\lambda\). Efficiency and monotonicity then give a discrete PMAS. So Corollary 10’s efficient recognition algorithm carries over; the masses affect the per-capita densities, but not whether a scheme exists. I expect this mirror to be tractable, by the same support-graph characterization and BFS check.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem in a continuous population regime: the participants, coalition expansion, project value, and payoff conditions are preserved. It also has a credible high-multiplicity interpretation—many interchangeable participants per project-pair type—rather than treating a seven-person committee as a limit object. The proposed mirror covers the PMAS-existence recognition result, not the paper’s discussion of convexity or other cooperative games.

The weakest point is that the continuum extension collapses to the finite support graph. Since project completion requires full participation by each relevant type, the mass values do not create new combinatorial behavior; the result does not reveal a new continuous optimization method or a hardness boundary. I still consider it a sound mirror because that collapse follows from preserving the paper’s all-participants cooperation rule, rather than weakening its question. Further questions include whether one can characterize or optimize over all continuous PMASes, and whether an efficient characterization survives when projects have different values or tolerate partial participation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this game’s population monotonicity turns on unanimity within a type. A project contributes to \(\gamma(S)=\alpha(G[V\langle S\rangle])\) only when every incident edge-player is in \(S\). With many participants of each edge type, the natural mass version makes a type count as present only when its entire mass participates.

That gives a sharply discontinuous value function: removing any positive amount of a type can disable a project, however small the amount. In finite populations, removing one person has the same effect, even as that person’s share tends to zero. If coalitions are identified up to measure-zero differences, the continuum erases the veto of a null-mass participant. If exact membership is retained, the distribution of type masses no longer records who is present. Either choice loses part of the finite game’s individual-level semantics.

The proposed mirror chooses the first convention. It is internally coherent, and its correspondence with the finite PMAS is convincing: the allocation scheme exists exactly when the support graph passes Theorem 9’s tests. But this also exposes the limitation. The masses do no computational work; the recognition question remains the same BFS check on the support graph. The continuum supplies a high-multiplicity story without creating a mass-sensitive complexity landscape.

A smoother alternative could let projects tolerate partial participation or produce value in proportion to participating mass. That would avoid the all-or-nothing jump, but it would replace the paper’s unanimity-based characteristic function and its independent-set game with a different production game. It would no longer be a mirror of Corollary 10.

This is the best negative case, and it is limited. Corollary 10 is a named computational result, and the consortium interpretation makes repeated participants per edge type plausible. The programme allows the answer to coincide with the discrete one, and treats high-multiplicity work as support for a mirror. So the discontinuity and mass-independence make this a less compelling contribution to the programme’s goals; they do not establish that the mirror is invalid or not worthwhile. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor outright.
