The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(j\) and \(q\le \lfloor j/2\rfloor\), let \(\mu=(1-\beta,\beta)\) describe the mass of apple and brick voters, with total mass \(1\) and each of \(j\) districts receiving mass \(1/j\). For odd district size \(K\), let \(\theta_{j,K}(q)\) be the least brick share \(n/(jK)\) for which B can force at least \(q\) wins in the paper's finite game. Determine whether \(\theta_j(q)=\lim_{K\to\infty,\ K\ \mathrm{odd}}\theta_{j,K}(q)\) exists, compute it, and provide strategies certifying the win threshold on either side.

The model it lives in

A society with mass distribution \(\mu=(1-\beta,\beta)\) over apple and brick voter types, divided among \(j\) districts of mass \(1/j\). Define the candidate threshold through the limiting values of the finite alternating games, and ask for its value and strategy certificates.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper: it classifies no problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time, FPT, or otherwise. Theorem 1 is attributed to Mixon and Villar and cited as prior work. The closest results proved here are Theorem 8 and Theorem 9, but they are strategic guarantees, not complexity classifications.

Theorem 8 proves that if \(n\ge 2q(m+1)\), B’s strategy in Algorithm 1 guarantees at least \(q\) districts. Theorem 9 proves that B can win \(q\) districts only if
\[ n\ge 2q\left(1-\frac{q}{j+q}\right)(m+1)-1. \]
Theorem 8 is the stronger of these as a constructive result; Theorem 9 supplies a complementary necessary bound. Both are proved in this paper. They are useful anchors for a strategic continuous extension, but do not meet your stated requirement for computational anchors.

That extension has a natural high-multiplicity regime: hold the number of districts \(j\) fixed while each district’s population \(K=2m+1\) grows. The two complete voter types are apple and brick, with society \(\mu=(1-\beta,\beta)\), where \(\beta\) is the brick share. There are \(jK\) voters, each district has mass \(1/j\), and the parties retain the paper’s turn order and objective: B moves first, either player may assign either type, and B tries to win at least \(q\) districts. A move assigns one voter, hence mass \(1/(jK)\); these moves become infinitesimal as \(K\) grows. This is the paper’s abstract game in a many-voter, two-type regime, and the authors would recognize the objective and strategy question.

A precise continuous follow-on is to define \(\theta_{j,K}(q)\) as the minimum brick share \(n/(jK)\) for which B can force \(q\) wins in the finite game with district size \(K\), then ask whether
\[ \theta_j(q)=\lim_{\substack{K\to\infty\\K\ \mathrm{odd}}}\theta_{j,K}(q) \]
exists and, if so, to determine it and give strategies that certify the threshold. A computational version would take \(j,q,\beta,\epsilon\) and decide whether the society with brick share \(\beta\) lies at least \(\epsilon\) below or above that threshold, with strategy certificates. Theorem 8 gives the sufficient side and Theorem 9 the necessary side: their bounds imply
\[ \frac{q}{j+q}\le \liminf_K\theta_{j,K}(q) \le \limsup_K\theta_{j,K}(q)\le\frac{q}{j}. \]
Thus they motivate a concrete threshold problem, but do not themselves classify its complexity. I cannot responsibly assign that new problem to Class A, B, or C from this paper.

The mirror covers only the abstract strategic guarantee about how many districts B can force. Its weakest point is that shrinking the mass per move does not automatically yield a well-defined continuous sequential game: existence of the threshold limit and convergence of strategies are questions to prove. And even if they hold, this remains a promising extension of the paper’s game analysis, rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational-complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 8 gives B a strategy that guarantees \(q\) wins above a voter-count bound; Theorem 9 gives a necessary bound. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Algorithm 1 is a strategy for a player, not an algorithm for deciding an input problem. Theorem 1 is attributed to earlier work. That leaves no paper result in ChoCo’s target form.

The two anchors do have natural population limits. With \(K=2m+1\), total population \(jK\), and brick share \(\beta=n/(jK)\), Theorem 8’s sufficient bound approaches \(\beta=q/j\), while Theorem 9’s necessary bound approaches \(\beta=q/(j+q)\). But these are limits of strategic guarantees, not complexity bounds. Asking whether the limits coincide, or what strategies achieve the exact threshold, would be a continuous game-value question. That could be worthwhile research; it does not supply the computational-complexity mirror the programme is seeking.

A stronger version would define a fluid game with mass in each district, specify how the players act and respond over time, and ask for the complexity of computing its value or an optimal strategy. That avoids the weak “does the threshold limit exist?” formulation. It also exposes the obstacle: a move in the paper assigns one voter, but in the limit each such move has zero mass. To retain the strategic force of alternating turns, the fluid model needs a continuous-time control or differential-game semantics. That may be a sensible extension, but it changes the central question from the paper’s two count bounds to a new model of strategic control. A complexity problem could then be posed, but it would be newly introduced rather than mirrored from a computational result in this paper.

I would not object that high multiplicity is implausible here. A large electorate with two partisan types is a sensible society, and the paper’s normalization already points toward population shares. Nor is it an objection that a continuous answer might be simple or resemble the finite bounds. The negative case is narrower: the proposed mirrors of Theorems 8 and 9 yield strategic mass guarantees, while turning them into computational questions requires adding a different problem.

So the case against is strong as a reason to exclude this paper from ChoCo’s computational-mirror programme. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: a continuous strategic extension remains plausible, just not anchored in a computational result this paper proves.
