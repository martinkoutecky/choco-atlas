The continuous mirror question

Given a finite hierarchy with team cohorts indexed by types \(t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-team harmonic budgets \(b_t^r\), a resource-conversion graph, and mass-normalized supplies \(s_v^r\), compute an \(\epsilon\)-accurate harmonic market equilibrium. A type records its hierarchy position \(v(t)\) and complete budget profile; define the mass-weighted subtree budget \(\beta_v^r=\sum_{t:\,v(t)\in\operatorname{SubT}(v)}\mu_t b_t^r\). At each node \(v\), with its parent’s prices fixed, solve the paper’s local concave price program using \(\beta_v^r\) in place of the finite subtree budget, recover conversion amounts from the dual variables, and recursively recover flows. The resulting type allocation is \(x_t^r=b_t^r/p_{v(t)}^r\); seek a running-time bound in the hierarchy size, \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\).

The model it lives in

A fixed organizational hierarchy with a continuum of interchangeable team cohorts at its leaves. Each cohort has mass \(\mu_t\) and a complete per-team budget profile; subtree budgets and supplies are aggregated by mass, while teams retain the paper’s harmonic demands and market-equilibrium conditions.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.1 is a local result, and the paper does not establish a bit-complexity bound for the mass-weighted version or its full recursive computation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the harmonic market computation using Theorem 3.1 across the hierarchy. It leaves the general-demand Budget Descent results, their convergence guarantees, and the simulations outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the recursive convex-program computation achieve a polynomial bit-complexity bound in the hierarchy size, \(m\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\)?
  • How closely does the mass equilibrium approximate a finite population of teams, and can the capped-demand Budget Descent results be extended to this aggregation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I believe in is an accelerator market with a large population of teams arranged under a fixed organizational hierarchy. A team’s type records its place in the hierarchy and its per-resource budget and demand profile. Suppose there are \(N\) teams but only \(\tau\) distinct types, with rational mass \(\mu_t\) for type \(t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type’s mass is its share of the teams; it is not fractional supply. The hierarchy and resource-conversion graph stay as in the paper, while supplies are expressed per team so the model scales with the population.

This is plausible where many teams use a small number of standard workload and budget profiles across a modest number of business units. The team count can then be much larger than \(\tau\). If budgets and demand caps are nearly unique to every team, \(\tau\) approaches \(N\), and this regime offers little compression. A type must include the team’s complete budget profile and organizational position.

My lead is the Continuous Harmonic Hierarchical Market Equilibrium problem, mirroring Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper. For each type \(t\) and resource \(r\), let demand per team be \(D_t^r(p)=b_t^r/p\). At hierarchy node \(v\), aggregate demand is
\[ D_v^r(p)=\sum_{t:\,v(t)=v}\mu_tD_t^r(p). \]
The instance includes the finite hierarchy, resource-conversion graph, type masses and budgets, and per-capita supplies \(s_v^r\). The variables are local prices \(p_v^r\), flows \(f_v^r\) from parent to child, conversions \(c_v^{(r,r')}\), and per-team allocations \(x_t^r=D_t^r(p_{v(t)}^r)\).

The question is to find prices, flows, conversions, and allocations satisfying the paper’s market axioms: local flow conservation with free disposal only at zero price, zero-profit money conservation at each non-root node, and conversion complementarity. The objective is not to maximize a welfare function: as in the paper, the task is to compute a feasible market-clearing outcome. A solution is an equilibrium satisfying those conditions; its allocation is effectively unique.

Theorem 3.1’s content is narrower than that whole task, and I would not overstate it. For a node \(i\) with its parent’s prices fixed, any local price vector solving the paper’s concave program admits conversion amounts satisfying modified flow conservation and conversion complementarity. Also, each price \(p_i^r\) is unique when the budget summed over \(i\)’s subtree is positive or the node has positive local supply of \(r\). The program maximizes
\[ \sum_r \beta_i^r\log p_i^r -\sum_r p_i^r\left( \frac{\mathbf{1}[i\ne\mathrm{root}]}{p_{\pi(i)}^r}\beta_i^r+s_i^r \right), \qquad \beta_i^r=\sum_{j\in\mathrm{SubT}(i)}B_j^r, \]
subject to the price-order constraints induced by the conversion graph. In the mirror, subtree budget coefficients become mass-weighted sums of type budgets. The paper proves the theorem here; it also says that the harmonic convex-program formulation yields a polynomial-time algorithm. The theorem itself does not state a bit-complexity bound for the continuous mirror.

I expect this problem to be tractable: solve the local convex programs down the finite hierarchy, after aggregating budgets by type mass. A computational version should ask for an \(\epsilon\)-accurate equilibrium allocation, with complexity stated in the hierarchy size, \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\). Establishing that precise bound for the mass-weighted instance is part of the mirror’s research question, not a theorem already proved by this paper. Further questions include whether the computation can depend chiefly on \(\tau\) rather than \(N\), how an equilibrium for a type distribution approximates a large finite team population, and whether the paper’s capped-demand results extend under the same aggregation.

This case covers the harmonic computation anchored by Theorem 3.1. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s general-demand Budget Descent results or its simulations. Its weakest point is that the paper’s agents are organizational nodes, while this mirror treats many teams inside a fixed hierarchy as the population. That is a genuine modeling extension. It seems acceptable in the specified regime because the organizational nodes and their authority structure remain intact; only interchangeable teams’ demands and budgets are aggregated by mass. The mirror continuizes the population, not the divisible resources or the equilibrium concept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is that Theorem 3.1 computes local prices on the paper’s organizational tree; the main problem size is the number of hierarchy nodes and resources, not repeated voters or agents. If each team keeps its own place in the tree and its own budget vector as part of its type, then typically \(\tau\) is close to \(N\), so there is little compression. The paper’s Google example also describes team-specific budgets and demand caps. And Theorem 3.1 is narrower than the proponent’s headline problem: it gives a local price program conditional on the parent’s prices; it does not by itself give the full equilibrium algorithm or a bit-complexity bound for a mass-weighted version.

That objection does not survive the best version of the mirror. Keep the business-unit hierarchy fixed and model many interchangeable leaf teams in each unit as a cohort. Let a type record the unit and its per-team harmonic budget vector. For type mass \(\mu_t\), replace the cohort by aggregate budget \(\mu_t b_t^r\). The subtree coefficients in the paper’s convex programs then become the corresponding mass-weighted sums. Identical teams receive the same per-team allocation, and their identities play no role in the market axioms. This is a coherent high-multiplicity regime that continuizes the population while retaining the hierarchy and equilibrium concept.

The paper’s snapshot may have many distinct types, and a useful finite-to-mass approximation would need its own analysis. But those are limitations of the observed instance, not reasons the cohort regime is incoherent. The related equilibrium literature is analytic background, not an existing computational treatment of this population model. I can make a case that this is a modest extension with uncertain practical compression; I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 3.1 survives the strongest objection.
