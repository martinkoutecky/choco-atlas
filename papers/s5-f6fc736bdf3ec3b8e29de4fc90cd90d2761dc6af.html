The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings with positive rational masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to \(1\), a target \(c\in C\), and one of Theorem 11’s distance limits, let \(R(t)\) be the rankings within that limit of \(t\). Decide whether there are \(x_{t,\rho}\geq 0\) for \(\rho\in R(t)\) such that \(\sum_{\rho\in R(t)}x_{t,\rho}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and \(c\) is the unique Borda winner in the resulting society; equivalently, for every \(a\neq c\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\rho\in R(t)}x_{t,\rho}\bigl((|C|-\operatorname{pos}(c,\rho))-(|C|-\operatorname{pos}(a,\rho))\bigr)>0\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete ranking types with rational mass; decision variables \(x_{t,\rho}\) allocate each type’s mass among rankings allowed by its local distance limit, and the resulting Borda margins must make the target the unique winner.

The objection that survived

The opponent’s strongest point is that the continuous problem already has a polynomial solution in an outside framework, with path-matching pricing for the maximum-displacement case; this limits novelty but does not invalidate the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 11’s Borda hardness results for swap distance at most \(1\), maximum displacement distance at most \(1\), and footrule distance at most \(2\); it leaves the paper’s other rules, distance thresholds, and results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For larger or input-dependent local distance limits, when does pricing over the reachable rankings remain polynomial-time solvable?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 11, proved in this paper by reductions from TSAT. It establishes that the paper’s unique-winner \( \mathrm{OB} \) problem is NP-complete for Borda when each voter may change their ranking by swap distance at most \(1\), and also when the maximum-displacement distance is at most \(1\). It further establishes NP-completeness for footrule distance at most \(2\). The reduction is proved here; TSAT’s NP-completeness is cited.

A direct mirror is Typed Continuous Local Borda Bribery. Its input is a candidate set \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings, a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each \(t\in T\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a target candidate \(c\), and one of the three distance limits in Theorem 11. For each original ranking \(t\), let \(R(t)\) be the rankings within that distance limit. Choose \(x_{t,\rho}\geq 0\) for \(\rho\in R(t)\), with \(\sum_{\rho\in R(t)}x_{t,\rho}=\mu_t\). The question is whether there is such an allocation for which \(c\) is the unique Borda winner: for every \(a\neq c\),
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\rho\in R(t)} x_{t,\rho}\bigl((m-\operatorname{pos}(c,\rho))-(m-\operatorname{pos}(a,\rho))\bigr)>0. \]
A solution is the allocation \(x\). Each person represented by the mass still casts one complete ranking within their individual distance limit; \(x_{t,\rho}\) says what fraction of type \(t\) reports \(\rho\).

The regime could be a large membership election in which many people choose among a modest menu of exact ranking templates and share the same local reluctance to change their ballot. For example, \(n\) could be in the hundreds of thousands while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct rankings is in the tens or hundreds. This fits the paper’s reputation-based motivation: the model preserves the same candidates, Borda rule, target, and per-person constraint, and reports how much of each voter group makes each permitted change. The authors would likely recognize it as their bribery question on a high-multiplicity population.

I expect Class A for this mirror. With swap distance \(1\), each type has only its original ranking and the \(m-1\) rankings obtained by one adjacent swap. Footrule distance \(2\) gives the same allowed changes. Thus an explicit LP has \(O(\tau m)\) allocation variables; maximizing the minimum Borda margin decides whether the optimum is positive. For maximum displacement \(1\), a type can make any set of nonoverlapping adjacent swaps. The implicit LP’s pricing problem is maximum-weight matching on a path, so I expect that variant to be tractable as well, using the matching pricing oracle.

This mirrors all three variants in Theorem 11, with the swap and footrule cases the clearest lead. It does not claim that the discrete NP-hardness disappears just because the population is large. The weakness is precisely the fractional allocation: given a finite profile with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), a discrete plan uses multiples of \(1/n\), while the continuous model may split type mass more finely. That difference can change the winner when margins are small. A natural next question is how much winning margin suffices to round a continuous solution back to a large finite population, and whether larger local distance limits retain efficient pricing.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s Borda mirror is already a special case of the continuous move model in [*Continuous Computational Social Choice: A Case Study in Bribery*](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25444). That model represents each type by its initial ranking and move costs, lets real-valued mass move among rankings, and permits infinite costs for forbidden moves. Its Theorem 1 solves scoring-rule instances in polynomial time when the reachable output rankings can be enumerated in polynomial time. Set a type’s cost to \(0\) for rankings within the paper’s local distance limit and \(+\infty\) otherwise. For swap distance \(1\) and footrule distance \(2\), there are at most \(m\) allowed rankings per input type, so \(T\leq m\tau\). The theorem therefore covers the proposed continuous decision problem directly. Its common-slack method also handles the requirement that the Borda winner be unique.

The maximum-displacement-\(1\) version does not rescue the anchor. Its allowed rankings come from disjoint adjacent swaps; pricing a linear objective over them is maximum-weight matching on a path. That supplies the oracle for the same configuration-LP approach. The finite-destination theorem does not itself state this variant, but the proposed mirror adds no new population model, and its remaining optimization step is the matching calculation the proponent already identifies.

The negative case is strongest as a novelty objection to this anchor, not as a claim that high-multiplicity elections are implausible. The continuous question is coherent, and voters with identical rankings can sensibly be grouped. But the exact fixed-radius Borda mirror is already represented and solved in the existing continuous framework. Extending to a variable radius could pose a further pricing problem; that would go beyond the result the proponent anchored on, so it does not restore this mirror’s novelty.
