What the mirror covers

The unanchored mirror covers computing NNV winners from ballot-type masses; it leaves the parameter derivation, voter-satisfaction simulations, and comparisons with ranked systems untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper’s rule is naturally defined on a population distribution: its outcome depends only on aggregate positive and negative votes, and its metric is homogeneous in those aggregates. Replacing voter counts by proportions therefore preserves the winner. A plausible regime is a large election using a fixed digital ballot menu: a type is a complete normed negative ballot, many voters may share it, and the number of distinct ballots \(\tau\) is small relative to the electorate. A finite menu is needed to fit ChoCo’s finite-type model; the paper itself allows arbitrary vote magnitudes.

There is a strict limit, though: the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, and no complexity classification to cite. Its equations define the rule and derive parameter constraints; its simulation table reports correlations with voter satisfaction. Neither is a qualifying anchor. So there are zero anchors, and no honest way to provide a continuous problem *per anchor*.

If an unanchored computational question is useful, the closest mirror of the paper’s actual rule is NNV Winner Determination\(_\infty\). Fix \(m\) candidates and a finite menu \(\mathcal B\) of ballots \(t=((p_j(t),n_j(t)))_{j=1}^m\), where \(p_j,n_j\ge0\), \(p_jn_j=0\), and \(\sum_j(p_j+n_j)=10\). Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and fixed admissible rational parameters \(c,b>0\), compute the set of winning candidates, assuming \(P_j(\mu)>0\) for every candidate, where
\[ P_j(\mu)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal B}\mu_t p_j(t),\qquad N_j(\mu)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal B}\mu_t n_j(t). \]
A candidate with \(N_j/P_j>1\) is disqualified; among the rest, winners maximize
\[ W_b^c(P_j,N_j)=\frac{P_j-cN_j}{1+bN_j/P_j}. \]
The solution is the set of maximizers, including ties. Direct aggregation and exact comparison solve this in polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. This is a sensible continuous-population version of the rule, and its outcome computation is tractable. But that tractability follows immediately from the definition; it is not a result proved or claimed by this paper, so it cannot serve as the requested anchor.

The mirror covers only winner selection under NNV. A further, unanchored question would be the minimum-cost mass transfer between ballot types needed to make a target candidate win, connecting the paper’s discussion of strategic voting to ChoCo’s computational questions. The weakest point is that a finite ballot menu may constrain the paper’s intended freedom to choose real-valued magnitudes; the authors might accept finite ballot precision as an implementation detail, but the text does not establish that. The positive case is therefore plausible as a new computational formulation, but weak as a case anchored in this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper offers no named computational result to continuize. It has no theorem or algorithm about the complexity of a computational problem: it defines a voting rule, derives constraints on its parameters, and reports simulations. So the proponent is right that there are zero anchors. There is no continuous question per result to defend, and the paper itself makes no complexity claim for ChoCo to classify.

The proposed Winner Determination\(_\infty\) is a reasonable formalization of the rule, but it adds no computational result from the paper. The rule already depends only on aggregate votes \(P_j,N_j\). For \(\lambda>0\), \(W_b^c(\lambda P,\lambda N)=\lambda W_b^c(P,N)\), while \(N/P\) is unchanged, so scaling an election to proportions preserves its winners. Given type masses, one can compute those aggregates and compare candidates in polynomial time. That makes winner determination easy to state; it does not turn the paper’s parameter derivation or simulation into a complexity result.

The strongest alternative is a minimum-mass manipulation question: how much society must change its ballots for a target candidate to win? That has a plausible large-election setting and connects to the paper’s discussion of strategic voting. But the paper does not define a manipulation problem: it supplies no formal truthful types, allowed reports, coalition objective, or cost of changing a ballot. Choosing those would create a new computational model, not continuize a result the paper proved. The authors’ claim about strategic incentives is informal, not a computational claim with a specified problem behind it.

This is a strong case against presenting the paper as an anchored ChoCo complexity contribution. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from the paper. A formal mass-manipulation problem could be valuable in its own right; that is where the negative case is weakest.
