What the mirror covers

No named result qualifies for a computational mirror: Theorems 2 and 5 establish rule guarantees, and Proposition 1 establishes a hierarchy of guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchored computational case for a continuous mirror: this paper has no named result asserting a problem is in \(P\), hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity.

The closest anchors are Theorem 2, proved here, which shows Randomized Death Match is Condorcet consistent, monotone, and \(2\)-MNM-\(3/2\) for every tournament, with \(3/2\) optimal among Condorcet consistent rules; and Theorem 5, proved here, which shows \(\BBWS\) is Condorcet consistent, monotone, and \(2\)-NM\(_2\), with that guarantee tight for the rule. Proposition 1, also proved here, establishes the implication hierarchy among the three non-manipulability notions. These are results about strategic guarantees and rule design, not computational complexity. The explicit rule descriptions make computation possible, but the paper does not give a named complexity result about computing their outputs. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor prover question to state.

The best positive foothold is that teams are strategic agents and the object being designed is a rule that assigns winner probabilities. One could study a continuum tournament: a population measure over team types, a measurable pairwise-results kernel, and a rule returning a probability measure over teams. The question might be to minimize a multiplicative or gain–loss bound against coalitions that alter results within a positive-mass bloc. But that changes the paper’s central manipulation: a coalition of two particular teams has zero mass in a nonatomic population, and changing their match is invisible to the aggregate kernel. Giving coalitions positive mass changes the coalition model; retaining the exact two-team manipulation keeps the relevant population atomic. I don’t think the authors or a referee would accept this as the continuous computational form of Theorem 2 or Theorem 5.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that none of the paper’s named results classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 2 proves a manipulation guarantee for Randomized Death Match and an optimal bound among Condorcet-consistent rules. Theorem 5 proves a guarantee for \(\BBWS\), tight for that rule. Proposition 1 derives implications among three axiomatic guarantees. The rules can be evaluated computationally, but the paper does not study the complexity of evaluating them, finding a manipulation, or designing a rule. Those could be new questions; they are not computational results here to mirror.

The attempted continuum tournament also lacks the population structure the programme needs. The input is an arbitrary orientation of every pair of teams, and the output assigns winning probabilities to individual teams. A type that preserves a team’s relevant tournament information must preserve its relations to the other teams; grouping teams by a coarser archetype leaves those pairwise outcomes unspecified. If the type includes the full interaction pattern, the grouping generally collapses back to individual teams.

The problem is especially sharp for the paper’s pairwise manipulation guarantees. Represent a nonatomic continuum of teams by a measure \(\mu\) and a pairwise-results kernel \(W(x,y)\). Reversing one match changes \(W\) on a \(\mu\times\mu\)-null set. A rule defined from the aggregate kernel therefore cannot register that deviation, and a diffuse winner distribution assigns zero probability to the two named teams. The \(2\)-MNM and \(2\)-NM inequalities then become vacuous. Retaining pointwise identities so that the changed edge still matters means the input is no longer just a population distribution over types.

A better proposal would let a positive-mass bloc manipulate its internal matches and measure the probability that the winner comes from that bloc. But this replaces a coalition of at most two teams changing one edge with a bulk coalition changing a positive-measure set of edges. It is a different strategic question. It also requires new continuum versions of the rules: RDM’s process removes one team at a time, which removes zero mass in the limit, while \(\BBWS\)’s almost-Condorcet winners are defined by having exactly one loss. Replacing these with depletion dynamics or fractional loss thresholds may be interesting, but it is new model design, not a faithful continuous formulation of Theorems 2 or 5. Proposition 1 fares no better: its implication is algebraic and needs no computational mirror.

I cannot rule out a worthwhile project on rule design for tournament limits, such as optimizing guarantees against positive-mass blocs. That is the weak point of the negative case: it is not impossible to invent a compelling adjacent problem. But it would be a new continuum tournament programme, with no clear high-multiplicity encoding or computational result in this paper to anchor it.
