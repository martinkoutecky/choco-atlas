The continuous mirror question

Given finite-support distributions of ideal-point types λ ∈ [0,1] with rational masses for EP and CM, simple majority in EP, a quota θ in CM, and \(q\) uniform on \([0,1]\), compute each chamber’s exact expected marginal pass-through. For each \(q\), use the direction-appropriate pivotal quantiles \(p_h(q)\); if both pivots favor change on the same side of \(q\), set \(x^*(q)\) to the pivot nearer \(q\), and otherwise set \(x^*(q)=q\), as in Proposition 1. Return \(ξ_h=∫_0^1 ∂x^*(q)/∂p_h(q)\,dq\) for each chamber.

The model it lives in

Each chamber is a unit-mass population over preference types λ with rational type masses. Internal voting maps those masses to a median or quota quantile, and the resulting representatives bargain over one-dimensional policies using the paper’s symmetric Nash rule; power is expected marginal pass-through under a uniform status quo.

The objection that survived

The derivative is with respect to a chamber’s pivotal representative position, not a perturbation of mass across types, so the measure does not capture how changes in population composition affect power.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s bargaining rule and the paper’s expected marginal pass-through comparison for large chambers. It leaves Proposition 2’s broader utility and policy-space conditions, detailed weighted-voting variants, and repeated-game logrolling aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Establish the exact bit complexity of computing both pass-through values from rational type masses and a rational Council quota.
  • Determine how the calculation changes under the Council’s weighted voting rule.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named computational result to anchor this case. The paper’s numbered results are Proposition 1, proved here, which characterizes the one-dimensional symmetric Nash bargain with linear distance utilities, and Proposition 2, also proved here, which gives a status-quo proximity result for a broader class of utilities and policy spaces. Neither asserts that a computational problem is tractable or hard. The paper’s headline power figures—such as \(2.3\%\) for EP and \(59.0\%\) for CM in Table 1’s varying-status-quo baseline—are numerical findings, not numbered computational results of the required kind.

The strongest plausible positive case is therefore a research question suggested by the paper, rather than a mirror anchored in one of its computational results. Call it High-Multiplicity Strategic Codecision Power. Consider two large chambers. A member’s type records their chamber, policy ideal point \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), utility function, and voting weight. In the basic version, members within a type have the same ideal point and utility \(u(x)=-|\lambda-x|\), and votes have equal weight. Each chamber is given by a finite-support distribution of types with rational masses; the European Parliament analogue uses simple majority, and the Council analogue uses a quota \(\theta\). The status quo \(q\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), as in the paper’s constitutional analysis.

For each \(q\), use the chamber rules to find the pivotal ideal points for a move to the right or left of \(q\). In the continuum, these are quantiles: the Parliament’s median and, for the Council, the \(1-\theta\) and \(\theta\) quantiles for rightward and leftward proposals, respectively. Apply the paper’s symmetric bargaining rule to predict the policy: when both chambers support changing the status quo in the same direction, the bargain selects the ideal point nearer \(q\); otherwise the outcome is \(q\). The problem is to return each chamber’s exact expected marginal pass-through, \(\xi_h=\mathbb{E}_q[\partial x^*/\partial p_h]\), where \(p_h\) is that chamber’s direction-appropriate pivotal ideal point and \(x^*\) is the predicted policy. This asks who shapes the decision under the same strategic bargaining model as the paper, with chamber membership represented by type masses.

A plausible regime would be a large membership federation or digital governing body with many delegates but a relatively small set of shared policy positions. If there are \(N\) members and only \(\tau\) distinct types, the input describes the composition by \(\tau\) rational masses rather than by \(N\) individually named people. The EU institutions in the paper are not themselves a convincing high-multiplicity regime: the Council has only 25 members, and the paper’s 732 MEPs are modelled as having independently drawn ideal points. The alternative scenario is what makes the mirror plausible.

I would expect this particular evaluation problem to be tractable: sort the type support, find the required quantiles, and integrate the resulting piecewise-constant sensitivity over \(q\). That is a derived expectation, not a result proved by the paper. Further questions include how the calculation changes with weighted voting, multidimensional policy, or cross-issue logrolling. The mirror covers the strategic power question built on the paper’s bargaining model; it does not continuize the outcome space as its main move.

The weakest point is the change in interpretation: the paper’s finite members’ ideal points are random draws used to define a priori power, whereas the mirror takes a deterministic distribution of types as the chamber’s composition. Proposition 1 supports the bargaining step, but it does not establish a computational result about the proposed problem. So the positive case is plausible as a new high-multiplicity formulation of the paper’s question, but it cannot satisfy the requested named-anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes a bargaining outcome, and Proposition 2 gives a proximity property under broader assumptions. The power figures in Table 1 are quantitative findings from the model; none states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm for one. So the proponent’s “High-Multiplicity Strategic Codecision Power” is a proposed new question, not a mirror anchored in a result of this paper.

The proposed large-assembly version is coherent: give each chamber a finite-support distribution of ideal points, derive pivotal quantiles, and measure expected policy sensitivity over the status quo. The EU’s own bodies are a poor high-multiplicity instance, but a larger federation could supply a plausible one. The deeper mismatch is what the quantity measures. In the paper, power is sensitivity to shifting the ideal point of a pivotal member drawn from a finite institution. In the proposed continuum, the derivative is with respect to the chamber’s pivotal quantile. That is a sensitivity of the chamber’s representative position, not an effect of changing the society’s type masses. To ask about mass changes instead, one must choose a perturbation—moving a bloc’s ideal point, reallocating mass between types, or changing the quota. Each defines a different power measure, and none is supplied by the paper’s results.

One could build an algorithmic problem around one of those choices, or add costs and ask how to campaign to change a chamber’s position. But that would add an intervention objective and its rules; it would be a new problem suggested by the paper’s setting. The paper itself asks who shapes a predicted bargaining outcome under an assumed prior. Its results do not pose a search, feasibility, or optimization problem over a society that a continuous-population formulation can carry forward.

That is the strongest case against the mirror: the proponent has a plausible continuous model, but not a continuous version of a named computational result in this paper, and making it an intervention problem requires substantial new modelling. The case is weaker as a universal claim. A reader who counts algorithmic evaluation of the proposed sensitivity measure as a worthwhile ChoCo question could reasonably accept it; the paper does not rule that out.
