The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a bundle set \(B=\prod_{r=1}^{R}\{0,\ldots,q_r\}\), rational valuations \(V_t:B\to\mathbb{Q}\), and per-capita supplies \(b_r\), choose nonnegative masses \(x_{t,a}\) to maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in B}x_{t,a}V_t(a)\), subject to \(\sum_{a\in B}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in B}a_r x_{t,a}\le b_r\) for every resource \(r\). Return an optimal rational assignment, with each unit of mass receiving one integral bundle.

The model it lives in

A continuous population of recurring cloud-job types, where each type specifies a complete valuation table and \(\mu_t\) is its population share. The decision variables \(x_{t,a}\) distribute each type’s mass among integral bundles; the objective is aggregate welfare and the constraints are per-capita resource supplies. With explicit valuation tables, this is an LP with \(|T||B|\) variables.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s welfare-maximizing allocation problem. It leaves aside VCG payments and individual truthfulness, the paper’s runtime analysis for its specialized algorithm, the data-structure comparisons, and the experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • How large must a finite cohort be for its optimal allocation to approximate the mass optimum within a specified additive error?
  • How does the optimization complexity change when valuation functions are represented succinctly instead of as explicit tables?
  • Can a continuous-population mechanism retain meaningful VCG-style incentives when an individual agent has negligible effect on the allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for any subset \(G\) of agents and any resource bound \(\vec m\), the authors’ algorithm finds an optimal feasible allocation \(\hat A_G^{\vec m}\): each agent receives an integral bundle, total use is at most \(\vec m\), and the allocation maximizes the sum of the agents’ valuations. That is the paper’s underlying welfare-optimization problem, not its payment computation.

There is an anchor caveat: Theorem 2 proves correctness but states no running-time bound. The paper’s complexity claims appear in Section 5’s prose, not in a numbered theorem. So this is a named algorithmic near-anchor; if the requirement is strictly a numbered result asserting a complexity classification, the paper has no qualifying anchor.

A credible population regime is a large market of recurring, standardized cloud jobs: many jobs compete for resources, but only a modest number \(\tau\) of workload classes occur. A type \(t\) is the job’s complete valuation table over the allowed bundles, so jobs of one type are indistinguishable in every respect used by the allocation problem. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of jobs in that class. Per-capita resource supply scales with the population. This is a scenario-specific case, not a claim that ordinary IaaS clients generally share valuations.

Here is the lead mirror, Continuous Mass Multi-Resource Welfare Allocation. Let \(B=\prod_{r=1}^R\{0,\ldots,q_r\}\) be the finite set of integral bundles, let each supported type \(t\) have a rational valuation \(V_t:B\to\mathbb Q\), and let \(\mu_t\ge0\) be rational masses summing to \(1\). Given per-capita supplies \(b_r\), choose \(x_{t,a}\ge0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(a\), to maximize

\[ \sum_{t}\sum_{a\in B} x_{t,a}V_t(a) \]

subject to

\[ \sum_{a\in B}x_{t,a}=\mu_t \quad\text{for each }t, \qquad \sum_t\sum_{a\in B}a_r x_{t,a}\le b_r \quad\text{for each }r. \]

A solution is an optimal rational mass assignment \(x\). Every unit of mass receives one whole, integral bundle; it is the aggregate assignment that is fractional. Thus the mirror continuizes the population, not the goods or the outcome space.

For this explicit valuation-table input, the problem is a linear program with \(\tau|B|\) variables, so I expect it to be tractable (Class A) in \(\tau\), \(|B|\), and the input bit length. It also has a concrete high-multiplicity bridge: because the LP has rational data, a rational optimum can be scaled to a sufficiently large finite cohort, with integer numbers of jobs of each type and bundle. Conversely, normalizing such a finite allocation gives a feasible mass assignment. This correspondence is for suitable cohort sizes; it does not claim that every small finite auction has the same optimum as the continuum.

The scope is deliberately limited to Theorem 2’s welfare-maximizing allocation. It does not claim a continuous VCG mechanism with truthful individual incentives or the paper’s exclusion-compensation payments. In an atomless population, a single agent’s report may not affect the outcome, so those mechanism questions need their own model. Natural follow-ups are to bound how well the mass optimum approximates smaller finite cohorts, and to study whether useful guarantees survive when valuations are given succinctly rather than as complete tables.

The weakest point is the gap between this mirror and the paper’s main computational contribution. The LP preserves the exact welfare objective and integral bundle choices per agent, so it is a sensible analogue of the problem in Theorem 2. But it replaces the paper’s distinct-client allocation setting with a type-homogeneous population and makes the mass assignment directly solvable by standard LP machinery. A referee may accept it as a clean high-multiplicity version of the authors’ welfare problem while viewing it as a basic relaxation rather than a continuation of their specialized algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against the named anchor is strong: Theorem 2 proves that the finite-agent algorithm finds an optimal allocation, but gives no complexity bound. The runtime claims appear in the abstract and Section 5’s prose. Under the programme’s anchor criterion, Theorem 2 is not a numbered complexity result to mirror.

That does not dispose of the proponent’s best welfare mirror. Their type-mass LP is coherent: each unit of mass still receives an integral bundle, while repeated agents of one complete valuation type can be split among bundles. With explicitly listed valuation tables, it has \(\tau N\) variables, where \(N=\prod_r(q_r+1)\), so standard LP methods solve it in time polynomial in the explicit input size. This is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the welfare problem. Calling it a routine configuration LP weakens its claim to be a worthwhile continuation of the paper’s specialized algorithm, but routine tractability is still a valid Class A outcome under the programme. I cannot use “the answer is easy” to defeat it.

The sharper objection applies to the paper’s VCG contribution. In an atomless population, removing one agent changes no mass, so the leave-one-agent-out externality vanishes. Treating a whole type as the bidder instead changes the exclusion operation; taking a derivative or a shadow price defines a different payment rule. Thus the LP does not mirror the paper’s VCG payments or individual truthfulness. But the proponent explicitly limited the anchor to welfare maximization, so this objection does not defeat that anchor.

My strongest negative case is therefore narrow: the paper lacks a numbered complexity theorem, and its continuous VCG-payment analogue loses the individual pivotal effect the mechanism needs. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is weak, though. The welfare LP survives the main objections as a sensible population mirror, even if it is a modest one.
