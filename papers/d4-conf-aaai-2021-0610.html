What the mirror covers

The proposed model addresses only Theorem 4.7's multi-item auction guarantee; it leaves Theorem 3.3 and the paper's negative results unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is Cohort-Mass Privatized-Auction PoA, anchored on Theorem 4.7. It is an original result of this paper, not one cited from elsewhere: the conference version states it and sketches the proof, with technical details deferred to the authors’ full version. It says that for \(m\) items and \(n\ge m\) unit-demand agents with \(\gamma\)-heterogeneous, single-crossing valuations, if \(d\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\ge\mathrm{OPT}\), then the Bayesian price of anarchy of simultaneous privatized second-price auctions under no-overbidding is \(O(d\gamma^2)\).

This is a welfare-performance result, not a conventional complexity classification: the paper gives no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. So it is a usable anchor only if the programme accepts mechanism-performance guarantees as computational results.

For the mirror, take a finite set \(T\) of buyer cohorts, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Members of a cohort share the same valuation and information role; a cohort’s signal is private to its members but may affect other cohorts’ values. This is a plausible regime for a large marketplace with many buyers in a small number of information-sharing groups. Give the market a supply \(\rho\) of identical copies per unit population. That scaling is needed for allocation to remain meaningful as the buyer population becomes continuous.

For a signal profile \(s\), let \(v_t(s)\) be a cohort member’s value and let \(\tilde v_t(s)=\min_{r\ne t}v_t(s_{-r},0_r)\), the cohort-level analogue of the paper’s truncated value. Define \(\mathrm{OPT}(s)\) and \(\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}(s)\) by allocating at most mass \(\mu_t\) from each cohort and at most supply \(\rho\), using respectively \(v_t\) and \(\tilde v_t\). Cohort strategies map their signals to bids and participation; the auction serves mass \(\rho\) in order of privatized values, splits ties at the cutoff, and charges the marginal losing bid.

The question is: for this continuum auction, under the paper’s single-crossing and \(\gamma\)-heterogeneity conditions and \(d\,\mathbb E[\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}]\ge\mathbb E[\mathrm{OPT}]\), is the worst-equilibrium welfare ratio bounded by \(O(d\gamma^2)\), independent of cohort masses? A solution would be a proof of that bound, or a continuum instance and equilibrium violating it. I expect the bound may carry over: the paper’s proof separates “SELF” and “OTHER” welfare and bounds them through deviations, which may admit a mass-integral version. The next questions are whether finite markets converge to this model and what dependence on \(d,\gamma\) is tight.

The weak point is substantial: the mirror changes the market by scaling item supply with population, and the cohort-level truncation changes the paper’s operation of removing one bidder’s signal. Those changes are what make a continuous allocation and nontrivial information asymmetry possible, but they need the authors’ assent. The result would be an equilibrium-analysis question, not yet a member of ChoCo’s complexity trichotomy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about the kind of result being mirrored. Theorem 4.7 proves a welfare guarantee for equilibria; it gives no complexity classification or algorithm. The simultaneous auction itself is simple. Its contribution is an equilibrium analysis, built around the “SELF” and “OTHER” decomposition. That makes the proposed question—whether the guarantee extends to a mass auction—an equilibrium-limit question, not one of ChoCo’s computational questions about algorithms for intervention or winner robustness.

The mass-supply model is a sensible co-modification, so that alone does not defeat the mirror. But it changes the strategic setting. With atomless buyers, each buyer has zero effect on market clearing, and the question becomes how equilibrium efficiency behaves in a price-taking Bayesian auction. If instead each cohort acts as a single player controlling its mass, the model gives coordinated cohorts strategic power that individual bidders do not have. Either version may be worth studying, but neither is simply Theorem 4.7 with population size replaced by mass. The single-item result in Theorem 3.3 faces the same issue: a fixed indivisible item serves zero population mass in the limit, so a nondegenerate mirror also needs scaled supply.

One could formulate a new computational problem, such as computing or certifying the worst equilibrium ratio from a finite encoding of cohort masses, signal priors, and valuation functions. But that requires choices the paper does not make, and the resulting problem would be computational equilibrium analysis for a nonatomic auction—not a computational result already anchored by this theorem. The proposed anchor therefore does not establish a worthwhile mirror for ChoCo as scoped.

This is not an airtight argument that no useful mirror exists in any scenario. A continuous equilibrium-computation project could be worthwhile on its own. The honest negative case is narrower: the proponent’s strongest mirror changes an equilibrium-performance theorem into a different equilibrium question, without connecting it to the programme’s complexity landscape.
