The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) divisible goods of supply \(1\) and a finite support \(T\) of buyer types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita budgets \(B_t\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t B_t=1\), and nested CES-Leontief utility parameters, start from positive prices \(p^0\) summing to \(1\) and run the paper’s synchronous update using aggregate type demand \(D_j(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{J\in\mathcal J_t}x^J_{t,j}(p)\). Does the resulting price-demand pair become a \(\delta\)-approximate equilibrium in the paper’s Definition 1 after \(T\ge \kappa_2\ln(1/p^0_{\min})/(\epsilon^2\delta^3)\) iterations for a permitted \(\epsilon\), with the bound and its computation expressed in the type data?

The model it lives in

A distribution over finitely many complete buyer types, each specifying a per-capita budget and nested CES-Leontief utility, faces divisible goods of supply \(1\). At prices \(p\), each unit of type mass chooses its optimal per-capita basket \(x_t(p)\); aggregate demand is the mass-weighted sum of those baskets, and the paper’s price update seeks a \(\delta\)-approximate equilibrium. For homogeneous utilities, this is exactly represented by one finite-market agent per type with budget \(\mu_t B_t\), while retaining the interpretation as a population distribution.

The objection that survived

The exact quotient means no separate convergence proof is needed, and constants may deteriorate with small effective type budgets; the proponent acknowledges the bit-complexity gap, while the quotient narrows the mirror’s novelty without invalidating it.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 22’s convergence guarantee for nested CES-Leontief utilities; it leaves the paper’s other utility families and results outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the iteration bound retain the \(\delta^{-3}\) dependence when stated in type-mass parameters?
  • Can the constants and computation be bounded polynomially in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), including dependence on the smallest effective budget \(\mu_t B_t\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is Type-Mass Nested CES-Leontief Tâtonnement, anchored on Theorem 22, which the paper proves. It shows that for finite-agent Fisher markets with nested CES-Leontief utilities, there are constants \(\kappa_1(a,b,c,\rho)\) and \(\kappa_2(a,b,c,\rho)\) such that, from a positive initial price vector whose coordinates sum to \(1\), choosing \(\epsilon\le 1/\kappa_1\) guarantees that after any \(T\ge \kappa_2\ln(1/p^0_{\min})/(\epsilon^2\delta^3)\) iterations, the resulting price-demand pair is a \(\delta\)-approximate equilibrium in the paper’s Definition 1. That definition requires each agent’s demand to be optimal at the current prices, each good’s excess demand to be at most \(\delta\), and any good undersold by more than \(\delta\) to have price at most \(\delta\). The theorem concerns the paper’s synchronous price update, \(p^{t+1}_j=p^t_j(1+\epsilon z_j(p^t))\). The paper has no comparable hardness result to anchor on, so this algorithmic result is the one I would put forward.

In the mirror, there are \(m\) divisible goods, each with supply \(1\), and a finite support \(T\) of buyer types. The instance gives each type a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a per-capita budget \(B_t\), normalized so \(\sum_t\mu_tB_t=1\). A type completely specifies its members’ budget and nested CES-Leontief utility parameters. For example, its utility has the form \(u_t(x)=\left(\sum_{J\in\mathcal J_t}\left(c_t^J\min_{j\in J}\{x_{t,j}^J/a_{t,j}^J\}\right)^\rho\right)^{1/\rho}\), with parameters in the paper’s permitted range. At prices \(p\), each unit of type mass chooses its optimal per-capita basket \(x_t(p)\) subject to budget \(B_t\). Aggregate demand is \(D_j(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{J\in\mathcal J_t}x_{t,j}^J(p)\), so \(z_j(p)=D_j(p)-1\).

The problem is: given those type data, a positive initial price vector \(p^0\) summing to \(1\), a permitted \(\epsilon\), and an accuracy \(\delta>0\), compute a price vector and per-type optimal baskets that form a \(\delta\)-approximate equilibrium under those aggregate-demand conditions. A precise target is to establish a convergence bound of the Theorem 22 form, with constants expressed in the type data, and to compute the resulting prices and baskets using the type support. As in the paper, the per-type demand optimization is assumed tractable. A rational distribution \(\mu\) corresponds to a high-multiplicity discrete market: if \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), each member of type \(t\) can have budget \(B_t/n\), and homogeneity of the utilities gives the same aggregate market after rescaling individual bundles. The update uses only aggregate demand, so collapsing identical buyers into mass-weighted types matches the substance of the process.

The regime I find convincing is a large managed cloud or cooperative computing market of the kind the paper itself motivates. There may be millions of users or workload instances, but many share a workload class, resource preferences, and budget tier; the support could be dozens or hundreds of types. Type mass then means a fraction of the participating population, and the type-level basket is the bundle requested by each member at the prevailing prices. This is not a claim that divisible goods make the population continuous: it is the population whose distribution is the model’s input.

I expect this mirror to be Class A in a regime with controlled utility and budget parameters. The proof route is to express the aggregate market and Eisenberg-Gale quantities as mass-weighted sums over types and adapt the paper’s convergence argument. The mirror covers Theorem 22’s nested CES-Leontief convergence result; it does not claim to cover every utility family or the paper’s other results. Further questions include whether the weighted proof yields the same \(\delta^{-3}\) rate, how its constants depend on the smallest type mass and budget, and whether the resulting algorithm has polynomial bit complexity in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input length \(L\).

The weakest point is that this is a high-multiplicity extension of an algorithmic market result, not a hardness result whose complexity might disappear in the continuum. Theorem 22 itself is for finitely many agents, and its stated constants do not establish a population-size-independent bit-complexity bound for the type-mass formulation. Still, the paper’s proof works through aggregate demand and convex-program structure—the objects that type masses preserve—and the cloud scenario makes a compact support description a sensible computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that its best version is already an exact quotient of the paper’s finite-agent market. For each type \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\), per-capita budget \(B_t\), and homogeneous utility \(u_t\), form one paper-style agent with budget \(b_t=\mu_t B_t\) and the same utility. At any prices, that agent can demand \(\mu_t\) times the type’s per-capita basket. Aggregate demand, excess demand, and therefore the entire price trajectory are identical. The Eisenberg–Gale objective changes only by the additive constant \(\sum_t \mu_t B_t\log\mu_t\). Theorem 22 thus applies directly to the proposed type-mass instance, with no new dynamics or optimization step. ([Avigdor-Elgrabli, Rabani, and Yadgar](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.6637))

That makes the mirror look low-yield as a new algorithmic result. The remaining technical question is whether the convergence bound gives polynomial bit complexity in \(m,\tau,L\) for unrestricted rational inputs. The theorem’s constants depend on market parameters, including the effective budgets \(\mu_tB_t\), and the paper does not establish such a uniform input-size bound. But that gap could itself motivate a useful computational question; restricting masses and utility parameters to controlled ranges also leaves a credible cloud-market regime.

So this is the best negative argument I can make, and it is limited. Exact reduction to the paper’s theorem is not a reason to reject a well-posed mirror under the programme’s rules, and the finite-support population scenario is sensible. I would argue for low priority on novelty grounds, but I cannot honestly defend the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
