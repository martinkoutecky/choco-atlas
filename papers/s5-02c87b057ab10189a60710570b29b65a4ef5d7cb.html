The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its only numbered theorem, Theorem 1 (proved here), says that under an \(M\)-optimal or \(W\)-optimal stable algorithm, any matching that is asymptotically stable under payoff-positive selection dynamics is stable. That is a structural implication; it gives no complexity classification. The paper’s simulations concern \(3\times3\) markets, and its discussion says checking asymptotic stability becomes infeasible as the number of players grows, but it proves no hardness or algorithmic result. The complexity results cited from other work are not results of this paper.

The closest positive lead is the paper’s model of large replicator populations and its strategy-share vectors \(x^i_h\). A possible extension would take a large matching market with finitely many complete preference-and-utility types \(t\), each carrying rational mass \(\mu_t\). Let \(x_{t,r}\) be the share of type \(t\) reporting ranking \(r\); aggregate reports determine a mass matching through a continuum version of deferred acceptance. One could ask whether a specified allocation has a product of report supports that always yields that allocation and is closed under weakly better replies. That is a concrete computational question about asymptotic stability in a high-multiplicity market.

But this would be a proposed extension, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result. The paper studies a fixed finite set of marriage-market roles; its replicator populations describe strategy shares for those roles. Extending the market itself to masses also requires choices about matching types and handling within-type indifference. That gap is the strongest weakness, and it prevents me from honestly presenting the extension as a qualifying case under the requested anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 proves a structural implication: under an \(M\)-optimal or \(W\)-optimal stable algorithm, an asymptotically stable matching is stable. It gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or hardness result. The simulations are for \(3\times3\) markets, and the paper’s remark that checking asymptotic stability becomes infeasible as markets grow is a motivation for future work, not a complexity result. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, those are not qualifying anchors.

The proposed extension also mistakes the paper’s replicator populations for a high-multiplicity matching market. Each simplex \(x^i\) tracks report shares for replicators occupying one particular player role \(i\); the paper still has a finite set of named players, and one replicator from each role plays the game. These shares are not masses of market participants who can be matched to one another. In the underlying marriage problem, reports rank named people and the outcome pairs particular people.

The strongest repair would define two continuum populations with finitely many complete preference-and-utility types, reports over the opposite side’s types, and a mass matching that determines type-level payoffs. One could then study whether report supports are closed under weakly better replies, or seek algorithms for that question. That is a coherent possible research project, but it defines an anonymous mean-field matching game; it does not continue any computational result in this paper. Even if the authors recognized it as a worthwhile extension, it would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a named computational result.

So the negative case is strong against a qualifying mirror under the programme’s anchor rule. It is weaker as a claim that no continuous follow-up could be worthwhile: the repaired mean-field game may be worth studying on its own. The paper does not give enough computational content to establish that as its continuous mirror.
