The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many opportunity types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), bidders \(B\), and normalized monotone diminishing-returns utilities \(F_b\), determine the worst-case ratio of the fluid greedy allocation to the offline optimum. Type \(t\) arrives at rate \(\mu_t\), and infinitesimal mass is assigned to a bidder maximizing its current marginal utility; in particular, determine whether the ratio is at least \(0.5096\) and whether a fixed-support instance can attain at most \(19/33\).

The model it lives in

A fluid online allocation model with finite opportunity types, mass \(\mu_t\) per type, integral assignment of each unit of mass to a bidder, and a diminishing-returns utility over type-level allocations. Greedy assigns arriving mass at time \(s\in[0,1]\) to a bidder with maximum current marginal utility.

The objection that survived

The randomized units in the paper are arriving items or partition stages, while its economic agents are bidders; the proponent does not establish that treating impression cohorts as the population is the authors’ intended high-multiplicity regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the random-order greedy guarantees and obstruction in Theorems 1 and 2, including their Submodular Welfare Maximization interpretation. It leaves the paper’s offline, general-matroid, and other arrival-model results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which mass-level utility scalings and convergence assumptions make the opportunity-type model a faithful high-multiplicity version of the paper’s discrete inputs?
  • What is the worst-case fluid greedy ratio, and does the four-type weighted-coverage obstruction survive?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is the random-order online Submodular Welfare Maximization problem. The arriving items become the population: think of a very large stream of ad opportunities drawn from a small set of inventory categories. A type \(t\) specifies everything relevant about an opportunity—its eligibility and its effect on each advertiser’s value for the portfolio already assigned to them. Millions of opportunities might fall into a dozen such types. The advertisers remain the recipients; the mass is the fraction of opportunities of each type.

Call the continuous problem Fluid Random-Order Submodular Welfare. Its input is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a finite bidder set \(B\). An allocation is a vector \(x=(x_{t,b})\), where \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to bidder \(b\), with \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\). The objective is \(F(x)=\sum_{b\in B}F_b((x_{t,b})_{t\in T})\), where each \(F_b\) is a normalized, monotone, diminishing-returns submodular utility over the mass of each type it receives. The offline benchmark is \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_x F(x)\) over feasible allocations.

For the online rule, normalize arrival time to \(s\in[0,1]\). In the high-multiplicity limit of a uniform random permutation, type \(t\) arrives at rate \(\mu_t\). When infinitesimal mass of type \(t\) arrives, the greedy policy assigns it to a bidder with maximum current marginal value, \(\partial F/\partial x_{t,b}\), with a fixed tie-breaking rule. Its solution is the final allocation \(x^G(1)\). The continuous question is to determine the worst-case ratio
\[ \rho_{\mathrm{fluid}}=\inf_{(T,\mu,F)}\frac{F(x^G(1))}{\mathrm{OPT}}. \]
This is the population limit of the allocation problem itself: each opportunity still goes to one bidder, while type-level averages \(x_{t,b}\) can be fractional.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that Algorithm 1 achieves an approximation ratio of at least \(0.5096\) for maximizing a non-negative monotone submodular function subject to a partition matroid constraint, when the parts arrive in uniformly random order. By the paper’s standard reduction, the same guarantee applies to online SWM. Its continuous mirror asks for a proof that \(\rho_{\mathrm{fluid}}\ge 0.5096\), and, beyond that, for the exact worst-case fluid ratio. The expected direction is tractable: for regular type-symmetric instances whose normalized utilities and greedy trajectories converge, the finite guarantee should pass to the limit. The paper proves the discrete bound here; the limiting statement would establish whether its improvement over \(1/2\) remains in a genuinely high-multiplicity market.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here, with its stronger construction in the appendix. It says that there exist a partition matroid \(M\) and a non-negative monotone submodular function \(f\) such that Algorithm 1’s approximation ratio is at most \(19/33\le 0.576\). Its continuous mirror is the other side of the same \(\rho_{\mathrm{fluid}}\) question: exhibit a fixed-support mass instance with ratio at most \(19/33\), or establish that the finite obstruction disappears in the fluid model. A concrete prover task is to use four cohort types, each of mass \(1/4\), with finite action sets corresponding to the four parts in the paper’s weighted-coverage construction. For covered features \(u\) of weight \(w_u\), use the continuous coverage objective
\[ F(x)=\sum_u w_u\min\left\{1,\sum_{t,a:\,u\in S_{t,a}}\frac{x_{t,a}}{\mu_t}\right\}, \]
where \(S_{t,a}\) is the feature set of action \(a\) for type \(t\). When each cohort puts all its mass on one action, this recovers the finite coverage value; fractional mass can split across actions, as a proper high-multiplicity mirror allows. A solution would give an explicit instance and its greedy trajectory, or rule out such a witness in this four-type coverage class. I expect this restricted question to be tractable to analyze, and I expect some strict upper obstruction to survive, but I would not claim the exact \(19/33\) bound without that construction. The finite theorem is evidence and a template, not a proof of the continuous claim.

These are separate mirrors of the lower guarantee and the upper obstruction; together they ask whether the fluid greedy ratio lies in a comparable interval. The scope is the online random-order allocation results for partition matroids and SWM. The paper does not need to have its entire offline or general-matroid landscape continuized for this case to be worthwhile.

The weakest point is the passage from random finite order to proportional fluid arrivals. In the limit, type counts along prefixes concentrate, so the random sequence becomes a deterministic type flow; the finite theorem’s gain might depend on fluctuations that vanish. Also, the discrete paper does not prescribe a unique continuous extension of every utility function. The mirror therefore needs to state its mass-level utility class and convergence assumptions, as above. That softens the claim about the precise numerical ratios, especially Theorem 2’s obstruction. It does not undermine the central case: many repeated, interchangeable allocation opportunities over few types are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the resulting question remains the paper’s online submodular allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch between the paper’s unit of arrival and the proposed population type. In the paper, a partition part is one distinct online decision stage; its elements are alternative actions. The random permutation is over those stages. In the proposed fluid model, a type is a cohort of many arrivals, each with the same menu. That is a coherent new model, but the paper’s theorems do not specify its objective or dynamics.

This matters most for Theorem 1. A type in a high-multiplicity instance must preserve everything that affects an individual’s treatment. For general submodular valuations, that includes how each item interacts with every possible set already assigned to a bidder. Often those interactions distinguish the items, so the number of types grows with the instance. If items are exact copies, the original set function may give the first copy a full coverage benefit and later copies none. The proponent’s fluid coverage formula instead spreads that benefit across the cohort and allows fractional coverage. That may be a sensible per-capita model, but it is a chosen scaling and extension of the utility, not one determined by the paper’s discrete input. Different continuous extensions can yield different marginal values and greedy trajectories.

There is a second gap in the proposed Theorem 1 mirror. A fixed-support population arriving at rates \(\mu_t\) produces a deterministic type flow in the large-population limit. The paper randomizes the order of distinct parts. To get that fluid flow from the discrete model, one must replicate parts, specify how utilities scale across copies, and show the replicated greedy process converges. The lower bound \(0.5096\) may transfer under suitable assumptions, as the proponent suggests; the paper itself does not establish those assumptions or that limit. The fluid ratio is therefore a reasonable research question, though its connection to this theorem needs its own model justification.

Theorem 2’s \(19/33\) construction has the same issue, and it is not an independent anchor: it bounds the same fluid ratio. Its four-part instance has one arrival per part. The proposed four cohorts instead let mass split continuously among actions, changing the process and objective. The displayed coverage formula recovers the finite value when each cohort chooses one action, but that does not show that fluid greedy follows the finite bad trajectory or achieves the same ratio. A larger repeated-instance construction would still need a defensible rule for scaling feature values and proving the fluid trajectory.

These objections defeat the claim that either numerical theorem already has the stated continuous mirror. They do not defeat the better version: a market with many similar impressions, a finite set of genuinely interchangeable impression types, and a specified concave or coverage utility over assigned mass gives a recognizable online allocation problem. The authors could reasonably regard its fluid greedy ratio as their problem. Adjacent work on online continuous submodular maximization studies regret for sequences of continuous objectives, rather than this population-type arrival model, so it does not settle that question ([Chen, Hassani, and Karbasi](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v84/chen18f.html)).

I cannot make a strong universal case against every mirror. The paper’s arbitrary submodular model has no canonical fixed-support fluid limit, and the proposed numerical bounds are not established for one. But the impression-cohort model is plausible, and its ratio question is well posed once the type-level utility is specified. The honest negative case is therefore narrow: the paper does not itself supply the continuous model or validate the claimed transfer. It does not show that the resulting research question is unworthy.
