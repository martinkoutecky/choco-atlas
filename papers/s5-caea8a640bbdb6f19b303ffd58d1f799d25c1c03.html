The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this book. Its numbered results are mathematical properties of degresively proportional allocations: for example, Fact 4.1 classifies gains and losses under balanced rules, and Facts 4.2–4.5 establish properties of linear and piecewise-linear allocations. Lemma 4.1 shows that joining two such functions preserves degresive proportionality. None asserts a computational complexity result or gives a formulation that the authors compute with. The integer linear program discussed for Serafini’s method is a cited approach, not a numbered result proved by this book. So I cannot honestly provide a continuous problem *per anchor*.

The strongest positive case I can make is an unanchored extension. Imagine a large federation allocating representative seats among many local authorities, with \(N\) authorities but only \(\tau\) distinct entitlement types, where an authority’s type records its population entitlement and any relevant caps. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of authorities of type \(t\), and let \(q_t\) be its entitlement. As the federation grows, let the total seat supply scale with \(N\), giving a fixed average budget \(H\) seats per authority. For \(k\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\), let \(x_{t,k}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) authorities receiving exactly \(k\) seats. Require \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,k}k x_{t,k}=H\). Each authority still receives an integer number of seats; the type mean \(s_t=\sum_k kx_{t,k}/\mu_t\) may be fractional. Require those means to be degresively proportional: when \(q_t\le q_u\), \(s_t\le s_u\) and \(s_t/q_t\ge s_u/q_u\). One concrete objective is to minimize total mass-weighted absolute deviation from proportional quotas:
\[ \sum_{t,k}x_{t,k}\left|k-\frac{q_tH}{\sum_u\mu_uq_u}\right|. \]
Call this High-Multiplicity Degressive Apportionment. With \(K\) included in the input, it is a linear program of size \(O(\tau K)\), so I would expect it to be tractable in \(\tau,K,L\): a Class A question.

The regime is plausible as a high-multiplicity allocation problem: many local authorities share the same entitlement and allocation constraints, with \(\tau\ll N\). My weakest point is that this replaces the book’s small set of heterogeneous states with repeated authorities and adds an optimization objective. The authors might recognize it as a high-multiplicity extension of their allocation problem, but it is not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this book.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the book offers no computational result to mirror. Its Facts 4.1–4.5 establish properties of allocation rules: who gains or loses under balanced rules, when linear allocations sum to the total supply, and when linear or piecewise-linear allocations are degresively proportional. Those statements have no complexity bound, algorithmic input model, or reduction. Serafini’s integer program appears as cited prior work, not as a result proved here. A direct continuous analogue of the Facts would replace finite sums with averages and ask for the same analytic properties over a distribution; that does not create the computational question ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed federation example is more serious than a literal objection about named countries. Many interchangeable local authorities could share an entitlement type, and each could still receive an integer number of seats while type averages are fractional. That is a coherent high-multiplicity regime, and the book’s discussion of applying degresive proportionality beyond the European Parliament gives it some connection to the authors’ subject.

But the example adds a quota-deviation objective and asks for an optimization problem the book never studies. Its \(O(\tau K)\) LP follows directly from enumerating the integer allotments \(k\) and imposing linear constraints on their masses; it is a new, readily formulated problem, rather than a computational mirror of one of the book’s results. A closer analogue would ask how the book’s allocation properties behave over a distribution of entitlement types. That remains a question about properties of rules, not their computational complexity. If \(K\) is binary encoded, the stated LP size also need not be polynomial in the input bit length.

So I would reject this as a paper-specific computational mirror, while acknowledging that the federation model could be a reasonable adjacent project. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is weak: the high-multiplicity allocation regime is plausible, and the book itself invites broader applications. The firmer negative conclusion is narrower: the book is a poor anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme because its own results are analytic allocation properties, not computational results.
