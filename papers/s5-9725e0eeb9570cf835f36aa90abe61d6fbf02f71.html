What the mirror covers

The proposed LP captures Proposition 2’s first-best expert-weight allocation claim, but omits its equilibrium incentives and does not cover the paper’s other equilibrium, dominance-solvability, or welfare-comparison results.

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly applying your anchor rule, this paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results puts a problem in P, proves it hard, or gives a parameterized complexity classification. The closest useful anchor is Proposition 2, proved in the paper’s appendix. It is a structural characterization, not a complexity theorem.

Proposition 2 assumes a flat prior, \(n_A\ge n_B\), \(n_U\) uninformed independent voters with precision \(0.5\), and \(E\) independent experts with precisions above \(0.5\). It proves that there is an \(n^*\) such that, when \(n_U>n^*\), a best liquid-democracy equilibrium has these properties: \(n_A-n_B\) uninformed voters vote unresponsively for \(B\); each remaining uninformed voter either abstains or delegates to the same expert at both signals; the election implements the first-best outcome with probability \(1\); and, as \(n_U\) grows, the ratio of votes held by any two experts approaches the ratio of their optimal log-odds weights, \(w^*(i)/w^*(j)\), where \(w^*(i)=\log(q_i/(1-q_i))\). This gives a strong high-multiplicity pattern: many exchangeable, uninformed voters supply the votes that let a small expert group carry power in proportion to its information.

The most convincing setting for a mirror is a large membership vote on a technical issue: most members have no private information, a few recognized experts do, and some members are committed partisans. The ordinary members share the same preferences, precision, prices, and access to delegates, so they form a small number of types. Here \(N\gg\tau\): the uninformed and partisan groups are large, while the expert set and the type support are small. A type is the voter’s preference class, precision, and delegation role; mass is its share of the electorate. The action is a mass allocation: uninformed mass can offset the partisan imbalance, delegate to experts, or abstain. The objective is to give the experts’ signals exactly the influence needed to reproduce the Bayesian first-best decision.

One precise follow-up is First-Best Mass Delegation. Its input is a flat-prior binary election with rational masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B,\mu_U\), a fixed set of \(E\) experts with rational precisions \(q_i>0.5\), and expert ballot masses \(\epsilon_i\) (for a single expert among \(N\) voters, \(\epsilon_i=1/N\)). Let \(\Delta=|\mu_A-\mu_B|\). Choose delegation masses \(x_i\ge0\) with \(\sum_i x_i\le\mu_U-\Delta\); the remaining uninformed mass may abstain. After \(\Delta\) mass offsets the partisan imbalance, expert \(i\) carries mass \(\epsilon_i+x_i\). Write \(\ell_i=\log(q_i/(1-q_i))\). For every expert signal vector \(\eta\in\{-1,+1\}^E\) with \(\sum_i\eta_i\ell_i>0\), the first-best chooses \(A\). The problem is to maximize \(\delta\) subject to \(\sum_i\eta_i(\epsilon_i+x_i)\ge\delta\) for all such vectors. A positive optimum gives an allocation whose weighted expert vote selects the first-best winner for every non-tied expert signal profile; the output is the allocation and its margin. This is an LP with exponentially many signal-profile constraints. Their signs can be determined from the rational odds, so the question is well-defined from a binary input.

I expect the fixed-\(E\) version to be tractable; enumerating the signal profiles already gives a \(2^{O(E)}\)-time method. For variable \(E\), the separation question—whether some signal vector makes the vote weights disagree with the log-odds rule—is the natural complexity question. Proposition 2 does not answer it. To be a full mirror of the strategic result, an allocation also needs support by a neutral equilibrium in finite refinements; the LP captures the first-best implementation core, not that incentive condition by itself.

The weak point is the expert hubs. The mirror keeps their individual signals while allowing them to collect population mass. A limit in which every voter is infinitesimal can erase the unilateral pivotal incentives that drive the paper’s equilibrium analysis. The mirror is strongest in the regime Proposition 2 studies—many interchangeable uninformed members and a few delegates—and covers that result’s first-best allocation claim, not the paper’s general equilibrium theory. If a referee requires every type to have a positive limiting population share and rejects a finite delegate set with small mass, this case weakens substantially.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to continuize. Propositions 1–6 and Theorem 1 characterize equilibrium welfare, information aggregation, and dominance solvability. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm with a running time. Dominance solvability is a game-theoretic solution concept, not a complexity result. On the programme’s stated criterion, that is a decisive mismatch.

Proposition 2 is the best anchor for a high-multiplicity regime: many exchangeable, uninformed voters can delegate to a small expert group. I would accept that as a sensible regime, not object that high-multiplicity voting is already studied. But the proposed “First-Best Mass Delegation” LP chooses an allocation centrally. It does not capture the result’s strategic content: individuals choose whether to delegate, and the paper compares equilibria of those choices. In an atomless donor population, one voter’s action changes no aggregate mass, so pivotal incentives disappear. If experts remain finite atoms to preserve their private signals and pivotality, the model becomes a hybrid of a continuum of donors and a finite set of decisive individuals. That is coherent, but the continuous population supplies only divisible voting power; the experts retain the strategic uncertainty.

Giving the expert types positive population mass does not fix this while preserving the paper’s information structure. With conditionally independent signals, a positive mass of experts produces deterministic signal proportions in the continuum limit. The finite signal uncertainty that makes information aggregation nontrivial disappears. Keeping finite experts instead preserves that uncertainty but leaves the key strategic actors atomic. The proposed LP can still be a well-defined planner’s allocation problem, but its equilibrium support condition becomes vacuous for the atomless donors: their actions cannot individually change the outcome. It therefore does not chart the computational landscape of the paper’s equilibrium problem.

The negative case is weakest at exactly this anchor. A hybrid model with atomless uninformed voters and finitely many expert delegates is plausible, and one could study its allocation problem. But that would be a new centralized optimization question, or a mixed atomic–nonatomic game, rather than a continuous-population complexity mirror of the paper’s results. I would not claim that no worthwhile model can be built; I would argue that the paper does not provide a computational result whose continuous mirror fits ChoCo’s target.
