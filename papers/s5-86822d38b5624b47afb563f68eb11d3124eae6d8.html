What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers asymptotic aggregate Shapley-Shubik power for weighted-quota societies, while leaving the paper's axiomatic characterizations, Banzhaf results, normalization results, and cross-player axioms without computational counterparts.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: this paper has a natural population mirror, but it has no admissible computational anchor in the programme’s strict sense. Its named results are axiomatic characterizations, not claims of membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or similar classes. Thus the positive case must be presented as a new computational problem built from the paper’s underlying object, not as a computational result already established by the authors.

The best anchor is Theorem 2(i), proved in this paper. It states that an index \(\Phi:SG_n\to\mathbb R^n\) satisfies anonymity, Null Player\(^*\), Symmetric Gain-Loss, and Constant Total Gain-Loss Balance if and only if
\[ \Phi=\alpha\,\mathrm{Sh}+\kappa\mathbf 1 \]
for some \(\alpha>0\) and \(\kappa\in\mathbb R\). In other words, those axioms characterize the Shapley-Shubik index up to scale and additive normalization. Theorem 6(i), also proved here, extends this across different numbers of players: with the corresponding cross-\(n\) equivalence conditions for null and dictator players, the family must be
\[ \Phi_n=\alpha\,\mathrm{Sh}_n+\kappa\mathbf 1 \]
with common \(\alpha\) and \(\kappa\).

My lead mirror would be Continuum Shapley Type-Power Evaluation. A society consists of a finite set of voting types \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), a rational mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb Q^\tau_{\ge 0}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational quota rule
\[ g(x)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{t\in T}w_t x_t\ge q, \]
where \(x_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) in a coalition and \(q\) is chosen so that the game is proper, hence superadditive in the paper’s sense. A target type \(a\in T\) and accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon>0\) are also given.

The intended regime is a large federation, shareholder body, union, or electorate with millions of members but perhaps only \(5\)–\(20\) institutional types: members with the same voting weight, eligibility, and constitutional role. Thus \(N\mu_t\) members belong to type \(t\), and members of one type are indistinguishable for the voting rule. The input is the distribution \(\mu\), not a list of named voters.

For every sufficiently large integer \(N\), let \(G_N\) be the finite weighted voting game with \(N\mu_t\) players of type \(t\). Let
\[ P^{\mathrm{Sh}}_{a}(G_N) =\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=a}\mathrm{Sh}_i(G_N) \]
be the total Shapley-Shubik power of type \(a\). The continuous problem asks for an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate value of the high-multiplicity limit
\[ P^{\mathrm{Sh}}_{a}(\mu,w,q) =\lim_{N\to\infty}P^{\mathrm{Sh}}_{a}(G_N), \]
under a promise that the limit exists, or alternatively for a certified asymptotic approximation when \(N\) is supplied as a scale parameter.

A solution is therefore a numerical value, or an approximation certificate, for the pivotality share of an entire mass class. The random ordering underlying the Shapley-Shubik index remains the paper’s decision procedure; only the population is continuized. This is recognisably the authors’ problem: their object is still power in a simple superadditive voting game, and Section 7 explicitly studies comparing power across different numbers of players.

I would expect the fixed-\(\tau\), structured-rule version to be Class A: the finite-dimensional type description should permit dynamic programming, coefficient extraction, or polyhedral integration, while avoiding dependence on the astronomical number \(N\) of individual voters. If the type count or the representation of the winning rule becomes variable, hardness may survive, placing that version in Class B. The natural follow-up questions are whether the limit exists for broader simple games, whether it can be computed exactly or only approximated, and whether the paper’s axioms admit a unique continuum formulation.

The weakest point is substantial. Theorem 2(i) and Theorem 6(i) are not computational results, and the continuum limit is not defined by the paper. Banzhaf is even less canonical: its normalization behaves differently under duplication, and the paper itself distinguishes raw, normalized, and relatively equivalent variants. Consequently, this is a plausible high-multiplicity research problem, but not a clean mirror of a named computational theorem. Under the strict anchoring rule, the correct verdict is that this paper supplies no computational anchor; the positive case survives only as a motivated new problem about computing type-level power in a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest under the programme’s strict anchoring rule: this paper contains no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2(i) and Theorem 6(i) are axiomatic characterizations. They neither define an algorithmic input and output problem nor assert membership in \(P\), hardness, approximation guarantees, or parameterized complexity. Section 7’s comparison across different numbers of players is still only an axiomatic extension. Thus the proposed task is an externally invented computational problem motivated by the paper, not a continuous counterpart of one of its results.

The proposed Shapley type-power problem also changes the paper’s object substantially. The paper’s theorems quantify over all simple superadditive games \(SG_n\), whose essential structure is the finite lattice of individual coalitions and the operation of deleting one minimal winning coalition. A distribution \(\mu\), weights \(w\), and a quota \(q\) describe only a narrow family of weighted games. They do not continuize the axiomatization; they select a new voting model on which one particular index is to be evaluated.

Worse, the natural high-multiplicity limit largely destroys the interesting quantity. In the ordinary scaling with fixed finitely many types, per-player pivotality goes to zero. Under the usual regularity conditions for a weighted quota rule, the total Shapley-Shubik power of type \(a\) converges to the type’s weighted population share,

\[ \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i:\operatorname{type}(i)=a}\operatorname{Sh}_i(G_N) = \frac{\mu_a w_a}{\sum_t \mu_t w_t}. \]

The quota and most of the finite coalition structure disappear from the limit. With equal weights, the answer is simply \(\mu_a\). This is not merely a disappointing theorem: the paper’s distinction between Shapley-Shubik and Banzhaf power, and its gain-loss axioms, have ceased to be the operative structure. The limit is an asymptotic accounting identity for mass or weight, not a continuous version of the paper’s characterization problem.

A stronger proposed mirror could replace weighted quotas by an arbitrary rule \(W\subseteq[0,1]^\tau\). But then the computational input must encode \(W\); the population distribution alone is insufficient. If \(W\) is given by a regular finite-dimensional description, the resulting task is a new problem about a continuum capacity or boundary integral. If it retains arbitrary coalition structure, it has no effective finite representation. If one instead treats the \(\tau\) types as the players, one has returned to a finite Shapley game and is merely allowing fractional type coalitions. If one lets the number of types or exceptional players grow with \(N\), the fixed-type high-multiplicity regime has been abandoned.

The atomless model also has no canonical counterpart of “delete one minimal winning coalition”: there is no distinguished finite coalition edge in the coalition lattice, and deleting one measurable representative does not provide the local modification used throughout the paper. A replacement based on deleting a mass region or perturbing a boundary could be worthwhile, but it would be a new axiomatic theory rather than a mirror of Theorems 2 or 6.

The honest limitation is that the federation interpretation is genuinely plausible; high multiplicity is not the objection. A sensible paper could study asymptotic type power. The negative conclusion is narrower but decisive for this programme: this paper contributes no named computational anchor, and its best population continuation either collapses to weighted mass shares or requires replacing the paper’s central finite-coalition structure with a new continuum capacity problem.
