What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 characterizes mechanisms by axioms, and the paper describes a sequential allocation procedure; neither is accompanied by a named computational guarantee or hardness result.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its Theorem 1, proved here, says that a mechanism satisfies minimal tradability, individual rationality, strategy-proofness, consistency, independence of unallocated objects, neutrality, and non-wasteful tie-breaking if and only if it is a serial dictatorship with reservation prices for some reservation-price vector and priority ordering. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about the complexity of computing an outcome or solving an optimization problem.

Lemma 1 is also not a computational anchor: it is cited from Klaus and Nichifor (2020) and characterizes a single-agent, single-object mechanism by a reservation-price threshold. Neither result asserts tractability, hardness, or another complexity classification. The paper gives a sequential procedure for the characterized mechanism, but does not analyze its running time, and its general utility functions have no specified computational representation.

A clinic-cohort model is imaginable: types could encode preferences, valuations, reservation prices, and a priority class, with mass representing the share of patients in each type and appointment supply scaling with the cohort. But turning that into a computational problem would add a task the paper does not study; faithfully representing its arbitrary individual priority ordering may also prevent many agents from sharing a type. A continuum characterization of the mechanism would be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s scope.

So I can’t give a continuous problem per anchor without inventing a computational result or changing the paper’s question. The strongest honest positive case is that the application could support a high-multiplicity population model; this paper itself supplies no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that this paper has no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 1 is a characterization: it identifies which mechanisms satisfy a list of axioms. It says nothing about the complexity of computing an outcome or solving an optimization problem. Lemma 1, which the paper cites from earlier work, is also a characterization, for a single agent and a single object. Neither supplies a computational anchor.

The best computational-sounding reconstruction would ask how quickly one can compute the outcome of a serial dictatorship with reservation prices. But the paper gives a sequential procedure, not a complexity claim, and its utilities have no specified finite representation. An analysis of that procedure would add a computational question the paper does not study. A continuum version of Theorem 1 would remain an axiomatic characterization, which is outside the programme’s stated scope.

The clinic-cohort scenario makes a high-multiplicity population plausible, but it does not create a computational result in this paper. To represent its mechanism faithfully, a type must include both the person’s reservation price and their place in the priority ordering: both affect the outcome. With a strict ordering over individuals, including priority in the type generally makes the support grow with the population. Replacing that ordering with finitely many priority tiers and scaling appointment supply could define an interesting mass-allocation problem, but it would require a new rule for resolving competition within tiers. That may be a worthwhile adjacent project; it would not mirror Theorem 1.

So none of the proposed anchors survives. The honest limit of this negative case is that one can invent related continuous computational problems; the stronger claim is that this paper supplies no named computational result for ChoCo to continue, and the plausible cohort model does not change that.
