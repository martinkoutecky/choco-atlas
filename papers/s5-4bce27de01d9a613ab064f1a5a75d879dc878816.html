What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers only Theorem 5’s interval-optimality claim, and then only as a degenerate benchmark; it does not preserve Theorem 6’s strategic nonmonotonicity result or the paper’s other finite-agent mechanism results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result placing a problem in P or proving it hard. The closest computational anchor is Theorem 5, proved here: for the paper’s incentive-compatible mechanism-design problem, an optimal mechanism can be chosen as an interval voting rule. In that rule, the probability of choosing \(A\) is positive on a consecutive interval of vote counts, equals \(1\) at the interval’s interior counts, and is \(0\) outside it; the boundary counts may be randomized. This is a structural optimization result, not a complexity classification.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a very large jury whose members share the paper’s preferences and signal technology. Let the two voter types be their privately observed signals, \(T=\{a,b\}\), and let mass mean the fraction of jurors with each signal. Conditional on state \(\omega\), the signal-mass distribution is \(\mu^\omega_a=p_\omega\), \(\mu^\omega_b=1-p_\omega\). Thus the population grows while the number of types stays at two.

The corresponding candidate problem, Atomless Jury Mechanism Design, takes the prior, signal probabilities, and principal and juror payoffs as input. The principal chooses a measurable rule \(x:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), where \(x(q)\) is the probability of choosing \(A\) when mass \(q\) reports signal \(a\). The objective is to maximize
\[ \Pr(\alpha)V(\alpha)x(p_\alpha)+\Pr(\beta)V(\beta)x(p_\beta). \]
A solution is such a rule, together with a proof that it is optimal under truthful reporting.

This is a coherent continuous-population model, and it has an interval-rule optimum: since \(p_\alpha>p_\beta\), the truthful signal share identifies the state, so the principal can set \(x(p_\alpha)=1\) and \(x(p_\beta)=0\), choosing an interval around \(p_\alpha\) that excludes \(p_\beta\). The problem is tractable in closed form. The paper’s principal prefers \(A\) in state \(\alpha\) and \(B\) in state \(\beta\), so this rule achieves the state-by-state first best.

That tractability is also the mirror’s central weakness. A single juror has zero mass and cannot change \(q\), so the paper’s individual incentive constraints become vacuous. With finitely many jurors, every signal count has positive probability in either state, and a pivotal vote creates the incentive problem that Theorem 5 resolves. In the atomless model, the observed signal share instead reveals the state exactly. The interval conclusion survives, but the strategic trade-off that gives it substance does not. The authors might accept this as a large-jury benchmark; I would not present it as a faithful continuous analogue of their main result.

So the positive case is narrow: Theorem 5 admits a precise continuous benchmark with a small support of voter types, and its interval form remains optimal there. It does not show that the paper’s incentive problem or its computational substance survives continuization. Further questions would be whether finite-jury optima converge to this benchmark, at what rate, and whether any continuum formulation preserving meaningful individual incentives also preserves the interval result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it establishes a structural fact about an optimization problem, not a computational complexity result. The paper’s finite problem is already an LP with \(n+2\) variables and two incentive constraints. In the usual representation where \(n\) is the number of jurors, there is no exponential type space or pricing problem for a continuous algorithm to unlock.

The proposed two-signal continuum exposes a more fundamental problem. Under truthful reporting, the reported mass is \(p_\alpha\) in state \(\alpha\) and \(p_\beta\) in state \(\beta\), with \(p_\alpha>p_\beta\). A threshold between them identifies the state, so the principal’s rule can select the state’s preferred alternative in both states. An individual juror has zero mass and cannot change the report share. Their report therefore has no effect on the outcome, and the individual incentive constraints that drive Theorem 5 become vacuous. The paper’s interval mechanism can be imposed around \(p_\alpha\), but its values elsewhere do no work.

A stronger proposed anchor might be Theorem 6’s anti-monotonicity result. It runs into the same degeneracy: once the only relevant shares are \(p_\beta\) and \(p_\alpha\), a monotone threshold between them implements the state-contingent choices. Whether the rule is set to zero or one at shares outside those two points is arbitrary. The continuous model has no pivotal juror whose incentives require an upper boundary to offset a lower one.

One could try to retain pivotality through a local-asymptotic model that tracks finite-jury fluctuations, or by treating positive-mass cohorts as strategic players. But the first keeps finite-agent randomness as a continuous noise approximation; the second replaces individual incentive constraints with coalition or bloc constraints. Either may motivate a different mechanism-design project, but neither makes the society itself an atomless population while preserving this paper’s strategic problem. Changing the mechanism to respond specially to one person’s report would likewise abandon the anonymous population model.

So I would reject this as a continuous computational-social-choice mirror. The negative case is not a proof that no adjacent project could be worthwhile: asymptotic mechanism design or bloc manipulation could be interesting on its own. Its strength is narrower: every faithful atomless version removes the individual pivotality that gives this paper’s optimization result its content, while the finite problem already has a compact LP formulation.
