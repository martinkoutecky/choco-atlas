What the mirror covers

The proposed distributional calculation covers only the Section 2 Bayesian decision formula; it leaves the voting-equivalence results in Theorem 1, Corollaries 1–2, and Theorem 2 outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result in the sense required for an anchor: it states no result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved here: the optimal decision rule can be represented by a weighted supermajority rule if and only if each decision maker’s odds ratio can take at most two values. That is a structural characterization of when voting reproduces the optimum, not a complexity result. So there is no valid per-anchor question to supply.

The strongest plausible continuous problem is Mass-Weighted Bayesian Choice, mirroring the paper’s unnumbered optimal decision rule in Section 2. Let the instance specify a finite set \(T\) of complete evidence types, rational masses \(x_t\geq 0\), and common environmental parameters. A type records the decision maker’s information experiment and realized report; its log-likelihood ratio is \(\ell_t\). The total mass \(M=\sum_t x_t\) is the number-equivalent population, and \(x_t=M\mu_t\) when the society is described by normalized shares \(\mu_t\). Ask which proposal, \(A\) or \(B\), maximizes expected gain. The aggregate log-odds are
\[ L=\sum_{t\in T}x_t\ell_t. \]
The solution is \(A\) if \(L>\log\Psi_A^*\), \(B\) if \(L<\log\Psi_A^*\), and either proposal at equality. For a computational version, \(\ell_t\) and the threshold can be supplied as rationals, making the decision a polynomial-time weighted-sum comparison.

A plausible regime is a large decision panel or crowdsourced expert group using a small catalogue of evidence protocols: many participants, relatively few experiment-and-report types, and a shared goal and common gains. The authors could recognize this as a high-multiplicity version of their model: the optimal rule already aggregates individual likelihood ratios multiplicatively, and masses replace repeated individual factors with their type-level totals. The expected outcome is tractability. A natural follow-up is whether Theorem 1’s two-possible-odds condition has an exact mass-based counterpart for representing the optimum with a weighted two-option voting rule.

The mirror covers choosing the optimal proposal from the population’s evidence. It does not cover the paper’s broader comparison of voting mechanisms unless that follow-up is taken on separately. The weakest point is that Mass-Weighted Bayesian Choice computes the ideal rule the paper starts from; it does not capture the paper’s central theorem about when voting can implement that rule. Also, fractional evidence mass is a high-multiplicity relaxation, whose Bayesian interpretation the paper does not establish. This is a plausible population mirror, but the absence of a named computational anchor leaves the positive case limited.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and its corollaries characterize when voting rules reproduce the optimal decision rule; they do not study the complexity of computing a decision or designing a rule. The paper gives the optimal rule directly through the collective odds ratio. That is an important result, but it leaves no computational problem whose complexity landscape this programme could chart.

The proposed Mass-Weighted Bayesian Choice is a sensible description of a large panel with repeated evidence types. In that regime, masses can replace repeated reports, and the aggregate log-odds can be written as \(L=\sum_t x_t\ell_t\). But that formalization turns the paper’s formula into an algorithmic input-output task; it does not mirror a computational question the paper raised. More importantly, it bypasses the paper’s central concern: whether coarse votes preserve the information needed for the optimal choice. The masses and log-likelihood ratios already supply that information.

The best attempt to retain the central concern would ask whether a voting rule based on type masses reproduces the optimal choice, or whether one can design such a rule from the types’ possible signals. The first is a population version of the paper’s comparison, but it remains a representability question. The second adds a design problem the paper does not formulate. Neither gains a computational target from continuization: the paper’s conditions concern the possible posterior values available to each decision maker, not the frequency of those values in the realized population. Replacing repeated decision makers with mass therefore does not turn the result into a problem about algorithms for a continuous society.

This is not a case against high multiplicity as a plausible regime. Large panels or crowdsourced expert groups with repeated evidence protocols are easy to imagine, and existing high-multiplicity work would support that modeling choice. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers an analytic characterization of information aggregation and voting, rather than a computational result about a population. A continuous model could be built around it, but the computational question would have to be supplied from outside the paper. That makes it a weak candidate for ChoCo’s mirror programme, though not proof that no worthwhile extension could be developed.
