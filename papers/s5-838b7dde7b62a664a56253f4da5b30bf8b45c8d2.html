The case FOR (proponent)

One qualification first: this paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or parameterized. Theorem 1 is the strongest named anchor for a continuous mirror, but it is an existential welfare-approximation barrier; the authors explicitly say it is independent of computational considerations. So this is a case for mirroring a named result of the paper, not for continuing a discrete complexity classification.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that there is a market with two submodular buyers in which *every* stable outcome gives only an \(\Omega(\sqrt m)\)-approximation to optimal welfare. Equivalently, stable outcomes can lose a factor of order \(\sqrt m\). The paper connects this barrier to the configuration LP: the construction makes a fractional allocation strictly better than the best integral allocation, and that gap rules out high-welfare stable prices. ([Theorem 1 and its proof](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.02399))

A plausible mirror is a market with many buyers in a small number of homogeneous segments. Let \(T\) be the support of valuation types, with \(|T|=\tau\), masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and submodular valuations \(v_t:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\). Good \(j\) has supply \(q_j\) per unit of population. Let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\). Require
\[ x_{t,S}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j. \]
Welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). A pair \((p,x)\) is stable when \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^m\) and every bundle assigned positive mass is demanded at those prices:
\[ x_{t,S}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ S\in\arg\max_{R\subseteq[m]} \left(v_t(R)-\sum_{j\in R}p_j\right). \]

Call the problem Fluid Stable Pricing Approximation: given the rational type masses, supplies, and valuations, determine the tight worst-case ratio
\[ \rho(m,\tau)= \sup_I\frac{\operatorname{OPT}(I)} {\max\{W(x):(p,x)\text{ is stable in }I\}}, \]
and return a stable price-and-allocation pair attaining the claimed guarantee. The direct question corresponding to Theorem 1 is whether this ratio can still grow as \(\Omega(\sqrt m)\) with only two submodular types.

In the mass model, it cannot: the configuration LP’s fractional allocations are actual assignments of population mass to bundles. LP duality supplies item prices supporting an optimal allocation, so \(\rho(m,\tau)=1\). Each individual still receives an integral bundle; it is only the share of a type assigned to each bundle that may be fractional. With full valuation tables as input, this is an LP of size polynomial in the table encoding. For succinct valuation representations, the further algorithmic question is the complexity of producing the supporting prices and allocation; that depends on how valuations are represented.

The regime is plausible for a large market with a few stable buyer segments purchasing from a standardized catalogue: many buyers share each segment’s complete preferences, while stock of each item category grows with the market. The paper’s two constructed buyers become two large cohorts, so \(\tau=2\) while the population grows. To reproduce the theorem’s instance, normalize each cohort to mass \(1/2\) and each item’s supply to \(1/2\); equivalently, use mass one per cohort and supply one per item. This keeps the bundle choices discrete and makes fractional type-level assignments part of the population model, rather than making the goods or individual bundles divisible. The paper’s own discussion of increasing the number of identical copies makes the supply adjustment recognizable as a natural extension.

That is why the mirror seems plausible to me: it asks the same question about stable item prices and welfare for submodular valuations, and the paper already uses the configuration LP that becomes the continuous allocation model. It is not merely a continuation of fractional outcomes; the continuum is in the buyer population.

The weak point is substantial. Theorem 1’s \(\Omega(\sqrt m)\) barrier is a structural existence result, not computational hardness, and this mirror removes that barrier by making cohort-level fractional allocations implementable. If the authors or a referee require each named buyer to receive exactly one bundle while each unique item remains a single copy, this mirror would fail their test. The case rests on treating a large homogeneous cohort—and per-capita supply—as the appropriate population limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational result. It says a poor stable outcome must exist for some instance; it gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or input representation. The proposed “Fluid Stable Pricing Approximation” adds those choices. With full valuation tables, the configuration LP is polynomial in the table size; with succinct valuations, the algorithmic question depends on the chosen representation and its demand or pricing oracle. That may be worthwhile research, but it is a new computational question built around the theorem, not a complexity result the paper itself asks.

That objection does not defeat the mirror. Two large cohorts with identical valuations and supply growing with the market are a coherent population regime, and the paper itself names increasing item copies as a direction for future work. Splitting cohort mass among bundles also preserves integral bundles for each buyer. Theorem 1’s configuration-LP argument gives a direct bridge to that model. Continuum-market equilibrium existence is already studied—even with indivisible goods and broad preferences—but that is an existence result, not a computational treatment that settles the proposed algorithmic question. [Azevedo, Weyl, and White, “Walrasian equilibrium in large, quasi-linear markets”](https://doi.org/10.3982/TE1060)

So the negative case is weak: the only substantial point is that this paper offers no named computational problem to mirror. I cannot honestly argue that the cohort-and-scaled-supply scenario is implausible or that its continuous question is unworthy. The proponent’s anchor survives; its fit with the programme is indirect, but the model is unusually natural.
