What the mirror covers

Proposition 5 is the sole anchor raised; the paper’s other equilibrium and welfare results and its experiments provide no computational guarantee.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest possible positive lead is a large public referendum committee facing several binary proposals. A type would be a complete valuation vector \(v\) over the proposals, and \(\mu_v\) the fraction of participants with that vector. Each participant allocates one bonus vote among the proposals; the agenda chair also chooses their order. One could ask for a symmetric equilibrium strategy and the induced outcomes and welfare, given rational finite-support \(\mu\), \(B\), and \(T\).

But the model’s central mechanism depends on an individual voter’s chance of being pivotal. In an atomless population, one person’s regular or bonus vote has zero mass, so that incentive disappears. Giving a whole type cohort a coordinated vote would restore influence only by changing who acts. That makes this a weak mirror of the paper’s strategic problem.

More decisively, the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: no result classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard, and no algorithmic formulation is computed with. Propositions 1–6 are equilibrium and welfare results. The nearest candidate is Proposition 5, proved in the paper: for \(n\in\{3,4\}\) and \(B\in B(n)\), it identifies \(T\) payoff-distinct quasi-symmetric equilibria for all \(G\) and \(T\). In the equilibrium indexed by \(S\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}\), the chair puts their \(S\) lowest-intensity proposals first, in any order, and randomizes uniformly over the order of the remaining proposals; the chair spends the bonus vote on their highest-intensity proposal. A non-chair voter spends theirs on the highest-intensity proposal among the remaining \(T-S\) if that intensity is at least \(\alpha(n,G,T,S)\) times their highest intensity among the first \(S\), and otherwise on the highest among those first \(S\), where \(\alpha\geq1\) is unique. These are the only payoff-distinct quasi-symmetric equilibria that hold for all the stated parameter values. This is a substantial analytical characterization, but it is not a computational result under the requested standard.

So I can offer a plausible population scenario and a possible computational extension, but I cannot honestly present a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem *per anchor* for this paper. The apparent continuous element \(G\) is a distribution of individual valuations; the committee itself remains finite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts at the programme’s gate: this paper states no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. Propositions 1–6 characterize equilibria and welfare. Proposition 5 is the closest candidate the other analyst identifies, but it is an equilibrium characterization for committees with \(n\in\{3,4\}\), not a computational result. The paper’s use of a valuation distribution \(G\) does not change that: valuations are random across a finite set of voters, while the committee itself remains finite.

The public referendum is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and valuation vectors could serve as types. But in an atomless society, one voter’s regular or bonus vote changes zero mass. The pivotal-vote incentives that determine the paper’s strategies and the threshold \(\alpha\) disappear; even a tie cannot be broken by one zero-mass voter. With a fixed distribution of types, voters’ choices no longer select an equilibrium through those incentives. Aggregate outcomes can depend on arbitrary mass-level strategy choices, so the paper’s characterization does not provide a canonical equilibrium or welfare value for a computational solver to return. Identical types do not solve this: they remain individual voters unless they are given the new role of a coordinated bloc.

The strongest upgraded proposal would be to compute equilibria or welfare from a rational finite-support distribution over valuation vectors. At fixed \(n\), that is a possible computational question about the paper’s finite game, but the population has not been continuized. Taking \(n\) large and replacing the electorate by its limiting type distribution removes the finite-population fluctuations that generate pivotality. Retaining them, or assigning positive voting mass to coordinated blocs, changes the strategic model the results analyze.

So the proposed mirror has no qualifying anchor, and its most promising computational reformulation loses the paper’s central incentives in the continuous-population limit. The negative case is weakest as a universal claim: one could study finite-size asymptotics or algorithms for the finite game as new work. Those may be worthwhile, but they do not establish a computational continuous-population mirror of this paper.
