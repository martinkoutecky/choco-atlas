The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) course types \(G\), valuation types \(t\in[\tau]\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_g\in[0,1]\), additive valuations \(v_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}a_{t,g}\), and a common ranking of \(G\), find rational masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=q_g\), and \(v_t(B)\ge v_t(D\setminus\{g\})\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\), \(x_{u,D}>0\), and \(g\in D\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity capacitated fair-allocation model with valuation-type masses \(\mu\), integral bundle configurations \(B\), mass variables \(x_{t,B}\), exact per-capita course capacities \(q_g\), and support-wise EFX feasibility rather than average-utility EFX.

The objection that survived

The theorem does not show that its envy-cycle construction can enforce one-seat-per-course constraints under repeated capacities or yield a succinct representation over valuation types and bundle columns.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem B.1's common-ranking EFX algorithm, while leaving the query-complexity lower bounds, leximin and Pareto-optimality results, counterexamples, and \(1/2\)-EFX result untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can support-wise EFX allocations be found in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit-length rather than the expanded population size?
  • Does the common-ranking structure yield a polynomial-support certificate or a column-generation formulation for the mass allocation?
  • How does the problem change with several ranking classes or with Pareto optimality imposed simultaneously?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity course-allocation mirror of the paper’s Appendix-B result.

The anchor is Theorem B.1, proved in this paper: for additive valuations with identical rankings over the goods, Algorithm 4 terminates with an EFX allocation in \(O(mn^3)\) time. The theorem covers any number \(n\) of players; every player may value goods differently, but all players agree on the ordering of the goods.

I would name the mirror Continuous Common-Ranking EFX Allocation.

There are \(m\) course or item types \(G=\{g_1,\ldots,g_m\}\), \(\tau\) valuation types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(q_g\). A type \(t\) has additive valuation

\[ v_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}a_{t,g}, \]

where all types share the same ranking \(g_1\succeq\cdots\succeq g_m\). The regime is a large university or platform with \(N\) students, \(N\gg \tau\), where students fall into a small number of recurring preference profiles. Each course has \(Nq_g\) indivisible seats, and every student receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\).

The decision variable is a mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\): the fraction of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t}\sum_{B\ni g}x_{t,B}=q_g \]

for every good \(g\). The question is to find a rational \(x\) satisfying, whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{u,D}>0\),

\[ v_t(B)\ge v_t(D\setminus\{g\}) \qquad\text{for every }g\in D. \]

Thus every individual unit of mass receives an integral bundle, and EFX is imposed support-wise between actual bundles. We do not replace EFX by a comparison of average utilities.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity object. Clearing denominators in \(\mu\), \(q\), and \(x\) produces a finite course-allocation instance with \(N\) cloned students and \(Nq_g\) seats. Conversely, any such finite allocation induces \(x\). The population is continuous; the bundles remain indivisible. The resource scaling is forced by the application rather than being an arbitrary relaxation.

The authors should recognise this as their problem: the paper explicitly discusses course allocation, and the common-ranking assumption is exactly the structured setting of Theorem B.1. The mirror preserves additive utilities, EFX, indivisible bundles, and the common-ranking structure. It changes only the representation of a large population and the capacities needed to serve it.

I expect this problem to be Class A, but the classification is not supplied by Theorem B.1. Its \(O(mn^3)\) bound is polynomial in the explicit number of students \(n\), not in \(\tau\), the bit-length of the masses, and \(m\). The continuous question is whether Algorithm 4’s descending-order and envy-cycle argument can be compressed to a polynomial-size mass allocation, perhaps through a column-generation or monotone-flow formulation. The main technical obstacle is that EFX is a support condition over potentially exponentially many integral bundles.

Further questions include whether the allocation can be found in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time, whether it admits a polynomial-support rational certificate, and what happens when common rankings are replaced by a bounded number of rankings or when Pareto optimality is added.

I would not lead with the paper’s query-complexity lower bound. It holds even for two players with identical submodular valuations, so its difficulty is driven by black-box valuation information rather than population multiplicity. A naive many-student mirror also becomes vacuous when there are too few goods, since singleton bundles automatically satisfy EFX. The common-ranking theorem is therefore the safer anchor: it yields a faithful, application-motivated continuous problem without pretending that the paper already proves the high-multiplicity algorithm.

The weakest point is precisely the missing compression theorem. The finite algorithm does not by itself imply a high-multiplicity algorithm, and exponentially many bundle columns may make this a continuum-specific hard problem instead. But that is a substantive ChoCo question, not a change of subject, and the mirror covers a named theorem of the paper cleanly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem B.1 has no nontrivial high-multiplicity regime unless the resource side is changed.

In the paper, there is one copy of each of the \(m\) goods. If the number of players \(N\) becomes large while \(m\) remains fixed, the problem collapses. Give each good to a different player. Every nonempty bundle then has size one, so for every \(g\in A_j\),

\[ A_j\setminus\{g\}=\varnothing, \]

and EFX follows immediately from nonnegative valuations. The remaining players receive nothing. Thus the population masses, valuation types, and common ranking become irrelevant. If one requires bundles to remain nontrivial, then \(N\) must be \(O(m)\); the population is no longer compressed relative to the explicit goods input.

The proposed model avoids this only by introducing \(Nq_g\) copies of every course. That is not forced by continuizing the population in the paper’s model. It changes a finite set of indivisible goods into a repeated-resource inventory. If copies are kept distinct, the paper’s theorem merely applies to an expanded instance containing \(N\sum_g q_g\) goods. If copies are identified as course types, one must add new constraints—such as at most one copy of a course per student—and clarify whether removing “one good” means removing one course or one seat. Either choice creates a new capacitated configuration problem. Its difficulty lies in compressed inventories and bundle configurations, not in the population continuum that Theorem B.1 studies.

The support-wise EFX condition is the correct way to preserve the paper’s semantics, but it reinforces the problem. EFX depends only on which bundles have positive mass, not on how much mass receives them. Replacing it by average or expected EFX would produce a more tractable mass formulation, but would no longer mirror EFX. Keeping exact EFX yields a nonconvex support-feasibility problem whose interesting structure comes from repeated goods and capacity constraints newly introduced by the proposed scenario.

So the anchor passes the formal “named computational result” test, but the clean continuization fails a more substantive test. With the original goods, the continuum is vacuous; with enough goods to avoid vacuity, \(N\) is already controlled by the explicit input size; with per-capita course capacities, the question is a new repeated-resource fair-division problem rather than a population mirror of Algorithm 4.

This negative case is not airtight. The authors explicitly mention course allocation, and a repeated-course version with exact support-wise EFX is a defensible open problem. If such resource scaling is accepted as an author-recognisable co-modification, the mirror survives. I therefore would not honestly claim that no worthwhile extension exists in any scenario; I would claim only that the proposed anchor does not establish a clean, population-driven continuization of Theorem B.1.
