The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed facility set \(M\), rational costs \(c_k\ge 0\), and supported coverage types \(t\) with \(H_t\subseteq M\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where every facility has positive mass of types with \(H_t=\{k\}\), represent the full type-symmetric core. For charge rates \(q_t\ge 0\) per unit population mass, require total charge \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=\sum_kc_k\) and, for every measurable coalition \(E\), require its charge \(\sum_t\lambda(E\cap T_t)q_t\) not to exceed the minimum cost of facilities covering every positive-mass type in \(E\); output the exact feasible set.

The model it lives in

The facilities and their costs stay fixed. The population is a unit-mass distribution over supported coverage types \(H_t\subseteq M\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\); the decision variables are type-level charge rates \(q_t\). The target is a compact exact representation of the core, whose constraints reduce to \(q_t=0\) for \(|H_t|>1\) and \(\sum_{t:H_t=\{k\}}\mu_tq_t=c_k\) for each facility \(k\).

The objection that survived

Proposition 1 is not framed as a computation problem and gives no runtime bound; the proponent acknowledges the missing complexity claim, while relying on the explicit compact core representation as the computed object.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1's exact core description; it leaves the paper's separate characterizations of the nucleolus, equal-split and serial rules, and its monotonicity results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-symmetric core of a finite \(n\)-agent instance correspond exactly under the scaling \(q_t=n x_i\) for agents \(i\) of type \(t\)?
  • What happens when a facility's singleton-coverage mass tends to zero or vanishes?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named theorem in this paper that classifies a cost-sharing problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its strongest defensible computational anchor is Proposition 1, proved here: it gives an exact description of the core, which the paper then uses to derive allocations. I would use it as a closed-form computation anchor, not claim it is a complexity theorem.

Proposition 1 says that, for a problem with no redundant facilities, the core is exactly the set of allocations \(x\in\mathbb{R}^N_+\) such that \(\sum_{i\in N}x_i=c(P)\), every agent covered by more than one facility pays zero, and for every facility \(k\), the charges to agents covered only by \(k\) sum to \(c_k\). This is the single anchor I would rely on.

A plausible regime is a city maintaining a fixed set of service facilities. Many residents may share the same set of facilities—for example, the same emergency depots or clinics cover them—so \(n\) can be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct coverage patterns stays modest. The type \(t\) is a resident’s complete coverage pattern \(H_t\subseteq M\); mass \(\mu_t\) is that pattern’s fraction of the population. The fixed facility costs remain \(c_k\). This keeps the paper’s setting of allocating the cost of facilities already chosen, rather than changing the decision to open them.

My lead mirror is Continuous Core Computation. An instance consists of facilities \(M\), rational costs \(c_k\ge 0\), and a finite support \(T\) of coverage types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Require each facility \(k\) to have positive mass of residents covered only by \(k\), mirroring the paper’s no-redundant-facilities assumption. For a measurable resident coalition \(E\), let \(c_\mu(E)\) be the minimum cost of a subset of facilities covering everyone in \(E\). A type-symmetric allocation is a per-unit-mass charge \(q_t\) for each type. It is in the core if total charges equal \(\sum_k c_k\) and every coalition’s charges are at most \(c_\mu(E)\). The task is to output the full set of such allocations, represented compactly.

The direct continuous counterpart of Proposition 1 is
\[ \left\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{T}_{\ge 0}: q_t=0\ \text{if }|H_t|>1,\quad \sum_{t:H_t=\{k\}}\mu_t q_t=c_k\ \text{for every }k\in M \right\}. \]
Here the decision variable is the type’s charge rate; the criterion is efficiency and coalition stability, rather than a scalar minimization objective. The displayed constraints are a compact description of the answer, with size polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Thus I expect this mirror to be tractable. Its high-multiplicity link is concrete: in an \(n\)-person instance, charges of about \(q_t/n\) per resident of type \(t\) aggregate to the continuous charge \(\mu_tq_t\). Averaging charges among identical residents is natural because the problem treats them identically.

The scope is just Proposition 1’s core characterization. The NP-completeness mentioned in the introduction concerns choosing facilities for the set-covering problem; this paper fixes the facilities in advance, so it is not an anchor for this mirror. I would also leave the paper’s axiomatic characterizations and monotonicity results out of scope: continuizing those properties would not add a computational result.

The follow-up questions are whether the type-symmetric discrete core and this continuous core correspond exactly under the \(q_t/n\) scaling, and what happens when a facility’s private cohort has vanishing mass or disappears.

The weak point is substantial: Proposition 1 already makes the continuous computation easy, and the paper does not present core computation as a complexity problem. This is a plausible population mirror of a named result, but it offers no hardness that could dissolve, and the cited set-covering hardness belongs to a different problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has one anchor: Proposition 1’s characterization of the core. The strongest case against it is about fit with ChoCo’s computational remit. The paper does not study the complexity of computing an allocation or a core. Its NP-completeness result concerns choosing facilities, which this paper explicitly takes as already settled. The remaining results characterize cost-sharing rules and their properties. Proposition 1 is a structural theorem about the core, not a complexity result for a computational problem.

That distinction does not make the proposed population model unsound. Coverage pattern is a natural complete type, and a city’s residents could plausibly form large cohorts with the same pattern. With positive mass of residents covered only by each facility, the type-level rates \(q_t\) satisfy the displayed constraints \(\sum_{t:H_t=\{k\}}\mu_tq_t=c_k\), with \(q_t=0\) for multiply covered types. That is a coherent continuous core question; averaging discrete charges among identical residents gives its type-symmetric counterpart. Vanishing private cohorts are a real limit issue, but a regime with stable positive-mass cohorts avoids it.

So the negative case is that this is an adjacent cost-sharing project, not a paper offering the kind of computational result ChoCo set out to mirror. Calling compact core representation “computation” supplies a task the paper itself did not pose. That is a scope objection, not a fundamental objection to continuization. If ChoCo includes core computation in its remit, I cannot honestly rule out this mirror: the high-multiplicity setting is sensible, and Proposition 1 has a valid continuous counterpart.
