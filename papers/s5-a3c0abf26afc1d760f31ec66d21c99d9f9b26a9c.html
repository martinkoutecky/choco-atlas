Why no mirror

Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 are computational results, but their equilibria are defined by unilateral deviations of named agents. The proposed distributional version makes a whole type cohort coordinate on a report and replaces individual partner comparisons with distributional dominance; an individual deviation in an atomless population would not register in the mass matching. That is a plausible related game, but the cases do not establish it as a continuous version the authors would recognize as their problem.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror would cover the equilibrium-computation claims in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, but no population-level version of either survives the strategic-unit mismatch; the paper’s other results remain finite-agent results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a type-cohort version of the accomplice game. It is a credible extension of the paper’s problem, though I would not present it as a direct high-multiplicity restatement.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every accomplice manipulation game with strategic pairs \(P\subseteq M\times W\), a pure strategy Nash equilibrium exists and can be computed in polynomial time. In the game, strategic men report rankings while women report truthfully; a pair \((m,w)\) can deviate when the man’s report makes the woman strictly better off without making him worse off. An equilibrium is a profile with no such deviation.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. Call it Distributional Accomplice Equilibrium. The two sides are finite sets of types \(I\) and \(J\), with rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) summing to one on each side. A type records its members’ true strict ranking over opposite-side types and its strategic role. The regime is a large matching market—say, a ride-sharing market with many drivers and passengers, where time, location, and service preferences produce many copies of each type but far fewer types than people.

A matching is a mass matrix \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with row sums \(\alpha_i\) and column sums \(\beta_j\). Fix a split-mass Deferred Acceptance rule: unmatched mass proposes in ranking order; each receiving type holds its preferred proposal mass up to its capacity and rejects the rest. A strategic male type \(i\) may submit one reported ranking for its cohort. Let \(R\subseteq I\times J\) specify the linked strategic type pairs. A deviation by \(i\) for beneficiary type \(j\) is valid if, under the true rankings, the post-deviation partner distribution of cohort \(i\) is weakly better in first-order stochastic dominance, and that of cohort \(j\) is strictly better. The question is to output a reported ranking for each strategic male type and the resulting mass matching \(x\), with no valid type-pair deviation. The expected classification is Class A, with a target runtime polynomial in \(|I|,|J|,|R|\), and the input bit length \(L\). The paper’s push-up argument suggests a route, but the fractional matching and the corresponding equilibrium potential need to be established.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every one-for-many manipulation game, with strategic men \(P_m\) and beneficiary sets \(P_w^m\), an equilibrium can be computed in polynomial time, and at least one such equilibrium yields a stable matching under Deferred Acceptance. Its mirror is Distributional One-for-Many Equilibrium: each strategic male type \(i\) has a designated set \(B_i\subseteq J\); its cohort’s true partner distribution must not worsen, every beneficiary type in \(B_i\) must weakly improve, and at least one positive-mass beneficiary type must strictly improve. The requested solution is an equilibrium report profile and its mass matching. I would also expect Class A, provisionally, since the paper reduces one-for-many equilibrium computation to accomplice equilibrium computation. Further questions are whether the resulting mass matching is stable under the true type rankings and whether the push-up dynamics still has a polynomial bound.

The scope here is just the paper’s accomplice and one-for-many equilibrium computation results. The paper supplies no named hardness result, so this is not a case that discrete hardness might dissolve. Its computational contribution is the polynomial equilibrium algorithm, and the mirror asks whether the structure behind that algorithm survives for rational type masses.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s strategic unit is an individual man, whereas this mirror makes a whole type cohort one strategic actor. A measure-zero individual’s report ordinarily cannot change a continuum matching, so preserving the original unilateral deviation semantics may leave no meaningful incentive problem. The cohort model repairs that by giving a type’s mass a common report, but that introduces coordination and replaces rankings over named individuals with rankings over counterpart types. The paper’s own ride-sharing motivation and its one-for-many variant make a cohort extension plausible; they do not establish that the authors would accept it as the same problem. That is why I would argue for this as a concrete, paper-connected extension, not as an already faithful continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the unit of strategic action. Theorem 1 is a genuine computational result, so there is no basis for dismissing it as merely axiomatic. But the game it solves is built around named individuals: a particular man changes his report, a particular linked woman benefits, and the man’s regret is judged by comparing his own partners under his true ranking. The matching itself records who is paired with whom.

The proponent’s cohort model is the best obvious repair. Give each finite type a common report, match type masses fractionally, and judge gains by changes in partner distributions. That is coherent, but it changes the deviation in Theorem 1: a whole positive-mass cohort now acts in coordination. In the paper, a strategic pair is a man and a woman; there is expressly no coordination among accomplices or beneficiaries. The cohort model also has to choose a new payoff test, such as first-order stochastic dominance, in place of the paper’s comparison of two named agents’ partners. Those choices define a new coalition game, rather than a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s unilateral-accomplice game.

Keeping the original unilateral deviation exposes the other side of the problem. In a population represented only by type masses, one man’s change affects zero mass in the limit. The aggregate matching cannot register a change to his or his beneficiary’s individual partner, so it cannot evaluate the paper’s equilibrium condition. One could add an identity-level matching rule or define deviations by inserting a tagged individual into the continuum. But then the mass matrix is no longer enough to specify the relevant outcome: the added individual and the rule for matching them carry precisely the micro-level information the aggregate representation discarded. That is a substantial new model choice, not a consequence of the paper’s game.

Corollary 1 does not escape this issue. Its one-for-many game still has a single man deviating; the larger beneficiary set does not become a coordinated player. Requiring a positive-mass beneficiary cohort to improve, or requiring its partner distribution to improve in some aggregate sense, may produce a sensible cohort game. But it replaces the paper’s condition that every designated woman weakly benefits and at least one strictly benefits. In the continuum, an individual beneficiary’s strict gain has zero mass; making it observable again requires an identity-level account. Since Corollary 1 reduces equilibrium computation to the accomplice game, it inherits the same strategic-unit mismatch rather than supplying an independent continuous anchor.

So both anchors face the same fork: preserve individual deviations and lose them in the aggregate state, or make cohorts the deviators and change the game. The latter is the strongest positive proposal, and large matching markets make it plausible as a research question. The honest weakness in the negative case is that such a coordinated type-level manipulation game could itself be worthwhile, and the paper’s ride-sharing motivation gives it some connection to the authors’ interests. What the paper does not establish is that this is a continuous mirror of either theorem. If the programme requires that connection, I would argue against spending its time on these anchors; I would not claim to have proved that no related continuum matching game could be worthwhile.
