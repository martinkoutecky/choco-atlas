The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(p\), a finite support \(T\) of ranking-and-price types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a per-capita budget \(B\), and an affected-share limit \(\alpha\), is there an allocation \(x_{t,j}\geq 0\) for integral upward shifts \(j\) such that \(\sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,j>0}x_{t,j}\leq\alpha\), \(\sum_{t,j}\pi_t(j)x_{t,j}\leq B\), and \(p\)'s resulting Borda score is at least every other candidate's?

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking-and-price types. The decision variables \(x_{t,j}\) give the mass of type \(t\) assigned shift amount \(j\), where each individual shift remains integral; mass conservation, affected-share, budget, and Borda winner constraints give a rational LP with polynomially many variables and constraints in \(m\) and \(\tau\).

The objection that survived

The LP does not retain Theorem 7.11's exact \(k\)-voter bound for a fixed electorate, since a type's mass may split across shifts; the proponent identifies this as a relaxation and leaves fixed-population rounding to follow-up.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers continuous Borda campaign feasibility for ranking-and-price types, with affected-share and per-capita budget limits, across the price families considered for Theorem 7.11. It leaves the thesis's maximin and Copeland results, other parameterizations, and exact fixed-population hardness outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What additive rounding guarantee transfers a continuous solution to a fixed-size electorate of \(n\) people?
  • For which support-size regimes does the continuous model yield useful exact or approximation results for the corresponding discrete campaign problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

Lead mirror: continuous Borda Shift Bribery. The anchor is Theorem 7.11, proved in this thesis: Borda SHIFT BRIBERY is \(W[2]\)-hard when parameterized by the number of affected voters, for each price family considered—unit, convex, sortable, arbitrary, and all-or-nothing prices. The proof reduces from SET COVER: it first establishes hardness for unit prices, then adapts the construction to all-or-nothing prices, which gives the other stated families. The underlying question is whether a campaign can make its preferred candidate win by shifting that candidate upward in voters’ rankings within a budget.

Here is a continuous version of that same question. An instance has candidates \(C\), a target \(p\), and a finite support \(T\) of types. A type \(t\) consists of a complete ranking \(\rho_t\) and its per-voter shift price function \(\pi_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Given a per-capita budget \(B\) and an affected-mass limit \(\alpha\), choose \(x_{t,j}\geq 0\): the mass of type \(t\) whose members shift \(p\) upward by exactly \(j\) positions. Here \(j=0\) means no shift, and each positive \(j\) is an integral shift for every person in that mass. Require
\[ \sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t \quad\text{for each }t,\qquad \sum_{t,j>0}x_{t,j}\leq\alpha,\qquad \sum_{t,j}\pi_t(j)x_{t,j}\leq B. \]
The resulting Borda score of candidate \(c\) is
\[ \sum_{t,j}x_{t,j}\,s_B(\rho_t^{(j)},c), \]
where \(\rho_t^{(j)}\) is the ranking after the shift. The question is whether there is such an allocation for which \(p\) is a Borda co-winner; a solution is the allocation \(x\). This is the Continuous Borda Shift Bribery decision problem.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a large membership or online-platform vote: many people share a ballot and the same outreach-cost schedule, while the number of ballot-and-price cohorts is much smaller than the population. For example, an organizer might ask whether shifting at most \(0.5\%\) of a very large membership, within a per-member campaign budget, can make a proposal win. The type must include the price schedule as well as the ranking. Grouping people who share both is precisely the high-multiplicity assumption, not a distortion of the bribery question.

The expected outcome is tractability (Class A). The constraints above and every Borda winner condition are linear, so this is rational linear-program feasibility with \(O(\tau m)\) variables, where \(\tau=|T|\) and \(m=|C|\). Thus the mirror is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. It puts the population—not the outcome space or time—on a continuum, while each individual still receives an ordinary integral shift.

There is a direct finite-to-continuous dictionary: in a discrete election with \(n_t\) people of type \(t\), an action shifting \(a_{t,j}\) of them by \(j\) positions gives \(x_{t,j}=a_{t,j}/n\), where \(n\) is the total population. A discrete action therefore yields a continuous solution with affected mass \(k/n\) and cost normalized by \(n\). Conversely, rational masses and a rational feasible allocation can be scaled to integer counts in a sufficiently large replicated electorate. Further questions include how much rounding error is needed to transfer a continuous solution back to a fixed finite population, and how the minimum campaign cost trades off against the affected fraction.

The mirror covers Theorem 7.11’s Borda hardness result only; it does not claim to cover the thesis’s maximin or Copeland results or its other bribery models. Its weakest point is that the relaxation permits a type’s mass to split across different shift amounts, whereas a finite campaign acts on whole voters. That difference is the intended high-multiplicity relaxation, and the underlying decision remains the paper’s: how much population to shift, at what cost, to make \(p\) win. The theorem’s SET COVER reduction may use many distinct preference types, so it does not itself establish hardness when \(\tau\) is small; that is a separate regime to investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that the proposed mirror does not preserve Theorem 7.11’s fine-grained parameter: that theorem asks whether a campaign can succeed while affecting at most \(k\) individual voters, whereas the mirror caps affected *mass*. If we insist on an exact electorate of fixed size \(n\), the variables must lie on the grid \(x_{t,j}\in\{0,1/n,2/n,\ldots\}\). Dropping that grid permits a type’s members to split across different shifts and can turn a discrete no-instance into a continuous yes-instance. The SET COVER reduction relies on selecting indivisible voters; this relaxation removes that selection structure.

That objection does not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s stated scope. Replacing a small headcount by a share of society is the intended population continuization, and a type can include both the ranking and its price schedule. For a society with moderate support \(\tau\), the continuous campaign question has variables \(x_{t,j}\), mass-conservation constraints, a budget constraint, an affected-mass constraint, and linear Borda winner inequalities. It is a rational LP of size polynomial in \(\tau\), \(m\), and the input bit length. Requiring integral shifts for each person does not prevent this: \(j\) remains an integer shift, while a continuum of identical people can be divided among shift amounts.

The stronger version of the mirror survives the same objection. Minimizing per-capita campaign cost subject to an affected-share cap, or tracing the tradeoff between those quantities, is still an LP and remains recognizably shift bribery. The discrete hardness dissolving through divisible mass is exactly the kind of result the programme seeks. The paper contains no continuous-population treatment that collides with it, and high-multiplicity concerns about grouping people by their complete type do not undermine the model.

So the negative case is weak. One can argue that this particular mirror may be a short, routine LP result and does not answer the fixed-electorate cardinality question. That may affect its priority, but it does not show there is no worthwhile mirror: Theorem 7.11 is a clear computational anchor, and its population-level version is well-posed and tractable.
