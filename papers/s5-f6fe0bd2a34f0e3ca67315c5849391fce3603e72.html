The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(j\in[\tau]\) with rational masses \(\mu_j>0\), \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\), per-capita entitlements \(e_j>0\) with \(\sum_j\mu_je_j=1\), known bounded loads \(w_{jt}\), thresholds \(\alpha_j\), and unknown nondecreasing payoff functions \(f_j\) satisfying the paper's Lipschitz and positive near-threshold-gradient assumptions, each type has unit demand \(\eta_j^\star=f_j^{-1}(\alpha_j)\). In each round, a monitor chooses per-capita allocations \(x_{jt}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j\mu_jx_{jt}\le 1\), then receives one type-level feedback observation with conditional mean \(f_j(x_{jt}/w_{jt})\) and the paper's sub-Gaussian noise bounds. Does there exist an online policy, using weighted MMF allocations, with high-probability sublinear population-weighted inefficiency \(L_T^\mu=\sum_{t=1}^T\min\{1-\sum_j\mu_jx_{jt}+\sum_j\mu_j(x_{jt}-w_{jt}\eta_j^\star)_+,\sum_j\mu_j(w_{jt}\eta_j^\star-x_{jt})_+\}\), sublinear per-type fairness gaps \(\sum_{t=1}^T[u_j(e_j/w_{jt})-u_j(x_{jt}/w_{jt})]\), and a per-round computation bound in \(\tau\), numeric input bit lengths, and maintained confidence-tree sizes?

The model it lives in

A monitored nonatomic population of standardized workload cohorts with masses \(\mu_j\). Each type shares its per-capita entitlement \(e_j\), load path \(w_{jt}\), payoff function \(f_j\), threshold \(\alpha_j\), and feedback law. Given demand estimates \(\hat\eta_{jt}\), allocate \(x_{jt}=\min\{w_{jt}\hat\eta_{jt},\lambda_te_j\}\), choosing \(\lambda_t\) so \(\sum_j\mu_jx_{jt}=\min\{1,\sum_j\mu_jw_{jt}\hat\eta_{jt}\}\); learn from one specified noisy type-level observation per round. The monitor seeks sublinear population-weighted inefficiency and per-type fairness gaps, without an incentive-compatibility requirement.

The objection that survived

The type-level feedback channel needs a specified interpretation, such as a cohort-level measurement with the stated sub-Gaussian noise; Theorem 8 does not itself establish bounds for that channel or the weighted-mass model.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8's nonparametric stochastic demand learning and its cumulative inefficiency and fairness guarantees. It leaves the deterministic and parametric results, the other nonparametric strategy-proofness setting, and the static MMF result in Theorem 1 aside; it makes no strategy-proofness claim.

  • Specify the feedback oracle or cohort-level noise interpretation and establish a worst-case per-round runtime bound in \(\tau\), numeric input bit lengths, and confidence-tree sizes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a continuous population of monitored service cohorts. It is a natural mirror of the paper’s demand-learning problem, though the paper does not contain the kind of computational-complexity result your anchor rule asks for.

That distinction matters. The numbered results are proved in the paper, but none establishes that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class, and none gives a worst-case running-time bound. Theorem 1 proves that static MMF is efficient, fair, and strategy-proof; Theorems 2–8 give online loss, fairness, and incentive guarantees. “Efficient” in those results means allocation efficiency, not polynomial-time computability. So there is no qualifying complexity anchor here.

The nearest useful anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. It analyzes Algorithm 3 with Algorithms 7–10 under stochastic nonparametric feedback, assuming nondecreasing \(L/\eta_{\max}\)-Lipschitz payoff functions with positive near-threshold gradient, bounded loads and noise, and truthful data. With probability at least \(1-\delta\), it bounds cumulative inefficiency and each user’s cumulative fairness gap. In the paper’s notation, the bounds are
\[ L_T \le C_1 n\left( \frac{\sqrt L\,w\eta_{\max}\sigma}{G^{3/2}}\beta_{2T}\sqrt T +\frac{Lw\eta_{\max}^3\sigma^2}{G^3\epsilon_G^3}\beta_{2T}^2 +\frac{w\eta_{\max}^2\sigma^2}{G^2\epsilon_G^2}\beta_{2T}^2+C_3 \right), \]
and, for each user \(i\),
\[ U^{e_i}_{iT}-U_{iT}\le C_2L_i^u\left( \frac{\sqrt L\,\eta_{\max}\sigma}{G^{3/2}}\beta_{2T}\sqrt T +\frac{Lw\eta_{\max}^3\sigma^2}{G^3\epsilon_G^3}\beta_{2T}^2 +\frac{w\eta_{\max}^2\sigma^2}{G^2\epsilon_G^2}\beta_{2T}^2+C_3 \right). \]
Here \(G\in(0,G_0]\), \(\epsilon_G\) is the radius in the paper’s near-threshold condition, \(C_1,C_2\) are global constants, \(C_3=L^2/G^2+L\eta_{\max}/G+1\), and \(\beta_t=\sqrt{(4+2\log 2)\log(n\pi^2t^3/(6\delta))}\). The theorem gives sublinear cumulative inefficiency and fairness gap. It imposes no strategy-proofness guarantee. That last feature fits the paper’s explicitly discussed setting where a central monitor observes loads and feedback.

Here is a concrete mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous Nonparametric MMF Demand Learning. The population has finite support \(T\), with \(\tau\) types and rational masses \(\mu_j>0\) summing to one. A type completely specifies a member’s payoff curve, SLO threshold \(\alpha_j\), per-capita entitlement \(e_j\), load path, and feedback-noise model; normalize entitlements by \(\sum_j\mu_je_j=1\). Each type has an unknown nondecreasing payoff function \(f_j\), a unit demand \(\eta_j^\star\) at its threshold, and the paper’s Lipschitz and positive near-threshold-gradient assumptions. In each round, a central monitor observes the type’s load \(w_{jt}\), chooses the same per-capita allocation \(x_{jt}\) for every member of that type, and receives one noisy type-level feedback observation with mean \(f_j(x_{jt}/w_{jt})\).

The allocation must respect \(\sum_j\mu_jx_{jt}\le1\). Given current demand estimates \(\widehat\eta_{jt}\), the continuous MMF rule allocates
\[ x_{jt}=\min\{w_{jt}\widehat\eta_{jt},\,\lambda_te_j\}, \]
where \(\lambda_t\) is chosen to use the resource whenever estimated demand exceeds supply. The objective is to learn the demands while keeping cumulative population-weighted inefficiency sublinear:
\[ L_T^\mu=\sum_{t=1}^T\min\left\{ 1-\sum_j\mu_jx_{jt}+\sum_j\mu_j(x_{jt}-w_{jt}\eta_j^\star)_+,\ \sum_j\mu_j(w_{jt}\eta_j^\star-x_{jt})_+ \right\}. \]
The mechanism should also make each type’s per-capita fairness gap sublinear, comparing \(u_j(e_j/w_{jt})\) with \(u_j(x_{jt}/w_{jt})\). A solution is an online allocation and learning policy, with a high-probability bound on these quantities and a worst-case per-round computation bound in \(\tau\), the input bit lengths, and the maintained confidence-tree size. Feedback is directly monitored, so this version makes no incentive-compatibility claim.

The regime is a large cluster with many replicas or tenants running standardized workload templates. Members of a type share a performance curve, SLO, entitlement, and per-capita load path; \(\tau\) workload classes is small relative to the number of replicas. The paper itself motivates learning demands in shared compute clusters and notes that management may directly observe loads and rewards. The mirror’s new continuous object is the population distribution over cohorts. Resource divisibility is already in the paper, so that is not the continuization claim.

I expect this finite-support version to be algorithmically tractable in the spirit of Theorem 8: learn once per type and compute weighted MMF by sorting or water filling across \(\tau\) types. The open work is to prove the population-weighted regret and fairness bounds, account for type masses and feedback aggregation, and establish worst-case runtime. I would not claim Theorem 8 already proves those results, or call this a P-versus-NP classification.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: the same MMF allocation, unknown unit demands, thresholded utilities, and online feedback remain. The weakest point is the grouping assumption. Real tenants may have different load paths or performance curves; they belong to different types unless those differences are part of a shared, complete cohort description. The mirror is strongest for replicated workloads, not for an arbitrary organization’s user population. It also does not test whether continuization dissolves discrete hardness: the paper offers no hardness anchor, and its core contribution is statistical performance rather than worst-case complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 8 is the only serious anchor. Its best mirror is coherent: take many monitored replicas of a small set of standardized workloads. Members of each type share the same entitlement, load path, payoff curve and feedback law; the monitor observes feedback and makes the same per-capita allocation to each member. Represent the cohorts by masses \(\mu_j\), and use population-weighted inefficiency and per-type fairness. That is a genuine continuous-population question, and the authors could recognize it as their demand-learning problem. Individual identity is no objection in this regime, and scaling resource supply per capita is an admissible co-modification.

That concession makes the universal negative hard to defend. The strongest case against is about fit with ChoCo’s research target. Theorem 8 proves high-probability cumulative inefficiency and fairness bounds for an online learning algorithm. It does not classify the worst-case complexity of a computational problem or give a bit-complexity bound. The paper treats payoff functions as unknown and learns them through feedback; it gives no finite encoding for those functions. Adding a runtime bound in \(\tau\), input bit length and confidence-tree size would therefore be a new result under a specified oracle or function representation, not a mirror of Theorem 8’s stated guarantee.

The proposed cohort model also changes the feedback channel. The paper obtains an observation from each user; a type-level observation could mean pooling many users’ noisy rewards, changing the noise, or retaining a shared cohort shock. Those are reasonable models, but a weighted extension does not follow just by replacing \(n\) with \(\tau\). If the task keeps the unknown payoffs and round-by-round feedback, it remains primarily a statistical learning problem. If it removes them to obtain a static, explicitly encoded optimization problem, it has moved away from the result that supplied the anchor.

So the case against succeeds only under a narrower claim: this is a plausible neighboring online-learning project, but it does not fill ChoCo’s stated gap in computational complexity for problems over continuous societies. The honest weakness is substantial. If “worthwhile mirror” includes continuous-population extensions of online learning guarantees, Theorem 8 survives; I cannot honestly argue that no sensible scenario exists.
