The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) indivisible projects with costs \(c_x\), budget \(1\), unit-sum utility types \(u_t\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\), partition each type mass into \(m\) candidate-query strata, apply independent thresholds \(T\sim U[0,1]\), observe the exact positive-report mass \(a_x=\sum_t\mu_tu_t(x)\) for each project, and output a feasible set \(S\) maximizing \(\sum_{x\in S}a_x\); determine its welfare guarantee and computational complexity.

The model it lives in

Participatory budgeting over a finite set of complete utility types \(t\), with population masses \(\mu_t\), indivisible projects, costs \(c_x\), unit budget, and exact mass-level one-bit threshold reports. The decision variable is a feasible project set \(S\), and the objective is per-capita welfare \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{x\in S}u_t(x)\).

The objection that survived

The continuous version supplies exact aggregate approval masses, so the finite sampling and information constraints disappear and the mechanism effectively computes the welfare vector before solving a budgeted selection problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 2 and plausibly extends Theorem 5; it does not establish mirrors for the paper's other numbered results, and Theorem 7 is excluded by the computational-result requirement.

Open questions for a prover

  • What formal input and communication model should charge for exact aggregate threshold-report masses without treating them as an unpriced oracle?
  • What is the precise finite-replication dictionary for truthful sampled-agent mechanisms over \(\mu\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, especially for its large-electorate threshold result. It does not provide a named NP-hardness theorem, so the case is algorithmic and approximation-theoretic rather than a hardness-transfer story.

The natural regime is participatory budgeting in a large municipality or organization. There are \(n\) residents, \(m\) recurring projects, and a fixed budget. Residents fall into a relatively small number \(\tau\) of complete utility types: a type includes the entire unit-sum utility vector \(u_t\), not merely a ranking. Thus the society is represented by masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\tau\ll n\). This is plausible when preferences are generated by recurring household, neighbourhood, or demographic templates. Projects remain indivisible: the continuous object is only the population.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It concerns the mechanism I.I.D.-THRESHOLDS-AND-CANDIDATES. Agents are randomly partitioned among the \(m\) candidates; an agent assigned to \(x\) receives an independent threshold \(T_i\sim U[0,1]\) and reports the single bit \(b_i=1\) iff \(u_i(x)\ge T_i\). For every candidate, the mechanism estimates its welfare by the fraction of positive reports in its assigned group, then returns the budget-feasible set maximizing the sum of these estimates. The theorem states that, with \(\delta=m^{5/2}\sqrt{72\log(4mn)/n}\), the returned set \(S\) satisfies \(uw(S)\ge OPT(1-\delta)\) with probability at least \(1-1/n\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuum Binary-Threshold Budgeting. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), rational project costs \(c_x\), budget \(1\), a finite utility-type set \(T\), and masses \(\mu_t\). For each candidate \(x\), create a query stratum of mass \(1/m\) containing mass \(\mu_t/m\) of every type \(t\). Each unit of mass in that stratum receives an independent threshold and reports only whether \(u_t(x)\) exceeds it. From the resulting approval masses \(\bar V_x\), output a feasible set \(S\) maximizing \(\sum_{x\in S}\bar V_x\). The objective is to maximize \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{x\in S}u_t(x)\).

In the continuum, \(\bar V_x\) is exactly \(W_\mu(\{x\})\), because \(\Pr[T\le u_t(x)]=u_t(x)\). Hence the statistical error in Theorem 2 disappears: the continuous mechanism achieves distortion \(1\), subject only to solving the resulting finite knapsack instance. Exact selection retains the usual knapsack difficulty, but an FPTAS is available; this is inherited finite-outcome computation, not continuum-specific hardness. The question is therefore Class A in its approximation version.

This is a particularly credible mirror because the paper itself motivates the mechanism by the phrase “when the number of agents is large.” The continuous version does not give the mechanism cardinal reports, fractional projects, or a different objective. It gives exactly the paper’s one-bit information protocol, with the finite sampling noise replaced by population mass.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 5, proved here. The paper’s \(k\)-WINNER-SELECTION mechanism samples an agent uniformly, chooses a candidate \(z\) from that agent’s ranking with probability proportional to \(1/r_i(z)\), independently samples a uniformly random \(k\)-set \(Y\), and returns \(Z=\{z\}\) or \(Y\), each with probability \(1/2\). The mechanism is truthful because the \(Z\)-part is a point-voting mechanism and the \(Y\)-part is independent of reports. The theorem states that its expected social welfare is at least \(OPT/(4\sqrt m\log m)\), where \(OPT\) is the welfare of the best \(k\)-set.

The corresponding problem is:

Continuum Truthful Harmonic \(k\)-Selection. An instance consists of \(m\) candidates, \(k\), a finite set of unit-sum utility types \(T\), strict rankings \(r_t\), and masses \(\mu_t\). A mechanism may sample a unit of population according to \(\mu\), observe only its ordinal ranking, and output a set of \(k\) candidates. Its objective is expected per-capita welfare \(W_\mu(S)\), and truthfulness is required for a sampled agent: conditional on being sampled, reporting its true ranking must maximize its expected utility.

The paper’s mechanism is immediately well-defined: sample \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t\), choose \(z\) with probability \(1/(H_m r_t(z))\), independently sample \(Y\), and return \(Z\) or \(Y\). Its expected welfare is a finite sum over types, and the theorem’s guarantee remains \(OPT/(4\sqrt m\log m)\). Thus this is also an explicit Class A mirror for evaluating and implementing a truthful mechanism over a high-multiplicity society.

The continuum version needs one semantic safeguard: ordinary dominant-strategy truthfulness becomes vacuous if an individual has literally zero mass and cannot affect an aggregate outcome. The clean interpretation is the finite-replication semantics: the continuous rule must be the limit of truthful rules on every finite society containing \(q\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\). For the sampled-agent mechanism above, this condition is automatic, since the sampled agent can affect the outcome with positive probability.

The third anchor is Theorem 7, also proved here. It states that every truthful ordinal mechanism for one-winner selection has distortion at least \(\sqrt m\log m/8\). The proof constructs, for each rank \(r\), an instance in which a special candidate has rank \(r\) for a carefully chosen fraction of agents, while the remaining candidates are balanced across ranks. The theorem uses the characterization results in Theorems 3 and 4, which are cited from Gibbard, Barbera, and Filos-Ratsikas–Miltersen.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuum One-Winner Ordinal Distortion Frontier. An instance consists of \(m\) candidates, a finite ranking-type set, and ranking masses \(\rho_\pi\). A solution is a randomized ordinal rule, interpreted through truthful finite replications of the mass profile. Its distortion is the worst ratio, over all unit-sum cardinal utility assignments compatible with the ranking masses, between the best candidate’s welfare and the rule’s expected welfare. The task is to determine or approximate the minimum achievable worst-case distortion.

I expect Theorem 7’s lower bound to transfer essentially unchanged. The construction uses population fractions such as \(r\sqrt m\log m/(2m)\), and those fractions become ordinary masses in the continuum. Theorem 5 with \(k=1\) supplies the matching \(O(\sqrt m\log m)\) upper bound. Thus the continuous frontier should remain \(\Theta(\sqrt m\log m)\): infinite population eliminates sampling noise, but it does not eliminate the information-theoretic loss caused by ordinal reports. This is a transferred impossibility boundary, not continuum-specific hardness.

These mirrors cover Theorems 2, 5, and 7. They do not claim to continuize every result in the paper. Theorem 6 should admit a similar general-cost participatory-budgeting mirror, while Theorem 9’s two-candidate result is less valuable as an anchor because it already works directly with population fractions. The paper contains no numbered NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity theorem; the introductory knapsack remark is not a suitable hardness anchor.

My weakest point is the type regime. Exact repetition of cardinal utility vectors is stronger than repetition of rankings, and the threshold mirror assumes that such utility templates are meaningful. The ordinal mirror also requires care about truthfulness in a nonatomic population. I would therefore present finite replication as part of the formal definition, rather than pretending that standard continuum strategyproofness is automatically meaningful. Even if that objection weakened the truthful anchors, the threshold mirror remains strong: it is precisely the paper’s large-\(n\) mechanism, with the population estimator replaced by an exact mass integral while the projects, budget constraint, one-bit reports, and welfare objective all remain unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that the paper’s \(n\) is not a population parameter in a computational problem. It is mainly a sample size in a statistical mechanism, or an index in an anonymity argument. Replacing finite sums by integrals therefore risks producing either an oracle model or a restatement, rather than a new continuous-social-choice problem.

For Theorem 2, the apparent mirror relies on declaring the aggregate mass of threshold-positive reports observable exactly. That is not the same information model as the paper’s finite mechanism: it gives the mechanism an infinite-precision population oracle. If the type distribution and utility vectors are already part of the input, the mechanism can directly compute
\[ W_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_tu_t(x), \]
without eliciting any threshold reports. If only reports are observable, exact aggregate reporting requires an idealized continuum of parallel queries. If one instead uses \(q\) finite replicas, one has simply returned to the original theorem with \(n=q\). Encoding thresholds as types gives a continuum type space and shifts the continuization from the population to the randomization device.

Thus the proposed version does not preserve the paper’s substantive information constraint. It replaces finite-sample estimation by exact revelation of the target welfare vector; the remaining task is ordinary knapsack over candidates. That is a legitimate limiting abstraction, but it is much closer to a consistency limit or statistical oracle model than to a new ChoCo computational problem.

Theorem 5 is weaker still as an anchor. The mechanism deliberately samples one voter. Its expected welfare already depends only on the empirical distribution of rankings, so replacing the empirical distribution by \(\mu\) changes notation but not the mechanism or its analysis. High multiplicity plays no operative role. Moreover, in a genuinely nonatomic society, an individual has probability zero of being selected and cannot affect the outcome, so ordinary dominant-strategy truthfulness becomes vacuous. The proposed finite-replication interpretation restores the finite mechanism, but then the continuous rule is only a limit annotation on a sequence of finite games, not an intrinsic truthful mechanism for a continuum society.

Theorem 7 has the same problem. Its lower-bound construction can indeed be written using masses, but that merely shows that the finite distortion argument is invariant under replication. It does not yield a new computational question over distributions. Standard continuum truthfulness is again vacuous; finite-replication truthfulness simply imports the finite theorem. The ranking masses also do not by themselves determine welfare, so a proper version must carry cardinal utility types as well, making the proposed “ordinal” mirror a distribution over complete utility types rather than merely rankings.

This is a serious negative case against Theorems 5 and 7, and it exposes a modelling weakness in the strongest reading of Theorem 2: either the population is given to the mechanism, or the mechanism receives exact aggregate information unavailable in the finite model. None of the three anchors naturally produces the programme’s characteristic exponential-variable optimization, separation problem, or population-dependent complexity transition.

The negative case is nevertheless not airtight. If ChoCo accepts exact aggregate report masses as a legitimate continuum primitive, then Theorem 2 does provide a well-posed Class A mirror: the finite sampling error disappears while projects remain indivisible, and the resulting knapsack computation is meaningful. I therefore cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The proponent’s Theorem 2 anchor survives under that modelling choice; the strongest defensible conclusion is that Theorems 5 and 7 are poor mirrors, while Theorem 2 remains a plausible—though computationally rather degenerate—one.
