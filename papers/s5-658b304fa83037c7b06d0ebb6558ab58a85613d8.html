Why no mirror

The paper's numbered results compare an informational statistic but contain no qualifying algorithm, complexity bound, hardness result, or computational formulation. Theorem 2 therefore cannot serve as a ChoCo computational anchor. The proposed continuum additionally changes individual certification into cohort certification and replaces strict individual priorities and unit information costs with new modelling choices.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: this paper has a plausible continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s strict rule. Its named results are comparative economic theorems, not results asserting membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), NP-hardness, parameterized tractability, or an optimization formulation. Theorem 2 is nevertheless a strong substantive anchor for a proposed follow-up.

The lead mirror is Continuum Informational Size for School Choice. Let \(O\) be a finite set of schools and let \(\Theta\) be a finite set of complete student types. A type includes a complete preference ranking and its priority position at every school. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), with \(\mu_\theta\) the mass of type \(\theta\). School capacities are per-capita quantities \(q_o\), and each student still receives an integral school; only the mass of identical students assigned to each school is fractional.

IA, DA, and TTC operate on masses: a mass of each type applies to its best remaining school, schools accept or retain the highest-priority mass, and TTC removes the minimum mass on each active cycle. Ties within a type are resolved by a fixed anonymous rule. This is a natural regime for a large admissions system with hundreds of thousands of students but relatively few repeated preference-and-priority profiles.

For a marked cohort \(\theta^\star\), school \(o\), and target mass \(\alpha\), a partial disclosure \(A\) reveals prefixes of preference rankings and prefixes of school-priority orders. Its cost is the population-scaled information amount

\[ I_\infty(A) = \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\theta,\ell} + \sum_{o\in O}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\eta_{o,\theta}, \]

where \(\lambda_{\theta,\ell}\) is the mass of type \(\theta\) for which the first \(\ell\) preferences are revealed, and \(\eta_{o,\theta}\) is the mass of type \(\theta\) whose position in school \(o\)’s priority order is revealed. A disclosure secures \((\theta^\star,o,\alpha)\) if every complete population description consistent with \(A\) assigns at least \(\alpha\) mass of \(\theta^\star\) to \(o\).

The continuous problem is:

CIS-\(f\). Given rational \(\mu\), capacities \(q\), type priority orders, \(f\in\{\mathrm{IA},\mathrm{DA},\mathrm{TTC}\}\), a marked target \((\theta^\star,o,\alpha)\), and a budget \(B\), decide whether there exists a securing disclosure \(A\) with \(I_\infty(A)\le B\). The associated optimization problem minimizes \(I_\infty(A)\), and its worst-case version takes the maximum of that minimum over all populations in which the target assignment occurs.

This is recognizably the paper’s problem: it preserves the mechanisms, the partial-ranking information structure, the worst-case guarantee, and the question of how much information is needed to certify an assignment. It continuizes the population rather than the allocation rule. The per-capita capacities and fractional masses are the necessary high-multiplicity co-modification, while individual assignments remain integral.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the paper in Appendix B.3. In heterogeneous-priority school choice it states:

\[ IS(\mathrm{IA})\dot{<}IS(\mathrm{TTC}) \quad\text{and}\quad IS(\mathrm{IA})\dot{<}IS(\mathrm{DA}), \]

and, if \(n\ge4\),

\[ IS(\mathrm{TTC})\dot{<}IS(\mathrm{DA}), \]

whereas for \(n<4\),

\[ IS(\mathrm{DA})\dot{<}IS(\mathrm{TTC}). \]

The continuous question is whether the corresponding population-scaled quantities satisfy the large-market ordering

\[ \mathrm{CIS}_{\mathrm{IA}} \dot{<} \mathrm{CIS}_{\mathrm{TTC}} \dot{<} \mathrm{CIS}_{\mathrm{DA}} \]

once the population contains at least four positive-mass priority cohorts, and whether the reversal for small finite type populations can be characterized exactly.

I would expect the fixed-\((m,\tau)\) version to be tractable, perhaps through a finite robust LP or dynamic program over type masses and ranking-prefix variables. The mechanism outcomes themselves are computable in polynomial time in \(m\) and \(\tau\), and the continuum may remove irrelevant dependence on the number of named students. But hardness could survive in the disclosure problem: choosing which partial rankings to reveal may encode combinatorial interactions between priority classes. That would make this either a Class B mirror, if finite hardness embeds by assigning rational masses, or a Class C problem if the adversarial completion structure creates genuinely continuum-specific hardness.

The weakest point is serious: Theorem 2 is not a computational-complexity theorem, so this cannot honestly be presented as a compliant ChoCo anchor. There is also no canonical continuous unit of “information”; the mass-weighted disclosure cost above is natural but not forced by the paper. Thus the paper supports a strong modelling proposal and a worthwhile computational follow-up, but not a formal positive case under the programme’s named-result requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is stronger than the proponent admits: the paper supplies no qualifying ChoCo anchor. Lemma 1, Theorems 1 and 2, and Propositions 1 and 2 are comparative statements about an information statistic. None asserts a complexity bound, gives an algorithmic formulation, proves hardness, or studies an optimization problem. Thus the proposed CIS-\(f\) is a new computational problem inspired by Theorem 2, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed mirror also loses the paper’s central primitive. The paper asks how much partial information is needed to certify the assignment of a named student \(i\) to a school \(o\). That depends on strict priority orders over individual students. If a type includes everything relevant to the mechanism, including its priority position at each school, then strict priorities imply that two students cannot have the same complete type: their positions in every strict priority list are distinct. The exact model therefore has no positive-mass repeated types.

The natural rescue is to suppress individual priority positions and replace them by priority cohorts with anonymous tie-breaking. But this is not a harmless population limit. The tie-breaking order is precisely the information that determines which individual receives a seat when a cohort is cut by capacity. If it is retained, identities return and multiplicity disappears; if it is discarded, the model no longer certifies the assignment of a named student. A positive-mass target cohort avoids that problem only by changing the target: it asks whether some mass receives \(o\), not whether the paper’s student \(i\) receives \(o\). If the cohort is split, the omitted tie-breaking information becomes necessary again.

The proposed information cost is likewise not inherited from the paper. In the finite model, revealing one student’s first preference is one unit, regardless of how many other students happen to share it. In a high-multiplicity model, revealing a common type-level preference could cost one message, its population mass, or the cost of individually communicating it to every member. These yield different problems. A per-type cost makes multiplicity largely irrelevant; a mass-weighted cost changes the paper’s unit of information; and a per-person cost preserves the individual model instead of exploiting high multiplicity. The paper gives no principle selecting the proponent’s \(I_\infty\).

Theorem 2’s proof makes the mismatch especially clear. Its comparisons rely on chains of \(n-1\) distinct students, distinct priority positions, and successive one-student proposals, rejections, and trading cycles. The quantities in Appendix B, such as \(2(n-1)\), \((n+1)(n-1)\), and \((2n-3)(n-1)\), count individual ranking entries along those chains. In an aggregate model, a positive mass of identical students moves simultaneously and one disclosed type-level prefix can settle the behaviour of the entire mass. Replacing the chains by priority quantiles or type blocks requires a new disclosure language, a new target, and a new statistic. The threshold \(n=4\) does not become a threshold of four positive-mass types without proving an entirely different theorem.

There is also an unresolved formal issue before continuization: the main definition takes a maximum over sufficient partial problems, although the proofs use least sufficient disclosures and Appendix C explicitly switches to a smallest-message definition. CIS-\(f\) silently repairs this by turning the object into a budgeted minimization problem. That may be a sensible research direction, but it confirms that the proposed problem is not the paper’s informational-size statistic carried to a continuum.

A coherent aggregate certification problem could certainly be invented, and it might be worthwhile on its own. That is the weak point in any universal negative: one cannot prove that no researcher could formulate an interesting model. But the proponent has not produced a continuous mirror of a named computational result. Their construction changes individual certification into cohort certification, strict priorities into type-level priorities with tie-breaking, and unit information into an externally chosen mass-weighted cost. Under ChoCo’s remit, that is a new problem rather than a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
