The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: preference types and population shares are natural inputs for this paper’s subject, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

A plausible regime is a large public vote in which many voters share the same complete ranking of the alternatives. Let each ranking \(R\) be a type, and let \(\mu_R\) be its share of the population. The total number of voters could be enormous while the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings is moderate. This fits the paper’s anonymous, tops-only setting: the rule treats voters alike and chooses using their reported top alternatives.

But none of the paper’s numbered results asserts a computational classification such as tractability or hardness. The main result, Theorem 1 (proved here), says that if a simple rule—unanimous, anonymous, and tops-only—is strategy-proof on a basic domain \(D\) that is rich relative to that rule, then every preference in \(D\) must be local and weakly single-peaked relative to the rule’s corresponding partial order. For a preference \(R\) with top \(x\), this means: along the order’s paths moving away from \(x\), alternatives become weakly less preferred; for an alternative \(w\) not above \(x\), the supremum of \(x\) and \(w\) exists and is weakly preferred to \(w\). The relevant partial orders are built from the rule’s outcomes when at most three alternatives receive distinct levels of support. Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 (proved here) give a maximality result for these domains. These are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results, so I would not present any of them as an eligible anchor.

The nearest concrete continuous question I can offer is therefore unanchored: given a finite alternative set \(A\), a finite set \(D\) of ranking types, and a unanimous rule \(F\) from rational polyhedral pieces of the top-share simplex to \(A\), decide whether \(F\) is immune to profitable mass-cohort misreports. An instance has a society distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(D)\); its top-share vector is \(p\). A cohort of mass \(\delta\leq\mu_R\) with true ranking \(R\) can report \(R'\), changing the distribution to \(\mu'=\mu-\delta e_R+\delta e_{R'}\). A “no” solution is a witness \((\mu,R,R',\delta)\) for which \(F(p')\) is strictly preferred to \(F(p)\) under \(R\). With the rule explicitly represented by rational polyhedral pieces, this looks tractable by checking feasible deviations between pieces. A further question would be whether such rules force a continuous analogue of the paper’s rule-relative domain restriction.

The weak point is substantial: the paper studies individual strategy-proofness. In a nonatomic population, one person has zero mass and cannot change the distribution; allowing a positive mass to deviate turns the question into coalition strategy-proofness, which the authors do not study. So the proposed decision problem changes the incentive notion, and it is not a result this paper anchors. The paper gives a sensible high-multiplicity population scenario, but its actual contribution is axiomatic rather than computational.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is categorical: this paper has no computational result for a continuous population to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes preference domains that admit a simple, strategy-proof rule. Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 establish maximality of those domains. All three are axiomatic results; none states a complexity bound, gives an algorithm, or defines a computational optimization problem. Calling them anchors for a computational mirror would change what the paper contributes.

A large electorate with many voters sharing complete rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But it does not make these results population-sensitive. The domain \(D\) is the set of preferences agents may report, while the theorem’s conditions concern individual deviations across profiles. The theorem works for every finite \(n\ge 2\); it does not analyze the observed frequencies of preference types. Reweighting those types into a distribution \(\mu\) therefore supplies a natural input representation, but no computational question from the paper follows from that change.

The proposed mass-cohort test is the closest computational candidate, and its mismatch is fundamental. If agents are nonatomic and the rule sees only top shares, an individual’s report changes those shares by zero, making individual strategy-proofness vacuous. Giving a positive mass of agents a joint deviation restores a nontrivial question, but that is coalition strategy-proofness, which the paper does not study. The rule’s explicit representation by rational polyhedral pieces also adds a computational model absent from the paper. The test may be worth studying on its own; its answer would not classify or compute anything established here.

The same problem defeats a more ambitious mirror of the maximality results. Maximality asks whether adding a preference to \(D\) preserves strategy-proofness. A population measure over preferences does not change that set-inclusion question; assigning positive mass to a deviating group changes the incentive notion again. A measure-theoretic version could be formulated, but it would be a new axiomatic question, not a computational continuation of Proposition 1 or Corollary 1.

So I would reject these results as anchors for ChoCo, while conceding that the electorate scenario itself is plausible. The negative case is strong within the programme’s stated computational remit, but it cannot establish that no worthwhile continuous research question could be built nearby. A computational study of mass-cohort manipulation might be worthwhile; the paper gives no result that makes it this paper’s continuous mirror.
