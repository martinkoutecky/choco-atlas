Why no mirror

The repeated-template cohort is a plausible population extension of the paper’s sibling-reporting game. But the only named anchor, Theorem 1, establishes an equilibrium property; it gives no algorithm, complexity guarantee, or computational formulation used to compute. The required computational-result gate therefore fails regardless of the model’s plausibility.

What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort model extends Theorem 1’s local truthful-reporting equilibrium; it leaves contract formation, payment computation, budget and accuracy simulations, and root aggregation quality outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for each parent, children are paid by scoring a reference child’s report against their own; if no child references itself, the siblings know each other’s signal distributions, and every child’s observation is stochastically relevant to every other’s, then truthful reporting by all children is a strict Nash equilibrium. There is no named result asserting a complexity class such as P, NP-hardness, or FPT. Theorem 1 is an equilibrium result, so it supports a strategic mirror, not a claim that this paper establishes an algorithmic complexity result.

My lead mirror is Mass Peer Prediction for a Sibling Cohort. Fix one parent’s cohort and its reference rule. Let \(Q\) be a finite set of complete child types: each type includes the child’s truthful feedback, signal model, referral role, and any costs or contract terms that affect its incentives. The society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) on \(Q\), with rational \(\mu_q\) and \(\sum_q\mu_q=1\). A plausible regime is a large product-feedback or anomaly-reporting platform using repeated referral templates: there may be millions of participants but only a modest number \(\tau\) of distinct signal, expertise, and contract types.

For type \(q\), let \(h_q\) be the common-knowledge distribution of the reference child’s report when that type’s observation is \(a_q\) and the reference reports truthfully. A member of type \(q\) chooses a report \(b_q\in\Delta(S)\), which induces the forecast \(g_{b_q}\) over reference reports. With a strictly proper score \(S\), its expected payoff is
\[ U_q(b_q)=\int_{\Delta(S)} S(g_{b_q}(y))\,h_q(y)\,dy. \]
The type-level objective \(\sum_{q\in Q}\mu_q U_q(b_q)\) records the cohort’s mass-weighted expected score; the strategic solution concept is an individual agent’s best response, as in Theorem 1. The problem is: given the finite type support, masses, prior and signal model, reference rule, and scoring rule, find the report profile that maximizes each type’s expected score and determine whether truthful reports \(b_q=a_q\) are uniquely optimal for every supported type. A solution is the type-contingent report profile and the resulting strict-equilibrium determination. For a fixed finite tree, the same question can be asked at each parent’s sibling cohort.

Under the theorem’s common-knowledge and stochastic-relevance conditions, I expect this problem to be tractable by the scoring rule’s structure: the truthful profile is the unique best response profile. In a finite-support instance with explicit reference kernels, the profile is direct to output, and checking whether the finite conditional distributions are distinct takes polynomial time in \(\tau\). That is a claim about this stated mirror; Theorem 1 itself gives no running-time bound. Natural follow-ups are whether payment computation stays efficient for the paper’s full continuous-density model, and whether truthful reporting survives coordinated deviations or collusion.

This mirror covers the paper’s local truth-telling result, applied to a fixed hierarchy. It does not cover contract formation, budget minimization, or the quality of the root’s aggregated answer. Nor is its rationale that the paper’s probability reports are already continuous: the mirror makes the population a distribution over agent types, while reports remain actions.

The weakest point is that this is a modest extension of an equilibrium theorem, not a case where continuous population dissolves discrete hardness. It also relies on many interchangeable participants sharing complete types and on individual deviations; Theorem 1 does not establish coalition resistance. I think the local mirror is still one the authors would recognize: it preserves their sibling-reference game and payment rule, replacing counts of exchangeable children with type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its Theorem 1 is an incentive theorem for a parent’s children: it gives conditions under which truthful reporting is a strict Nash equilibrium. The payment derivations in Section 4 and the budget–quality simulations do not analyze a computational problem’s complexity or give an algorithmic result. Theorem 1 is therefore a weak anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme.

The proposed finite-type cohort recasting does not bridge that gap. To represent the original game, its input needs the reference relationships and the conditional joint behavior of siblings, not just their type masses. If types include each child’s signal and tree role, a general tree may have as many types as children; if the report observations have the continuous densities used in the paper, a finite support requires an additional modeling choice. The proposed polynomial check of stochastic relevance checks a condition on supplied finite kernels. It does not establish a complexity result for the paper’s mechanism.

A stronger version is possible: imagine many repeated referral templates, with mass assigned to each child type and a kernel that chooses each child’s reference. That is a coherent population model, and it could support questions about expected payments or the budget–accuracy trade-off. But with a fixed kernel, masses mainly weight totals across local games; making the kernel or contracts depend on mass adds a new design problem. Optimizing that design might be worthwhile, but it is not a continuous mirror of Theorem 1’s stated result.

So I would reject this as a strong computational mirror for ChoCo. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous model is less secure: large crowdsourcing platforms are a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the repeated-template version may interest the authors. The negative case is strongest on fit to the programme, not on the claim that population continuization has no research value here.
