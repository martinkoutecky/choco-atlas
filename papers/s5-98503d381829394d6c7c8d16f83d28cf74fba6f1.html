What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see a qualifying anchor for a continuous computational mirror: the paper contains no named result about computational complexity. Its results are axiomatic impossibilities, and continuizing an axiomatic question while the society remains finite—or turning players into a continuum—would not meet ChoCo’s scope.

The closest named results are Proposition 3.1, proved here: no anonymous and neutral individual scoring method satisfies self-consistency; and Theorem 4.1, also proved here: no scoring method on the universal domain satisfies both order preservation and self-consistency. Proposition 4.2 says no scoring method satisfies neutrality, symmetry, strong order preservation, and self-consistency; it follows using Proposition 4.1 and a cited impossibility result. These are genuine impossibility theorems, but none asserts a problem is tractable or hard in the computational sense required for an anchor.

A tempting analogy would model a large league as a distribution over player types, with mass representing the share of players of each type, and ask whether a ranking rule on types can satisfy continuous versions of self-consistency and order preservation. But that remains an axiomatic compatibility question, and the output and axioms would also need substantial reinterpretation. I would not present it as a ChoCo mirror. The honest positive case is therefore too weak to supply the required continuous problem: this paper has no named computational result to anchor one to.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The fact that the paper proves no named complexity theorem is real, but it does not settle whether its results motivate a useful continuous computational problem. I would not recast Theorem 4.1 itself as an algorithmic result. I would use it to motivate a winner-robustness problem: quantify how much match evidence must change before the rank reversal at the heart of the theorem can occur.

A credible high-multiplicity setting is a large league with many players in recurring, behaviorally indistinguishable classes. A type would include everything the ranking method uses: its per-capita matchup profile against every other type, relevant costs, and other parameters. The input is a rational mass for each type and, for each of two seasons, a type-level matrix of match-result rates. The output ranks types, which is useful when the question is how player classes compare rather than which named individual is strongest. Fractional type-level rates are the natural averages of integral matches; they do not require a player to play a fractional match.

For a specified scoring method \(F\), target types \(a,b\), and a cost for changing match-result mass, ask for the least-cost change \(\rho\) such that \(F\) ranks \(a\) above \(b\) in each season but ranks \(b\) above \(a\) after aggregation. Equivalently, decide whether \(\rho\le B\). This is a computational rank-reversal resilience or campaigning problem on a continuous player population. If \(F\) is given by a finite formula, the input size and running time can be stated in the number of types, the rational bit length, and the rule’s parameters. Theorem 4.1 makes that question salient: it shows why one might need to measure the vulnerability of a chosen self-consistent method to order reversals. It does not determine the computational answer.

The proponent’s proposed “continuous axiom compatibility” question is rightly outside the programme’s computational focus. The stronger mirror is the mass-robustness problem above. Theorem 4.1 is the one anchor needed; there is no need to manufacture separate mirrors for Proposition 3.1 or Proposition 4.2. Proposition 3.1 concerns a restriction on individual scoring procedures, while Proposition 4.2 is a weaker impossibility statement derived using another result. Neither defeats the Theorem 4.1 mirror.

The main weakness is that this is a computational follow-on, not a direct continuization of the paper’s theorem: it requires choosing an explicit ranking method and a meaningful cost for changing match evidence. But those are standard choices for a robustness problem, and the large-cohort scenario makes the population axis meaningful. That leaves a worthwhile question, even though the paper itself contains no complexity theorem.
