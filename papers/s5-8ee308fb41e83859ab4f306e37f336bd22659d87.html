The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible, narrow population mirror here, but the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Equation (2) formulates the finite-buyer welfare-maximization problem; §3 says it is convex and that piecewise-linear utilities yield an LP that can be solved efficiently. Those are real computational contributions, but they are not eligible named anchors under your rule. I won’t invent one. The best hook is therefore Equation (2), explicitly as an unanchored candidate.

The regime I find plausible is a large batch market for bonds in which many accounts follow the same standardized mandate and demand curve. A type is the complete pair \((c_t,u_t)\): the same acceptable assets, encoded by the paper’s partial-order preference vector \(c_t\), and the same concave utility function \(u_t\). Suppose many accounts share a relatively small set of such types—for illustration, hundreds of thousands of accounts and a few hundred mandate types. Their mass \(\mu_t\) is their fraction of the market. This is plausible only where mandates and demand schedules really are shared; accounts with different utilities belong to different types.

My lead candidate is Continuous-Type Semi-Fungible Market Clearing. Given a finite partially ordered asset set with \(n\) items, a finite support of \(\tau\) buyer types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and per-capita supply \(\bar q\in\mathbb Q_+^n\), compute a welfare-maximizing allocation and supporting item prices. For type \(t\), let \(c_t\in\{0,1\}^n\) encode which items that type accepts, and let \(u_t\) be its utility. The problem is

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{z_t\in\mathbb R_+^n}\quad &\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,u_t(c_t^\top z_t)\\ \text{subject to}\quad &\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t z_t\le \bar q. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(z_t\) is the per-agent allocation for type \(t\), so \(\mu_t z_t\) is the quantity allocated to that type’s mass. To make the computational question exact, take each \(u_t\) to be a rational, concave, nondecreasing piecewise-linear function given by its breakpoints and slopes. A solution is an optimal allocation, its welfare value, and a nonnegative dual price vector for the supply constraints, all represented exactly.

This mirrors the underlying problem in Equation (2): it keeps the same partial-order acceptance, divisible items, concave utilities, supply constraints, welfare objective, and dual item prices. It changes the buyer list into a distribution over complete buyer types and scales supply per capita. For a finite market with \(n_t\) identical buyers of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/B\) and \(\bar q=q/B\), where \(B=\sum_t n_t\). Concavity means that replacing different allocations to identical buyers by their average allocation preserves feasibility and cannot reduce welfare. Conversely, rational masses can be scaled to integer type counts. So this is an exact high-multiplicity formulation for that regime, not a claim that divisible assets alone continuize the population.

The expected answer is Class A: with the stated utility encoding, the problem is an LP, with size polynomial in \(n\), \(\tau\), the total number of utility segments, and the input bit length. The paper’s authors should recognize it as their market-clearing problem with buyers aggregated by type. This case covers the welfare allocation and price computation around Equation (2); it does not claim a mirror of the DSIC payment rule in §4.2. For atomless agents, the effect of an individual report on an externality payment needs its own treatment.

The main weakness is substantial: there is no eligible named anchor, and this is not a hardness result whose complexity might change under continuization. The paper’s own optimization formulation already gives the natural tractability story, while exact aggregation only helps when many buyers really share complete types. Follow-up questions include how complexity changes for utilities given by evaluation or optimization oracles, and what continuum payment concept—if any—recovers the paper’s finite-buyer externality payments.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no named computational result to mirror. Equation (2) is the paper’s market-clearing formulation; §3 says it is a convex problem and that piecewise-linear utilities give an LP, but the paper states no complexity theorem or algorithmic bound. By the assignment’s standard, that is decisive: the proposed result is a new question prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.

The best version of their question is nevertheless coherent. In a large batch market, many accounts might share a complete type \((c_t,u_t)\), such as a standardized acceptance rule and demand curve. For that regime, aggregating their allocations into type masses is exact: concavity lets identical buyers’ allocations be averaged, and per-capita supply is just a normalization. I would not reject it on the ground that buyers must be individuated, or that exact aggregation is inherently uninteresting.

The difficulty for the proponent is what the mirror adds. With finite support and explicitly represented piecewise-linear utilities, the proposed problem is an explicit LP. It is the clone market with duplicate buyers grouped by type, and its basic tractability follows from the LP formulation. That may make a useful modeling dictionary or implementation, but it does not extend a complexity result in this paper: the paper provides no such result. Making the question substantially harder—for example, by using utility oracles or a distribution over a continuum of distinct utility functions—requires an input model and computational assumptions absent from the paper. That would be a new problem, not a mirror of its stated result.

The incentive-compatible payment rule is a less promising alternative. Its finite-market externality removes one named buyer. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass, so that deletion has no effect on the aggregate problem. A marginal-impact or price-taking formulation might be worthwhile, but it would require a new incentive model; the paper’s rule does not itself supply one.

So the strongest negative case is narrow: this paper has no eligible named computational anchor, and its most plausible replacement is an exact aggregation of a convex program rather than a continuation of a complexity result. I cannot honestly make the universal claim airtight: the standardized-account regime is plausible, and the aggregation may be useful. But that supports a new ChoCo problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result this paper actually proves.
