The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(S\) of duty classes, a rational per-capita supply vector \(\rho\in([0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q})^{S}\), and an identical negative-Boolean utility \(u:2^{S}\to\{0,-1\}\), decide whether there exists a distribution \(x=(x_B)_{B\subseteq S}\) with \(x_B\ge0\), \(\sum_{B\subseteq S}x_B=1\), and \(\sum_{B\ni s}x_B=\rho_s\) for every \(s\in S\), such that every pair of bundles in the support satisfies the paper's \(EFX^{+}_{0}\) condition; if so, compute such an \(x\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic unit-mass population with one agent type and identical utility \(u:2^{S}\to\{0,-1\}\). Each agent receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq S\), while \(x_B\) is the mass receiving \(B\); the vector \(\rho\) gives per-capita supplies, and the task is \(EFX^{+}_{0}\)-feasibility and construction.

The objection that survived

The paper does not specify how an arbitrary non-additive utility over one-copy items should interpret repeated duty classes, so the supply-scaled model requires an explicit symmetry and bundle-semantics convention.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6's identical negative-Boolean \(EFX^{+}_{0}\) existence and computation result; it does not cover the arbitrary-utility EF1 result, the non-existence result of Theorem 2, or the broader monotone-chores result of Theorem 3.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can feasibility and construction be solved in time polynomial in the encoding length of \(u\) and rational \(\rho\), rather than in a denominator-expanded population?
  • Which structural properties of negative-Boolean \(u\) characterize supply vectors \(\rho\) admitting an \(EFX^{+}_{0}\) distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s negative-Boolean result.

The relevant anchor is Theorem 6 (proved in this paper): when all agents have identical negative Boolean utility functions, \(u:2^S\to\{0,-1\}\), an \(\gsee\) allocation always exists, and such an allocation can be computed in polynomial time. Here \(\gsee\) is the paper’s generalized \(EFX^+_0\) condition, with the full non-monotone marginal-value definition.

My lead mirror is Negative-Boolean-\(\gsee_\infty\). The population has unit mass and one voter/agent type: every agent has the same utility function \(u\). There is a finite set \(S\) of item classes, with rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_s\in[0,1]\). A solution is a distribution \(x_B\), \(B\subseteq S\), where \(x_B\) is the mass receiving the integral bundle \(B\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{B\subseteq S}x_B=1,\qquad \sum_{B\ni s}x_B=\rho_s\quad(s\in S),\qquad x_B\ge 0. \]

Every unit of mass therefore receives an indivisible bundle; only the aggregate allocation is fractional. The solution must satisfy the paper’s \(\gsee\) condition for every two bundles \(B,B'\) in the support of \(x\): either \(u(B)\ge u(B')\), or

\[ u(B)\ge u(B'\setminus\{s\}) \quad\text{for every }s\in S^+_u(B'), \]

and

\[ u(B\setminus\{s\})\ge u(B') \quad\text{for every }s\in S^-_u(B)\cup S^0_u(B), \]

with the relevant marginal-item union nonempty. The computational task is to output any such finite-support distribution \(x\); there is no additional welfare objective, just the fairness-feasibility question.

A natural regime is a very large workforce of interchangeable agents—say, employees subject to the same combinatorial rule about which bundles of duties are unacceptable—while only \(m\) duty classes and one utility type exist. Thus \(N\) is enormous while \(\tau=1\). The item supplies scale with the workforce: \(\rho_s=0.37\) means that \(37\%\) of the workforce receives duty class \(s\), not that one named employee receives a fractional duty.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. It preserves negative Boolean preferences, indivisible bundles, the same \(\gsee\) fairness notion, and the same identical-agent regime. The only co-modification is the necessary one: finite item stocks become per-capita stocks so that almost all agents do not receive the empty bundle.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A, although Theorem 6 does not itself prove the continuous result. Its proof supplies the right structural reason: the potential

\[ \Phi(x) = m\sum_{u(B)=-1}x_B + \sum_{u(B)=0}|B|x_B \]

increases under the algorithm’s corrective transfers. In the continuum, individual item moves become mass transfers, suggesting a batched potential-improvement or flow/column-generation algorithm. For rational data, clearing denominators gives a finite high-multiplicity interpretation, but the paper’s polynomial algorithm may take time polynomial in the expanded denominator rather than in its bit length. Establishing denominator-independent polynomial time is therefore a genuine continuous computational question.

The paper’s Theorem 3 gives supporting evidence: for identical monotone non-increasing utilities, a \(\gsee\) allocation of chores always exists for any number of agents, with a pseudo-polynomial construction. I would not use it as a second anchor because its displayed theorem is primarily existential rather than a clean polynomial-complexity result. The paper’s non-existence result, Theorem 2, also marks an honest boundary: arbitrary non-monotone, non-additive EFX does not survive even for two finite agents, so this mirror should not be advertised as covering the whole paper.

The weakest point is that the continuum algorithm is not automatic. The paper’s potential argument moves individual items between named bundles; with mass distributions, one must batch transfers and control support size and encoding length. That is a real gap, but it is precisely the sort of high-multiplicity algorithmic question the continuization programme is meant to expose, rather than a reason to reject the mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that its only non-degenerate version changes the item model, not merely the population model.

Theorem 6 is genuinely algorithmic, so I would not claim that the paper lacks a computational anchor. Nor is identical-agent high multiplicity inherently implausible. The problem is that the theorem has a fixed set \(S\) containing one copy of each item. If \(n\) tends to infinity while \(S\) stays fixed, at most \(m\) agents receive nonempty bundles. In the continuum, almost all mass receives \(\emptyset\), and the item allocation disappears into a measure-zero part of society. That is a degenerate population limit.

The proponent avoids this by introducing per-capita supplies \(\rho_s\). But this is not a mechanical high-multiplicity version of the paper. The paper gives an arbitrary set function \(u:2^S\to\{0,-1\}\); it says nothing about copies of an item class, repeated chores, or whether copies should be collapsed, counted separately, or have diminishing marginal effects. With non-additive utilities, that choice is part of the problem.

The instability is concrete. Let \(S=\{a,b\}\) and define

\[ u(\emptyset)=u(\{b\})=u(\{a,b\})=0 \]

would not be the intended example; the relevant negative-Boolean choice is

\[ u(\emptyset)=u(\{b\})=0,\qquad u(\{a\})=u(\{a,b\})=-1. \]

With one copy of each item, allocating \(\{a\}\) to one agent and \(\{b\}\) to another is \(\gsee\): deleting \(a\) removes the envy. But under the proposed continuum supplies \(\rho_a=\tfrac12\) and \(\rho_b=1\), half the population must receive \(\{a,b\}\) and half \(\{b\}\). The former bundle envies the latter, and deleting the zero-marginal item \(b\) leaves \(\{a\}\), still valued at \(-1\), so the \(\gsee\) condition fails. There is no alternative support satisfying those marginals.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer differs from the discrete answer. It shows that the proposed object is not the theorem’s high-multiplicity relaxation: replicating item classes creates a new supply-constrained support problem whose fairness constraints are not inherited from Theorem 6. A multiset-valued utility model would be another possible rescue, but then the utility domain and marginal semantics have been changed. Keeping one finite inventory gives the degenerate limit; scaling the inventory gives a new fair-division model.

Theorem 3 has the same difficulty, with the additional weakness that its algorithm is pseudo-polynomial and depends on the discrete utility gap. Theorem 2 does not repair the case: its two-agent counterexample concerns a partition into two bundles, whereas a continuum distribution may use several bundle types. Preserving that obstruction would require an extra restriction to two positive-mass cohorts.

Thus the paper does not supply a continuous computational result for the proposed mass-allocation problem. The resulting problem may still be worth studying as a new fractional-support or configuration-feasibility problem, but that is precisely the problem: its substance comes from the newly invented item-replication model, while the original paper contributes only a finite-agent existence algorithm. The negative case is therefore serious, though not airtight: a carefully justified repeated-duty interpretation could still make the proponent’s model a legitimate new Class A question.
