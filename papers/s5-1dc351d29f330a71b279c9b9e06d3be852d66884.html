The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result—no theorem about complexity or algorithms for a computational problem.

The tempting anchors are Theorem 1, which proves that under condition (C1) or (C2), every neighborhood of a qualifying alternative contains a continuum of simple solutions; and Theorem 2, which proves that, for sufficiently patient players, those solutions correspond to absorbing sets of no-delay stationary bargaining equilibria. Both are proved in the paper. Corollary 1 says dynamically stable alternatives are dense under its stated density condition. These are equilibrium existence and indeterminacy results, not computational results. And the “continuum” here is a continuum of equilibria and a continuous policy space \(X\); the society remains a finite set of \(n\) named players.

A plausible population extension might model a large legislature through masses of delegates sharing the same preferences, discount factors, recognition probabilities, and coalition roles. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that none of the proponent’s three candidate anchors is a computational result. Theorem 1 establishes a continuum of simple solutions near certain policy alternatives; Theorem 2 turns those into absorbing sets of stationary bargaining equilibria; Corollary 1 makes dynamically stable alternatives dense. In each, the continuum is in the policy space or the set of equilibria. The society remains \(n\) named players. So there is no complexity claim, algorithm, or computational task in these results for a continuous-population mirror to carry forward.

A better proposed mirror would put a large legislature into groups sharing complete types: preferences, discount factors, recognition chances, and coalition roles. But this paper’s strategic structure is built around individual proposers and a decisive-coalition family over named players. A population model would need new rules for how a type’s mass affects proposer selection and acceptance. In a nonatomic version, an individual has zero mass and cannot be pivotal in the usual way. One could design a mass-threshold voting rule and type-level proposal process, but those choices define a different strategic game. The paper’s equilibrium claims do not identify a computational question for that game.

Nor does the paper supply a finite input model for complexity analysis: its feasible set may be any nonempty subset of Euclidean space, and its utilities are general bounded, continuously differentiable functions. Restricting these to finitely encoded classes could make equilibrium computation a well-posed project, but finding such a problem would be new work, not mirroring Theorem 1, Theorem 2, or Corollary 1. Asking whether stable policies can be found near a target likewise shifts the question from the paper’s density result to a new algorithmic task.

A large assembly with interchangeable delegates and explicitly defined type-level recognition and voting rules could be worth studying. That possibility is the weak point in any universal negative: it is hard to rule out every useful extension. But it would be a new bargaining model, and its equilibrium analysis would not make this paper a computational-social-choice mirror. Its policy space is already continuous; its population is not.
