What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation would address the equilibrium and cost-design analysis in Theorems 1–4; it leaves the alternative-game equivalences in Section 5 aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible lead is a mass-turnout cost-design problem. Let the complete voter types be \(P_A,P_B,N_A,N_B\): partisan or nonpartisan, with preference for A or B. A national referendum with many voters in these four groups is a credible regime: the number of voters is large while the type support is fixed at \(\tau=4\). Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population fraction, and let \(q_A,q_B\in[0,1]\) be the turnout rates of nonpartisan A- and B-supporters. Given a population scale \(N\) and voting cost \(c\), ask for all type-symmetric equilibrium pairs \((q_A,q_B)\), and identify the costs for which every equilibrium makes A the expected-ballot winner. In the paper’s criterion, that means \(\mu_{P_A}+q_A\mu_{N_A}>\mu_{P_B}+q_B\mu_{N_B}\).

The strategic detail matters. A nonpartisan voter’s gain from voting is the chance of being pivotal, less \(c\). In the Poisson version, if \(D\) is the difference between other A- and B-votes, the pivotal benefits are \(\tfrac12\Pr(D\in\{0,-1\})\) for an A-supporter and \(\tfrac12\Pr(D\in\{0,1\})\) for a B-supporter. At an interior turnout rate, the relevant benefit equals \(c\); at rate \(0\) it is at most \(c\), and at rate \(1\) it is at least \(c\). Thus the solution is the equilibrium correspondence over turnout rates, followed by the safe-cost set. This preserves the paper’s strategic question rather than turning it into a tallying problem. The expected direction is tractability for this fixed four-type model, with the thresholds and equilibrium regions as the computational output; a broader version would ask how complexity changes as the type support grows.

There is a strong analytical foothold, though it does not meet your required anchor rule. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says there is at most one coin-toss equilibrium and gives its existence condition: with \(x_A=Npp_A\) and \(g(z)=(I_0(z)+I_1(z))e^{-z}\), it exists exactly when \(g(2x_A)>2c>g(2N(1-p_A))\); in that equilibrium the expected vote totals are equal. Theorem 4, also proved here, gives five large-\(N\) cost regions: above the upper \(g\)-threshold, partial absenteeism and no-queue equilibria may occur, all favoring A; between the two \(g\)-thresholds, a coin-toss equilibrium coexists with A-favoring equilibria; in the next region, partial absenteeism, partial saturation, and no-queue equilibria favor A; in the following region a partial-saturation equilibrium exists; below the final \(h\)-threshold, only the all-swipe equilibrium exists. These results directly support the proposed equilibrium-computation question and identify coin-toss equilibria as the design hazard.

The weakness is substantial: neither theorem asserts a computational-complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The paper contains named analytical equilibrium characterizations, but no named computational result of the kind the task requires as an anchor. So this is a credible candidate mirror, not an anchor-compliant case. There is a second risk: the paper already describes the electorate through group proportions and Poisson aggregate counts. A reviewer may see a mass formulation as a reparameterization of its existing model. A strict nonatomic limit also removes the individual pivot effect; keeping it requires a finite voter scale or a carefully derived limiting pivotality correction. The mirror therefore covers the paper’s equilibrium and cost-design analysis, while its strongest claim is plausibility as a high-multiplicity computational problem—not a demonstrated new complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central weakness is decisive under the requested anchor rule: this paper proves no computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 characterizes when a coin-toss equilibrium exists; Theorem 4 classifies equilibrium regions. Both are analytical results, not complexity claims about a computational problem. The proponent acknowledges that gap, so neither theorem supplies a compliant anchor.

The best proposed mirror is still coherent: given \(N\), the four population shares, and voting cost \(c\), compute the equilibrium set and the costs for which every equilibrium favors A. But those four type masses are already encoded by the paper’s parameters \(p\) and \(p_A\), and its turnout rates are already type-level mixed strategies. The paper’s game retains integer-valued Poisson vote counts and individual pivot incentives. Turning that into an algorithmic wrapper does not, by itself, identify a new computational problem of the kind the programme is charting.

A true atomless population creates the more fundamental problem. One voter has zero effect on the outcome, so at any positive cost a nonpartisan voter has no pivotal benefit and abstains; at zero cost, turnout can be indeterminate. That removes the strategic mechanism behind the paper’s results. Keeping pivotality requires keeping a population scale and one-voter granularity, which returns to the Poisson game. Rescaling costs with population size does not open a new direction here: the paper already studies thresholds that shrink like \(N^{-1/2}\) and thresholds that shrink exponentially. Even adding voter types with heterogeneous costs looks limited as a route to computational complexity: within each preference group, voters face the same pivotal benefit, so cost types respond by a cutoff.

The four-type national-election regime is plausible, and high multiplicity supports that regime; it is not a valid objection to the mirror. Nor can I rule out a worthwhile future problem built around equilibrium computation or a richer cost-design model. The negative case is weaker there. Its strongest point is narrower: for this paper, the best mirror either preserves the Poisson model and adds a computational wrapper, or takes a genuinely atomless limit that erases pivotal voting. Neither gives a named computational result to anchor a ChoCo case.
