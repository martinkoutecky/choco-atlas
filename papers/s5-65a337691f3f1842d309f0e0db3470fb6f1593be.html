The continuous mirror question

Given a rational high-multiplicity peer election with voter-type masses \(\mu\), integer candidate-copy multiplicities \(q\), a compact copy-level functional delegation graph with transitive approvals \(A_t\), and committee size \(k\), compute a \(k\)-committee maximizing \(\mathrm{PAV}_\mu(w)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t H_{\sum_{u\in A_t}w_u}\), and determine whether weighted Liquid d’Hondt/sequential PAV always returns that optimum in polynomial time.

The model it lives in

Voter types \(T\) carry masses \(\mu_t\), while candidate copies retain integer multiplicities \(q_t\) and liquid reachability; choose integral \(w\) with \(0\leq w_t\leq q_t\) and \(\sum_t w_t=k\), optimizing mass-weighted PAV.

The objection that survived

The stated \((T,g,\mu,k)\) instance is under-specified: identical voter masses can correspond to different candidate-copy multiplicities, feasible committees, and PAV values.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the PAV–LDH–sequential-PAV equivalence and its computational consequence. It leaves the paper's domain-characterization results, most axiomatic implications and independence results, and the MES, Phragmen, GJCR, PR, and Core analyses largely untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which encodings of candidate-copy multiplicities \(q\) and liquid reachability does weighted LDH/seq-PAV remain PAV-optimal?
  • Does the copy-aware mass model preserve the paper's sEJR guarantee and HJCR characterization?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity, role-based version of the paper’s peer-selection problem. The paper contains no named NP-hardness or W[1]-hardness theorem of its own, so there is no honest hardness anchor to prefer. My lead is therefore the paper’s strongest algorithmic result, Theorem 12.

The regime is a large association, professional community, or online governance platform with \(N\) members but only \(\tau\) recurring delegation roles: for example, \(N\) in the tens or hundreds of thousands, \(\tau\) between \(20\) and \(100\), and committee size \(k\) in the single or low double digits. Members in the same role have the same trust/delegation behaviour and hence the same transitive approval set. A type is therefore a complete role description: its delegation successor, its approval set, and its eligibility as a representative. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\).

Let \(T\) be the finite type set and \(g:T\to T\) the functional delegation graph. Define \(A_t=\{g^j(t):j\geq 1\}\), the candidates approved by type \(t\). Let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution on \(T\), and let \(W\subseteq T\), \(|W|=k\), be the selected representative roles. A type \(t\) receives \(\mathrm{sat}_W(t)=|A_t\cap W|\) representatives. For a candidate type \(c\), let \(D_c=\{t:c\in A_t\}\) and \(s_c=\mu(D_c)\). Thus \(s_c\) is the fraction of society supporting \(c\).

This is a population continuization, not outcome-space continuization: \(W\) is still an integral committee of \(k\) representative roles, and there is no lottery or fractional committee. Only voter counts have become masses. For rational \(\mu\), the usual discrete thresholds translate exactly: an \(\ell\)-cohesive group has mass at least \(\ell/k\).

My lead problem is:

Mass-PAV Liquid Selection. Given \((T,g,\mu,k)\) and a fixed tie-breaking order, output a committee \(W\) maximizing

\[ \mathrm{PAV}_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t H_{\mathrm{sat}_W(t)}, \]

where \(H_r=\sum_{j=1}^{r}1/j\). Equivalently, compute the sequence obtained by repeatedly selecting

\[ c\in\arg\max_{c\notin W} \frac{\mu(D_c)}{1+|A_c\cap W|}. \]

The question is whether this greedy mass version of Liquid d’Hondt computes an optimal mass-PAV committee.

This directly mirrors Theorem 12, proved in this paper: “On liquid profiles, LDH is equivalent to seq-PAV, which is equivalent to PAV.” In other words, on the paper’s liquid domain, the authors prove that Liquid d’Hondt, sequential PAV, and the globally optimizing PAV rule return the same committee, under suitable tie-breaking. The result is proved here, not merely cited. Its computational consequence is that the usual PAV optimization barrier disappears on liquid profiles: the PAV committee can be obtained by a polynomial number of graph-support and ratio computations.

I would expect Mass-PAV Liquid Selection to be in Class A. The proof idea should survive replacing component cardinalities by component masses: every comparison in the paper’s proof is between supports of candidates and their already selected upstream representatives, and those comparisons remain meaningful for rational masses. The input size would be polynomial in \(\tau\), \(k\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\). This is a genuine continuization of their problem because the objective still measures proportional representation under the same transitive approval structure; only the electorate is represented by type frequencies.

The second problem is the fairness question attached to that algorithm:

Mass-Strong-EJR Selection. Given \((T,g,\mu,k)\), compute the committee returned by mass-PAV or by mass-MES, and determine whether it satisfies the following continuous strong-EJR condition: for every \(\ell\leq k\) and every set \(S\subseteq T\) with \(\mu(S)\geq \ell/k\) and \(|\bigcap_{t\in S}A_t|\geq \ell\), every type \(t\in S\) must satisfy \(\mathrm{sat}_W(t)\geq\ell\).

The continuous MES rule has the paper’s same budget interpretation. Each unit of population starts with budget \(k\), so the total budget is \(k\). A candidate costs one unit. Candidate \(c\) is affordable at price \(\rho\) when

\[ \sum_{t:c\in A_t}\mu_t\min\{b_t,\rho\}\geq 1, \]

after which the supporting mass pays according to the same rule as in MES. If MES stops before selecting \(k\) candidates, the result can be completed arbitrarily; adding representatives cannot destroy strong-EJR.

This mirrors Theorem 15, also proved here: “On liquid profiles, PAV and MES satisfy sEJR.” The full content is important: both standard PAV and MES, despite their different behaviour in general approval elections, satisfy the stronger universal version of EJR on liquid profiles. In the mass problem, I would expect the same Class-A outcome. Mass-PAV is supplied by the greedy problem above; mass-MES can be simulated by maintaining type budgets and comparing finitely many rational breakpoints. The substantive question is whether the fairness guarantee survives replacing every population count by its mass. The paper’s proof is based on hierarchy and common upstream paths, not on one-person granularity, so this is a particularly credible continuation.

The third problem asks whether the paper’s canonical minimal solution also survives:

Mass-HJCR Characterization. Given \((T,g,\mu,k)\) and a tie-breaking order, run HJCR from sink-cycle types outward. When processing \(u\), let \(\mathrm{sat}(u)=|A_u\cap W|\). Select \(u\) if there exists \(\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\) such that

\[ \mu(D_u)\geq \ell/k \quad\text{and}\quad \mathrm{sat}(u)<\ell. \]

Otherwise reject \(u\) and the types in \(D_u\). The output is the compulsory representative set \(W_{\mathrm{HJCR}}\), which may then be completed to size \(k\). The continuous question is whether \(W_{\mathrm{HJCR}}\) satisfies mass-sEJR and whether every mass-sEJR committee contains it.

This mirrors Theorem 20, proved in the paper: “On liquid profiles, Hierarchical Justified Cohesive Rule is sEJR-characterizing.” The theorem has two parts. HJCR itself satisfies sEJR, and every rule satisfying sEJR must return a committee containing the HJCR outcome, under a fixed tie-breaking convention. Thus the rule identifies the minimal set of representatives forced by proportionality, while PAV, MES, and sequential Phragmén can be understood as completion procedures.

I would again expect Class A for the mass version. HJCR only compares support masses with thresholds \(\ell/k\), processes the finite graph in a prescribed order, and updates integer satisfaction counts. It should therefore run in polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(k\), and the bit length of \(\mu\). The interesting research question is not whether the algorithm is computationally difficult, but whether the characterization remains valid for arbitrary rational type masses rather than equal-weight individuals.

The authors should recognise these as their problems. The delegation graph, transitive approval, peer-selection interpretation, committee size, representation guarantees, and named rules are all retained. The only modelling change is that a large cohort of interchangeable peers is represented by one type and a mass. This is exactly the high-multiplicity regime in which the programme is interested. It is also not merely a story change: the continuous thresholds \(\ell/k\), support values \(\mu(D_c)\), and PAV objective are the mathematically natural translations of their cardinal definitions.

The weakest point is the identification \(C=V\). In the paper, every individual peer is also an individually selectable candidate. In the mirror above, candidates are representative roles, so selecting two interchangeable members of one role is not a separate option. A strict referee could object that this changes the candidate side of the problem rather than merely continuizing the population. The best defence is that this is the forced role-level quotient in a high-multiplicity peer-selection regime: when all members of a cohort have identical trust relations and all properties used by the rule, the representative role is the relevant candidate object. If the authors insist on preserving multiple same-type candidates, the model can be refined to a multiplicity vector of selected candidates, but the clean LDH/PAV equivalence would then require a new proof rather than following automatically.

That caveat limits the claim; it does not destroy it. The mirror is strongest for organizations in which representation is explicitly by role, cohort, or delegation position. It generates further worthwhile questions: whether the mass versions of PR and Core Stability remain independent of mass-sEJR; whether mass-core-stable committees exist; and what happens when delegation is probabilistic, has out-degree greater than one, or includes distance-sensitive trust. Those are natural continuations of this paper’s programme, not unrelated fractional voting questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious case against a mirror is that all three proposed anchors silently replace peer selection by a different role-selection problem.

In the paper, \(V=C\): every candidate is an individual peer. A high-multiplicity population therefore needs to preserve not only the mass of voter types, but also the multiplicity and identity of candidate copies. The distribution \(\mu\) over voter types does not contain that information.

For example, consider two role types \(S\) and \(L\), with \(\mu_S=\mu_L=\tfrac12\), and the type-level delegation pattern \(S\to S\), \(L\to S\). One realization has one \(S\)-peer and one \(L\)-peer. Another has two \(S\)-peers forming a cycle and two \(L\)-peers pointing to one of the \(S\)-peers. The normalized type data look identical, but with \(k=2\) the second realization can select both \(S\)-peers. Its PAV value is larger than that of selecting one \(S\)-peer and one \(L\)-peer. The first realization cannot make that choice at all.

Thus \((T,g,\mu,k)\) does not determine the paper’s election. The proposed \(W\subseteq T\) model has collapsed several selectable people into one candidate role. That may be a sensible new model, but it is no longer the paper’s peer-selection problem: the elected object is a set of roles, not a committee of peers.

This defeats the proposed reading of Theorem 12. Weighted PAV and Liquid d’Hondt may well remain equivalent on a deliberately defined role graph. But that would be a theorem about the quotient model. In the actual high-multiplicity version of the paper, one must also specify candidate multiplicities and which individual candidates are reached by each delegation type. Once those data are restored, the ratio \(\mu(D_c)/(1+|A_c\cap W|)\) is indexed by candidate copies, not merely by types, and the stated mass problem is no longer the same problem.

The same issue defeats the proposed use of Theorem 15. The mass-sEJR condition over \(S\subseteq T\) is a new axiom for role types. The paper’s sEJR quantifies over groups of individual voters and counts individual representatives. If a type contains several selectable peers in a sink cycle, replacing it by one type erases the possibility of receiving several representatives from that cycle. Restoring those representatives requires candidate capacities or copy multiplicities, together with the individual reachability structure. The claimed extension of the theorem then needs a new proof; it does not follow merely by replacing cardinalities with masses.

Theorem 20 is even more identity-sensitive. HJCR processes actual sink-cycle vertices and vertices at successive distances, and its rejection operation removes particular descendant sets. Collapsing a sink cycle to one type removes the very distinctions that determine how many representatives can be selected and which vertices are compulsory. Retaining the copies restores the original combinatorial problem rather than producing the proposed finite type algorithm.

This is not an objection that the continuous answers might be easy or unchanged. It is an objection that the proposed model is not a continuization of the paper’s model unless the candidate side is changed as well. If candidate-side multiplicities are added, that could become a worthwhile new high-multiplicity problem, but it is no longer a population distribution \(\mu\) alone and none of the three anchors has yet established the required theorem.

The negative case is nevertheless weak in an important respect. If the authors are willing to reinterpret peer selection as selecting representative roles rather than individual peers, then the weighted role model is coherent, natural, and likely supports weighted versions of all three results. Under that permissive interpretation, I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower: the proponent’s three mirrors are not mirrors of the paper as written; they become plausible only after a substantive change from individual peer selection to role-level selection.
