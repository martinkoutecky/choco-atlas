Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4.1 — survived: The block-model degeneration and weak atomless incentives limit the interpretation but do not defeat the well-posed mass version of the mechanism.
  • Theorem 3.1 — fell: It fails bit (a) under the stated rubric, independently of whether its finite impossibility dissolves in the atomless setting.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), a rational block support matrix \(\rho\), quota \(\kappa\in(0,1)\), and integer \(r\), represent each type as atomless mass, randomly partition the population into \(r\) equal-mass cells, select mass \(\kappa/r\) from each cell by cross-cell indegree, and compute the resulting selection probabilities and expected welfare relative to \(\operatorname{OPT}_\kappa\), while proving unilateral strategyproofness and an approximation guarantee.

The model it lives in

Let \(X=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}X_t\) with \(\lambda(X_t)=\mu_t\) and block kernel \(R(x,y)=\rho_{st}\) for \(x\in X_s\) and \(y\in X_t\). The decision is a measurable selector \(q:X\to\{0,1\}\) with \(\int q\,d\lambda=\kappa\); welfare is \(W_R(q)=\int q(x)\int R(y,x)\,d\lambda(y)\,d\lambda(x)\), compared with \(\operatorname{OPT}_\kappa(R)\).

The objection that survived

A finite block kernel suppresses target-specific neighborhoods, while unilateral strategyproofness is effectively vacuous for atomless agents; a non-vacuous type-block deviation notion would be a new mechanism-design problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1's \(m\)-RP strategyproofness and approximation guarantees, including the \(4\)-approximation for \(r=2\) and the asymptotic guarantee in finite approximants. It does not faithfully cover Theorem 3.1 or the individual- and coalition-based lower bounds in Theorems 4.2 and 4.4.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a finite-type \(R\) with \(\rho_{st}\) be defended as a complete high-multiplicity description of target-specific support rather than only an aggregate block model?
  • What positive-mass type-block or coalition strategyproofness notion preserves the paper's incentive question, and does \(m\)-RP satisfy it?
  • Can the \(1+O(k^{-1/3})\) guarantee be formulated directly at fixed \(\kappa\), without importing \(k=\kappa n\) through a finite sequence?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is qualified: this paper has no named NP-hardness, P, FPT, or similar complexity theorem. Its usable anchors are proved mechanism-design bounds, especially Theorem 4.1.

The natural regime is a very large reputation or recommendation network with \(n\) users but only \(\tau\ll n\) recurring user types. A type includes everything relevant to the problem: utility (only whether the user is selected), reporting rights, and its support behaviour. Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of type \(t\), and let \(\rho_{st}\) be the density of links from type \(s\) to type \(t\). The platform selects a fraction \(\kappa\) of the population, so \(k=\kappa n\) in a finite realization. This is the forced population co-modification: a fixed \(k\) would have vanishing mass in the continuum.

Normalize the graph by population size. For a measurable population \(X\) of mass one, with support kernel \(R\), define

\[ d_R(x)=\int_X R(y,x)\,d\lambda(y) \]

and, for a selected set \(A\subseteq X\) with \(\lambda(A)=\kappa\),

\[ W_R(A)=\int_A d_R(x)\,d\lambda(x). \]

This is exactly the finite objective after dividing total indegree by \(n^2\). Individuals are still selected integrally; only the aggregate mass of selected members of a type may be fractional.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It states that, for every \(k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\), the Random \(m\)-Partition mechanism is strategyproof for every \(m\); \(2\)-RP has approximation ratio four; and \(\lceil k^{1/3}\rceil\)-RP has approximation ratio \(1+O(k^{-1/3})\). The paper proves this, with the detailed proof in Appendix A.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Random-Partition Selection, \(\mathrm{CRP}_\infty\):

Given a rational finite-type society \((T,\mu)\), a rational support kernel \(R\), a selected mass \(\kappa\), and an integer \(r\), construct and evaluate the following mechanism. Randomly partition the atomless population into \(r\) equal-mass cells. In each cell \(S_j\), ignore links originating inside \(S_j\), compute each candidate’s cross-cell indegree

\[ d_j(x)=\int_{X\setminus S_j}R(y,x)\,d\lambda(y), \]

and select mass \(\kappa/r\) with the largest \(d_j(x)\). The solution is the resulting selection-probability function \(q(x)\), together with its expected welfare \( \mathbb E[W_R(A)]\) and an approximation certificate relative to

\[ \operatorname{OPT}_\kappa(R) =\max_{\lambda(B)=\kappa} W_R(B). \]

For \(r=2\), the paper’s proof carries over with sums replaced by integrals: every ordered pair of agents is separated with probability \(1/2\), and the same averaging argument gives

\[ \mathbb E[W_R(A)]\ge \frac{1}{4}\operatorname{OPT}_\kappa(R). \]

The strategyproofness argument also survives exactly. An agent’s outgoing links affect the scores of candidates in other cells, but not the scores used to select agents from its own cell. Thus the mechanism is universally strategyproof. On rational \(\tau\)-type inputs, the scores and selected masses are computable by sorting or a small LP in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the encoding length. In finite realizations with \(k=\kappa n\), the paper’s choice \(r=\lceil k^{1/3}\rceil\) suggests an approximation tending to one as \(n\) grows.

This is plausibly the authors’ problem rather than a softened substitute: the report is still an outgoing-edge report, utility is still own selection, and welfare is still total incoming support. Only the high-multiplicity regime and population-proportional selection quota have changed. The expected classification is Class A: the partition mechanism is explicit, and the proof is an integral version of the paper’s proof.

A second, more revealing anchor is Theorem 3.1, also proved here. It states that for every \(n\ge2\) and every \(k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\), no deterministic strategyproof \(k\)-selection mechanism has a finite approximation ratio. The proof uses stars, represents them by the Boolean cube \(\{0,1\}^{n-1}\), and derives a parity contradiction.

Its continuous mirror is Deterministic Atomless \(\kappa\)-Selection, \(\mathrm{DAS}_\infty\):

Given a type distribution \(\mu\), a support kernel \(R\), and \(\kappa\in(0,1)\), construct a deterministic mechanism \(F\) that returns a measurable set \(A=F(R)\) with \(\lambda(A)=\kappa\). It must satisfy individual strategyproofness: changing one agent’s outgoing report cannot change whether that agent belongs to \(A\). The mechanism should minimize the worst-case ratio

\[ \frac{\operatorname{OPT}_\kappa(R)}{W_R(F(R))}. \]

For a finite-type kernel, write

\[ d_t=\sum_s\mu_s\rho_{st}. \]

The optimal continuous selector solves

\[ \max_{0\le q_t\le1} \left\{ \sum_t\mu_tq_td_t: \sum_t\mu_tq_t=\kappa \right\}. \]

It selects all mass of the highest-indegree types, and a suitable fraction of the threshold type. A fixed auxiliary coordinate inside each type turns that fractional type mass into an actual deterministic subset of agents. The resulting selector achieves ratio one. Since a single atomless agent has zero population mass, changing that agent’s outgoing row does not change any aggregate indegree, so the selector is strategyproof.

Thus the finite impossibility is not expected to transfer: the parity obstruction lives in the finite Boolean cube and disappears after replacing individual counts by mass. This is a Class A, continuum-specific dissolution of the paper’s impossibility result.

The weakest point is clear. Individual strategyproofness becomes substantially weaker in an atomless population: one agent cannot move an aggregate statistic. A referee may reasonably ask for a stronger variant in which an entire positive-mass type may jointly change its outgoing profile. That “type-block strategyproof” problem is the serious follow-up, and I would not claim that the ratio-one result survives it. Likewise, if \(k\) is fixed rather than proportional to \(n\), the selected mass vanishes and the mirror becomes degenerate. Subject to those caveats, however, the paper supports a credible continuous mirror: its random-partition guarantee extends naturally to population mass, while its finite deterministic impossibility is precisely the sort of population-multiplicity obstruction that continuization can remove.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper does not supply the kind of computational result ChoCo is designed to continuize. Its optimization problem is already just sorting agents by indegree. The numbered results concern incentive compatibility and approximation guarantees of mechanisms, not the complexity of computing a solution, an exponential type-space LP, or a high-multiplicity optimization problem. A continuous version may be legitimate mechanism design, but it is not yet a continuous computational-social-choice mirror.

There is also a structural problem specific to this paper. Agents and alternatives are the same named objects. An agent’s type is therefore not merely “supports candidate class \(t\)”: it includes its entire outgoing row, namely which particular agents it supports. For general graphs, two agents with the same aggregate support density can have completely different target-specific neighborhoods, and those neighborhoods determine the indegrees being optimized.

The proposed model has only two possible resolutions. If \(R(x,y)\) is an arbitrary individual-level kernel, then the input is a graphon or other infinite-dimensional object, not a finite-type society \((T,\mu)\). “Rational kernel” is not a finite encoding unless a separate representation model is supplied, and the resulting complexity concerns the representation of \(R\), not population continuization. If \(R\) is block-constant by finitely many types, then the target-specific graph has been quotiented away. The model is no longer the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s graph problem.

This is fatal to the proposed reading of Theorem 4.1. In a block-constant society, if every type is split proportionally among the \(r\) cells, then for a type \(t\)

\[ d_{j,t} = \sum_s \left(\mu_s-\frac{\mu_s}{r}\right)\rho_{st} = \left(1-\frac1r\right)d_t. \]

Thus every cell has the same type ranking. The random partition does not reproduce the finite mechanism’s interaction with a random graph; it merely repeats the same type-level selection problem in every cell. The finite proof’s concentration and partition effects have disappeared because an atomless, finite-type population has no composition fluctuations at the individual scale. Choosing partitions that do not split types proportionally adds microscopic structure absent from \((T,\mu)\).

The suggested \(1+O(k^{-1/3})\) limit has the same problem. In the continuum there is no integer \(k\); setting \(k=\kappa n\) produces a sequence of finite approximations with \(r\) changing with \(n\), not a single continuous problem. That sequence could be studied, but it is an asymptotic analysis of finite random partitions, not a finite-type continuous computational model.

Theorem 3.1 is even less suitable. The proposed ratio-one selector is strategyproof only because an individual has measure zero. Changing one point’s outgoing row does not change any aggregate indegree, so essentially every almost-everywhere-defined aggregate selector becomes strategyproof. The Boolean-cube parity argument has not been meaningfully resolved; its strategic constraint has vanished. The auxiliary coordinate used to select a fractional amount of a threshold type simply hides this loss by choosing an arbitrary subset of indistinguishable clones.

A better formulation would require a positive-mass type, or a coalition of clones, to be able to change its aggregate outgoing report. But that is no longer the individual strategyproofness problem of Theorem 3.1. Under such deviations, the selector based on \(d_t\) is generally manipulable, and the random-partition argument is no longer strategyproof either: a type’s aggregate report can affect the cross-cell score of its own type. One has defined a new type-block or coalition-strategyproofness problem, not continuized the theorem in the paper.

The same objection applies to trying to rescue the paper through Theorem 4.4. Its lower bound is driven by a coalition of two named agents. In an atomless population that coalition has zero mass. Replacing it by two positive-mass blocks may well produce a meaningful new model, perhaps with a bound related to \(1/\kappa\), but it requires a new coalition notion and new assumptions about homogeneous candidate blocks. That is the only plausible salvage, and it is not an anchor from the paper’s individual-agent results.

So the proposed \(\mathrm{CRP}_\infty\) either becomes a graphon problem outside the finite-type programme or collapses to a block-type sorting problem; \(\mathrm{DAS}_\infty\) makes strategyproofness vacuous; and the non-vacuous repair changes the game. The negative case is therefore strong for ChoCo’s present computational remit, though not mathematically absolute: a deliberately constructed type-block coalition model could be worth studying as new mechanism design. It would not, however, be a faithful continuous mirror of this paper’s named results.
