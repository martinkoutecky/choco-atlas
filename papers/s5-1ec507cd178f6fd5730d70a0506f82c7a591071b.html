The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper gives ChoCo a plausible computational follow-up, but it has no qualifying computational anchor. It states no result classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, W[1]-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results concern equilibrium and information-policy properties. So I cannot give the required anchored continuous problem without presenting a new research question as though it were a result of the paper.

The closest hook is Theorem 2, proved here. In the linear-payoff environment \(X=[0,\bar x]\), \(Y=[0,\bar y]\), with \(\pi(x,y)=y-x\), it says: (1) if the seller-type density \(g\) is log-concave, a lower-censorship policy is optimal; (2) if \(g\) is weakly decreasing, full disclosure is optimal; and (3) if the platform maximizes only the matching rate, \(\gamma=1\), and \(g\) is unimodal, a lower-censorship policy is optimal. This is an analytic characterization, not a complexity or algorithmic result.

A natural *new* computational question would use seller types as complete categories of providers with the same relevant skills and preferences, and buyer types as task categories. Let \(q_y\) and \(p_x\) be rational masses over finite type sets \(Y\) and \(X\); let \(\pi(x,y)\), \(\beta\), \(\tau\), \(u\), and \(\gamma\) be rational inputs with \(\beta\tau<1\). The platform chooses a stationary Bayes-plausible signaling policy over buyer types, and sellers respond in the steady-state equilibrium defined by the paper. Given a signal limit \(K\) and target \(R\), ask whether there is a policy using at most \(K\) posterior signals whose induced equilibrium achieves \(J=\gamma U+(1-\gamma)V\ge R\). A solution would specify the posterior vectors and their weights, the seller responses, and the resulting equilibrium. This is a concrete problem to classify, but it is a proposed extension, not something anchored by a computational result in the paper.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large ride-hailing market in which many drivers share each pay-off-relevant combination of service area, route preferences, and congestion tolerance. Then \(q_y\) is the fraction of drivers in each type, and the buyer flow is distributed over destination or trip types. The paper’s own model already treats the sellers as a unit mass with distribution \(G\), and buyer arrivals as a deterministic mass flow. That makes the computational question recognizable to the authors, but it also exposes the main weakness of the positive case: the paper already makes the population continuous. The proposed question adds a computational lens; it does not supply a discrete result whose population continuization this mirror can study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 establishes equilibrium existence and uniqueness; Proposition 2 establishes market failure under full disclosure; Theorems 1 and 2 characterize implementable outcomes and optimal information policies. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm whose continuous-population version could be studied. Theorem 2 is the best anchor, but it is an analytic characterization proved with variational methods, not a computational result.

There is a second, independent obstacle: the paper has already made the population continuous. Sellers have unit mass and distribution \(G\); buyers arrive as a deterministic mass flow; and the platform chooses among distributions over posterior beliefs. Theorem 2 optimizes over a continuum of information policies. This is not a case where a discrete population remains to be continuized.

The proposed \(K\)-signal decision problem could be a sensible new project. One could give a finite type model rational masses, bound the number of signals, and ask whether an equilibrium policy reaches a target surplus. A ride-hailing interpretation could make the high-multiplicity regime plausible; grouping drivers with identical relevant characteristics is not itself a distortion. But the signal bound and the computational decision problem are additions to the paper. The question becomes how to compute information design in a dynamic matching equilibrium, not how a discrete computational result changes when its population is continuized. Without a signal bound or another finite policy representation, specifying the input and certificate would itself require further modeling.

The strongest alternative is to ask for the optimal policy in a finite-type version without fixing \(K\). That remains a plausible computational economics question, and the authors might recognize it as their problem. But the paper’s current model already has a continuous population, and its results do not identify a discrete computational problem for this variant to mirror. The same applies if one starts from Theorem 1 or Proposition 2 instead: their analytic claims can motivate computational follow-ups, but do not supply computational anchors.

So the negative case is strong about *this paper as a ChoCo mirror*: it already continuizes the population and contributes no named computational result. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile computational follow-up exists. Computing information design in these markets may be valuable; the case against is that this would be a new computational research direction, not a continuous mirror of a result in the paper.
