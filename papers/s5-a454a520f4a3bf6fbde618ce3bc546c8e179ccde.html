The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many agent types \(\mathcal T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), finitely many good kinds \(\mathcal G\) with per-capita supplies \(0\le\rho_g\le1\), and monotone submodular valuations \(v_t:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\), choose masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) of type \(t\) assigned the integral bundle \(S\subseteq\mathcal G\), subject to \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\le\rho_g\), to maximize \(\operatorname{NSW}_\infty(x)=\exp(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S))\), with value zero when positive mass receives zero value; determine whether a \(1/5\)-approximation can be computed and characterize its pricing complexity.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity fair-allocation market with finitely many agent types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), repeated standardized good kinds \(g\), per-capita supplies \(0\le\rho_g\le1\), and common monotone submodular type valuations \(v_t:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\). The variables \(x_{t,S}\) assign continuous population mass to integral bundles, while \(\operatorname{NSW}_\infty\) is the exponential of average log utility; finite instances with \(K\mu_t\) agents and \(K\rho_g\) copies converge exactly to this objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity allocation market, not a claim that the paper’s fractional variables are already a continuous population.

Take \(K\) agents, drawn from a fixed set of valuation types \(\mathcal T\), with \(\tau=|\mathcal T|\ll K\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and all agents of that type share the same monotone submodular valuation \(v_t:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\). Goods also come in finitely many kinds \(g\in\mathcal G\), with per-capita supply \(\rho_g\). Thus a finite realization has approximately \(K\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(K\rho_g\) copies of good kind \(g\).

A solution is a mass allocation \(x_{t,S}\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq\mathcal G\). It must satisfy \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\le\rho_g\). The individual bundles remain integral; only the population is continuous. This is plausible for large markets of households choosing from standardized service packages, housing slots, employee benefits, or course bundles. The high-multiplicity regime is precisely \(K\gg\tau\), with repeated valuation types and repeated goods.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It states that for monotone submodular valuations, if \(\OPT\) is the optimal Nash social welfare, the paper’s algorithm—initial matching, continuous local search, non-uniform pipage rounding, and rematching—returns an allocation whose Nash social welfare is at least \(\frac15\OPT\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathsf{NSW}_\infty\): Given \((\mathcal T,\mu,\mathcal G,\rho,(v_t)_{t\in\mathcal T})\), find a feasible mass allocation \(x\) maximizing

\(\operatorname{NSW}_\infty(x)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal G}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\right)\),

with the convention that a positive mass receiving value zero makes the objective zero. The approximation version asks for \(x\) with \(\operatorname{NSW}_\infty(x)\ge\frac15\OPT_\infty\).

This is genuinely the continuous-population version of their objective. If \(x_{t,S}\) is restricted to multiples of \(1/K\), then the exponent is exactly the average logarithmic utility of the \(K\) individual agents, so the finite geometric mean converges to this expression. It is not an outcome-space relaxation: each unit of population receives an indivisible bundle.

I expect the stated constant-factor problem to be Class A. Its natural formulation is a configuration LP: the variables are masses \(x_{t,S}\), the feasibility constraints are linear, and the Nash objective becomes linear after taking logarithms. The paper’s matching, local-search, and cycle-cancellation ideas are directly relevant to compressing this formulation from named agents to types. Exact optimization for unrestricted submodular value oracles may instead be continuum-specifically hard, because the pricing problem becomes something like maximizing \(\log v_t(S)\) minus item prices. That is a useful boundary question rather than a defect in the mirror.

The second anchor is Theorem 3, also proved in this paper, although its fractional subroutine is Theorem 2, cited from earlier work. Theorem 3 states that for a submodular Santa Claus instance with optimal max-min value \(\OPT\), and with \(Max=\max_{i,j}v_i(\{j\})\), for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\) there is a randomized polynomial-time algorithm producing an allocation in which every agent receives value at least \((1-\frac1e-\epsilon)\OPT-Max\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathsf{MaxMin}_\infty\): Given the same type distribution and per-capita supplies, find a feasible mass allocation \(x\) maximizing

\(\operatorname{MM}_\infty(x)=\sup\{z:x_{t,S}>0\Rightarrow v_t(S)\ge z\text{ for every }t,S\}\).

Thus every individual of positive mass must receive a bundle worth at least \(z\); this is not merely a type-average guarantee. Let \(\OPT_\infty\) be the optimum and let \(\Delta=\max_{t,g}v_t(\{g\})\). The theorem-shaped approximation question is to compute \(x\) satisfying

\(\operatorname{MM}_\infty(x)\ge(1-\frac1e-\epsilon)\OPT_\infty-\Delta\).

In the small-good regime \(\Delta\le\eta\OPT_\infty\), this becomes a multiplicative guarantee of \((1-\frac1e-\epsilon-\eta)\OPT_\infty\), mirroring the paper’s Corollary 2.

I again expect the approximation version to be Class A in a meaningful high-multiplicity regime. For a fixed target \(z\), feasibility is a configuration problem using only bundles \(S\) with \(v_t(S)\ge z\). The paper’s continuous-greedy relaxation and cycle-cancellation procedure provide a natural route to a type-compressed algorithm. Exact feasibility with arbitrary submodular value oracles may be Class C because its pricing problem resembles submodular cover. Hardness driven by the number of good kinds could transfer, while hardness driven only by the number of named agents is precisely what the continuum may remove.

These two mirrors cover the paper’s general max-min and general Nash-social-welfare results. I would not stretch the case to Theorems 4 and 6, whose premise is a constant number of agents, or to the MMS results, where the definition itself depends awkwardly on the finite number of agents. Theorem 8 might admit a separate per-capita-share formulation, but it is not needed for a strong case.

The main weakness is that the paper’s goods are individually given, whereas my scenario replaces them by repeated good kinds with scaled supply. That would be illegitimate if the application depended on every good having a unique identity. It is legitimate, however, for standardized slots or services, and the programme only requires one convincing high-multiplicity scenario. A second weakness is that the paper does not contain a numbered hardness theorem for these objectives; its hardness remarks are not suitable anchors under the requested standard. The positive case therefore rests on Theorems 3 and 5, both proved here, and on the claim that their allocation problems have a natural and nontrivial high-multiplicity form.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirrors are not canonical continuizations of the paper’s actual input model.

The paper has \(n\) named agents, \(m\) individually indexed one-copy goods, and arbitrary oracle valuations \(v_i:2^{\mathcal M}\to\mathbb R_+\). If \(m\) is fixed while \(n\) grows, the limit degenerates: only \(m\) agents can receive nonempty bundles, so Nash social welfare and max-min welfare become zero. Thus a meaningful limit must also replace the goods by \(\Theta(n)\) repeated copies.

That creates a more serious problem. A fixed valuation type must now specify a valuation over every bundle of an expanding set of copies. If the copies retain individual identities and arbitrary submodular interactions, the type description grows with \(n\); there is no fixed finite type space. If copies are treated as identical kinds, then one has imposed a new valuation model—typically a valuation over quantities or over the set of kinds present. That is a legitimate allocation problem, but it is not the paper’s arbitrary submodular-allocation problem. The choice between perfect substitutes, quantity-sensitive values, and copy-specific interactions is mathematically substantive, not a harmless limiting convention.

This objection does expose a weakness in the proposed \(\mathsf{NSW}_\infty\) anchor. Its formula is correct only after specifying how repeated goods are valued. The displayed bundles \(S\subseteq\mathcal G\) cannot represent multiple copies of a kind when \(\rho_g>1\), and replacing the paper’s goods by standardized kinds changes the valuation domain. The proponent has therefore described a promising new market model, but not yet shown that it is the high-multiplicity version of Theorem 5 rather than a related problem.

The same issue applies to Theorem 3. With fixed goods, max-min welfare collapses in the large-population limit. With replicated goods, the valuation type again requires a specified lift to multisets or quantities. Different lifts produce different max-min problems. The paper gives no reason to privilege the proposed one.

That is the full negative case. It does not defeat the best repaired version, however. If the authors accept a co-modified model with finitely many standardized good kinds, per-capita supplies, and fixed submodular valuations over integral bundles or quantities, then both proposed questions are genuine continuous-population problems. For Nash social welfare,

\[ \exp\!\left(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\right) \]

is exactly the high-multiplicity limit of the finite geometric mean, and the bundles remain integral. Likewise, the max-min threshold genuinely requires every positive-mass agent to receive a sufficiently valuable bundle. Neither objective depends on agent identity, and neither degenerates after the goods are scaled appropriately.

Consequently, I cannot honestly sustain the universal negative claim. The proposed anchors survive once the repeated-good model is made explicit. The negative case can reject the proponent’s under-specified formulation as not yet a faithful mirror of the paper, but it cannot show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
