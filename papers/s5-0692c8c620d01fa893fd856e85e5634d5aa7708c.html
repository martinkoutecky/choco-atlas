What the mirror covers

Theorems 5.1, 5.2, 6.1, and 6.2 establish mechanism and schedule properties; none supplies a computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population setting is a large pool of small firms jointly bidding for a spectrum license, as the paper itself describes. Let a type be a firm’s complete utility function \(U\), and let \(\mu_U\) be its fraction of the group. With \(N\) firms and relatively few utility types, the decision remains the paper’s: submit one joint bid, then divide the resource and payment among the winning firms. Since the resource is a single unit, per-firm shares scale with \(1/N\). This continuizes the buyers, not the already-shareable resource, and it seems like a scenario the authors could recognize.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor that mirror. The closest results are Theorem 5.1, proved here, which says that under its stated auction and preference assumptions every monotonic aggregation mechanism is coalition-strategyproof; and Theorem 5.2, proved here, which says such a mechanism with fixed shares is individually consistent and includes buyers who value the whole resource above the auction price in the winning set. Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 establish that particular sharing schedules satisfy the mechanism’s monotonicity condition. These are incentive and structural results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or fixed-parameter tractable. The conclusion’s statement that the mechanism is “not efficient” concerns welfare, not running time.

So, under the requested anchor rule, there are zero qualifying anchors: no lead result and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover. The positive intuition is that a high-multiplicity spectrum-buyer regime is plausible. The weak point is that the paper’s mechanism and its strategyproofness theorem are built around subsets of named, finite buyers. Extending them to type masses and coalitions that can deviate over a continuum would require new definitions and a new result; it would not be a mirror of a computational result proved in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that this paper proves no computational result to mirror. Theorem 5.1 is a coalition-strategyproofness guarantee; Theorem 5.2 is an individual-consistency guarantee; Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 show that particular sharing schedules satisfy a structural condition. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm with a running-time bound. The paper’s remark that its mechanism is “not efficient” concerns welfare, not computation. On the programme’s stated terms, there are zero qualifying anchors.

The spectrum-buyer pool is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but it does not supply the missing anchor. A continuous version of Theorem 5.1 would need to define deviations by masses of buyers: in an atomless population, an individual has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate outcome, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Requiring positive-mass coalitions gives a meaningful alternative, but it changes the incentive question rather than mirroring a computational result. Theorem 5.2 has the same issue: its claim about a buyer who could afford the whole resource has no direct individual counterpart when each buyer has zero mass.

The best attempted rescue is to ask for an algorithm that designs or optimizes monotonic sharing schedules over a finite set of utility types and their masses. That could be a worthwhile new mechanism-design project. But it adds a computational objective and input representation the paper does not provide; it is not a continuous mirror of one of its proved results. The negative case is strong against admitting this paper as a ChoCo mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim: the paper itself points to further work on schedule design and welfare, and a well-specified continuous optimization problem there might be valuable on its own.
