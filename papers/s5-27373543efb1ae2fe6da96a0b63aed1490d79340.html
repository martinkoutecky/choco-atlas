The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of recipient–donor pair types and nondirected-donor types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), type-level compatibility relations, and rational arc scores, determine the lexicographically optimal mass assignment to feasible cycles of size at most \(3\) and chains of length at most \(4\). For each type \(t\), require \(\sum_P a_{tP}x_P\leq\mu_t\), where \(x_P\geq0\) is the mass assigned to pattern \(P\) and \(a_{tP}\) counts its uses of type \(t\); optimize the paper’s five objectives over these pattern masses, and classify the complexity in \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\) for fixed exchange-length bounds.

The model it lives in

A fractional pattern-packing model over a type-level compatibility graph: rational mass \(\mu_t\) is available for each complete agent type, and a variable \(x_P\) assigns mass to each feasible whole exchange pattern. Type-capacity constraints account for repeated uses of a type; the original five objectives apply to the resulting mass assignment.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the five hierarchical objectives over cycles and chains; it does not transfer the integer diving and deactivation techniques or the paper’s discrete ILP experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Formalize the correspondence between finite high-multiplicity KEP instances and the type-level mass model, including repeated type labels within an exchange.
  • Determine whether the LP optimum admits an additive rounding guarantee for finite pools as a function of \(\tau\) and the exchange-length bounds.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s hierarchical kidney exchange problem, with exchangeable cohorts replacing named participants. It keeps the paper’s central decision—choosing cycles and chains under a lexicographic hierarchy—and makes the population itself continuous.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that when Algorithm 2 terminates, each returned value \(\ddot z_k\), for \(k=1,\ldots,5\), equals \(\bar z_k\), the optimal value for objective \(f_k\) in the paper’s hierarchical kidney exchange problem. In other words, the diving algorithm returns the exact objective values for the full sequence: maximize transplants; minimize length-4 chains; minimize size-3 cycles and chains; maximize cross arcs; then maximize arc scores. This is an exactness theorem, not a polynomial-time result: it gives no worst-case running-time bound. The article also cites Abraham et al. (2007) for NP-hardness of maximizing transplants when 3-cycles are allowed, but does not give that result a numbered theorem here, so I use it as context rather than as an anchor.

I would call the mirror High-Multiplicity Hierarchical Kidney Exchange. An instance gives rational masses over finitely many recipient–donor pair types and nondirected-donor types, plus the compatibility arcs and arc scores between them. A type must include everything the model uses: its compatibility behavior, available donors, scores, and relevant clinical or operational attributes. Let \(\tau\) be the number of types with positive mass, and \(L\) the input bit length. The intended regime is a growing national or multinational pool with \(N\) participants and \(N\gg\tau\): for example, many participants sharing the same complete compatibility and scoring profile. The paper itself points to future merged pools exceeding 1,000 recipients; repeated complete profiles in such pools are a further assumption, not something its experiments establish.

The decision variables are masses \(x_P\geq 0\), one for each feasible exchange pattern \(P\): cycles of size at most 3 and chains of length at most 4, with the paper’s cross arcs and arc scores retained. For each type \(t\), the total mass of pattern occurrences using \(t\), counted with multiplicity, must be at most its available mass \(\mu_t\). A solution is a rational vector \(x\) satisfying those capacity constraints and lexicographically optimizing the same five objectives, interpreted per unit mass: maximize transplant count; minimize mass assigned to length-4 chains; minimize mass assigned to size-3 exchanges; maximize cross arcs; maximize arc scores. Each infinitesimal unit still participates in a whole exchange pattern; only the amount of each pattern is divisible.

The question to hand a prover is: given this finite rational type system, compute a feasible mass assignment \(x\) that is lexicographically optimal for those five objectives, and classify the problem’s worst-case complexity in \(\tau\), \(L\), and the fixed exchange-length bounds. For the bounds used in the paper, I expect this to be tractable: there are polynomially many type-level patterns—at most on the order of \(\tau^4\) for length-4 chains—and the five stages are linear programs with rational data. That is a prediction about the mirror, not a claim proved by Theorem 2.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem: it preserves their exchange structures, compatibility constraints, and objective hierarchy, while asking what those same choices mean for a society with repeated participant types. Its weakest point is the type regime. Exact compatibility and score profiles can be highly individual, and including all of them may make \(\tau\) nearly \(N\). The mirror is most convincing where compatibility really is determined by recurring clinical and operational profiles; it is much less useful if each participant’s HLA-based compatibility signature is unique. Further questions are whether the continuous optimum admits a useful additive rounding guarantee for finite pools, whether the hierarchy changes that guarantee, and what happens when chain length is part of the input rather than fixed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s best anchor, but it establishes less than their continuous question asks. It proves that Algorithm 2 returns the exact objective values for the finite, binary kidney-exchange model. It gives no running-time or complexity bound. The proof depends on integer objective thresholds, binary exchange choices, and backtracking when an assumed integer value is infeasible. In a mass model, those thresholds need not be integral; the five stages are simply successive LPs. Asking for the mass problem’s complexity may be worthwhile, but it is a new question about a type-level packing model, not a continuous counterpart of Theorem 2’s result. The paper’s cited NP-hardness result does not repair the anchor: it is attributed to earlier work, not proved here.

The proposed mass model is also most convincing when compatibility and scores are constant between type classes. That requires more than recurring blood groups or similar clinical attributes: every member of a class must have the same relevant compatibility and score relationships to every other class. In a real KEP, HLA-based compatibility is pair-specific, and the hierarchy uses arc scores and cross arcs. Including those details in the type, as the programme requires, may leave almost every recipient–donor pair with a distinct type. Coarsening classes to keep \(\tau\) small can create type-level cycles and robustness arcs that no corresponding set of actual exchanges supports. That changes the central compatibility graph rather than merely smoothing its population.

There is a legitimate exception: a large pool whose compatibility and scores really do fall into a small number of exact, recurring blocks. The authors could recognize the resulting type-level exchange problem, and its mass assignment would preserve whole exchanges while dividing only how much population takes each pattern. I cannot rule out such a regime. But the paper provides no evidence that its UK, Spanish, or Dutch instances have that structure; its scale argument is about larger pools, not repeated complete compatibility profiles. So the negative case is strongest against the practical relevance of this mirror, and against using Theorem 2 as its anchor. It is weak as a universal claim: if exact block structure is plausible for a KEP setting, the mirror survives.
