The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), hospitals \(H\), acceptable type sets \(A(h)\), strict doctor-type and hospital priorities, and rational quota functions \(p_h,q_h\) with \(\lvert C(p_h,q_h)\rvert\le4\) for each hospital, decide whether there is a mass assignment \(x_{t,h}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_h x_{t,h}\le\mu_t\) and \(p_h(B)\le\sum_{t\in B}x_{t,h}\le q_h(B)\) for every \(B\subseteq A(h)\), with no justified infinitesimal envy: no \(t,t',h\) has \(x_{t',h}>0\), \(t\succ_h t'\), and either unassigned mass \(\mu_t-\sum_g x_{t,g}>0\) or mass \(x_{t,g}>0\) at a hospital \(g\) with \(h\succ_t g\), while \(x_h+\varepsilon(e_t-e_{t'})\) remains quota-feasible for some sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\); if so, compute \(x\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity classified stable matching market: \(T\) is the finite set of complete doctor types, \(\mu\) is their mass distribution, \(x_{t,h}\) are assignment masses, and each hospital has rational quota regions with at most four non-trivial constraints; the task is exact envy-free existence and construction.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the bounded-constraint existence problem of Theorem 3.4 and the paramodular algorithm of Theorem 4.4; it leaves the HR-LQ characterization and algorithm in Theorems 2.6–2.7 and the structural propositions unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the bounded-constraint mass CSM problem solvable in polynomial time in \(\lvert T\rvert\), the quota description, and the rational bit-length of \(\mu\)?
  • Does paramodularity yield an exact mass analogue of Theorem 4.4 through generalized-polymatroid choice or separation oracles?
  • Can mass solutions be rounded to finite markets with controlled quota violations or envy?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Classiﬁed Stable Matching model (CSM), with Theorem 3.4 as the lead anchor and Theorem 4.4 as a structured companion. This is a population mirror, not merely a fractional-outcome reformulation: the continuum is in the doctors, while hospitals, their priorities, quota classes, and envy relation remain the paper’s objects.

A type \(t\) is a complete doctor profile: the doctor’s acceptable hospitals and strict preference order, every quota-relevant attribute or class membership, and the hospital-side priority information relevant to that doctor. Two doctors have the same type exactly when the paper’s instance cannot distinguish them. Let \(T\) be the finite type set and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of doctors of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A natural regime is a national residency, teacher-placement, or graduate-admission market with \(N\) applicants but only \(\tau\ll N\) recurring combinations of preference template, qualification profile, specialty, and quota-class memberships.

Hospitals’ lower and upper quota functions are normalized per capita. For each hospital \(h\), let \(p_h,q_h:2^{A(h)}\to\mathbb Q\), where \(A(h)\subseteq T\) is the set of acceptable types. A mass matching is a vector \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_hx_{t,h}\le\mu_t\) and, for every \(B\subseteq A(h)\),

\[ p_h(B)\le \sum_{t\in B}x_{t,h}\le q_h(B). \]

This is the direct high-multiplicity normalization: with \(N\) doctors, type \(t\) represents \(N\mu_t\) doctors and the corresponding quota is \(Np_h(B)\) or \(Nq_h(B)\). The decision problem has no additional objective; it asks whether there exists an envy-free mass matching and, if so, returns one.

The continuous envy condition is the infinitesimal version of the paper’s definition. A positive mass of type \(t\) has a source either because it is unassigned or because some mass of \(t\) is assigned to a less-preferred hospital \(g\). It has justified envy toward hospital \(h\) if \(h\succ_t g\) (or \(t\) is unassigned), there is a type \(t'\) with \(x_{t',h}>0\) and \(t\succ_h t'\), and for some sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ x_h+\varepsilon e_t-\varepsilon e_{t'} \]

still satisfies all quota constraints at \(h\). As in the paper, the test concerns whether \(h\) could replace \(t'\) by \(t\); it does not require that the doctor’s present hospital remain above its lower quota after such a claim. A solution is envy-free when no such type-level claim exists.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper by a reduction from the NP-complete problem \((3,B2)\)-SAT. It states that deciding whether a CSM instance has an envy-free matching is NP-hard, and in fact NP-complete even when each hospital has at most four non-trivial quota constraints, that is, \(\lvert C(p_h,q_h)\rvert\le 4\). The theorem applies with incomplete preference lists and quota functions supplied through evaluation oracles. Its reduction uses variable hospitals whose feasible assignments encode one of two literal sets, and clause hospitals whose lower quota forces at least one satisfied literal.

The corresponding problem is:

\[ \textsc{Continuous-CSM-EF}_{4}. \]

An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu\), hospitals \(H\), type and hospital preferences, acceptable type sets, and rational lower and upper quota descriptions with at most four non-trivial constrained subsets per hospital. The question is whether there exists an envy-free mass matching \(x\) under the definition above.

This is a faithful mirror rather than a softened matching problem. It retains incomplete acceptability, overlapping lower and upper quota constraints, hospital priorities, and the paper’s precise replacement-based notion of envy. Only the population representation changes: repeated doctors with identical data become mass. The original authors should recognize it as their CSM existence problem in a high-multiplicity market.

My forecast is that the particular SAT hardness in Theorem 3.4 will not automatically transfer. In its reduction, a variable hospital’s feasible family is exactly \(\{D_i^+,D_i^-\}\). With many indistinguishable copies, the normalized quota inequalities admit mixtures between those two assignments. The Boolean “true or false” choice can therefore dissolve into a fractional population split, and the direct reduction no longer forces a satisfying assignment. That makes this a serious continuization question, not a restatement of the theorem. I would tentatively expect the bounded, explicitly represented version to be tractable through continuous feasibility and exchange methods, while regarding a Class C result for general non-paramodular quota systems as a genuine possibility. The important point is that either answer would be informative: it would identify whether the paper’s hardness is caused by indivisibility or by the quota structure itself.

The companion anchor is Theorem 4.4, also proved in this paper, using the generalized Gale–Shapley and generalized-matroid machinery developed by Fleiner. It states that, when every pair \((p_h,q_h)\) is paramodular, the existence of an envy-free matching can be decided in \(O(|E|^2)\) time, assuming evaluation oracles for the lower-quota functions. The algorithm computes a stable matching for the transformed instance with zero lower quotas and the complementary upper-rank functions, then checks whether every hospital receives exactly \(p_h(A(h))\) doctors.

Its continuous counterpart is:

\[ \textsc{Continuous-CSM-EF}_{\mathrm{pm}}. \]

The instance is the same type-mass market, but every quota pair is rational paramodular. The question is whether an envy-free mass matching exists, with the answer consisting of an exact rational mass assignment or a certificate of nonexistence. Here I expect Class A. Paramodularity turns each hospital’s feasible mass region into a generalized polymatroid, so the natural continuous tools are precisely polymatroid separation, generalized flow, and continuous choice oracles. In laminar or staffing versions, the relevant oracle computations should reduce to the same flow machinery that supports the paper’s discrete algorithm.

This second mirror is especially plausible in practice. Large matching markets routinely contain repeated applicant categories and laminar staffing constraints: hospitals may require minimum and maximum numbers of applicants in specialties, training streams, or demographic categories. The paper’s paramodular condition is not an artificial relaxation of the problem; it captures exactly these structured quota systems. The continuous model also raises useful questions absent from the paper: whether exact mass solutions can be rounded with bounded envy or quota violation for finite \(N\), whether the discrete and continuous optima converge under duplication, and whether non-paramodular quota systems produce genuine continuum-specific hardness.

My weakest point is the lead anchor’s realism. The reduction behind Theorem 3.4 uses highly engineered variable and clause hospitals and a non-paramodular feasible-set gadget. Its Boolean structure is precisely what mass splitting may destroy, and a market with \(\tau\) distinct types comparable to \(N\) would not be a convincing continuum regime. The positive case therefore rests more securely on the structured paramodular mirror than on a claim that Theorem 3.4’s NP-hardness survives. Still, this is not a novelty collision: the paper studies a discrete population, whereas these questions make the population itself continuous. Theorem 3.4 and Theorem 4.4 provide two meaningful continuations of the paper’s underlying problem, with the former probing where hardness disappears and the latter giving a strong, natural Class A candidate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is against Theorem 3.4, not against the whole paper.

Its reduction encodes each Boolean variable through the feasible family \(\{D_i^+,D_i^-\}\) of four named doctors. That is precisely the kind of indivisible gadget that does not survive anonymisation. If one replicates the doctors while preserving the pairwise quota constraints, the copies must retain information about their particular gadget, so they are not genuinely identical types. The type space grows with the population. If instead one aggregates copies into two types, the natural feasible region is the convex hull of the two alternatives: a variable may assign fractions of its mass to \(D_i^+\) and \(D_i^-\). The SAT reduction then loses its Boolean choice. Reintroducing purity constraints such as \(x_i\in\{0,\mu_i\}\) simply puts indivisibility back into the model.

Thus the bound \(\lvert C(p_h,q_h)\rvert\le 4\) does not transfer in any canonical way. The theorem’s hardness depends on constraints over a small collection of named agents, whereas a plausible high-multiplicity market would impose quotas over semantic classes such as specialties or demographic categories. Those are much more naturally laminar or staffing constraints, and therefore move directly toward the paramodular setting of Theorem 4.4. Theorem 3.4 can motivate a fractional relaxation, but it does not provide a convincing population mirror of its own hardness result.

The best objection to Theorem 4.4 is that its continuous version risks being only a rescaled generalized-matroid matching problem. For laminar and staffing quotas, the mass-feasible sets are generalized polymytopes. With rational data, one can multiply by a common denominator and obtain a finite clone instance; integrality of the relevant polymatroid structures means that the fractional population model introduces no genuinely new feasibility phenomenon. The generalized Gale–Shapley algorithm is then being applied to a compressed representation of the same matching market.

That objection is not enough, however. Compression in the number of types and polynomial dependence on the bit-length of rational masses are themselves legitimate high-multiplicity questions. A national residency market with recurring preference, specialty, and qualification profiles is a plausible regime, and its continuum is genuinely in the doctor population. The paper’s paramodular constraints are not merely an artificial gadget; they describe precisely the structured quota systems such a market could use. Exact mass algorithms, compressed oracle procedures, and rounding guarantees would all be recognizable computational questions.

Consequently, I would not endorse the universal negative verdict. Theorem 3.4 is a poor anchor: its Boolean hardness is tied to named-agent indivisibility and becomes non-canonical under continuization. But Theorem 4.4 survives the serious objections. At least one worthwhile continuous mirror exists, so the claim that no scenario can produce one is not supportable.
