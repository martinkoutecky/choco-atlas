The closest result is Theorem 7, proved here: for \(n\) candidates, approval ballot hypertriangles \((A_{n-2},A_{n-3},\ldots,A_1)\), with each \(A_s\) a compatible approval ballot triangle of size \(s\), are in bijection with strict-sense ballots. Theorem 2, also proved here using earlier bijections, says that approval ballot triangles of size \(n-1\) are in bijection with TSSCPPs in a \(2n\times2n\times2n\) box. Proposition 10, proved here, bijects approval ballot triangles of size \(n\) with approval ballot sequences of length \(n\). These are combinatorial correspondences, not complexity results. The SSB counting formula in the introduction does not supply a numbered computational anchor either.

Approval ballots make a continuous population sound superficially natural: one could assign mass to approval-set types. But the paper’s key constraints concern the order in which votes arrive and the lead at every prefix. Aggregating voters by approval set erases that order; retaining it would require adding time or sequence information to the model. Theorem 7’s decomposition does not show that such a population model is a continuous analogue of a computational problem studied in the paper.

So there is no honest continuous problem to state per qualifying anchor. The strongest relevant result, Theorem 7, is worth identifying, but treating its bijection as a complexity anchor would overstate what the paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against starts with a category problem: none of the paper’s named results classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 7, Theorem 2, and Proposition 10 are bijections between finite combinatorial objects. The SSB counting formula is not a complexity result either. There is therefore no computational result here for ChoCo to continuize.

Theorem 7 is the strongest possible candidate because strict-sense ballots describe an ordered voting process. One could preserve that order with a continuum of voter positions \(u\in[0,1]\), assigning each position a distribution over approvals and imposing cumulative lead constraints. But then the continuous coordinate is the position in the ballot history. If position is included in a voter’s type, voters at different positions are distinguishable and cannot be aggregated as interchangeable mass. If position is omitted, the cumulative constraints—and thus the feature Theorem 7 encodes—disappear. Either way, this constructs a new feasibility or optimization problem; the paper supplies no computational task whose mirror it would be.

Proposition 10 has the same obstruction in its most literal voting model. Voter \(k\) may approve only candidates in \([k]\), and the condition concerns every prefix. A distribution over approval-set types forgets which voter cast each ballot and when. Keeping the arrival index in the type preserves the process but makes the types position-specific. A continuum of arrival positions may be a coherent model of an ordered process, but its computational question would be added from outside the paper.

Theorem 2 is further removed from a population model: its ABTs encode TSSCPPs through row constraints and lattice paths. Treating array entries, rows, or paths as mass would continuize the combinatorial object, not the society. Recasting approval voters as mass would discard the array structure that the bijection concerns. The same applies to the ABH in Theorem 7: its triangles are compatible components of one ballot history, not groups of voters.

One can imagine worthwhile continuous questions about ordered approval flows, fractional triangles, or volumes of associated combinatorial regions. But those would continue the paper’s mathematics in new directions; they would not mirror a computational result it establishes. That is the firm negative case. The universal claim that no such model could ever be worthwhile is not provable from the paper alone, but any surviving proposal would need to supply both the missing computational problem and a reason its continuous population model is part of this paper’s subject.
