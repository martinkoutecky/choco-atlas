The continuous mirror question

Given locations \(L\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita capacities \(\kappa_l\), and for each type its features \(x_t\), default location \(d_t\), observed default outcome \(y_t\), and predicted scores \(p_{tl}=g_l(x_t)\), find the infimum of \(\sum_{t,l}\mu_t|\breve p_{tl}-p_{tl}|\) over shared type-level scores \(\breve p_{tl}\ge 0\), subject to every maximizer of \(\sum_{t,l}\breve p_{tl}q_{tl}\) over \(q_{tl}\ge 0\), \(\sum_lq_{tl}\le\mu_t\), and \(\sum_tq_{tl}\le\kappa_l\) satisfying \(q_{t,d_t}=\mu_t\) for every type with \(y_t=1\). Specify an attaining or arbitrarily close score table, including tie handling.

The model it lives in

A continuum of refugee types carries mass; fractional \(q_{tl}\) allocates that mass across locations, while one modified score \(\breve p_{tl}\) is shared within each type and edits are weighted by \(\mu_t\). The default placement and observed outcome define which mass must be retained for the counterfactual safety guarantee.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s hindsight minimum-edit problem. It leaves aside the Transformer’s experimental generalization results and the paper’s other propositions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-shared minimum-edit problem admit an exact polynomial-time primal-dual algorithm, including the tie-breaking condition?
  • Does its optimum equal the normalized optimum of a replicated finite pool with the same types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper’s per-pool inverse matching result has a natural high-multiplicity version. In the published version, the anchor is Theorem 4, proved by the authors in Appendix A.4; it adapts an inverse-assignment framework cited from Yang and Zhang. Given one finite refugee pool, its default placements, observed outcomes, predicted employment scores, and location capacities, Theorem 4 gives the minimum \(L_1\) change to the scores that makes every maximum-predicted-utility matching counterfactually harmless. The construction keeps the original scores except on edges used by a policy that retains existing placements and optimally assigns the previously unplaced refugees. On those chosen edges, it sets the modified score to \(\hat u_i+\hat v_l+\epsilon\), where \((\hat u,\hat v)\) is an optimal dual solution to the original matching LP and \(\epsilon\) is infinitesimal to break ties. The paper allows modified scores above \(1\). [Theorem 4 in the published paper](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlresearch/v275/main/assets/lee25a/lee25a.pdf), [PMLR record](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v275/lee25a.html)

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. Call it Continuous Counterfactually Harmless Inverse Matching. An instance has locations \(L\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita location capacities \(\kappa_l\), and, for each type \(t\), features \(x_t\), default placement \(d_t\) (or “unplaced”), observed default outcome \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\), and predicted scores \(p_{tl}=g_l(x_t)\). A type groups refugees identical for this problem: the classifier-relevant features, default placement, and observed outcome are the same. An allocation \(q_{tl}\) is mass of type \(t\) sent to location \(l\), with
\[ \mathcal Q(\mu,\kappa)=\left\{q\ge 0:\ \sum_l q_{tl}\le\mu_t,\quad \sum_t q_{tl}\le\kappa_l\right\}. \]
The safe allocations are those in \(\mathcal Q_{\mathrm{safe}}=\{q\in\mathcal Q:q_{t,d_t}=\mu_t\text{ for every }t\text{ with }y_t=1\}\).

Given this instance, find a nonnegative modified score table \(\breve p\) minimizing the per-capita edit
\[ \inf_{\breve p\ge0}\ \sum_{t,l}\mu_t\,|\breve p_{tl}-p_{tl}| \quad\text{subject to}\quad \arg\max_{q\in\mathcal Q}\sum_{t,l}\breve p_{tl}q_{tl} \subseteq\mathcal Q_{\mathrm{safe}}. \]
A solution is the infimum edit value and a safe modified table attaining it, or arbitrarily close to it; the latter handles the same infinitesimal tie-breaking convention as Theorem 4. The assignment variables are fractional because a mass of otherwise identical refugees can be split across destinations. That is the population continuization. Scores remain input data; the continuous object is the society and its assignment.

A plausible regime is a large jointly allocated national or multi-year intake. Using the paper’s categorical features—five age bands, six origin regions, three education levels, and two sex categories—there are at most \(180\) feature profiles. Including ten destinations plus unplaced status and two observed outcomes gives an illustrative upper bound of \(3{,}960\) types, while a large pooled intake could have hundreds of thousands of refugees. This is a different regime from the paper’s \(100\)-refugee synthetic pools, but it preserves the same assignment question and the same reason for using types: many people receive the same relevant description and score vector. The paper’s Proposition 2 explains why retaining successful refugees at their default locations is a valid safety certificate: those successes persist under the counterfactual, while the remaining binary outcomes cannot subtract from the total. Theorem 4 turns that certificate into a computed inverse-matching solution.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. For rational type masses and capacities, the allocation problem is a transportation LP with \(O(\tau |L|)\) variables, and the edit objective is linearizable. Theorem 4’s primal-dual construction is a strong reason to expect a polynomial algorithm in \(|L|,\tau\), and input bit length \(L\). I would ask the prover to establish the exact minimum and tie handling for type-shared score edits; the paper’s theorem edits individual refugee-score entries, so it does not automatically settle that extension. The authors would, I think, recognize the question: it keeps their predicted-utility matching, capacity constraints, retrospective baseline, and “first, do no harm” objective. It does not claim that fractional outcomes or probabilities are the novelty; only the population is continued.

The weakest point is that type-shared edits constrain the postprocessor more than the individual edits in Theorem 4, and fractional type flows may change which edits are cheapest. So the theorem is a credible algorithmic lead, not a proof that the continuous optimum equals the paper’s finite-pool optimum. Still, the underlying inverse assignment and its dual structure survive, making this a well-motivated question rather than a relabeled outcome-continuity problem.

This case covers Theorem 4’s hindsight, per-pool minimum-edit problem. The paper offers no comparable named hardness result, so this is the best available anchor, though it is an algorithmic one. Further questions include whether the continuous optimum matches the normalized optimum of a replicated finite instance, and whether group-specific safety requirements preserve tractability. I would leave the paper’s Transformer generalization claim out of this mirror: its evidence is experimental, and its unseen-pool learning problem is distinct from the theorem’s inverse matching problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 4’s per-pool inverse-matching result. The paper has no named complexity classification to carry over: Theorem 4 gives a constructive formula for the minimum score edits, while the Transformer results are experimental. That makes the paper a weaker fit for a programme centered on complexity landscapes.

The strongest objection to the mirror is that its safety claim is built from individual records: which refugee was placed where, and which one found work. The SCM also gives counterfactual meaning to each person’s outcome. Grouping only by demographic features would erase those distinctions, and a mass allocation could then obscure who was protected. To preserve all of the causal detail, one might need to include each refugee’s potential outcomes in their type; with continuously varying latent characteristics, that could leave almost no repeated types.

But this does not defeat the best version of the mirror. Theorem 4’s safety certificate needs only the observed default placement and binary outcome, not the full latent response profile. Split types by features, default placement, and observed outcome. Then the constraint \(q_{t,d_t}=\mu_t\) for successful types keeps all successful mass at its original location. The proof that total employment cannot fall carries over to mass utility. The paper’s categorical feature space also makes repeated types plausible in a large intake. That is a credible continuous population, not a distortion of the theorem.

The residual case against is about research value: after grouping, this looks like a transportation problem with a type-level score-edit constraint, close to standard inverse assignment. It may offer little of the complexity boundary or new optimization structure ChoCo seeks, and the individual-edit theorem does not by itself prove the shared-type-edit version. Still, those are reasons to expect a narrow or routine project, not reasons the question lacks a sensible mirror. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor across all plausible scenarios; the negative case is weak here.
