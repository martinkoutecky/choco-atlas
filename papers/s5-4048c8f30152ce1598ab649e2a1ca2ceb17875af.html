The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the legislative bargaining game the paper studies. A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a very large assembly with many representatives but relatively few complete types—for example, representatives who share the same district interest, discount factor, and recognition weight. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s share of seats. A proposal assigns benefits by type; the closed rule puts it to an up-or-down majority vote, while the open rule permits a randomly selected nonproposer to amend it before the vote.

One possible research question is Atomless Amendment Bargaining: given rational type masses \(\mu\), type utilities and discount factors, and a closed or open amendment rule, compute the stationary type-symmetric subgame-perfect equilibria and their outcome distributions, including the proposer’s expected share and the probability of agreement in the first round. The relevant actions are type-contingent proposals, votes, and amendments; a solution is an equilibrium strategy profile. That would connect to the paper’s questions about proposer power, coalition size, and delay.

But this is only an unanchored candidate. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered tables and empirical findings are not complexity anchors; its equilibrium predictions are attributed to Baron and Ferejohn, not stated as numbered results proved here. So there are zero qualifying anchors, and no honest per-anchor classification as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The candidate also exposes the main weakness. In an atomless assembly, a single proposer has zero mass, so their personal allocation does not affect the aggregate budget; likewise, an individual vote cannot change whether a strict majority accepts. That removes strategic features central to this paper’s results. Treating a whole type as a voting bloc could restore pivotality, but would change the game. The paper’s experimental legislature of five subjects is not itself a convincing high-multiplicity regime, either. The positive case is therefore a possible extension of the game they study, not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The paper reports laboratory findings about five-person legislatures: behavior changes over repeated elections, and outcomes differ between open and closed amendment rules. Its equilibrium predictions come from Baron and Ferejohn; this paper neither proves a computational theorem about them nor studies the complexity of finding equilibria. The tables and statistical tests are empirical results, not computational anchors.

The proposed “Atomless Amendment Bargaining” question is coherent, but it would launch a different project: compute equilibria of a type-based bargaining game. A stronger version could retain type-specific utilities, random recognition, and an aggregate majority rule, so the proposer would still shape the agenda even if any one member had negligible mass. That shows the zero-mass objection alone is not decisive. But solving that game would not mirror the paper’s main findings about how actual subjects learn, change their offers, and vote over fifteen elections. Those dynamics are precisely what the experiment measures; replacing subjects with a stationary type distribution changes the object of study.

A finite set of positive-mass blocs could preserve more of the paper’s coalition structure, but it would make blocs the strategic players. That may be a useful model of group bargaining, yet it is still a new model, and it supplies no computational result from this paper to continue. Conversely, keeping individual legislators as the players preserves the paper’s bargaining setup, but does not turn its empirical conclusions into a complexity question.

So none of the proposed anchors survives: there are no qualifying computational anchors in the paper. The honest limit of this negative case is that this does not show continuous equilibrium computation is uninteresting in general. It shows that such a project would be an extension of the paper’s theoretical background, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper established.
