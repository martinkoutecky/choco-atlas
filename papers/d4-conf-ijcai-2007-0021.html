The continuous mirror question

Given a total reporter mass \(N>0\), a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over rational report types \(q_i=(b_i,d_i,u_i)\), a valid upper bound \(B\) on each type's implied evidence total, and \(\varepsilon>0\), compute \(E=N\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i Z^{-1}(q_i)\) to additive error at most \(\varepsilon\) in each coordinate.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete reporter types, each specified by its trust report and any provenance used by the paper's combination rule, with mass \(N\mu_i\) for type \(i\). Apply \(Z^{-1}\) to each supported report, sum the resulting evidence vectors weighted by type mass, and map the exact aggregate through \(Z\) to obtain the combined trust report.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the inverse calculation in Theorem 4 applied to supported report types and the paper's evidence-summing composition; it leaves the certainty-shape results, graph aggregation, temporal discounting, and multivalued events aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bit complexity suffices to evaluate the certainty integral and invert \(Z\) to the required precision?
  • How should per-type precision be chosen to guarantee a requested error after aggregation and mapping the result back through \(Z\)?
  • How should the model handle dependent or correlated reports?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible, though modest, mirror is Population Trust Aggregation at precision \(\varepsilon\). Imagine a large reputation service combining independent reports about one provider and one binary outcome. Many reporting agents use the same report template, so \(N\) agents fall into \(\tau\) report types, with \(\tau \ll N\). A type is the report triple \(q_i=(b_i,d_i,u_i)\), together with any source information that affects how the paper’s combination rule treats it. Its mass is \(N\mu_i\), where \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of reporters of that type. This is plausible for a system with standardized auditors or quantized report templates; it is not a claim that every trust network has few types.

For a fixed finite catalogue of rational report triples, an instance gives the total mass \(N\), rational fractions \(\mu_i\) summing to one, a valid upper bound \(B\) on the evidence total implied by each report, and \(\varepsilon>0\). The paper’s inverse map assigns each report an evidence pair \(e_i=Z^{-1}(q_i)=(r_i,s_i)\). The task is to compute the combined evidence
\[ (R,S)=N\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i(r_i,s_i) \]
to additive error at most \(\varepsilon\) in each coordinate. That pair is a complete answer to the underlying combination problem: by Theorem 3, it determines a unique combined trust report. This is a computation problem, not an optimization problem, so it has no separate decision variable or objective. Fractional reporter mass contributes fractional evidence, which fits the paper’s real-valued evidence model for discounted reports.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved here: “The complexity of Algorithm 1 is \(\Omega(-\lg \varepsilon)\).” It concerns Algorithm 1’s binary search for \(Z^{-1}(b,d,u)\), which computes the evidence corresponding to a trust triple to precision \(\varepsilon\). The theorem gives a lower bound on the search’s precision dependence; the paper does not state a P or NP classification. The binary-search loop itself takes \(O(\log(B/\delta))\) iterations for per-type precision \(\delta\). Taking \(\delta=\varepsilon/N\) suffices for the stated aggregate error, so the mirror needs \(O(\tau\log(BN/\varepsilon))\) calls to the certainty calculation. That is a tractable-looking dependence on the support size and requested precision, but the paper does not analyze the bit complexity of evaluating that calculation. Theorem 3, also proved here, supplies the unique inverse needed for this task; it is supporting mathematics rather than a second complexity anchor.

The discrete bridge is direct. With \(N=n\) reporters and \(\mu_i=n_i/n\), the formula gives exactly the paper’s discrete sum of the \(n_i\) reports’ evidence vectors. In the continuous version, masses can be fractional; the paper already allows real evidence values. The population-level question is therefore whether many independent reporters can be represented by a small distribution over complete report types and aggregated through the paper’s rule. The paper’s Section 3 combination operation is precisely what the authors would be asked to compute.

I would expect this restricted task to be tractable—Class A—by applying the inverse once per supported type and summing by mass. Theorem 1 also makes the population scaling meaningful: at fixed conflict, more total evidence increases certainty. The mirror covers combination of independent trust reports, not the paper’s broader application space or network-level propagation. It raises questions about a full bit-complexity analysis of the certainty integral and inverse, the precision needed after mapping the aggregate evidence back to a trust triple, and how to model correlated reports.

The weak point is that this mirror may offer little new complexity: the discrete combination rule is already simple, and grouping repeated reports is a high-multiplicity representation of it. Also, Theorem 4’s lower bound does not itself prove that the numerical task is polynomial-time. Still, the population regime is a sensible one for this paper’s exact problem: the mirror makes the reporters themselves a mass distribution, and it preserves their report-combination rule rather than replacing it with a different trust question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the weakest part of this anchor: its \(\Omega(-\lg \varepsilon)\) bound is for the iterations of the paper’s chosen binary search, not a lower bound on the inverse problem itself or on population aggregation. In the proposed mirror, a fixed report catalogue lets you precompute each inverse; with variable support, you apply the same single-report conversion to each supported type and take a weighted sum. The population introduces no interaction or choice into the computation. That makes this a thin computational mirror, and Theorem 4 does not establish its complexity.

The strongest version is still easy to model: a large panel of independent auditors uses standardized report templates, with each type containing the report triple and any provenance that affects how it is treated. Then the mass of each type is meaningful, fractional evidence is already allowed by the paper, and combining reports is the authors’ own operation. This is not a prior-art collision: the paper’s evidence space is continuous, but its population of reporting agents remains finite.

That leaves a real weakness in the negative case. The proposed task may add little beyond applying the existing conversion and sum to masses, but the programme explicitly treats computational questions as worthwhile even when the answer is simple. I cannot rule out this mirror on modeling grounds, and “the computation is just a weighted sum” is not enough to defeat it under the stated standard. The honest negative case is therefore weak: Theorem 4 is a poor complexity anchor, but the population-level aggregation question itself survives.
