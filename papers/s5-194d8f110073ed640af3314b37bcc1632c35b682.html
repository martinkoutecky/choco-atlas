The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), a proposed committee \(W\subseteq C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many argumentative-ballot types \(t\), each specified by an attack graph \(R_t\), decide whether every subpopulation vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_t\ge 1/k\), and nonempty grounded extension of \(\bigcup_{t:x_t>0}R_t\) contains a subpopulation vector \(y\) with \(0\le y_t\le x_t\), \(\sum_t y_t>0\), whose pooled graph’s grounded extension intersects \(W\).

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution over complete attack-graph types. Subpopulation mass determines quota eligibility, while the types present in positive mass determine the pooled attack graph; the committee must represent every quota-sized group whose pooled graph has a nonempty grounded extension.

The objection that survived

The paper gives no canonical way to replace its count of eligible voter subsets; the proposed mass quota selects one plausible replacement, but does not derive it from the paper’s cohesion definition.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(\mathsf{ArgJR}\) verification-hardness case of Theorem 7. It does not mirror the other axiom variants, Theorem 8’s construction algorithm, or the paper’s existence and correspondence results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the exact complexity of mass-quota argumentative JR verification and its parameterized complexity in the support size.
  • Assess whether the mass quota is the most appropriate continuous replacement for the paper’s eligible-subset-count cohesion test.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Theorem 7’s verification hardness, mirrored as a mass-quota version of checking argumentative justified representation.

Theorem 7 is proved in this paper, by a reduction from Clique. It says that, for every \(h\), determining whether a given size-\(k\) winner set provides \(\mathsf{ArgJR}\), \(\mathsf{ArgPJR}\), \(\mathsf{ArgEJR}\), or \(\mathsf{ArgEJR}\text{-}\mathsf{Spot}\) is \(\mathsf{coNP}\)-hard. I would anchor on its \(\mathsf{ArgJR}\) case: it is the cleanest bridge to a continuous quota question, and the reduction’s combinatorics can be carried by ballot types.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Argumentative JR Mass Verification. An instance has a finite candidate set \(C\), a committee size \(k\), a finite support \(T_\mu\) of argumentative-ballot types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a proposed committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\). A type \(t\) is a complete attack graph \(R_t\subseteq C\times C\). For any nonempty set \(S\subseteq T_\mu\), its available mass is \(\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\), its pooled ballot has attacks \(\bigcup_{t\in S}R_t\), and \(A(S)\) is the grounded extension of that graph.

Call \(S\) quota-cohesive when \(\mu(S)\ge 1/k\) and \(A(S)\ne\emptyset\). The question is whether, for every quota-cohesive \(S\), there is some nonempty \(U\subseteq S\) with \(A(U)\cap W\ne\emptyset\). A nonempty \(U\) means a subpopulation of positive mass from each of its types; its mass need not itself reach the quota. The mass determines which coalitions can meet the quota, while attack claims continue to pool by the paper’s union rule.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for participatory budgeting: many residents share a relatively small set of complete argumentation templates about the same projects. The exact ballot graph is the type, so its members are indistinguishable for this problem. A finite realization can have \(M\) residents of each type, with \(M\) arbitrarily large relative to \(\tau=|T_\mu|\). This mirror concerns representation under argumentative collective approval; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s other axioms or its winner-construction result.

The expected answer is hardness transfers. The paper’s Clique reduction assigns a ballot to each graph vertex. For the mass version, add at most \(q-1\) isolated vertices so the resulting graph has \(N\) vertices divisible by \(q\), give each vertex type mass \(1/N\), and set \(k=N/q\). A quota coalition must then contain at least \(q\) types in its support. If the graph has a \(q\)-clique, those \(q\) types form a quota-cohesive coalition, and every subcoalition remains inside the clique; the reduction’s gadgets ensure none approves a member of \(W\). If it has no \(q\)-clique, every quota coalition contains a non-edge pair, whose two types form a subcoalition approving the candidates in \(W\). This gives the same Clique-versus-failed-verification distinction with rational masses. The reduction uses many types, so the hardness is carried by the type structure and agenda, not by population multiplicity; duplicating each type preserves the proportions.

My weakest point is that this is not a literal continuization of the paper’s full \(\mathsf{ArgJR}\) definition. Its argumentative cohesion condition counts eligible voter subsets and compares that count with \(2^{|V|/k}-1\); there is no canonical replacement for that subset count in an atomless population. The mirror instead uses a direct mass quota and nonempty grounded acceptance, retaining the union of attacks, eligible subcoalitions, and the group-representation question. An author could reasonably say that this is a new mass version of their problem rather than the continuous limit of their precise axiom. Still, the adapted hardness construction makes the proposed question concrete and recognizably about their central problem: when a group with enough population mass is represented under argumentative ballots.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 7’s distinctive object is not just a population quota. It is the paper’s particular cohesion test: among the subsets of a voter group, count how many are eligible, then compare that count with \(2^{|V|/k}-1\). That statistic is what makes the paper’s argumentative \(\mathsf{JR}\) differ from ordinary mass-quota representation.

There is no canonical way to carry that count to an atomless population. The set of measurable subpopulations is uncountable, and counting them gives no useful cohesion measure. One could put a probability distribution on subpopulations, but the paper supplies no natural sampling rule, and different rules would define different axioms. Taking limits of finite replicated populations does not resolve this: the original count depends on how a subpopulation selects replicas of each ballot type, while the proposed mirror depends only on type support and total mass.

The proposed mass-quota problem is nevertheless well-posed. Its Clique reduction appears to survive: a \(q\)-clique has mass \(1/k\), and without a \(q\)-clique every quota coalition contains a nonedge pair whose ballot gadgets approve a winner. So I cannot defeat the anchor by claiming the reduction fails. The concern is that it establishes hardness for a newly defined quota variant, not a continuous version of the paper’s central cohesion test. In that variant, mass only determines which supports meet quota; any positive amount of a type contributes its entire attack graph. The core computation remains over subsets of ballot types.

That is a real objection to calling this a mirror, but a weak objection to calling it worthwhile. A direct quota variant of argumentative representation is close enough to the paper’s subject that its authors might accept it as their problem. Since they explicitly allow a recognizable mass reformulation, I can’t rule that out. The honest negative case is therefore narrow: the paper’s own cohesion measure has no evident faithful continuum counterpart, while the proposed replacement is plausible but not forced by the paper. I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
